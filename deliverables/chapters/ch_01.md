# The Room With Two Doors

A blue salon above the darkened rue de Lille held the hour in a particular way. Rain that polished the blackout shutters until they looked like shut eyes pressed against its edges, and a silver cigarette case engraved with a dead man's initials seemed to have been left there by someone who understood that objects could outlive the hands that used them. Krystyna entered carrying the public story of the moment: Marion receives a delegation of German officers as the colonel's jeweled companion. She wore it as carefully as the dress, the coat, or the uniformed man's confidence required. The story made room for her, then closed its door.

Beneath it ran the other account. She memorizes the rail seals on the colonel's dispatch folder while appearing to pour brandy. She did not name that account, even inside her own head. Names were handles, and every handle offered the enemy a way to lift what it touched. Instead she noticed the floor, the hinges, the nearest window, the distance to the street. She noticed who looked away and who looked twice. A room announced its loyalties through such small movements.

Somewhere beyond the walls, Warsaw kept its own time. The city had learned to breathe through rubble, to make a market of a courtyard and a message of a loaf of bread. Krystyna felt that hidden breathing now, not as comfort but as an instruction. She had come to this place to change one line in a ledger. The line had changed her instead.

“The room has two doors, Colonel. You keep forgetting which one I use.”

The words did what words always did in an occupied room: they offered one meaning to the person who heard them and another to the person who had learned how to survive their echo. The danger waited in the shape of Riedel's aide watches every hand that crosses the room. Krystyna measured the silence first. Silence was not absence. It was a space in which a decision could be placed without being seen.

“You make everything sound like a riddle,” the other voice said.

“Only the things that have more than one answer.”

She kept her gaze on a silver cigarette case engraved with a dead man's initials. It was easier to let an object witness the exchange. Yet she memorizes the rail seals on the colonel's dispatch folder while appearing to pour brandy pressed against the conversation, asking to be spoken. She gave it a shape that could pass as irritation, vanity, or fatigue.

“You are afraid,” the voice said.

“Of being understood by the wrong person.”

For a moment the room held still. Then a boot sounded outside, or a floorboard settled, and the moment went on without granting either of them permission to call it a confession.

For a little while, the plan worked. Madame Vautrin, the Parisian housekeeper who leaves doors unlocked at the right hour gave way. A silver cigarette case engraved with a dead man's initials changed hands. Riedel's aide watches every hand that crosses the room turned toward some other doorway, some other name. Krystyna let herself feel the narrow satisfaction of it, no larger than the warmth of a match cupped between two palms.

Then the cost arrived. It was never the cost she had calculated. The dispatch folder goes with riedel when he leaves, but its carbon copy remains beneath the silver case. Someone would have to carry the news. Someone would have to believe the altered route, open the marked door, or keep silent when silence became dangerous. The resistance was not an idea hovering above the city. It was made of people who needed bread, who bled, who could misunderstand a signal.

She thought of her mother's Warsaw dining room before the war, when crystal still meant celebration. The thought did not soften the decision. It gave the decision a face. That was worse and better. A face could be mourned; it could also be remembered when the work became arithmetic again.
