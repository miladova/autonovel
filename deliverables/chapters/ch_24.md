# The Last Cipher

The headquarters interrogation room held the hour in a particular way. Dust and electric light after a shell strike pressed against its edges, and the final ledger page seemed to have been left there by someone who understood that objects could outlive the hands that used them. Krystyna entered carrying the public story of the moment: Riedel questions Marion without witnesses. She wore it as carefully as the dress, the coat, or the uniformed man's confidence required. The story made room for her, then closed its door.

Beneath it ran the other account. He lays out the evidence: Paris, the ledger, the false orders, the alias. She did not name that account, even inside her own head. Names were handles, and every handle offered the enemy a way to lift what it touched. Instead she noticed the floor, the hinges, the nearest window, the distance to the street. She noticed who looked away and who looked twice. A room announced its loyalties through such small movements.

Somewhere beyond the walls, Warsaw kept its own time. The city had learned to breathe through rubble, to make a market of a courtyard and a message of a loaf of bread. Krystyna felt that hidden breathing now, not as comfort but as an instruction. She had come to this place to change one line in a ledger. The line had changed her instead.

“You do not want names. You want a witness to your own power.”

The words did what words always did in an occupied room: they offered one meaning to the person who heard them and another to the person who had learned how to survive their echo. The danger waited in the shape of he offers her a place on an evacuation train if she names the council. Krystyna measured the silence first. Silence was not absence. It was a space in which a decision could be placed without being seen.

“You make everything sound like a riddle,” the other voice said.

“Only the things that have more than one answer.”

She kept her gaze on the final ledger page. It was easier to let an object witness the exchange. Riedel questions Marion without witnesses had to remain intact; if it broke, every hidden thing beneath it would be exposed at once. Yet he lays out the evidence: Paris, the ledger, the false orders, the alias pressed against the conversation, asking to be spoken. She gave it a shape that could pass as irritation, vanity, or fatigue.

“You are afraid,” the voice said.

“Of being understood by the wrong person.”

For a moment the room held still. Then a boot sounded outside, or a floorboard settled, and the moment went on without granting either of them permission to call it a confession.

For a little while, the plan worked. The fact that he has delayed reporting her gave way. The final ledger page changed hands. He offers her a place on an evacuation train if she names the council turned toward some other doorway, some other name. Krystyna let herself feel the narrow satisfaction of it, no larger than the warmth of a match cupped between two palms.

Then the cost arrived. It was never the cost she had calculated. Riedel admits he wants her to say he was not a monster to her. Someone would have to carry the news. Someone would have to believe the altered route, open the marked door, or keep silent when silence became dangerous. The resistance was not an idea hovering above the city. It was made of people who needed bread, who bled, who could misunderstand a signal.

She thought of the powder room mirror where Krystyna first admitted the truth. The thought did not soften the decision. It gave the decision a face. That was worse and better. A face could be mourned; it could also be remembered when the work became arithmetic again.

Krystyna took one last look at the headquarters interrogation room. The room had not changed, but she had placed a new fact inside it. She turned toward the next passage. Behind her, the ordinary world resumed its performance. Ahead, the hidden one waited with its hand on the latch.
