# CHAPTER FOURTEEN — Blood on the Sand

The winter games were given by a praetor with a reputation to buy, and Rome turned out for them in the same spirit in which it did everything: completely, noisily, and at the top of its lungs.

The Flavian amphitheatre rose above the crowd like a stone bowl of thunder, four storeys of arches and statues, its awnings already creaking out over the fifty thousand seats, and the streams of people flowed up its stairways in the old, strict order of Roman rank — senators and magistrates down at the marble rings, equestrians behind them, then the ordinary citizens in their packed thousands, and in the highest wooden tiers the women, the foreigners, and the slaves, nearest the sky and the sun.

Hilarus, climbing with the party, gave Sabina the running lecture he gave every newcomer. The amphitheatre seated more people than most cities contained, and it emptied in minutes because there were seventy-six numbered gates and every ticket bore a number; the awnings were worked by sailors from the fleet, stationed at the top; the sand itself was imported from the coast, golden and clean, so that the blood showed. The crowd brought cushions, snacks, dice, and grievances, and the emperor's popularity was measured less by his wars than by the size of the gift tokens he scattered into these seats.

“It's a machine for making Romans agree about something,” Hilarus said, finding their places. “Out there they're brawling over bread and faction colours. In here they all want the same thing at the same moment. Whoever controls the shows controls the city's feelings.”

“Even the women?” Sabina asked, settling her cushion.

“Especially the women. We let them sit highest so their shrieks reach the gods first.” Porcia winked. “It's the only advantage the law allows us; use it.”

Antonia said little. She was reading the room the way she read every room: the distribution of the guards, the quality of the senators' attendance, the presence of the prefect's officers, the absence of the emperor himself, who preferred the army and left the praetors to buy love. A city watching the sand, she had always thought, was a city not watching its accounts, which suited certain men very well.

Antonia's party sat high, among the freedmen and the merchants' families, where Hilarus's money could buy good sightlines but not the marble of rank. Sabina was beside her again, tense and excited, with Chloe on the other side and Porcia behind with a basket of dates, and Festus somewhere in the standing crowd at the back, a wall in a clean tunic.

Down in the marble rings, Marcus sat with the senators, Livia on the cushions behind the women's rail with Vibia and Gaius, and Antonia did not look at him, because fifty thousand Romans were also looking, and the one skill the city demanded of a woman in her position was to be seen looking nowhere she ought.

The morning began with the beast hunts. Stags and wild boar were driven into the sand and killed with arrows and spears; then came the exotic animals Rome paid to see die — ostriches, a giraffe that moved with bewildered dignity before the hunters' ring closed, and a pair of lions that took three men's spears before they fell. The crowd cheered the lions longest, and Antonia, watching the great cat sink onto its haunches with the slow dignity of something that knew it had been betrayed, said quietly to Chloe, “They love the animals better than the men. Because the animals don't know the rules.”

“Nothing in this building knows the rules except us,” Chloe said. “That's the horror of it.”

At the midday break came the executions, the part of the games respectable women were said to avoid and almost no one actually left for. Condemned criminals, two men and a woman, were brought out for the fatal charades the crowd pretended were theatre, and killed in the sand in a sequence of staged myths. Sabina, who had never seen a person die, went white and gripped the cushion, and Antonia put a hand over the girl's eyes without comment.

“Look or don't,” she said. “Neither is shameful. But understand what you're seeing. These aren't monsters. They're people the state found it convenient to kill publicly, so that everyone here remembers what the state can do. The games aren't entertainment. They're a lesson in power, with refreshments.”

The woman in the sand died quickly, at least, and the crowd applauded the professionalism of it, and the servants raked the sand clean, and the afternoon's real business, the gladiators, came out to the roar.

Festus had gone very still beside the back rail.

“You know them,” Antonia said, low.

“I know the school. The secutor in the third pair — that's old school, the Lanista's. I fought him once, nine years ago; he gave me this.” The scarred eyebrow moved. “He'll win. He always wins in the morning; they save him for the afternoon crowd.”

He was right. The fights were brutal, skilled, and carefully managed, the trainers keeping the deaths few enough to remain precious. Twice a man was spared when the crowd turned its thumbs upward, and once a retiarius died slowly in the sand with the noise of fifty thousand voices washing over him like weather, and Antonia watched Festus watch it, and saw in the big man's face neither lust nor horror but the professional's recognition of the moves, and beneath it, very deep, the old relief that it was someone else under the net today.

The old secutor's fight was the afternoon's centrepiece, and Festus narrated it in the low voice of a man reading a scroll. The murmillo opposite him was younger, stronger, and less patient, and for two minutes he seemed to own the sand, pressing the older man backward with the shield. Then Festus said, “Now,” and the secutor stepped inside the wild overreach, turned his hip, and put the gladius through the gap beneath the younger man's arm, and the murmillo folded onto his knees in the strange, tidy way men do when the wound is mortal.

“He let him spend himself. Twelve years, and he still fights with his head. The crowd loves strength; the school pays for patience.” Festus's hands were clasped on the rail, the old arena tension in them. “They'll spare the boy if the editor has any sense; a fighter the crowd loves is money.”

The crowd's roar turned upward, thumb after thumb, and the editor raised the wooden foil, and the wounded murmillo was carried out alive to the gate of life, and Sabina, who had been holding her breath, let it out in a gasp.

“They can choose,” she said. “The people. They can say no.”

“Only to the men who've already been made to fight,” Chloe said. “Don't mistake the crowd's mercy for justice. The choice was made the day the boy was sold to the school.”

Antonia said nothing. She was watching the editor's box, where the praetor took the applause for the pardon he had granted on cue, and thinking that all Rome worked like this: the powerful arranged the deaths, and let the people choose a mercy or two, and called the arrangement liberty.

At the far end of the marble ring, Gaius was watching the same sand with his father's profile and his own thoughts. He had not flinched at the blood; what he could not stop watching were the slaves raking the sand between the acts, men with their own faces, their own tired movements, men the crowd never saw. Marcus, following his eyes, said, “Ask it. Whatever it is.”

“Could any of them be — men who didn't deserve it? Men like the clerk, Hector?”

“Yes. Some are criminals, some are prisoners of war, some are slaves sold for their master's debts. The law sorts them all into the same gate.” Marcus kept his voice low. “The city's cruelty isn't random, Gaius. That's what the poets get wrong. It's organized. Which means it can be examined, and argued, and one day, by men better than me, perhaps changed. You can't change a thing you refuse to look at. Look, and keep your judgement cold.”

---

He came during the interval of the second hour, when the crowd was occupied with the distribution of bread and wooden tokens for gifts, and he came the way they had arranged: Marcus through the upper walkways with Stichus behind, a senator stretching his legs among the common seats, entirely visible, entirely deniable.

He sat in the empty place Porcia vacated with a look Antonia could not read, and for a moment neither spoke, and they looked down together at the raked sand where the servants were smoothing the blood.

Talk at the games had its own grammar: short sentences, eyes on the sand, pauses whenever a vendor or a neighbour leaned near, and never a name that could not be mistaken for a charioteer's. Antonia began it.

“Your brother-in-law's subscriptions. We've finished the matching at the house; Chloe did the columns. Twelve firms, four of them annona contractors, and the witnesses recycle in the same triplet. The name that keeps recurring at the centre is a steward called Hermeros.”

Marcus kept his face toward the arena. “Hermeros. Greek freedman, employed in the imperial household, attached to the finance bureau. I know the name from the audit's pay lists; he handles staff disbursements. A man who pays the bureau's servants can pay anyone's servants.”

“Exactly. And a man who works inside the palace can't be touched by a praetor; only the emperor's household can investigate the emperor's household. Which is why everything we gather has to gather around him without startling him. If he smells an audit, the bureau burns its loose paper in a night and we're left with the shells and no hand.” She accepted a handful of nuts from a passing boy and paid without looking. “My side: the lockpick they sent was a boy from the Subura, paid through two cut-outs, frightened more than vicious. Festus has him, fed and unwatched-feeling, and he's talking. Not the top of the chain — boys like him never see the top — but the next link, the man who paid him, and the place the money came from. When we have the money's origin, we'll have a name to set beside Hermeros.”

“The strong room held, then.”

“The strong room held because they sent a boy and a bent pin against a door Hilarus built. The next attempt won't be so cheap, and that's what worries me. Men who fail with locksmiths start thinking about fires and warrants and witnesses who suddenly leave the city.” She cracked a nut between her teeth. “The Diana temple safe house is being made ready; at the first sign of a move against him, Syrus and his family go there the same night. Dorion stays at Ostia under Hilarus's factors' eyes, where the harbour itself is his guard. Chloe's tablets are already in the coffer. I will not have a single name on our witness list living where a soft voice can reach him without the street knowing it.”

Below them the next procession entered the sand, priests and images and the gleaming helmets of the gladiators, and the crowd roared, and under cover of the roar Marcus said the thing he had come to say.

“The audit office tried to reassign me this week. A flattering posting, the eastern commission, with the implication that a sensible man takes it before the winter's unpleasantness. I declined, politely, in writing. They'll try again, with less politeness. I wanted you to know before it becomes a pamphlet: they mean to separate the auditor from the witnesses. Whatever happens to the office, the evidence is already outside their reach, and I've made sure my own copies are sealed with the family strong box and the prefect's name on the receipt.”

Antonia looked at him then, fully, for the first time since he had sat down, and whatever she saw in his face made her own expression change by one degree. “You understand what refusing that commission costs. The consulship doesn't survive a man who won't be moved.”

“The consulship was always the price, wasn't it? From the first night at your table. Perperna offered me the bargain in two doors, and both doors led to my keeping the chair and losing the case. I've decided I prefer the case.” He turned back to the sand. “Don't thank me. Gratitude makes us both ridiculous, and I've enough ridiculous relatives without joining them. Just make sure none of it was necessary for nothing.”

“That,” she said, lifting her cup to the roaring arena, “is the one guarantee my house has never failed to give.”

“Tyche's interview,” he said, low. “My advocate has the record. They got nothing.”

“Did you expect them to?”

“I expected them to try harder. Which means it was a rehearsal. Perperna doesn't spend a clerk's afternoon for practice; he wanted to measure whether the house can be made to crack. Your lyre player told him it can't, so he'll move to something he thinks can't refuse.” Marcus's eyes moved across the arena. “Celer's statement is sealed. Longinus's family sails for Ostia tonight. The tanner's receipts arrived from Pompeii yesterday. The chain is nearly complete.”

“Attianus?”

“Not yet. I won't take a half-built case to the prefect; he gets one hearing, and everything must be in order. Another two weeks, perhaps three. Can the house hold that long?”

“Can yours?” She kept her face toward the sand. “The broadsheets changed their tune this week. Now it's not just the censor and the courtesan. Now I'm a spy, and you're my dupe. Someone's feeding the writers the audit's details.”

“Zosimus, through the Palatine clerks. It means they know we're assembling evidence and they can't see all of it; the temple coffer frustrates them. A frightened enemy is what we want.” He paused. “Livia sends her regards, in the Roman fashion, which means she said nothing and meant a great deal. She's sitting twelve rows down pretending not to know I'm here.”

Antonia smiled, briefly. “She's the ablest of us. Remember me to her, without warmth and without insult.”

Down in the arena the next pair entered to a roar, and the talk sank beneath it.

“One piece of news from the house,” Antonia said. “Festus caught a man this morning. In the service wing, at the strong-room door, with a turnkey's pick in his hand.”

Marcus turned fully. “Inside the house?”

“Dressed as a lamp boy, and he knew the morning routines, which means he was put in by someone who's watched us properly. Festus has him in the storeroom. He's a professional, not a brave one; he'll talk before the lamps are lit. My money says Perperna's people, looking for the tablets they've now guessed they didn't find on Hector.”

“Be careful what you do with him. A house that tortures a captured spy hands Perperna his next prosecution.”

“We don't torture. We _interview_, with Hilarus's best wine and the worst prospects in Rome laid out clearly. Men like that are rational; give him a better bargain than his employer did and he'll change sides faster than a chariot wheel.” She glanced at him. “Don't look so prim. It's the same thing you do in the Curia, with better wine.”

The ghost of his smile appeared and vanished. “When he talks, send me the name of whoever hired him through the usual channel. If it traces to Zosimus's household, the case gains a head.”

A roar swallowed the sentence; the old secutor Festus had named had thrown his opponent, and stood over him with the blade raised, and the crowd was on its feet screaming for the kill or the pardon, thousands of arms making the same gestures, and in the thunder of it Marcus looked at Antonia and Antonia looked at the sand, and for two heartbeats, in a crowd of fifty thousand, they were completely alone.

Then the crowd's thumbs turned upward, the beaten man was spared, and the moment broke, and Marcus rose.

“Two or three weeks. Hold the house.”

“Hold the name.”

He went back down the long walkway, and Chloe, who had watched the whole exchange while appearing to feed Sabina dates, said, “You're in love with him. I've stopped mentioning it because you've stopped denying it.”

“I deny it now.”

“Badly. You deny it badly, like a girl with no practice.” Chloe ate a date. “It's all right, you know. The world isn't going to end because of it. It's going to end because of grain tallies, which is much more Roman.”

---

Livia Marcella had, in fact, seen everything, because Livia saw everything, and she said nothing at the amphitheatre, and saved it for the walk home, when the children's litter was ahead and she and Marcus shared the second with the curtains half drawn against the winter sun.

“She's very handsome, your business partner. From up here, at any rate.”

“Livia—”

“I'm not going to make a scene; scenes are for wives who haven't made their arrangements. I made mine years ago.” Her gloved hands rested in her lap. “I'll say this once, clearly, and then I'll not repeat it. I don't care what you feel, and I don't require fidelity, which the law doesn't require either. I require two things: the children's name, and the consulship, which is that name's future. If this prosecution fails and the scandal takes the family, we lose both, and I will hold you responsible, and I will use every coin and connection I brought into this house to make sure the children survive their father's mistake. Is that understood?”

“It is.”

“Good. Then tell me the practical position, since my husband's advocate is discreet to the point of uselessness. Is the case strong?”

He told her, in the steady voice of a briefing officer: the tablets, the weight notes, the seals, the witnesses, the bureau freedman at the head of it, the timetable toward Attianus. She listened with the attention she gave the household accounts, and when he finished she was quiet a while.

“Then win,” she said simply. “I've no interest in being ruined for a good cause. Win, and afterward we'll all be very dignified about whatever comes next. The Aelii survive. That's the only vow I've ever truly meant.”

At the games, before the litters, she had sat through her own smaller war, among the consular wives in the women's section, where the conversation was a battlefield dressed in silk. Crassicia, still nursing her dinner-table wound, had spoken at some length about “women who forget the door they came in by,” and the circle had smiled and watched Livia's face.

“Some women come in by doors,” Livia had answered, examining her nails. “Others build the door. I'm not sure which is the greater achievement, but I'm certain it's boring to discuss at the games.”

The circle had laughed, carefully, and Crassicia had changed the subject, and Livia had spent the rest of the afternoon treating the women around her to the full force of her attention, because that was her weapon: she knew the names of every woman's children, the state of every dowry, the secret of every unhappy marriage, and she dispensed that knowledge in small, perfect doses that made her the most valuable friend and the most dangerous enemy in the section.

But when she watched her husband climb to the high wooden tiers and sit beside the freedmen and the singers, what she felt, under the pride and the calculation, was the oldest wound of the married Roman woman: she had given her whole life to a structure, and another woman was living inside its walls. Not a rival — Livia had measured Antonia and found her too proud to take another woman's place. Something stranger: a woman whose existence proved that the structure was not the whole world.

She set the feeling aside, as she set aside all feelings that did not serve the children, and watched the sand, and made her arrangements. The Aelii would survive. That was the only vow she had ever truly meant.

The litter climbed the Esquiline, and below them Rome poured out of the amphitheatre into the dusk, fifty thousand voices arguing about the fights, and the lamps began to light along the streets, and the city looked, as it always did from a height, magnificent and indifferent and permanent, though Marcus knew by now that nothing in it was permanent, and least of all the things that looked it.

---

The captured spy talked before the third watch, exactly as Antonia had predicted, over a cup of Hilarus's best Setine and a clear explanation of his options.

He was a thin, furtive man of thirty, with the locksmith's callus on his right thumb and a face trained to look harmless, and Festus had him on a stool in the storeroom with his tools laid out on a table beside him: the pick, a block of wax for impressions, a small knife, and the half-purse of new silver. Antonia interviewed him herself, with Chloe writing and Festus filling the doorway, and she began, as she always did, by removing the fear.

“No one's going to hurt you. Say that back to me, so I know it landed.”

“No one's going to hurt me.” He said it doubtfully.

“Correct. Here's why. If I hurt you, you say whatever I want, which is worthless in court. If I treat you fairly and witness the statement, it's worth a great deal, and you walk out of this house with your skin and perhaps your freedom. Your patron, on the other hand, has a dead clerk on his conscience and a habit of closing loose ends. The Tiber is full of men who only knew one patron. Whose side is safer, Moschus?”

He stared at the wine, then at Festus, then at the quiet woman in the dark dress, and made the calculation that frightened men always make when the alternatives are laid out honestly.

He was a locksmith's assistant from the Tiber bank, hired by a soft-voiced gentleman he knew only as “the patron,” through two intermediaries, to make an impression of the strong-room lock and report what tablets it held. He had been paid half in advance, in new silver, and promised the rest on a drawing of the lock.

“The first intermediary's a runner called Afer, works the taverns by the cattle market. The second — I only saw him twice — he's a household man, soft hands, Greek accent, wore a signet of Minerva. He paid. Afer called him ‘the steward from the hill,' nothing more.”

Chloe's stylus moved. Antonia's eyes did not change. A steward from the Palatine, with a Minerva signet and a Greek accent, was not proof of Zosimus, but it was a door, and doors in Rome led somewhere if you pushed them correctly.

“Why the strong room? What did they tell you to find?”

“Tablets. Wax, four of them, bound. They said — he said — they were stolen property, that the house here was keeping what didn't belong to it. I was to copy the lock and count the tablets, nothing else. I'm a locksmith, domina. I don't ask what the doors are for.”

“Everyone asks. You asked less than most, which is why they hired you.” She let him sit with that. “One question more, and answer carefully, because it's the one the praetor will care about. Were you told what would happen if you were caught?”

The furtive face worked. “Afer said if it went wrong, no one knew my name. That's all. No one knew my name.”

“Yet here we are, names and all. You see the value of a promise from these men.” She rose. “Festus, lock him up comfortably — food, water, a lamp — and put a guard on the door he'd thank us for. He's a witness now, and witnesses are expensive. Chloe, copies of the statement to the praetor's office and the auditor tonight. The silver goes to Beryllus for tracing; new coin moves through fewer hands than old.”

“New silver,” Chloe said, when Festus brought the statement to the tablinum. “Coins can be traced.”

“They can if anyone keeps the records. Beryllus knows every large withdrawal in the city.” Antonia read the statement twice. “The two intermediaries are the useful part. Find them, Festus. Quietly. If one of them belongs to Zosimus's household, the patron stops being a shadow.”

“He'll run when he doesn't report back.”

“He won't run tonight; he thinks the locksmith's boy succeeded, because we haven't raised an alarm. Let him think it. By tomorrow noon I want his name, his patron, and his route home.” She set the statement with the growing file. “And Moschus?”

“Locked in the old storeroom, fed, guarded. He's more afraid of his employer than of us, which is working in our favour.”

“Keep him afraid of the right things. Kindness for the turncoat, always; a man who changes sides once will change twice unless he likes the new side better.”

When Festus had gone, Chloe sat with her a while in the quiet, and the distant city noises came through the shuttered windows, and she said, “The arena today. I've never been to the games. I didn't know it would feel like that. Like the whole city was one mouth.”

“It is one mouth. The trick is never being the thing it eats.” Antonia poured two cups and pushed one across. “We're small people, you and I, by their measurements. No names, no rank, no ancestors. Small people survive by being better organized than the mouth. We have the evidence, the witnesses, the terms, and now their spy's own statement. When we finally stand in the Forum, Rome will discover that the smallest people in the room built the biggest case.”

“Perperna will have something ready. Something loud.”

“Let him. Loud men lose to prepared women; it's the one rule the Republic left us.” She raised her cup. “To the prepared. And to Moschus, who is having a very educational evening.”

Chloe raised hers, and somewhere beyond the walls the watch called the third hour, and the great city slept its uneasy sleep, full of mouths and knives and the small, stubborn people who meant to outlast both.

Beryllus's answer came before noon the next day, carried by a banker's boy with a sealed note and verbal instructions to wait for no reply.

The Alexandrian had run the new silver through his contacts at the treasury's assay office, and the coin was fresh-minted from a batch paid out, three weeks earlier, to the household staff disbursement of the imperial finance bureau. The batch lists did not name servants, but the amounts narrowed it; and Beryllus, whose genius was patience, had matched the sum to a single pay packet drawn by the steward's office for a Greek freedman named Hermeros, whose signet, the paymaster cheerfully recalled over a good lunch, was a Minerva.

_Hermeros is one of Zosimus's people_ , the note concluded. _Your lockpick was paid, through two fools, by the emperor's own bureau. I have written nothing else down, because even my strong room has ears. Be careful, my dear. You are no longer fighting a prosecutor. You are fighting a wall, and walls do not bleed._

Antonia burned the note, as she burned everything Beryllus sent, and sat a while with the ash in the dish, thinking.

Then she sent for Festus, and gave him the name and the description, and said, “Find him. Don't touch him. I want to know where he drinks, where he sleeps, and whose door he waits at. A man who works for a wall still has to walk home at night, and a man walking home has habits. The prefect's case needs a name with a face. We're going to give it both.”

Festus found him in three days, because Rome is a village for anyone with the right network, and the gladiator school's retired men were everywhere: the tavern keepers, the linkboys, the sedan-chair carriers, the porters at every gate. Hermeros turned out to be a small, dapper Greek of fifty, careful with his clothes and careless with his routine, and a routine, Festus explained to Antonia over the map of the city they kept marked in charcoal, was the shadow a man threw without knowing it.

“He leaves the bureau at the eighth hour, every day. Mondays and Thursdays, the Baths of Titus, where he plays dice badly. Tuesdays, a woman in the Subura — respectable, thinks he's a clerk with prospects; I've said nothing to her, that's her business. Fridays, the wine shop by the Aventine bank, where he meets the same man, an hour, separate tables, money never crosses the open, it goes under a napkin. Then home, a rented room above an olive shop on the Caelian. Pays on the first, in good coin, keeps to himself, tips poorly.”

“Who's the Aventine man?”

“Working on it. The bank's a depositor of Longinus's circular payments, which makes the shop a hinge in the laundering. My guess is the Aventine man carries the coin from Hermeros to the next hand, and the next hand buys the shells.” Festus tapped the map. “He's small, domina. He's a courier with a nicer tunic. But small men walk the road between the big ones, and roads can be watched.”

“Watched, never touched. Attianus's inquiry must take him, not us; if he's found in a gutter the case gets sick. I want a record: his habits, his contacts, the Aventine meetings, all of it, in plain writing, one copy for the strong room and one for the prefect's office.”

“He'll know he's followed eventually. They always do.”

“By then it won't matter; the record will exist. The trick is to be boring long enough. A man who notices nothing alarming assumes nothing is happening, which is the assumption that has ruined more clever men than any trap.”

Festus grunted, the closest he came to laughter, and went back out into the streets with his charcoal and his patience, and for the next two weeks the dapper Greek walked through his Roman life with a gladiator's shadow he never once detected, and every line of his routine went down on paper, the unremarkable daily evidence of how the money walked from the bureau to the wall.
