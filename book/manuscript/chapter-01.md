# CHAPTER ONE — The House That Money Built

The last guest left at cockcrow, carried out between two of his own slaves with his garland askew and a line of Greek poetry drowning in his throat. Festus shut the gilded door behind him, dropped the bar into its brackets, and stood for a moment with his broad back against the wood, listening to the litter bearers' footsteps fade down the Carinae.

“Gone,” he said.

Antonia Cassia was seated at the counting table in the atrium, where the lamps had burned low and the dawn light was beginning to grey the opening in the roof. She had not sat down once during the six hours of the supper. Now she wrote in her ledger by the uneven flame, her stylus moving quickly across the wax, setting down what each man had drunk, what he had said, and what it was worth.

“Not all gone,” she said, without looking up. “Praetor Gratus left his signet ring again. Send it back at the third hour with a note saying nothing. His wife opens his correspondence.”

Festus grinned. The scar that split his left eyebrow whitened when he did it. “Third hour. She'll think he spent the night in a temple, praying for chastity.”

“She'll think whatever she likes, so long as she does not think of this house.” Antonia turned the stylus and smoothed the wax flat with its broad end. “The consular's fish: he had three helpings of the mullet and none of the wild boar. Note it. Men who refuse boar are either ill or afraid of dying, and either makes them generous. The olive merchant from Baetica drank four cups of unmixed wine and wept about his mother. See that he wakes to a wreath of the flowers she'd have liked. Whatever those were.”

“Roses,” said Phyllis, coming through from the garden court with a ring of keys at her belt and a folded cloth over her arm. “His mother grew roses. He told the cook. I've already had the garland made.” She was sixty years old, thin and straight as a reed, with grey hair drawn tight under her kerchief and the shrewd, tired eyes of a woman who had managed other people's households since before Antonia was born. She looked at the lamps, the scattered cushions, the dark wine pools on the mosaic. “You should sleep. The girls are abed. I'll see to the wreckage.”

“I'll sleep when the house is clean enough that I could eat off its floor.” Antonia closed the ledger. “In this house, my dear, the floor is the first thing they remember and the second thing they pay for.”

It was a house worth remembering. From the street a passer-by saw only a long blank wall of tufa and the celebrated door, its bronze leaves overlaid with gold leaf so that it glowed even on rainy mornings, flanked by two footmen in matched blue tunics. Beyond the door lay a vestibule paved in black-and-white mosaic showing leaping dolphins, then an atrium with Corinthian columns, a marble impluvium where fish moved under lily pads, and walls painted in the new style, deep red above and black below, with slender architectural fantasies rising between them. To the left was the dining wing, three triclinia that could be thrown into one; to the right the private rooms, the baths, the kitchens humming like a small town. At the back, a colonnade looked onto a garden of clipped box and roses, with a fountain in the shape of a boy wrestling a goose.

Everything in it had been bought, and everyone who came through the gilded door knew it. That was the house's particular genius. Old money pretended to have sprung from the earth like the stones of its own villas. Antonia's money admitted, calmly and without apology, that it was money — counted, weighed, and spent with perfect judgement.

She rose and walked the rooms now, as she did after every supper, while the household slaves moved around her with cloths and bowls and armfuls of fresh roses. In the great triclinium the couches still bore the hollows of men's bodies. A plate of dates had been overturned; a second-century silver cup — a Greek piece, worth a small fortune — lay under a table where a praetorian officer had kicked it. She picked it up herself and set it on the sideboard.

“Anything broken that I must pretend not to notice?”

“One glass goblet, Alexandrian,” said Phyllis, following her. “The one cut like a flower. The procurator's cousin. He knocked it over reaching for the dancer.”

“Add it to his bill with a tenth on top. He'll pay without a murmur and tip the messenger besides, to keep us quiet about the dancer.” Antonia touched a cushion; the silk was damp. “Burn the myrrh. The whole wing smells of a Syrian temple.”

Chloe appeared in the colonnade, barefoot, wrapped in a saffron mantle with her black hair loose to her shoulders. She was Greek, from Athens by way of the slave market at Delos, and she carried herself like a woman who had decided long ago that the world could take her as it found her. She yawned, saw Antonia, and lifted one hand in a kind of salute.

“Is he gone? The procurator's cousin? He told me his wife didn't understand him. At length. In verse.”

“His wife understands him perfectly,” said Antonia. “That is why he must come here to be misunderstood.”

Chloe laughed, then winced and pressed a hand to her temple. “Wine. I shall drink water for a month. Tell me I said nothing damaging.”

“You told him that a man with his income should buy better poets. He took it as flirtation.”

“It was flirtation. Flirtation is the house's trade. I'm going to the bath. Is there hot water?”

“There is always hot water in this house,” said Phyllis, with the pride of a general stating that the grain ships had arrived. “Go on. All of you. I'll call you at noon.”

Antonia watched her go with the particular attention she gave each of the women under her roof — checking the set of the shoulders, the tone of the voice, the small signs that told her whether a night had been merely long or had turned ugly. Chloe was tired, nothing more. Good. The rules were written into the doorposts as far as Antonia was concerned: the guests paid for music, wine, conversation, beauty, and the company of women who knew how to make a man feel remarkable. What happened beyond that, between consenting adults in a closed room, was arranged in daylight, with contracts, and never under this roof against a woman's will. Men who forgot the rule once were shown it by Festus. Men who forgot it twice were shown it by Festus in the street, in front of their litters.

It had taken her eleven years to make the rule stick. Rome had a thousand houses where a man with money could do exactly as he pleased. There was only one, Antonia had decided, where he could not, and the rarity of it was what filled the gilded door every night.

She was turning back toward the atrium when Festus appeared again, and this time the scarred eyebrow was drawn together.

“Someone at the postern. A man named Nucerinus, with a girl. He says you're expecting goods.”

“I'm expecting fish from the market and snow from the mountains,” Antonia said. “I am not expecting Nucerinus. What does he want?”

“He has papers. He wants money, and he wants it before the street wakes up.” Festus hesitated, which with Festus meant a great deal. “The girl's in the passage. She doesn't look like goods.”

Antonia set down her stylus. “Bring him to the tablinum. And bring the girl to the garden bench. Phyllis, give her something hot. Not wine — water with honey. And close the postern behind them. The neighbours count everything.”

---

Nucerinus was a freedman with soft hands and a mantle too fine for the hour, the sort of man who had discovered that Rome's debts were more profitable than Rome's goods. He came in smiling, bowing, holding out a folded document as though it were a birthday gift.

“Domina Antonia. Honoured. My patron, the lender Vipsanius, sends his deepest respects. A small matter of contract, settled discreetly at this hour so that no one's dignity need suffer in daylight.”

“Your patron's dignity has never kept me awake,” Antonia said. She did not ask him to sit. “Show me the paper.”

It was a contract of debt, written in the cramped hand of a public scribe and sealed with two witnesses' marks. Quintus Avianius, a smallholder from the land beyond the fourth milestone on the Appian Way, had borrowed three thousand sesterces against his next two harvests. The harvests had failed, or the price had failed, which came to the same thing in the end. Beneath the debt was written the security he had pledged: himself, his wife, and his unmarried daughter, to be bound to the lender's service until the principal and interest were discharged.

Antonia read it twice. The form was legal. Everything about it was legal, the way a knife between the ribs can be legal if the right man holds the handle.

“Interest at thirty-six per cent,” she said.

“Twelve per cent, domina, compounded each quarter. The law permits it.”

“The law permits a great many things it would be kinder to drown. Where is Avianius now?”

“His wife is sick. He stayed at the farm. The girl came of her own accord, under the terms.” Nucerinus smiled again, small and quick. “Vipsanius has no use for a farm girl in his own house. He thought of you. A handsome piece, young, sound, unbroken. In the Subura they'd take her for whatever she'd fetch and turn her out at thirty. In a house like this — well. A girl could learn a trade here. Refined company. Music. Perhaps you'd keep her yourself.”

The implication hung in the room like smoke. Antonia kept her face still. “How much?”

“Five thousand sesterces. The debt, the costs, and a small consideration for the discretion of the hour.”

“The debt is three thousand. The costs are invented. I'll give four thousand, and I'll take the contract and the girl together, with a full quittance signed before my own witnesses. At that price Vipsanius has his money back before breakfast and his consideration besides. At any higher price I send you to the public auction, where she'll go for two thousand and he'll wait a month for it.”

Nucerinus opened his mouth. Antonia looked at him. She had been looking at men like him across tables for fifteen years, and she had learned that silence, held one beat longer than was comfortable, was worth two cohorts of soldiers.

“Four thousand,” he said.

“Festus will count it out in the strong room. Sit there. Do not touch anything.”

She went out through the colonnade to the garden.

The girl was on the stone bench under the pergola, clutching a cloth bundle on her knees. Phyllis sat beside her with a cup, but the cup sat untouched. She was seventeen, perhaps eighteen, with the broad shoulders and sun-weathered hands of a girl who had carried water and cut grapes since she was small. Her dress was clean but mended twice at the hem. She had a wide, plain, honest face, and she was doing everything she knew how to do not to cry. Her eyes moved over the garden, the fountain, the painted colonnade, with the expression of a person watching a ship sink from the shore.

Antonia sat down at the other end of the bench and left a careful space between them.

“What is your name?”

“Sabina.” The voice was steady, though the hands on the bundle were not. “Sabina Aviania. My father is Quintus Avianius. He's an honest man. The grapes rotted on the vine two years running, and then the olive fly—”

“I know how farms fail. It is rarely the farmer's fault, and it is always the farmer's ruin.” Antonia spoke plainly, without the soothing tone men used to frighten slaves. “Do you know what this house is?”

Sabina's jaw tightened. “Yes, domina.”

“Then tell me what you think it is, and we'll find out how much of it is true.”

“A place where men come. Where women —” She stopped, swallowed, began again. “Where women entertain them. My mother said I'd be better off throwing myself in the Tiber. My father said honour was a luxury for people who ate regularly.” The steadiness cracked at the edges. “He cried when I left. He went down on his knees in the yard. I told him to get up, because my mother needed him standing.”

Antonia regarded her for a long moment. Under the fear there was something hard, a knot of something that would not bend. She recognized it. She had carried the same knot herself, once, into houses far worse than this one.

“Listen to me, Sabina Aviania, and remember it, because I say it once. In this house no man will touch you unless you agree to it, in writing, before witnesses, for a price you have seen and accepted. No one will beat you. No one will lend you out. In return, you will work — hard, at whatever you are given — and you will learn. Reading, figures, music, the proper way to carry a cup and the proper way to answer a consul. Girls who leave this house leave with silver in their hands and a trade. Do you understand?”

The girl stared. “Why would you buy me, then, if not—”

“Because Vipsanius would sell you to someone who would. Because four thousand sesterces is less than I lose in a bad evening at dice, and because a house is only as strong as the people in it.” Antonia stood. “Phyllis will take you to the bath. You'll sleep in the women's wing, in a room with a door that bolts on the inside. Tomorrow you begin lessons. The bolt is your right. The lessons are your duty.” She paused. “Your father's debt paper comes to me now, not to Vipsanius. That means he answers to me. I may have use for an honest farmer one day. Honest men are rarer than girls, and Rome has always been short of both.”

Sabina looked down at the cup in her hands as if she had never seen honey-water before. When she looked up, the tears had won, but she was angry at them, wiping them away with the heel of her hand.

“Why are you doing this?”

Antonia almost smiled. “Because I was the girl on the bench. A long time ago. And no one said any of it to me.” She turned toward the house. “Phyllis. See to her. And send Festus to me with the quittance. I want it in the strong room before Nucerinus's foot touches the street.”

On the Esquiline Hill, in the domus of the Aelii, the morning began as every morning began, with the salutatio.

Marcus Aelius Severus came down before sunrise in a plain white tunic, his sandals freshly soled, his beard trimmed by a slave whose one duty in life was to keep it exactly even. He was thirty-eight years old, tall and straight, with the long, narrow face of the old Roman statues and grey beginning at his temples that the senators' wives said made him look like a man out of the Republic. He carried himself the way his father had taught him, as though the whole city were watching even in an empty room, which in Rome was very nearly true.

The atrium was already full. Clients stood in careful order against the walls, in the careful order they had spent years earning — old friends near the open doors, newer petitioners further back, the professional morning-callers who lived on the sportula dole near the exit where their baskets could be filled without disturbing anyone. The nomenclator Stichus stood at his master's elbow, murmuring names and circumstances into his ear in a voice pitched for no one else.

“Lucius Gavius, farm dispute with his neighbour over a boundary wall. Gaius Memmius, the schoolmaster, comes about the rent. The widow Hortensia, her son's army discharge papers, she was here last month. The two Greeks are poets, they want a patron, they have been told not to recite before the eighth hour.”

Marcus took his chair — the old curule chair his grandfather had used, its ivory arms worn smooth — and the first man came forward.

He did the morning's work the way he did everything: thoroughly, without warmth but without cruelty, giving each man the full force of his attention and a decision he would have to live with. The boundary wall, he ruled, would be surveyed at the shared cost of both neighbours, and he would lend his own surveyor; the schoolmaster's rent would wait until the new term's fees came in, and he was not to be charged interest; the widow's papers he signed himself, cutting through three offices of army clerks with a single line and the name that opened every door in Rome. The poets he gave a small sum and a polite refusal, and bore their thanks with the expression of a man having a tooth drawn.

It took two hours. When the last client had bowed himself out, a small boy appeared in the atrium doorway with a scroll clutched to his chest and ink on his nose.

“Gaius,” said Marcus, and something in the formal face softened, just slightly, the way a frost softens when the sun reaches it. “You have ink on your nose.”

“I know, Father. I put it there to make the slaves think I've been working.” The boy came forward and presented the scroll. “It's my translation. The Xenophon. The part where the army reaches the sea.”

Marcus took it and read a page, slowly, while his son stood rigid with pretended patience. “Your word order is better. Here, though — you've made the soldiers shout ‘the sea, the sea' as if they were ordering dinner. They had marched through a desert. They thought they were going to die. Make the words smaller. Big feelings go in small words.”

Gaius considered this with complete seriousness. “Small words. Yes. I'll do it again.”

“Again, and then again after that. Nothing good is ever finished, it is only stopped in time.” Marcus handed back the scroll. “Where is your sister?”

“Trying to teach the cat to sit on a perch. The cat considers it beneath her.”

“A sensible cat. Go and wash your nose.”

The boy went, and Marcus watched him go with the private weight that fathers in great houses carried. Gaius was fourteen, bookish and tender-hearted, born to a class that required its sons to be neither. The army would take him in two years, as it took every young man of his rank, and Marcus had already begun the long, quiet work of making him hard enough to survive it without making him cruel enough to suit it. It was the most difficult thing he had ever attempted, and there was no one to ask whether he was doing it well.

Livia came down the stairs as the boy crossed the hall, and paused to let him pass with a hand on his shoulder. She was a handsome woman of thirty-three, dressed with the severe elegance of the old aristocracy — no eastern silks, no pearls the size of chickpeas, just fine wool, gold at her ears, and her dark hair wound in the fashion of the emperor's wife. She managed the household's five hundred slaves, its accounts, its rituals, and its reputation with a competence that Marcus respected entirely and a distance that had become, over fifteen years, the settled weather of their marriage.

“Good morning,” she said. It was a courtesy between diplomats.

“Good morning. The widow Hortensia's business is done. I signed the discharge.”

“You sign everything she brings. Half the widows in Rome are beginning to lose their husbands on purpose.” She sat across from him, and Stichus poured her water. “Your brother is breakfasting in the garden. He arrived an hour ago in a litter with four Syrians and perfume you can smell in the street. I have put him where the clients can't see him.”

“Thank you. I'll deal with Celer.”

“There's more. A note came from the consul's office. They want you at the treasury at the fourth hour, something about supply contracts. And an invitation.” She held out a folded square of pale, expensive paper, sealed with no name, only a small gilded door pressed into the wax. “A supper. Tonight. Every woman I know has a husband who has received one, and none of them will say what the place is, which means they all know perfectly.”

Marcus turned the seal over in his fingers. “Where is it?”

“The Carinae. The house of Antonia Cassia.” Livia's voice was perfectly neutral, the neutrality of a woman who had made neutrality into armour. “They say it is the best table in Rome. They also say a great many other things. You are not obliged to go.”

Marcus set the invitation on the table. His first instinct was to refuse, and he nearly said so; but he was a man trained to examine his instincts for self-indulgence, and he examined this one. The truth was that he disliked the idea of the house because he disliked the idea of being the sort of man who needed such houses, and that was a personal discomfort, not a reason of state.

“I'll think on it,” he said.

Livia rose. “Do that. Your brother is eating my best figs.”

---

He found Decimus Aelius Celer reclining on a garden couch under the plum tree, with a plate of figs, a dish of olives, and a young slave to fan him, looking like a portrait of the god Bacchus done by a painter who had been paid too much. Celer was four years younger than Marcus, handsome in a soft, golden way, and gifted with the family charm in the same proportion that Marcus had been given the family stubbornness. He smiled broadly and lifted his cup.

“Brother. The terror of the morning clients. Sit. Eat a fig. These are from the consul's own gardens, though I shouldn't tell you how I know.”

“You should not.” Marcus sat on the bench, upright, the way he sat on benches. “You are out early. And out of breath. The four Syrians again?”

“One must make an entrance. A litter with two bearers is merely transport. With four it's a statement.” Celer ate a fig, examined his fingers, and wiped them on the cloth the slave offered. “I came because I need a word before the treasury business. You're to look at the supply contracts, aren't you? The eastern preparations. Everyone knows the consuls want an honest name on the review, and your name is the only honest one left that hasn't got gout.”

“Who told you?”

“Rome told me. Rome is a woman with a very loud voice and no secrets.” The charm held, but Marcus saw the flicker beneath it, the quick calculation behind the smile. “I'd take it as a brother's kindness if you'd — well. Move carefully. There are good men in those contracts. Men with families. A review that turns into a persecution ruins people who never meant harm.”

Marcus looked at him. “Are you in those contracts, Decimus?”

“Me?” The laugh was a half-beat too quick. “I'm a gentleman, not a grain merchant. I merely know some of the firms. Subscribed to one or two, the way one does. Nothing that wouldn't bear the light.” He reached for another fig. “Do come to the supper with me tonight. Antonia Cassia's. I've an invitation, you've an invitation, all the best men have invitations. It's where the contractors talk when they think no one's listening. If you're going to audit their accounts, hear their confessions first. And the music is said to be extraordinary.”

“I've heard.”

“Then say yes. For Rome. For the treasury. For the honour of the Aelii.” Celer grinned. “And because if you spend one more evening reading legal commentaries in an empty house, Stichus will die of boredom and leave you without a nomenclator.”

Marcus did not smile. He was thinking about contracts, and about the way his brother's fingers had tightened, very slightly, on the fig.

“I'll decide at the treasury,” he said. “Eat your figs. And Decimus — if there is anything in those contracts that ought to bear the light and doesn't, come to me before it comes to the consuls. That is the only kindness I can offer, and it has an expiry.”

The charm held. The smile did not waver. But after Marcus had risen and walked back into the house, Celer sat for a while without eating, looking at the little fountain in the fishpond, and the fig in his hand slowly bruised black where his thumb pressed into it.

---

At the fourth hour, Marcus walked down to the Forum with Stichus behind him and two footmen ahead to clear the lane. Rome roared around him, as Rome did at every hour — bakers shouting from shop fronts, a mule train loaded with building stone, children chasing a dog with a tin cup tied to its tail, a water-seller ringing his bell, the long low thunder of a hundred thousand lives being lived at close quarters. He crossed the new forum the emperor had built beyond the old one, where workmen were putting the last touches to the great column that would carry his campaigns to the sky, and allowed himself one moment of the feeling that always touched him there: the cold, heavy sense of Rome's scale, of the machine he had been born to serve.

At the treasury the praetor in charge handed him a bundle of sealed tablets with the weariness of a man washing his hands.

“Army supply, eastern schedule. Grain, leather, tents, transport. The consuls want a name above suspicion on the tally, and yours came up first. Don't thank me. Half the firms are senators' investments, including men you dine with. You'll make enemies.”

“I have enemies,” said Marcus. “They are among my few comforts.”

He walked home through the crowded streets with the tablets under his arm and his brother's fig-bruised fingers in his mind, and somewhere on the slope of the Esquiline he made his decision. That evening, he told Stichus, he would go to the supper in the Carinae. He would eat the famous food, listen to the famous music, and watch the contractors when the wine went round. A man who intended to audit the accounts of the richest men in Rome could not afford to be the only man in the city who had never seen behind the gilded door.

He said the name to himself as he walked. Antonia Cassia. It meant nothing to him. He had never heard it. Why should he? Rome was full of clever foreigners and freedwomen with money. He expected a painted old woman with a shrewd eye and a flock of musicians.

He expected nothing at all, in fact, which is exactly when life chooses to strike.

---

In the house in the Carinae, the morning's work was done. The floors were clean, the lamps were trimmed, the fish in the impluvium had been fed, and the strong room now held, beside the night's silver, a signed quittance and the debt-paper of one Quintus Avianius.

Antonia went up to her private room at the top of the house, the only room in the building with a lock on the inside rather than the outside, and opened the small cedar box she kept beneath her bed. It held a few things: a pair of good earrings, her manumission papers, folded thin and soft with handling, and at the bottom, wrapped in a scrap of old cloth, a chipped wooden disc about the size of a coin, with letters carved into it by a boy's unsteady hand.

She unwrapped it and sat with it in her palm.

She did not do this often. The past was a country from which she had emigrated without papers, and she had no intention of visiting it. But the girl in the garden had opened a door in her, and behind that door there was a kitchen, much smaller than this one, and a hand covering hers around a stylus, and a boy's voice, low and patient, saying: Again. From the top. A — B — C. You're quicker than I am, and if the tutor knew I'd be beaten.

She could not remember the boy's face any more. She had trained herself not to. She remembered the hand, though. Hands were harder to forget.

She wrapped the disc again and locked the box and put it away, and was standing at her window looking over the tiled roofs when Phyllis came in with the evening's acceptances, written out in the clerk's neat hand.

“Twelve for supper, four for the smaller dining room, the governor of Syria's agent among them. The poet's coming back, the one Chloe frightens. And—” Phyllis paused, in the flat voice she used for things she considered dangerous. “One new name. Accepted this morning by his man.”

Antonia took the list. She read it down, past the praetors and the merchants and the officers of the guard, and stopped at the last line but two.

Marcus Aelius Severus.

The room was very quiet. Somewhere in the street a cart went by, its wheels shrieking on the stone.

Phyllis watched her. “I can send a refusal. We're full. A death in the house, even. I'll think of something.”

“No.” Antonia folded the list once, precisely, along its crease. Her voice had not changed. It never changed, which was how she had survived everything. “No. The door is open to everyone. That is the rule, and the rule is what protects us. A man's money is as good as his name here, and his name is as good as his manners.”

“You know what this will cost you.”

“I know what everything costs. It is my one talent.” Antonia set the list down on the table, and for a single moment, before the smooth mask came down, Phyllis saw the girl she had carried out of another house, long ago, white and bleeding and half dead in a dealer's cart.

Then the moment passed.

“Have the blue room made ready,” Antonia said. “The best lamps. And tell Chloe I want her beside me all evening. Tell Festus to double the door. Whatever happens tonight, Rome is going to watch us do it perfectly.”
