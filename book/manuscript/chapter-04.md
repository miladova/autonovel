# CHAPTER FOUR — The Tiber Keeps Its Own

He came at the tenth hour of the night, through the postern, with his hood up and his feet moving fast, and Festus, who had opened many posterns to many frightened men, noted the particular quality of this man's fear: not drink, not debt, not a husband's anger, but the clean, cold fear of a man who knows he is being followed.

“Hector, Greek, clerk, expected,” the man said, in a voice trying hard to be steady. “She'll know the name.”

“She does. Come in, and stop looking at the street; it gives you away.” Festus shut the postern and barred it, and walked him through the torchlit service corridor to the small room beside the strong room where Antonia received the business that could not be seen by guests. Chloe was already there, and she stood up so quickly that her stool tipped over.

“Hector.”

“Little sister.” He took her by the shoulders, and for a moment the frightened clerk vanished and he was simply her brother, the older boy who had shared a slave cabin on Delos and been sold away from her at eleven. “You're thinner. Rome doesn't feed you?”

“Rome feeds me beautifully; I waste away on worry. Sit down. Both of you.” She turned to Antonia. “Domina, this is my brother. The only family I have left in the world, which I mention because it means I am not objective about him.”

Antonia looked at the man across the table. He was thirty or so, slight, Greek, with ink stains on his fingers and a frayed mantle that had been good once and was now carefully mended, the garment of a clerk who took a pride the world did not reward. His eyes kept moving to the door.

“Chloe says you have papers,” Antonia said.

“I have evidence, domina. The difference is that papers can be explained away.” From inside his tunic he drew a bundle wrapped in oilskin, and unwrapped it: four wax tablets, bound with leather thongs, their seals unbroken, and beneath them a thin sheaf of parchment notes. “The duplicates. I keep the board's tallies — I've kept them for three years — and last month the numbers stopped making sense, so I began keeping my own. Two sets of accounts. The official ones, and these.”

Antonia took the first tablet and broke the seal. Her eyes moved quickly; she read Greek and Latin both, and read figures the way musicians read notes.

“Grain for the eastern schedule,” Hector said. “The army's ration contracts. In Ostia the shipments are weighed at the public granaries — I've a friend among the measurers there. Three hundred and twenty modii in a hold, say. By the time the same cargo reaches the Roman tally, it's four hundred and ten. The grain doesn't grow on the river. Someone writes the difference, sells the phantom supply back to the treasury, and the gold walks.”

“Names.”

“The firms are shell work — freedmen's names, dead men's names, a different set every quarter. But the money converges.” He turned the second tablet, and his finger, trembling slightly, touched a column. “Here. The subscriptions that capitalize the firms. Decimus Aelius Celer, eleven thousand. And here, the loans that bought the subscriptions: Sextus Perperna, lending at twenty-four per cent to half the men on the list. Perperna doesn't just know about the fraud, domina. He owns the men committing it.”

The room was very quiet. The fountain made its sound in the garden.

“Celer,” said Antonia. “The brother of the auditor.”

“Yes.” Hector swallowed. “The consul-designate's own brother, in up to his neck, and the auditor himself — with respect, domina, everyone in the counting houses knows Severus has been given the tallies — the auditor has no idea. It's a trap built like a temple. If Severus signs the accounts, the fraud passes with the most honest name in Rome above it. If he digs and exposes it, his own brother hangs first, and the name of the Aelii is destroyed. They don't want him bribed. They want him paralyzed.”

Antonia set the tablet down with care. “Why come to me? You could have gone to the prefect. To the emperor's own office.”

Hector's laugh had no humour in it. “And how long do you suppose a Greek freedman clerk lasts after he accuses senators and Perperna to the prefect's doorkeeper? I've seen what happens. My predecessor on the board — a man called Phaon, very honest, very dull — resigned last year to ‘retire to Naples.' His grave's by the Ostian road. I went. There's a nice stone, paid for by persons unknown.” He looked at his sister, and the fear showed through fully now. “I came here because this is the one house in Rome where Perperna can't put a man at the table without you knowing it. Because you collect what men say when they're drunk, and I need the woman who knows where all the bodies are. And because Chloe says you keep your word, and I'm out of houses to trust.”

Chloe reached across and took his hand. “He needs to leave the city tonight. Hilarus has a barge going down to Ostia with the tide; he can lie up at the harbour, then take ship for Massilia—”

“No.” Antonia's voice was flat, and both of them looked at her. “Not yet. He's right about one thing: the tablets are evidence, but evidence in a drawer is merely paper. I need a witness who can speak to the seals. The forgeries run through one engraver, don't they? Every false tally carries a seal-cutters' mark.”

Hector stared. “Syrus. On the Tiber bank, near the cattle market. How did you—”

“I know everyone who makes things that can be copied. Can he prove his hand? Distinguish a true seal from a forged one in court?”

“Blindfolded. He engraved half the official seals in the city. He'd know his own work from a forgery the way a mother knows twins.”

“Then tomorrow you'll take word to Syrus — not the documents, just the warning that his name may be wanted — and then you'll go to Hilarus's barge. One more day in Rome, under my roof, and then the harbour.” Antonia bound the tablets again with their thongs. “These go into the strong room, behind the silver, and no one but the four of us knows they exist. Festus?”

The ex-gladiator appeared in the doorway as though he had been part of the wall. “Domina.”

“Our guest leaves at dawn for the upper room, and he does not leave it. Food, water, a chamber pot, and your own man outside the door. If anyone asks, the room doesn't exist.”

Festus looked at Hector with the calm appraisal of a man measuring freight. “You'll be safe enough, Greek. Nobody gets past me except the wine merchant, and that's because I like him.”

Hector managed a smile. It did not reach his eyes.

Festus withdrew to the corridor, and the two Greeks sat for a while with the lamplight between them, and it was Chloe who spoke first, in their own language, the rough island Greek of the Delos slave market that neither of them had used in years.

“Tell me the rest. The part you left out of the letters.”

“There isn't much. After the dealer split us, I went to a counting house in Alexandria — clerk's boy, then clerk. The master found I could do sums in my head and treated me almost like furniture, which was a kindness in that house. I bought my freedom at twenty-six with the wages he didn't know I'd saved. You?”

“Delos to a rhetoric school, as the reader. Then Rome, then the house.” She poured him wine with her own hand. “I looked for you. Every Greek clerk who came through the Carinae got the third degree. There were a great many Greek clerks.”

“I know. I heard about a singer called Chloe who knew everyone, and I was afraid it was you, and then I was more afraid it wasn't. The market does something to people. I wasn't sure you'd still be—” He gestured vaguely.

“Human?”

“Kind. I wasn't sure you'd still be kind.”

Chloe's mouth trembled once, and was still. “Kindness in this city is an investment, like any other. Antonia taught me that. You spend it carefully and it compounds. Do you remember our mother's hands?”

“Calloused. She did the weaving. She used to let us play with the shuttle threads.”

“That's all I have. Hands, and a song she hummed that I can't remember the tune of.” Chloe set the jug down. “When this is over, Hector, you and I are going to have a dull life. A little house somewhere — Neapolis, perhaps, where the Greeks are still Greeks. You'll keep the accounts of olive merchants and I'll sing badly at weddings. That's my price for surviving. Hold me to it.”

“It's a bargain.” He drank, and for the first time that night some of the fear left his face, replaced by the older, simpler thing that brothers and sisters carry, the certainty that the world contains at least one person who knew you before it made you.

When Festus had taken him away, Chloe lingered by the table, pleating the edge of her mantle between her fingers. “He was twelve when they separated us. Our mother was sold to a mine. I don't remember her face, only her hands. Hector I remember whole.” Her voice was steady, which cost her visible effort. “If Perperna has him—”

“Perperna doesn't have him. He has a missing clerk, and missing clerks are common; a body is a statement, and statements bring the vigiles.” Antonia paused. “I'll keep him alive, Chloe. It's the one promise I know how to make, because it's also the one I most need to keep for myself. Now go to bed. Tomorrow's supper has fourteen covers, and I need you clever, not tragic.”

Chloe went. Antonia sat alone with the tablets for a while, turning the cold wax in her hands, and then she too went up through the sleeping house, and somewhere a watchman called the third hour, and Rome, which never fully slept, shifted and muttered in the dark like a great beast at rest.

The next night's supper was a large one, given over to the contractors and merchants of the annona, the grain supply, and Antonia had arranged it with the care of a general choosing ground.

The great triclinium was full. Cossus, the grain contractor who had bragged of the eastern war's profits on Marcus's first visit, was there with two partners, and the room smelled of roses and pepper and the rich, close smell of successful men beginning to sweat into their garlands. Chloe presided over the middle couch, Tyche over the further one, and Antonia moved like a weaver's shuttle between them, pouring attention where it would do the most work.

She had dressed the room for annona men rather than senators: fewer roses, more light, the tables set with the good silver but nothing so fine as to make a contractor feel provincial, and a menu of honest plenty — wild boar, mullet, three sorts of olive, the strong Alban wine rather than the delicate Greek. Men who weighed grain for a living distrusted elegance, she had found; they read refinement as a trick, and the whole evening's work depended on their reading nothing as a trick.

The work itself began before the first course. Tyche led the partners' junior clerk, a plump young man with ink on his thumb, into a conversation about harbour fees, and within ten minutes had him explaining the difference between the public weighing at the granary steps and the private re-weighing that happened inside, a difference, he confided, that “paid for a great many villas.” Chloe, on the middle couch, let Cossus explain his own importance to her for the length of two cups, nodding at the exact intervals, until the man, mellowed by being heard, began to name the firms he had beaten to the latest contracts — names Antonia had wanted in the air, because names spoken over wine could be compared against names written on tablets, and the gap between the two lists was where fraud showed its ankles.

Antonia herself worked the head of the table, where the senior partner, a grey-haired man named Varus who had supplied three emperors' armies, was holding court on the coming eastern campaign.

“Mark me,” Varus said, tapping his cup, “a war's fortunes are decided not by the eagles but by the contracts. A general can win a battle on short rations; he cannot win one on no rations, and the men who move the rations are the men Rome ought to cherish instead of taxing.” A murmur of agreement went round the couches. “The bureau understands it, anyway. Sensible fellows, the new men. Flexible. A contractor and a clerk who understand one another can move more grain in an afternoon than the law allows in a month, and nobody's the poorer — the army eats, the ships sail, the clerks eat. Where's the harm?”

“The harm,” Antonia said, smiling as she refilled his cup, “sounds like a question for an auditor, not a hostess. Tell me instead how a flexible clerk keeps his accounts. I'm a student of accounts; it's my one unfashionable vice.”

Varus laughed, and explained, and she listened with the bright, empty attention men found so flattering, while the part of her mind that did the real work placed each sentence beside the cold wax tablets upstairs: the fourth shipment, the ninety modii, the seal that repeated. By the time the boar came in, she had three new names, two new procedures, and one priceless phrase — _the private re-weighing_ — which she would write down the moment the company was safely drunk, because Hector's phantom grain, she was now certain, did not appear at the public scale at all. It was born in the second weighing, the one with no witnesses but the flexible men.

By the second wine service, Cossus was explaining the grain trade to a girl of nineteen who had been carefully chosen because she looked as though she admired him, and he had reached the stage of the evening where a man begins to reveal his importance through his indiscretions.

“—the thing people don't understand is that the tally _is_ the grain. The army eats paper, my dear. A quartermaster in Syria doesn't know a modius from a mule; he knows a sealed tablet. So whoever holds the pen holds the harvest. Our group does very well out of the pen.” He laughed, and his partners laughed, and the girl laughed on cue, and across the room Chloe's eyelids flickered once toward Antonia, a single silent note.

“Won't the auditor catch it?” the girl asked, beautifully earnest. “They say the consul-designate Severus is ferocious.”

“Severus.” Cossus drained his cup and held it out for more. “Severus is a saint, and saints are the easiest men in Rome to manage, because you always know what they'll do. His own brother's in the subscription — didn't you know? Decimus Aelius. Charming fellow, terrible head for figures. When the saint finds that out, he won't dare cry fraud, because fraud means his family's blood in the gutter. He'll sign. Honest men always do, once you've taken their relations hostage.” He belched softly, content with himself. “More wine. This is excellent. What is it?”

“Setine,” said Chloe, appearing beside him with the jug, her smile warm and her eyes like stones. “The emperor's own vintage. You have distinguished taste, Cossus. And such a gift for explanation. You must tell me about the pens again; I'm afraid I'm slow with figures.”

He told her. She drew him out for another hour, gently, expertly, until he had named the measurer at Ostia, the warehouse on the Aemilian wharf, and the name of the seal-engraver on the Tiber bank; and every word went into the room's true ledger, the one kept in Antonia Cassia's head.

In the pause between the roasts, Hilarus found her in the garden court, wiping his forehead with a napkin. He was a stout, sunburned man of fifty, freed in his thirties by a merchant whose trade he had inherited and multiplied, and he wore his wealth the way a soldier wears armour — comfortably, and never out of reach.

“I've insured two of those grain ships he's describing,” he said without preamble. “The _Isis_ and the _Felix_. Both came in light. My measurer at Ostia weighed them himself; the Roman tallies gained ninety modii between the harbour and the wharf, which is a remarkable rate of growth for dead grain.”

“Can your man testify to the Ostia weights?”

“He can, if he's alive to do it. He's gone to ground in the harbour district; says men have been asking for him at the taverns. Antonia, I did not get rich by being brave. I got rich by being absent when things became brave.” Hilarus lowered his voice. “But this is my trade they're stealing. Every phantom modius they sell the treasury is a modius that depresses the honest price and teaches the contractors that crime pays better than ships. If they get away with it, half my fleet is worthless inside two years.”

“Then bravery and self-interest have finally agreed, which is my favourite kind of partnership.” Antonia plucked a leaf from the jasmine and tore it slowly. “I want your Ostia offices open to me — every weight note, every wharf receipt. And I want your measurer kept alive. Hire him guards; put the cost to me.”

“Done. And the auditor? Severus? If the witness line on that loan is genuine—”

“We don't know yet whether he's a villain or a fool, and in Rome the distinction often doesn't matter, because the one is punished exactly like the other.” She let the leaf fall. “But he has the only name that can make a court listen. I'll need to arrange another conversation with the consul-designate, and this one won't be over wine.”

Hilarus looked at her shrewdly. “There's history between you two. The whole house knows, even the scullery boys.”

“The whole house knows nothing, and the scullery boys know less than that. History is a grave, Hilarus. Don't dig in mine.”

He held up his hands, palms out. “Not digging. Only observing that a woman usually hates a man less, or more, than you hate him. Never precisely that amount.”

She did not answer, because there was no answer that would not have cost her something, and they went back in together to the lamps and the noise.

The evening's ugly moment came late, in the smaller dining room, where a provincial tax farmer named Auctus had drunk past the point at which he could distinguish a paid companion from a possession. Tyche had been playing the lyre for his couch, and he reached for her wrist and pulled, and said something low and laughing that turned her face white.

The rule was the rule. Antonia was across the room before the sentence had finished, her own smile untouched, her voice low enough that only the nearest couches heard.

“Auctus. Let go of her hand.”

“Mind your trade, domina. I've paid for the evening.”

“You've paid for wine, music, and conversation. Her hand isn't on the menu. Let go, or Festus will explain it to you in the vestibule, and he demonstrates with the arm.”

The man's face darkened. He was fifty, rich, used to obedience, and for two beats the room balanced on a knife's edge. Then something in Antonia's still expression reached him — the knowledge that she would, without hesitation, ruin his standing with every man present and have him thrown into his own litter — and he let go, with a coarse laugh and a show of spreading his fingers.

“A joke. Only a joke. No sense of humour, you Greeks.”

“I'm not Greek,” said Antonia. “And I have a wonderful sense of humour. I'm saving it for men with manners.” She nodded to Tyche, who rose with the lyre and walked out with her head high, and to the room at large she said, “More wine, gentlemen. The night is young.”

But she made a mental mark beside the name Auctus, the way she marked every man who forgot the rule, because men who forgot it once were rehearsing for the time they forgot it where no one could stop them.

After the guests had gone, she found Tyche in the women's court, sitting alone in the dark with the lyre in her lap, and sat beside her without speaking.

“I didn't cry,” Tyche said.

“No. You were perfect.”

“I wanted to. I wanted to break the lyre over his head.”

“That would have been satisfying, and then we'd both be poorer and he'd be no wiser. Wanting and doing are different trades.” Antonia took the girl's hand, the same hand Auctus had gripped; there were red marks on the wrist. “Phyllis has salve. Remember what the house is: not a cage, a wall. Inside the wall, you decide. That's more than most women in Rome own, and I didn't build it to let a drunk farmer's son spoil it.”

Tyche leaned against her shoulder for a moment, briefly, the way a child does, and then straightened. “I'm all right.”

“I know you are. That's why you frighten them.”

Festus came up the garden stairs at dawn with the river still on his boots.

He had been down to the wharves before light, as he did twice a week, to talk to the watchmen and the stevedores and the tavern keepers who were the city's true morning gazette; and Antonia, who had slept three hours and bathed in cold water, saw his face as he crossed the atrium and felt something inside her close like a fist.

“Say it.”

“Vigiles fished a man out of the Tiber at first light, by the wooden bridge. Knife work, three cuts, professional. They'd weighted him with a stone, but the stone caught on a piling and he came up. The centurion's a man I know — Vibius Longus, straight as a ruler — he sent word when they found the ink stains and the tablet case.” Festus's voice was heavy. “It's the Greek, domina. Your guest. He got out of the upper room somehow in the night. The window bar's been sawed through, clean work, from the outside. Someone knew which room.”

Antonia closed her eyes. Behind them she saw the careful, ink-stained hands, the frayed mantle, the fear.

“Chloe,” she said.

“I'll tell her,” said Festus.

“No. I'll tell her. Where is she?”

“Sleeping. She came off the second watch.”

Antonia stood very still for a moment, and when she opened her eyes her face was the face the house knew in a crisis, smooth and cold and entirely without illusion. “Fetch her to the strong room. And Hilarus — send to the docks, now, before the streets fill. The barge plan may be dead, but the bargeman isn't; I want to know who knew it. Four people knew that room, Festus. Four. Find me the one who talks in his sleep.”

She went out with him instead, through the waking streets, down to the Tiber.

The vigiles kept their district station in a converted warehouse near the cattle market, and the river dead were laid out in a stone-paved yard behind it, under an awning, where the water could be sluiced away. The smell was the smell of all river yards, mud and fish and the cold sweetness of death, and Antonia wrapped her veil higher and crossed the flags with Festus beside her.

Vibius Longus was waiting, a square, grey-whiskered man in a stained tunic and a soldier's belt, the centurion's vine stick tucked under his arm. He did not bow or waste words; vigiles centurions saw too much of Rome to be impressed by it.

“Domina. Your man said you'd come. He's under the second sheet. Brace yourself; the river's had him half a day.”

He drew back the linen. Hector had not drowned. The three knife wounds were clean and close, the work of a man who did that sort of thing often; the throat cut was the mercy, or the habit. His hands were still ink-stained, the fingers slightly curled, and on one wrist there was a faint bruise where cord had bitten.

“They took him alive, then,” Antonia said.

“For a little while. Rope marks, no struggle cuts on the hands — he was bound before he was cut. Professionals, or soldiers; in this city sometimes the same men.” Vibius covered the face again. “The stone was a wharf stone, good and heavy, but the current rolled him onto a piling and the cord frayed. Lucky for us. The Tiber keeps its own, usually.”

“The window bar in my house. Sawed through, Festus says.”

“From the outside, and recently. Good blade, steady hand, knew which room. I'll come up and look at it, if you'll have me; my men double as carpenters, since Rome is mostly wood and fire.” The centurion's eyes were steady on her. “I'll be plain, domina. I know what your house is. I also know it's never given my watch a night's trouble in six years, while half the taverns on this street bleed a man a week. A dead clerk with knife work and contract tablets isn't a tavern quarrel. It's the kind of death that gets closed as persons unknown and forgotten by noon. If you want it remembered, you'll need to do the remembering yourself. I can give you the body for proper rites, and I can give you honest eyes when I have them. That's all the law's got in a yard like this.”

“It's more than the law usually offers. Take his statement about the bar, witnessed and sealed, and keep a copy yourself.” Antonia looked at the sheeted shape. “And find me who saw two men carrying a heavy bundle to the river between midnight and dawn. Wharf men sleep badly; someone always sees.”

“Someone always sees,” Vibius agreed. “Getting them to say it is the art.”

She left the arrangements in Festus's hands — a proper pyre outside the Esquiline gate, a Greek priest, the rites Chloe would want — and walked back up through the waking city with her veil drawn, carrying her grief for a man she had known one evening the way she carried everything, behind a face no one could read.

She went down to the strong room herself, unlocked the iron-bound door, and took out the four tablets and the parchment notes, and carried them to the table where the lamps burned even in daylight. Chloe came in a little later, white and dry-eyed, the dryness more terrible than tears, and sat down across from her without a word.

“I'm sorry,” Antonia said. “I keep promises, and I failed this one. I'll say it once, and then I'll spend my life making it good.”

Chloe's voice was flat. “They got into your house. Through a barred window, in the night, and they killed my brother in your upper room or on his way out of it, and they dropped him in the river like a bucket.”

“Yes.”

“Then we're at war.”

“Yes.” Antonia pushed the tablets across the table. “Read them. All of it. He died for these, and they're going to be worth his life, or I'll know the reason why.”

Chloe read. In the strong room's lamplight her face moved through grief into something harder and more durable, the face of a woman who had discovered that the world had taken everything it could from her and could now be dealt with without fear. At the third tablet she stopped, her finger on a line, and looked up.

“Antonia. The subscriptions. There — the Aelius name. Decimus, yes, but look at the witness line on the loan beneath it.”

Antonia looked. Beneath Perperna's loan to Celer, in the column of witnesses, in a small, formal, senator's hand, was a second name.

_M. Aelius Severus._

They stared at one another.

“He witnessed his brother's loan,” Chloe said slowly. “Marcus Severus put his name on Perperna's paper.”

“Either that, or someone put his name there without telling him.” Antonia's mind was already turning the possibilities like keys in locks. “If he knew, he's their creature. If he didn't, his own seal has been used to build the trap he's auditing. Either way—”

“Either way, he's inside it now.”

Antonia gathered the tablets and rebound them, her movements precise. “The Tiber took one man for these. It won't take the rest. We're going to need the auditor himself, Chloe. Whether he deserves it or not.”

They burned him at dusk beyond the Esquiline gate, in the field where the poor and the unlanded took their smoke, and the house closed its doors for the evening and went in force: Festus and the footmen, Phyllis with the offerings, Tyche and the other women veiled in dark cloth, Sabina at the back holding a lamp she had been told to hold and did not yet understand. Chloe had washed and dressed the body herself at the vigiles yard, refusing all help, and she laid the coin for the ferryman between her brother's lips with hands that only shook once.

A wandering Greek priest said the old words, the words for crossing water, and the pyre took, and they stood in the smoke while the sun went down over the tiled city. Antonia watched Chloe's profile against the fire: no weeping, no dramatic grief, only the fixed attention of a woman recording a debt.

When the others had drawn back to the litters, Chloe stayed at the edge of the embers, and Antonia stayed with her.

“He wanted a dull life,” Chloe said. “A house in Neapolis. Olive merchants and bad wedding songs. We agreed it last week, in that room, over your wine. He got a pyre in a potter's field.”

“Then the men who owe him this will pay for the house in Neapolis,” Antonia said. “Whether they're alive to see it or not. I don't believe in the gods much, but I believe in ledgers, and his is open.”

Chloe knelt, took a handful of the cooling ash, and tied it into the corner of her mantle with a piece of cord. “For the house in Neapolis. When I have one, he gets a shelf.” She rose, and turned, and her face was the face Antonia had seen over the tablets in the strong room, grief annealed into something with an edge. “I want to learn the accounts properly. All of them. Not just sing for them. If this war is going to be fought with paper, I intend to be a soldier.”

“Tomorrow. First lesson, first hour. Now come home; the dead don't need witnesses any longer, and the living have work.”

They rode back through the gates as the lamps were being lit in every window, Rome roaring around them in its thousand voices, the enormous indifferent life of a city that had swallowed Hector the Greek between one sunset and the next and would, if no one stopped it, swallow a great many more. Antonia looked at the lights as the litter climbed the hill, and thought that the city looked like a great beast lying down for the night, all gold eyes and warm breath, and she made herself the promise she made only once, in the presence of the dead: she would not be swallowed. She would make the beast choke.
