# CHAPTER TWENTY-ONE — Fire

The tenement next door caught at the fourth watch, in the hour when Rome sleeps deepest and the night watch is at its coldest, and the vigiles' trumpets came over the rooftops like the voices of frightened birds.

Chloe woke to the smell before the shout: oil, and burning wood, and the particular sharp sweetness of fire already well begun. She was on her feet with the house drill running through her head — the drills Festus had made them run every night for a week, until the women complained in their sleep — and she was in the corridor before the first scream reached them from the street.

“Fire! Next door — everyone up, quiet and quick, the courtyard, now!”

The household moved the way it had been trained. No one ran, because running in a burning street is what kills people; they came out in their pairs, musicians and courtesans and kitchen slaves, each with her bundle and her neighbour's hand, down the stairs and across the atrium to the garden court, where the impluvium and the great water jars stood full, and where Festus had placed the two strongest guards with the wet blankets.

Vitalis's brickwork held. The new plaster between the tenement and the house smoked and cracked but did not give, and the sealed lean-to was gone — he had demolished it himself that afternoon, with Hilarus paying for the loss without a murmur — so the flames, denied the bridge of timber, roared upward through the tenement's own dry wood instead of across the wall. But the wind was off the roofs, and the sparks came over in showers, and the house's upper eaves began to smolder in three places at once.

“Bucket chain!” Festus roared, from the front door, where he had thrown the whole width of himself into organizing the street. “You, you, and you — the well, now! Pass the buckets and don't break the line!”

Vibius Longus arrived with the watch within minutes, his vine stick in his hand and his men already deploying their axes and their siphon pump, and he took one look at the prepared house — the chains, the blankets, the cleared wall, the calm women in the courtyard — and said, to Festus, “Somebody knew.”

“Somebody always knows in this street. The question's over there.” Festus jerked his head at the tenement, where the fire was hottest at the ground-floor corner, far from any hearth. “No kitchen there. No lamp. That's an oil fire, centurion; I've started enough of them in my life to know the smell.”

Vibius's mouth tightened. He had seen the carver's hand on a doorframe and a clerk in the river already this winter; he did not need to be told twice. “I'll have the origin examined when it's cool. If it's arson, the report goes to the praetor's office and the prefect both.”

The street became a battle line. Neighbors poured out of the surrounding blocks with their own buckets, because a fire in Rome was nobody's private misfortune; let one insula go and the whole district went, and the Carinae knew it. The baker from two doors down organized the well line, the fuller's apprentices hauled the vigiles' pump, and even the nut-seller who had watched the gilded door for Perperna found himself passing buckets, Rome's appetites forgotten in the older appetite to survive. Above them, on the roof walk, Festus's ex-gladiators worked in the smoke with wet hides, beating out each new spark as it landed, while below, Vitalis directed the wet blankets against the blistering paint of the door, saving the gold leaf by the simple method of keeping it permanently soaked.

“Watch the upper windows!” Vibius shouted, as a section of the tenement's roof caved in with a sound like a giant's cough and sent a fountain of embers over the street. The siphon pump answered, its jet arcing into the glow, and the bucket chains redoubled, and somewhere a woman was screaming the names of her children, and two of Festus's guards went in after them through a stair that was already smoking and came out with both boys, one under each arm, to the rough cheer of the street.

It was in that noise and light, while the tenement burned like a signal on the hill, that Chloe, hauling a bucket with arms that no longer felt like hers, noticed the empty place in the courtyard's count.

---

At the custody house behind the temple of Juno, the same night arrived as noise without meaning.

Antonia woke to trumpets, distant but many, the sound she had been waiting for since Beryllus's tracing of the tenement's ownership, and she was on her feet before the guard had struck a light. The other women along the corridor woke too, crying out questions; Lalla came to the bars of her room with a face stripped bare by sleep and fear. From the high, barred window nothing could be seen but sky, and the sky, toward the Esquiline, was the colour of a heated blade, red and breathing, and now and then a gust of smoke smell reached even here, bitter and unmistakable, across a mile of rooftops.

“Where is it?” Lalla whispered.

“Toward the Carinae.” Antonia's voice was steady because she would not permit it to be otherwise, though her hands on the bars were not. “It may be any street. The city burns every week.” But she had counted the trumpets, and the direction, and the hour, and the arithmetic was the kind she did better than anyone in Rome, and it came out against her.

The young farmer's guard — the one whose father's dole had been weighed twice — appeared with the others, and she saw by his face that he knew more than the regulations allowed him to say. She did not beg him for news; begging was a debt with interest. Instead she made her voice practical, the voice of a householder organizing rather than a prisoner supplicating.

“Decurion Caprarius will want the corridor secured properly, not gaped at. If the wind shifts, the ash comes this far and the women need wet cloths, not panic. Tell him I can organize the wet cloths; it's my trade.” It gave the guards something to do, which gave her something to watch, and watching was the only action left to a locked room. Through the rest of the night she moved along the corridor with the others, doling water, calming the girls who had never heard Rome burn, keeping her own face at rest, while above the temple the red sky swelled and faded and swelled again with the rhythm of a fight that was being won or lost a mile away, among people she loved.

Once, near the fifth watch, the glow dimmed suddenly and did not return with the same strength, and she knew, with the householder's instinct that no custody order could take from her, that the worst of it had been turned or had consumed its fuel; but knowing a house still stood and knowing who stood inside it were two different sums, and the second one had to wait for daylight.

She did not sit down again. She stood at the window with her hand on the bronze ring at her throat, and watched the sky go from red to grey, and when at last the footsteps came down the corridor — heavy, familiar, the tread of a man who had carried a body's weight of bad news and meant to carry it honestly — she was already composed, and the only sign of the night was the whiteness of her knuckles.

Lalla came to the bars in the first grey light, and the two women looked at each other, and Lalla said, “Your house. It's your house burning, isn't it.”

“Perhaps. We'll know when the messenger comes.” Antonia's voice might have been arranging a supper. “Whatever the word is, watch what I do with it. This is the lesson no advocate can teach you: the worst news arrives once, but everyone in the building will read your face a hundred times. Hold the face, and you hold the room.”

“You're afraid. I can see you're afraid.”

“Of course I'm afraid. Courage isn't the absence of it; I've never met a brave person who wasn't frightened, only people who'd decided what mattered more than the fear.” She almost smiled. “For me, that's the women behind the door. For you, this morning, it's getting to your hearing with your letters dry. Hold to the thing, Lalla, and the face will follow.”

The fire ate the tenement. Rome's insulae were tinder, and this one had been made tinderer: the investigators would find later that its ground-floor storage had been quietly filled with dry timber and pitch-soaked rags, the careful preparation of men who needed a neighbour's house to burn without an obvious crime. The flames went up four storeys in the time it took the trumpets to bring the next watch, and the heat reached across the street and blistered the paint on the gilded door, and turned the gold leaf black at its edges.

Inside the Carinae house, the bucket chains held. Water went hand to hand from the impluvium and the street well, up the stairs and along the roof walk, where the guards beat out the sparks with wet hides. The women passed the buckets alongside the slaves; Tyche, her lyre abandoned, carried water until her arms gave out and then carried it again; Sabina worked beside Chloe without a word, the drill carrying her where her courage might not have. The kitchen staff, under the cook, kept the jars full and the wet cloths coming, and even the cat was removed to the courtyard in Vibia's old basket, yowling with outrage.

Phyllis was last seen clearly at the second hour of the fire, in the garden court, counting the women.

She had refused to leave with the first evacuation party, the older slaves and the frightened, because someone had to count, and she counted with the lamp in her hand and the smoke beginning to curl across the atrium, and at the end of the count she said the thing that froze Chloe's blood.

“Where's Sabina?”

She was not in the courtyard. The girl, in the first confusion, had remembered the tablets — not the originals in the temple, not the vault copies, but her own Twelve Tables and, worse, the house's current accounts that Chloe had been working in the tablinum, the running records of guests and debts that Perperna's men could use to build a hundred accusations. She had slipped back inside, alone, up the smoke-filled stair, while everyone was watching the wall.

No one even saw Phyllis go. Festus was at the front with the pump; the guards were on the roof; Chloe was three buckets deep in the chain. The old woman simply put down her lamp, picked up a wet blanket, and went into the house after her, sixty-three years old and a heart that had been failing for months, moving with the particular, unhurried certainty of someone who had decided the price of things a long time ago.

She found Sabina in the tablinum, coughing on the floor by the strong room door, the accounts clutched to her chest, her eyes streaming, a fallen beam smoldering across the threshold. The upper landing was burning; the air was black and hot and full of the roaring that fire makes when it has made up its mind.

“On your feet, child. Under the blanket, low, follow my voice.”

“I can't — the smoke—”

“You can, because I'm telling you to, and I've never once been wrong about a household.” She got the girl under the wet blanket and her own body between Sabina and the burning threshold, and she half-carried, half-dragged her back down the stair, along the corridor, through the atrium where the smoke lay like a grey sea, to the garden door and the night air, and the waiting arms of the guards.

In the last stretch, with the garden door ten feet away and the voices outside, Phyllis stopped, and turned, and looked back into the burning house with an expression Chloe, fighting through the crowd toward them, would never forget. It was not fear. It was the expression of a woman checking her work. The strong room door was shut. The accounts were in Sabina's arms. The women were counted. Everything that could be kept was being kept. Then she turned again and walked the last ten feet, and stepped out into the courtyard, and put the girl into Chloe's arms.

They got Sabina out. They did not get Phyllis out whole.

She made the courtyard, and put the girl into Chloe's arms, and said, quite clearly, with the old housekeeper's precision, “The accounts are dry. Check the seals,” and then she sat down on the stone bench by the impluvium, because her legs had stopped being hers, and her hand went to her chest, under the left breast, and she did not rise again.

Chloe was beside her at once, and the physician came running from the street where the injured were being gathered, but there was nothing to be done, and everyone there knew it; the heart, overtaxed by the smoke and the stairs and sixty-three years, had simply finished its work. Phyllis took Chloe's hand, and looked past her for a moment at something no one else could see, and said, very low, “Tell her I kept the wall,” and died in the courtyard of the house she had helped to build, with the firelight on her face and the water from the buckets running over her sandals.

By the fifth watch the tenement was a black shell, the wind had changed, and the Carinae house — its upper wing scorched, its gilded door blackened, its women alive and its vault intact — stood against the dawn like a wall that had been tested and had held.

The street counted its dead at first light: two in the tenement, an old couple who had refused to leave their room, and no one in the gilded house at all, a fact the neighbours repeated through the day with something like awe. The vigiles and the buckets had saved the walls; the drills had saved the women; and the old housekeeper, everyone agreed, had saved the girl.

Chloe walked the rooms at dawn with Vitalis, taking the inventory the way Antonia would have done it, in the cold light. The women's wing was scorched but standing, its new iron bars twisted by the heat; the dining wing had taken smoke but no flame; the atrium's painted walls were blackened at the edges and the dolphin mosaic was buried in ash; the strong room was untouched, its seals perfect, its vault dry. The gilded door was the saddest thing in the house, its bright leaf curled and blackened, and Chloe stood before it a moment and said, to Festus, “We'll have it regilded. Before she comes back. Whatever it costs.”

“Vitalis can start the frame today. The leaf takes the goldsmith a week.”

“Then tell him ten days' pay for six days' work. The door is the story, Festus. Rome has to see it shining again.”

The women set to work with the strange, fierce energy of people who have survived the night and refuse to give the day to grief. Ash was swept, linen was boiled, the smoke was chased out with burning herbs, and the kitchen produced a meal for the entire household and the neighbours' besides, because in Rome after a fire the fed are the calm and the hungry are the dangerous. Sabina, her voice hoarse from smoke, sat wrapped in blankets in the garden and would not let the accounts out of her arms until Chloe herself had checked every seal.

“Phyllis said to check them,” the girl kept saying. “She said it, at the end. The accounts are dry. Check the seals.”

“The seals are perfect. You did exactly what she taught you.” Chloe sat beside her and held her while the shaking came, and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the girl did not already know.

---

The word reached the custody house with the first light, brought by Festus himself, bandaged and smoke-stained, because he would not let a messenger carry it.

Antonia read his face through the little barred window of the door before he spoke, and the room, which had held so many disciplined hours, went very far away.

“Say it.”

“Phyllis. She went back in for Sabina and the accounts. The heart took her, in the courtyard, at the end. She didn't burn, domina. She went quickly, at the end, and she sent you a message.” He had to stop. “She said to tell you she kept the wall.”

Antonia did not weep in front of the guard, or in front of Festus, or at all that morning; she had built herself too carefully for that, and the city, which had taken so much from her, would not be given the sight. She stood very still at the barred window with her hands closed on the wood until the knuckles whitened, and she thought of the kitchen at Tibur, and the flogging, and the bronze ring on its cord, and an old woman's keys, and the thirty years of a life spent keeping a wall around the people the world had sold.

Then she said, in the voice the household knew, “The women?”

“All alive. Sabina's coughing, nothing worse. The strong room held, the vault copies are dry, the originals were never here. The tenement was set; Vibius has the rags and the pitch and he's writing it for arson. Beryllus's paper ties the building to the Minerva seal.”

“Then nothing she died for is lost. That's the only kind of death the city can't steal.” She released the wood. “Festus. I want the rites she'd want. The temple of Isis, the priestess, the proper prayers for the dead. No expense. And her name on something permanent — not a wooden marker, stone. I'll choose it when I'm out. Tell Chloe she's the house now, and to run it the way Phyllis would: every seal checked, every woman counted.”

He went, and she was alone, and then at last, with the guard looking carefully at the opposite wall, she sat on the edge of the narrow bed and put her own hand over her mouth the way she had after Marcus's visit, and she grieved the only way she could, in complete silence, without giving the room a single sound.

---

The fire became, within two days, the least secret arson in Rome.

Vibius Longus filed his report with the vigiles' prefect and the urban praetor: the tenement's origin point had no hearth or lamp, the ground floor contained pitch-soaked rags and stacked dry timber, and the building's recent change of ownership ran, through two shells, to a purchase witnessed under a Minerva seal. Beryllus's bank records made the chain of money. The warehouse attackers in the vigiles' cells, offered the same bargain of evidence over oblivion, identified the same paymaster's agents. Perperna's name was never written down, because it did not need to be; every clerk who handled the file knew exactly whose hand was behind it, and that knowledge was itself a change in the weather of the city.

Vibius Longus conducted the sift himself at first light, while the shell still steamed, and he took Antonia's two men — Festus and Vitalis — through the ruins with him, because he wanted independent eyes on what he found.

The origin point was unmistakable once the debris was cleared: the ground-floor storage room, its floor burned hotter than any hearth could explain, and beneath the ash the unmistakable remains of pitch-soaked cloth and split timbers stacked against the shared wall, as if the building's last tenants had been furnishing a bonfire rather than a lodging. The window onto the alley showed a new lock on the inside, fitted within the month, and the alley's mud held the print of two pairs of hobnailed boots, military issue, where no soldier had business at the fourth watch.

“Oil and pitch don't walk into a locked room by themselves,” Vibius said, prodding the blackened rags with his stick. “And this stack was built against the wall on purpose. They didn't want to burn the tenement. They wanted the tenement to burn your house. The tenants were fuel.”

“Two of them are dead,” Festus said.

“Yes. That's why this report writes itself, and why I'm putting my name to it without softening a word.” The centurion straightened, his face grim. “The boots, the lock, the rags, the ownership chain — the banker's paper will name the buyer. I've seen arson for insurance and arson for revenge. This is arson for evidence-destruction, which means the evidence was working. Tell your mistress the fire is a compliment, if she can take one.”

Rubrius moved at once. The arson evidence was entered into the preliminary hearing's record, the house's protected status was reaffirmed in writing, and the praetor, who had no wish to preside over a case that now included a fire and a dead woman, grew markedly more attentive to the procedural defects in the indictment.

On the Esquiline, Marcus read the vigiles' report and the bank records together in the tablinum, with Livia opposite him, and when he finished he was quiet for so long that she asked him what he was thinking.

“That I've spent my life believing the law would protect people like her. The law has spent this winter proving me wrong at every turn. Every time they fail with the statutes, they reach for something lower: clerks, alleys, fire. The law's for the respectable. For the rest, there's only being richer, better organized, and more stubborn than the men who want you gone.”

“Then be those things. You've two days.” Livia folded the report. “And Marcus — the old woman. I only met her twice. I've thought about her since. Some people make a whole morality out of one unbroken habit. Hers was keeping the door. Say that at the rites, if you say anything; the city will understand it better than poetry.”

He did not speak at the rites, in the end, because the guards and the case made any words of his dangerous; but he stood the whole time with his head uncovered in the cold, and Antonia, across the procession, saw him and knew the tribute for what it was, and that was enough between them for that day.

The funeral was held at the columbarium beyond the Esquiline gate, on a cold grey morning, and Antonia was permitted to attend under guard, because even the praetor's office balked at refusing a woman her own housekeeper's rites.

The household came in full, veiled and quiet, and Hilarus and Porcia, and Beryllus, and Vibius Longus in his uniform, and the priestess of Isis with the offerings; and Marcus came, with Livia, and stood with the family at the back, and did not approach her, because the guards were watching and the case was days away. Chloe spoke the eulogy, because Antonia could not, and she spoke it in Greek and then in Latin, the story of a woman sold at thirty who had spent the rest of her life making sure no one else was sold unaided, and the household wept openly, and even Festus had to look at the sky.

Chloe's eulogy told the whole of it that could be told in the open: the kitchen woman sold at thirty, the two great houses she had served, the flogging she had taken for arguing with a mistress, the girls she had raised and hidden and counted through every night of her life.

“She never held a title,” Chloe said, in Greek first, then in the careful Latin the court would have understood. “Rome gives titles to men who own things. She owned nothing but her word, and her word was better than any title in this city. When the fire came, she did the one thing the powerful never do: she went back into the burning room for someone smaller than herself. We are not here because a woman died. We are here because a woman lived, and the living in this city is the harder thing.”

The household wept without shame. Tyche played no instrument, but sang one of the old island laments, her voice carrying thin and clear over the columbarium, and even the vigiles' escort stood with their eyes fixed on the middle distance. At the end, Antonia placed the bronze ring of Eirene for a moment against the stone niche where Phyllis's ashes were laid, and said, for the dead woman alone, “You kept the wall. I'll keep the house.”

Then the guards closed around her again, and the procession went back down the road toward the city, and behind them the new stone waited for the inscription she had already ordered, in full:

_PHYLLIS, FREE WOMAN, WHO KEPT THE WALL._

Marcus came to the house the day after the rites, in daylight, with no attempt at concealment, because concealment no longer existed for either of them.

Chloe received him in the smoke-scarred atrium, where the ash was gone but the smell lingered, and walked him through the damage the way Antonia would have done, room by room, without dramatics. He saw the scorched women's wing, the twisted iron bars, the blackened upper landing where Phyllis had carried Sabina through, and he said very little, and that little in the voice of a man measuring an enemy's work.

“Tell me again the origin of the fire.”

“The tenement next door, ground-floor storage, pitch-soaked rags and dry timber, no hearth. The building changed owners a month ago; Beryllus has the money trail and the Minerva witness.” Chloe handed him a copy of the vigiles' report. “Vibius Longus will say it in court himself. He's angry; arson in his district is a personal insult.”

“The strong room?”

“Untouched. The vault copies are dry, and the originals were never here.” She permitted herself a thin smile. “They burned a tenement and blackened a door. The evidence is in a goddess's coffer and an oil merchant's warehouse and a praetorian prefect's office. They've spent their third instrument and gained nothing.”

“Except Phyllis.”

“Yes.” The thin smile vanished. “Except her. They'll answer for that, whatever else happens. The house has eleven new reasons, Senator, and I've stopped counting my own.”

He stood a while in the garden court, where the fountain still ran and the stone bench was scrubbed clean of the marks of that night, and he said, “At the hearing, Rubrius wants me to speak last among the defence witnesses. The praetor will allow it given the standing order. I'll put the whole audit connection into the record myself — the supply fraud, the murdered clerk, the reason this house became a target. If they want a scandal, I'll give them a conspiracy instead.”

“She says you'd say that. She also says don't speak from anger; the court uses angry men for kindling.”

“Your mistress knows me too well.” He turned at the colonnade. “Take care of the door. Take care of the women. And when you see her, tell her the family's seats are at the front, and Livia's bringing the consular wives. Rome is going to see exactly who stands together.”

Back at the custody house, Lalla was waiting at the corridor bars, having heard the guards talk about the fire and the dead housekeeper.

“I'm sorry,” the girl said, simply. “You taught me paper was a right. Was she the one who taught you?”

“Something like that. She taught me that the rule is only real when someone keeps it at a cost.” Antonia sat on the edge of her own bed and spoke through the bars, because the guards permitted it and she needed to say the words aloud. “She was sold the way I was sold. Most people never recover from that; they spend the rest of their lives making sure no one gets a kindness they didn't. She spent hers the other way. I don't know if I'm that good. But I know the court's the only place I can prove it.”

“My hearing's the same week. I'm going to do what you said. The letters first, the tears after.”

“Then we're both ready.” She almost smiled. “If Rome insists on making us witnesses, Lalla, we'll give it more witnessing than it knows what to do with.”

That night, in the custody room, she wrote the last of her notes for the hearing, and when she had finished she sat a long time with the strip of sky over the temple, and she did not pray, because she had never quite believed the gods were listening, but she made a vow, which was different: that the men who burned houses and buried clerks and broke old women's hearts would answer in the open, in the Roman daylight, with the whole city made to hear the truth, word by word, whether it wanted to or not.

At the Carinae, the goldsmiths came on the morning after the rites, and the gilded door came off its hinges and went into their workshop, and Vitalis rebuilt the scorched frame with new oak while the house carried on around him. Six days, Festus had promised. The women watched the work from the atrium with the intensity of soldiers watching a wall repaired during a siege, because they all understood what the door was: not vanity, but the first sentence of the story the city read about them. When it went back up, bright and new and even finer than before, the neighbours came out to look, and the watchers at the fountain wrote it down, and the door said what Antonia would have said herself: still here.

The hearing was in two days. She laid the notes beside the lamp and slept, finally, without dreams.
