# CHAPTER SEVENTEEN — The Witnesses

Phyllis chose the night of the first winter rain, when the house was shut and the gutters ran and the street noises were drowned, and she came to Antonia's upper room with a small oilskin packet and the expression of a woman at last laying down a load.

“I said I'd tell you all of it. This is the all of it. Sit down. You'll want wine.”

Antonia poured two cups, and the old woman sat by the lamp with her keys at her belt and her hands folded, and began at the beginning, the way a careful witness does.

“I was thirty when the Aelii sold me. I'd been their kitchen woman eleven years. I nursed the boy Marcus, and I watched your mother, and I watched you grow, and when your mother died of the fever, you were nine years old and the household had no use for a bright Greek girl with no protector, so I made myself yours. That part you know. What you don't know is that I tried.”

Antonia set down her cup. “Tried what?”

“To stop the sale. When the elder mistress found out about the baby, I went to her. On my knees, in her own room, and I told her the truth — that the boy loved you, that it wasn't a slave's trick to trap the family, that you'd done nothing but be born in the wrong room. She heard me out. She was very calm; the dangerous ones always are. Then she said that was precisely why it couldn't continue, because the boy's career needed a marriage with a clean name, and a house slave carrying his child was the one thing that could ruin it. She said the family was larger than any of us, and that kindness to one girl would be cruelty to the whole line.” Phyllis's voice was level, the levelness of old, old anger. “I argued. I actually argued with the mistress. Dromo had me flogged for it, and sold within the week. That's how I came onto the market, and how Cassia Prima found me, years later, in the house where she bought you. She bought me too, eventually, because I asked her to, because I'd heard where you were and I wanted to be near the one person in Rome I'd failed.”

“You didn't fail me.”

“I failed to stop them. There's a difference between losing and not trying, and I've carried both.” She opened the oilskin packet. Inside was a folded sheet, yellowed and soft with age, covered in a steward's cramped hand. “Dromo's private note of the sale. Not the household ledger — he kept his own book, the thief, because he skimmed every transaction and wanted the evidence against himself in his own hands. I stole it the night I was sold. I've kept it since, because I've never believed the powerful should be the only ones with paper.”

Antonia took the sheet. It recorded the sale of Anthe, vernula, to the dealer Dinis, the price, the date, and, in a margin, the two words that mattered: _by order of the mistress_. Not the master. The mistress. Her memory had always blamed the father's house as a faceless thing; here was the hand.

“Your Marcus didn't know,” Phyllis said. “I've watched him this winter, and I've made it my business to find out. He was four hundred miles away, and his letters came back, and his money came back, and he was told a story. The guilty ones are all dead and buried in expensive tombs. The living one is the boy, and the boy has become a man who's trying to make it right, in the only way the world permits.”

“Tell me about the money he sent. All of it.”

“Three letters, and with each one a sum, for your manumission. Dromo brought them to the mistress. She had them returned unopened, and the money with them, and she told the household, publicly, that you'd been freed and gone south of your own will and refused all contact. That's the story everyone believed. The servants believed it. The boy believed it. Rome believes whatever the mistress says inside her own walls.” Phyllis leaned forward. “There's one more thing, and it's the hardest, and I'll tell it because you've a right and I'm not getting younger. The dealer's house where the baby came. I got word of it through a woman at the market, after Prima bought me. The child wasn't stillborn alone in a garret. There was a midwife, and the midwife said the birth was early because you'd been beaten, in the first weeks, by the dealer's man, when you wouldn't be trained. The beating brought the labour. The girl child lived an hour. An hour. No one told you because you were out of your head with fever, and when you woke they said stillborn, because stillborn was easier.”

The rain ran down the shutters. Antonia sat very still, and the lamplight moved on her face, and Phyllis watched her without pity, because pity would have been an insult.

“An hour,” Antonia said at last.

“An hour. I've said prayers for her at the temple of Isis every year since. The goddess counts the unnamed ones; that's one of the reasons I trust her.” Phyllis folded her hands. “There. Now you have the whole of it, and I've kept nothing, and if you hate me for waiting, I'll understand, and I'll still sleep tonight because the waiting's over.”

Antonia rose and crossed the room and took the old woman's face in both her hands, and kissed her forehead, the Roman kiss of family.

“You didn't fail me. You're the only person who ever kept a piece of paper for me instead of against me. Now I need you to do one more thing. Put all of that — the flogging, the sale, the returned letters, the beating and the birth — into a statement, witnessed and sealed, with Dromo's note as its exhibit. Not for revenge. For the record. Rome's paper is going to tell the truth about one girl, even if it never tells it about any of the others.”

The old woman nodded, and sat a while with the rain, and then she said the thing she had kept longest, after the documents and the names.

“She came to me once. Your mother. The night before the fever took her. I don't think you remember; you were nine and they kept you in the kitchen. She gave me a thing to keep for you. I've kept it through two sales and a flogging and thirty years, because a dying woman's the only person in this world whose instructions are perfectly clear.”

From inside her tunic, on a cord around her neck, Phyllis drew a small cloth bundle, blackened with age and wear, and unfolded it. Inside was a bronze ring, cheap, the kind a slave might own, set with a tiny carved stone; and on the stone, worn almost away, was the figure of a woman at a loom.

“She said it was her mother's, brought from Greece, and that you were to have it when you were grown, and you were never to believe the family's lie that no one had ever wanted you. She wanted you. That's the whole of it. I should have given it sooner, but until this winter I didn't know whether you'd want anything from that life.”

Antonia took the ring. Her face did not change, because it never changed, but her hands did, very slightly, and Phyllis, who had watched those hands for thirty years, looked away to give her the privacy of it.

“I'll wear it,” Antonia said eventually. “Under the gloves, where it can't be used against me. Tell me her name.”

“Eirene. She came from Corinth, before the sale. She sang while she sewed; the household used to stop to hear her.”

“Eirene.” Antonia said it once, quietly, the way one says a word that has been waiting all one's life to be spoken. Then she fastened the cord around her own neck, inside her stola, and poured more wine, and the two women sat together through the rain with the names of the dead between them and the lamp burning steady.

---

Marcus came to the villa at Tibur again in the same week, and this time he went through the store rooms rather than the colonnades, with Barto's help and a lamp, into the chamber where the household's discarded records went to die.

They found Dromo's other books at the bottom of an old chest, under a generation of farm accounts, and Marcus carried them to the kitchen table — the same table, as he noted with a strange ache, where a girl had once written her first word — and read through two nights by lamp.

Barto, who had been a boy in the villa's kitchen when Dromo was its steward, sat across from him through the first night, mending harness and answering questions, and his memory turned out to be its own kind of ledger, illiterate but exact.

“Dromo kept everything,” Barto said, of the old steward. “Twice the books the household needed, because he said a man with one book believes it, and a man with two can catch it lying. The master laughed at him for it. Nobody laughs at a man like that until the day they need him.”

The store room bore the man out. Three generations of the villa's paper had been stacked there against the damp, on shelves Dromo himself had built, and the dust lay evenly except where the mice had nested, which told Marcus the chests had not been opened in years. He worked by the method the audit had taught him: first the inventory, every chest listed before one was opened; then the sort, farm accounts in one pile, household in a second, private in a third; then, only when the shape of the archive was clear, the slow reading. To rummage was to miss things; Dromo had hidden nothing, but Dromo had arranged everything, and a man who does not respect another man's arrangement deserves the confusion he gets.

Barto supplied the things paper doesn't hold: which chest had come from the mistress's own room, which steward had drunk and which had prayed, the year the olives failed and the year the old master's gambling did, and the single winter when the villa's people were sold off “like the spare oil,” as the old man put it, his harness needle moving steadily while he said it.

“Did they know,” Marcus asked, “the household, what became of any of them?”

“Nobody did. That's the point of sales; they make people vanish like smoke. The mistress said it was better for everyone concerned, and the household said yes, because households say yes.” Barto bit off a thread. “One or two didn't. You'll find their names in the books, if the books are as complete as he kept them. Dromo wrote the awkward ones the same as the rest; that's the one good thing about a thief with a system.”

Marcus made a note, though not on any tablet the household would see: a winter of multiple sales would tell a different story than a single one — a story of clearance, not punishment, which was the story the court would eventually need.

By the end of the first night the piles reached his shoulder, and the lamp had smoked a brown ring into the ceiling, and Barto had fallen asleep over his harness with his mouth open. Marcus sat on alone in the kitchen, in the silence of a sleeping villa, and looked at the table under his hands. A letter table once, a lesson table; a girl had formed her first characters on this wood under his own boyish instruction, and the wood was still here, and the girl was a woman in Rome with a gilded door and a murder case, and the gulf between those two facts was the whole history of an empire that measured people in sesterces and called the measurement order. He did not yet know what the books would say. He knew, sitting there, that whatever they said, he would not be permitted the comfort of surprise; the household that raised him had kept the receipts, and receipts were the one thing Roman virtue had never been able to argue away.

Barto helped him carry the chests into the kitchen, and worked beside him in companionable silence, the old slave and the senator sorting the paper of the dead, and from time to time Barto supplied the things paper doesn't hold.

“Dromo ran the house like his own shop. Took a cut of everything, did Dromo. Your father knew, I think; gentlemen prefer not to know, it saves them trouble. The mistress knew perfectly and used it, because a steward with his hand in the till is a steward who'll do as he's told.”

“And the girl. Anthe. Did anyone in the household object?”

“Me. I objected, in my own head, which is where most objections happen in a great house. And Phyllis — you'd remember her, kitchen woman, sharp as a knife — she objected out loud, which is why she got sold. There, look, that's her sale, two lines down from the girl's. Nine hundred for one, six hundred for the other; Dromo wrote it like a grocery bill.”

Marcus found the entry and stared at it. “I never knew what happened to Phyllis.”

“Nobody did. That's the point of sales; they make people vanish like smoke. Except people don't vanish, do they. They turn up somewhere else, being sharp.” Barto eased himself onto a stool. “Read the letters book, master. The thin one. That's where he kept what went out and what came back.”

The steward's private books were a careful record of his own corruption: kickbacks, skimmed sales, inflated purchases, and, in the volume for the year of the German commission, the full trail of the letters. Three entries, each with the word _returned_ and the mistress's initial, and beside each the amount sent, and each amount was greater than the price for which Antonia had been sold. He had sent enough, three times over, to buy her freedom; the money had come home to him unopened, and he had been told she wanted nothing.

There was more. The final entry, in a different hand, recorded a sum paid to Dinis the dealer above the sale price, marked _for the training_, and Marcus understood then, with a coldness in his stomach that the lamp could not warm, that his family had not merely sold her. They had paid to have her broken to the trade.

He sat in the kitchen a long time with that open before him, and Barto left him alone, and outside the rain came down on the olives, and he thought about the clean, virtuous house on the Esquiline, and his father's dignity, and his mother's calm, and the price at which all that dignity had been bought: nine hundred and forty sesterces, and a girl, and a daughter who had lived an hour.

He brought the books back to Rome himself, wrapped in oilskin, and put them into Antonia's hands in the garden court, and told her what they contained, and watched her read the line _for the training_ with a face that did not change, and he said, when she had finished, “I've no right to ask anything of you. I'll say it anyway. Tell me what to do with this. Whatever it is.”

“Use it.” She closed the book. “Not for me — the past's past, and I've made my life on top of it. Use it to understand the men we're fighting. Your mother and Perperna are the same machine. The one wrote _better for everyone concerned_ and the other writes _the public interest_. The language changes. The knife doesn't. When you stand before Attianus, remember that the fraud and the sale are one habit: the powerful deciding, over paper, that some people don't count.”

“Will you let me apologize?”

“No. Apologies are for debts that can be repaid. This one can't, so we'll spend it on something that can.” She looked at him directly. “But since we're being witnesses tonight, I'll tell you something I've said to no one. I love you. I have, in some form, since the kitchen. I built an entire life to make sure it couldn't matter, and it matters anyway. There. Now we've both paid the tax, and we can go back to the work.”

He reached across the table and took her hand, and they sat that way while the rain moved off over the rooftops and the lamps burned low, and neither of them said anything more, because nothing more was needed, and the silence was the first completely honest one either of them had known in fifteen years.

---

Phyllis's statement was signed three days later, in the tablinum, with Chloe and the priestess of Isis as witnesses and Dromo's stolen note sealed into its fold. The old woman's hand was steady throughout, but at the end she set the stylus down and pressed her own chest briefly, under the left breast, and Antonia saw it.

“That again.”

“It's nothing. Wind.”

“It's not wind, and we both know it. The physician tomorrow.”

The physician came, a Greek with a long face and longer bills, and he said the things physicians say: rest, no rich food, no exertion, potions of a bitter kind, and the unspoken thing in his eyes that said the heart was older than its owner's will. Phyllis heard him out, paid him from her own purse, and ignored every instruction, because the household needed running and the household was her life's work.

But Antonia moved her into the room beside her own, so that she could hear her at night, and gave the keys of the strong room to Chloe, and made the household understand, without ever saying the words, that the old woman's burdens were being quietly lifted from her, one at a time, the way one removes weight from an ageing horse.

The women took it in their different ways. Sabina, who had lost one household already, began appearing at Phyllis's door at dawn with hot water, a service no one had assigned her. Tyche composed a small tune on the lyre and played it badly and then better, and the old woman pretended to dislike it and asked for it each evening. Chloe simply took the keys and the ledgers and the thousand small decisions, and learned in a week what had taken Phyllis thirty years, and said nothing about it, which was the greatest respect she knew how to offer.

Phyllis noticed all of it, of course. “You're burying me before I'm dead,” she said to Antonia, dryly, on the third evening.

“I'm preparing the house to run without you, which is the only kind of compliment I know how to pay. You can die on your own schedule; the household will be ready either way.”

“Spoken like a Roman.”

“Spoken like your pupil.”

That pleased the old woman more than anything, and she said no more about it, and took her potion, and complained about the taste, and that, Antonia thought, was probably the whole of courage, when you got close enough to see it.

The answer from the Palatine came at the end of the week, through Ursus the praetor: the praetorian prefect Acilius Attianus would receive Marcus Aelius Severus in a private audience, to hear evidence of fraud in the eastern supply, on the condition that the materials were complete, the witnesses named, and the auditor himself prepared to answer for every charge.

Marcus read the note in the Carinae tablinum, with Antonia and Chloe across the table and the tablets and statements stacked between them in their oilskin and wax, and he said, “This is the moment. Once the prefect's door opens, there's no road back for anyone.”

“There was never a road back,” Antonia said. “Only forward, and the speed of it. Assemble everything. We give him the whole machine in one hearing — Ostia, the Aventine, the seals, the loans, the bureau, the names. No surprises held back; Attianus is an honest man, and honest men require the full meal.”

They spent two nights assembling it, the house turned into a scriptorium. Hilarus brought the factor's copies and the insurance tallies; Livia's diary and the steward's statements went in beside them; Longinus's account of the Aventine bank was copied three times; the tanner's receipts arrived from Pompeii by fast courier, still smelling of leather; and Marcus arranged the whole in the order he had learned in the courts — the chain first, the names last, each exhibit numbered and cross-marked so that a busy soldier-prefect could follow it without a lawyer.

“Attianus will ask three questions,” Marcus said, working by lamplight. “Does the fraud exist, who profits, and can it be proved without depending on a single disreputable witness. We answer all three before he asks. The weights answer the first; Longinus and the circular payments answer the second; the seals, the tanner, and the diary answer the third. The house's records support everything but stand alone nowhere.”

“You've learned my trade,” Antonia said.

“I've learned that evidence is architecture. Same stones, different temple.”

“And the indictment? Perperna's hearing is the same week.”

“Then Rome gets two trials in one season.” She permitted herself the thin edge of a smile. “Let the city enjoy itself. It's been a dull winter.”

Marcus gathered the documents into their case, and the household went about its evening, and later, when the lamps were low, Antonia sat with Phyllis beside the brazier and watched the old woman's slow breathing, and thought about witnesses — the living and the dead, the ones who spoke in court and the ones who spoke only in sealed notes kept for fifteen years — and she made herself a second promise, beside the one she had made over Hector's pyre: when this was done, the names of all of them would be remembered somewhere, on something more permanent than wax.

The audience with Attianus was held in the prefect's offices behind the Praetorian camp, in a room of military plainness that Marcus found immediately reassuring. Acilius Attianus was sixty, a soldier from the Spanish provinces, with a grey head and the unhurried manner of a man who had commanded guards for two emperors and feared very little in Rome. He had a secretary and a legal adviser present, and no wine, and he opened the meeting by saying, “You have half an hour, Severus. Make it worth mine.”

Marcus made it worth twenty-two minutes. He laid the chain out as the house had built it: the Ostia weights, with Dorion's tattooed numbers recorded in a sworn statement; the phantom ninety modii; the circular payments through the Aventine bank; Perperna's loans and the trapped subscribers; the reused witness seals, with Syrus's identification; his brother's evidence and the unauthorized use of the family seal; and, at the end, Longinus's statement naming Zosimus in the bureau _a rationibus_.

Attianus did not interrupt. When Marcus finished, the prefect sat for a while turning one of the tablets in his hands.

“You understand what you're asking me to believe. That the emperor's own finance office is party to a fraud on the army's supply, on the eve of a Parthian war.”

“I'm asking you to believe the documents. If they're false, my name answers for it.”

“Your name's in the documents itself, on a witness line.”

“A forgery, established by my household diary and my absence from Rome. My wife's records and my steward's statement prove it. I've concealed nothing, including what tells against my family.”

The prefect looked at him for a long moment, the soldier's assessment of another soldier. “The woman who keeps this house in the Carinae. The broadsheets say she's your mistress.”

“The broadsheets say many things. Antonia Cassia is the source of the evidence and a witness under the praetor's standing order. Her records are better than the treasury's; I'll bring her account books if you wish. What she is, legally, is infamis; what she is, factually, is the person who did the work your own clerks failed to do.”

Attianus almost smiled. “You've learned the answer already. Good. Here's mine. I'll open a quiet inquiry, in my office, not the courts — yet. Your witnesses go under guard; I'll assign praetorians to the Ostia warehouse today. The finance bureau will be watched, not touched; Zosimus is the emperor's household, and the emperor is with the army planning, so nothing moves until I can report to him through the proper channels. And Severus — the indictment hanging over you and this woman. I can't quash it; the praetor's court is its own house. But I can note for the record that the parties are material witnesses in a prefect's inquiry. A court that proceeds against them will have to do so knowing that.” He rose, ending the audience. “Win your case in the open, and my inquiry does the rest in the shadows. That's how Rome works when it works at all.”

Outside, in the winter sun, Marcus stood a moment by the camp wall with the case empty under his arm and felt the particular exhilaration of the soldier who has committed his reserves. There was no road back now, and the absence of a road was, he found, a kind of relief.

That evening he told Gaius the shape of it, in the tablinum, the boy across the desk with his school tablets forgotten. He told it without names he could not give, but honestly — the fraud, the danger, the reason the family's name might be spoken against in the coming weeks.

“Are we going to be ruined?” Gaius asked.

“Possibly. Ruin is a real possibility, and I won't lie to you about it. But the family isn't the house or the money. It's the people who tell the truth when it costs them. Your mother understands that better than anyone.”

“The Carinae woman. She's the one from the kitchen, isn't she. Uncle Celer let it slip once, when he was drunk. The slave girl.”

Marcus weighed it, and told the truth. “Yes. And I failed her, a long time ago, and I've spent this winter trying not to fail her twice. You're old enough to know that the men the city calls disreputable are sometimes the ones keeping its word.”

Gaius considered this with the seriousness he brought to everything. “Then I hope she wins. I'll put it in my prayers — the foreign goddess as well, if it helps.”

“It can't hurt. The foreign goddess has more worshippers in this city than the Senate has members.”

The boy went up to bed, and Marcus sat on alone among the ancestral masks, and thought that if the family were to fall, at least it would fall in front of a son who understood the reason, which was more than most ruined houses managed.

He told Antonia the prefect's answer that night, in the shuttered tablinum, and she heard him out with the still attention she gave the things that mattered, and said, when he had finished, “Then the two cases will run side by side. His inquiry in the shadows, Perperna's indictment in the open. He'll try to finish the open one first, because a conviction destroys our evidence before Attianus can use it.”

“That's my reading. The preliminary hearing is the key. If the praetor allows the indictment to proceed to a full trial, the house is under a cloud for months and the witnesses begin to reconsider their courage. If we break it at the hearing, Attianus's inquiry has room to move.”

“Then we break it. We have the better paper, the better witnesses, and the better cause. What we don't have is the law's friendship.” She considered the lamp. “There's a question you'll be asked, and you should decide your answer before they ask it. Whether you'll denounce me to save yourself. They'll offer it; they always do. The senator walks, the woman takes the full weight, and the honour of the Aelii is preserved.”

“You know my answer.”

“I know it tonight. I want it in writing, in front of witnesses, because I don't trust the version of you that gets frightened in a courtroom.” Her voice was not unkind. “Not because I doubt you. Because I've never met a frightened person I did trust, including myself.”

He wrote it, at her table, in his senatorial hand: that he would give no testimony against the house or its mistress, that he confirmed the partnership of evidence under the praetor's standing order, and that any statement attributed to him to the contrary was false. He signed, and Chloe witnessed, and the document went into the temple coffer with the tablets.

“Now the same question to you,” he said. “If it comes to a choice between the house and me—”

“There's no choice. The house comes first; it has eleven people in it and you're one man with a name and a family. I'd ruin you without a moment's hesitation to keep the doors open.” She said it calmly, and watched him take it. “Does that shock you?”

“No. It reassures me. At least one of us knows exactly what she's doing.” He almost smiled. “Livia asked me the same question, in different words. The women in my life have a remarkable grasp of the worst case.”

“Livia's the ablest of us. Treat her accordingly.” Antonia poured the last of the wine. “One more thing, while we're being honest on paper. If we survive this, there's no respectable ending for us. You know the law. A senator can't marry a meretrix; the censors wouldn't even let her sit in the public rows. Whatever this is, it lives outside the rules, and so do I, and I'll not be kept, and I'll not be hidden, and I'll not apologise.”

“I've stopped expecting respectable endings. They've never once arrived.” He set down his cup. “What I can offer is simpler. Whatever survives the spring, you and I will know the truth of, and I'll spend the rest of my life being worthy of that. The rest, Rome can litigate.”

She looked at him a long moment, and then, briefly, she smiled — the real smile, the one the guests never saw.

“That'll do, Marcus Aelius. That'll do for now.”
