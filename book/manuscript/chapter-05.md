# CHAPTER FIVE — Contracts and Silence

The Curia Julia smelled of old wood, lamp smoke, and the wool of several hundred men in winter tunics, and Marcus, entering at the third hour with the roll of senators swelling around him, felt the familiar pressure of the place the way a sailor feels the deck change under his feet when the weather shifts.

The consuls had convened them on the eastern supply schedule, and the house was full. Senators greeted one another in the aisles with the elaborate warmth of men who would ruin one another before the month was out, and Marcus moved through them with the nod-and-word economy he had perfected over fifteen years: a name for everyone, a stop for no one, his face arranged in the expression of dignified attention that passed, in this building, for an opinion.

Stichus walked at his elbow to the door, murmuring. “Praetor Gratus is three rows ahead and wants a word; tell him nothing about the audit. The praetorian prefect's deputy is here, which means the Palace is watching. Your brother's by the back columns with the Baeticans; he's been here since dawn, working the room like a candidate.”

“Thank you. Go and walk in the forum; come back at the dismissal.”

Inside, the consuls took their curule chairs, the augurs took the auspices with the usual dignified haste, and the sitting opened with a decree of public thanks for the health of the emperor's sister, which passed unanimously, as all such decrees did, and occupied the first half hour, as all such decrees did. Then the senior consul brought the house to the matter of the day, and the temperature in the Curia changed.

“The treasury requires confirmation of the eastern schedule's supply contracts,” the consul said, in the flat voice of a man reading a document he disliked. “Grain, leather, tents, transport, and the associated finance. A review has been entrusted to Marcus Aelius Severus, whose report the house will receive before the spring sailing. Are there statements?”

There were. There always were.

The first speaker was a former governor with investments in three of the contracting firms, who rose to praise the schedule as a model of administration and to warn the house against unsettling the markets at a moment of national preparation. The second was a Stoic senator of austere reputation, who rose to say that he had no investments anywhere and therefore no opinion, which the house received with the scepticism it deserved. A third wanted a full accounting of every contract since the beginning of the reign, which everyone understood as a method of ensuring that nothing would ever be accounted for at all.

Marcus listened, said nothing, and watched his brother. Celer was seated among the back benches with the Baeticans, his handsome face attentive, his applause perfectly timed, and once, when the former governor used the phrase “the proper confidence between the state and its contractors,” Marcus saw him lean to murmur in a neighbour's ear, and the neighbour nodded without looking at him. It was the body language of a man who had rehearsed.

Then Sextus Perperna asked for the floor.

He was not a senator. He sat with the equestrians and advocates at the back, in the position allowed to men of standing who were not members of the house, and he rose now with the diffident air of a man who regretted intruding upon his betters, and the consul, who owed him a favour Marcus had never been able to identify precisely, nodded him permission.

“Fathers conscript.” Perperna's voice was modest, well pitched, the voice of a schoolmaster explaining a difficult passage. “I claim no seat among you. I claim only the citizen's ancient right to warn. My profession, as the house knows, is the prosecution of fraud against the public; it has made me neither loved nor rich, and I have made my peace with one of those conditions.” A small laugh rippled through the benches, exactly where he had expected it. “I warn the house now. The eastern schedule is not merely expensive. It is, in part, invented. I have information — I will name sources only before the proper court — that certain contracts rest on inflated tallies, forged warehouse receipts, and subscriptions advanced by lenders who expect repayment from the treasury itself. A machine for turning public gold into private purses. And I warn the distinguished auditor—” here he turned, and his kindly eyes found Marcus with the accuracy of a bowman “—that the names in these accounts will reach closer to home than he anticipates. An auditor who cannot audit his own household audits nothing. The house should demand a report that fears no family.”

He sat. The Curia hummed, the way a hive hums when a stone has been thrown into it from a distance.

Marcus asked for the floor, and received it, and stood for a moment in the hush with every eye in the building on him.

“The citizen Perperna has warned the house,” he said. “I thank him, though warnings without evidence are among the few things Rome produces more cheaply than grain. I will say this. I accepted the audit because the consuls asked it, and I will deliver it because I gave my word. The report will name every firm, every tally, and every subscriber the documents reveal, without exception of friendship, family, or rank. If any name in these accounts is a name I bear, the house will read it in my own hand before it reads it in anyone else's. That is my statement.”

He sat. The applause was genuine in the middle benches and careful everywhere else. On the back row, Celer was smiling, but the smile had the fixed quality of a mask, and Perperna, across the aisle, inclined his head with the mild, satisfied air of a chess player whose opponent has just made the move he wanted.

The sitting ran to the ninth hour, through two more minor speakers and a wrangle about the price of public seals, and Marcus spent most of it watching the back of Perperna's head and reviewing, against his will, everything he knew about the man's method. Perperna never lost a prosecution he had chosen to bring. He chose them like a fisherman choosing water: slowly, once, and where the fish had nowhere to run. That he had stood up in the Curia and named the audit before it had even begun meant he had already decided what the audit would find, and the only open question was whose neck he intended for the demonstration.

When it broke, Marcus let the crowd carry him into the Forum, and Perperna was waiting for him beside the statue of Porsenna, as though the encounter were the most natural thing in Rome, eating olives from a paper cone with the calm of a man at the games.

“Severus. A strong statement. ‘In my own hand.' Brave. I do admire bravery, it's so seldom practical.” He held out the cone; Marcus declined with a motion of the hand. “Walk with me a little. The afternoon's fine.”

They walked among the bankers' stalls, two distinguished men taking the air, invisible in plain sight, which Marcus suspected was exactly why Perperna had chosen it.

“You made an accusation in there without a single document,” Marcus said.

“An accusation is a promise, and I keep my promises. The documents exist; I merely haven't decided who deserves to see them.” Perperna spat an olive stone into his palm and pocketed it, a man of tidy habits. “Let me save you a month of evenings. Your brother is fifteen thousand sesterces in my debt and his subscriptions are among the first I'd examine, were I auditing. You can imagine how it looks: the honest brother signs the tallies that absolve the debtor brother. The city's pamphlet writers have less imagination than I do, and they'll manage.”

“Is that a confession? That you hold paper on a subscriber to contracts under review?”

“It's an education. I hold paper on half of Rome; the difference between a banker and a villain is entirely a question of whose biography you're reading.” Perperna stopped by a fountain and turned to him, the kindly face sincere. “Here is my advice, offered free, which tells you how much I value your good opinion. Sign the schedule as presented. The war needs the supplies, the treasury needs the schedule, and your brother needs silence. One signature settles all three. Then in the spring, when the consular elections come round, no one has any reason to remember the name Aelius except with respect. You'd be surprised how cheaply a clear conscience can be bought, when the price is merely one's signature.”

“And the alternative?”

“The alternative is that you discover your family's shame in public, your brother goes into exile or worse, your consulship drowns in the scandal, and the audit that was meant to purify the schedule becomes the spectacle that discredits it. Everyone loses, except the man brave enough to tell the truth in court — which, in the circumstances, would be me.” Perperna smiled. “Rome rewards its teachers. Think it over. I'm a patient man; it's my only virtue.”

He drifted away toward the Argiletum with his paper cone, and Marcus stood by the fountain with the winter sun on his face and the cold knowledge, settling in his stomach like stone, that the choice Perperna had offered was the choice he had been engineered to face from the beginning. The audit was never a duty. It was a trap with two doors, both of them leading where Perperna wished.

He walked home through the Forum with the sun low over the temples, and did not speak to anyone, and did not need to; Stichus, meeting him at the door of the Curia, took one look at his face and followed him in silence up the Esquiline.

The family dinner that evening was, on its face, a success.

The dining room was warm, the silver was correctly placed, the cook had outdone himself with a dormouse dish in honey and poppy seed, and Livia had arranged the guests as she arranged everything: Celer, of course; the children at the smaller table where they could be seen and not heard; Livia's own cousin, the wife of a praetor, to make up the women's side; and an elderly jurist of Marcus's acquaintance, brought in, Marcus suspected, to give the evening a respectable cover.

The jurist, one Pacilius, had been a praetor himself in the year of the Dacian triumph and now spent his retirement giving legal opinions over other men's dinners, a role he performed with the slow pleasure of a man whose advice could no longer cost him anything. Over the fish course the talk turned, as dinner talk did that winter, to the fashion for prosecutions under the older statutes, and Pacilius was drawn out upon the lex Iulia de adulteriis, which he proceeded to explain to the table at the length of an entire sauce.

“The genius of the statute,” he said, waving a fish fork, “is that it makes the household itself the informer. The husband who fails to denounce within the statutory period becomes liable himself. You see the elegance? The law doesn't merely punish the offence; it punishes silence, and silence, dear friends, is the ordinary Roman husband's dearest privilege.” He beamed around the couches. “A reluctant husband is thus converted into a diligent prosecutor, and the treasury collects its share of the convicted woman's dowry. Everyone gains except, of course, the woman, which is customary.”

“Everyone gains,” Marcus said, carefully keeping his voice at dinner pitch, “or the delator gains? The statute assumes a husband with evidence. What of a husband presented with manufactured evidence, a seal used in his absence, a clerk's statement bought for coin?”

Pacilius blinked at the sharpness beneath the courtesy, and adjusted. “Manufacture is provable, where the manufacture is clumsy. Where it is skilled — and the skilled sort, I need not tell a senator, is the kind that reaches your table — the remedy lies less in the statute than in the household records. Diaries, steward's rolls, the daily account of who was where. Roman law, for all its grandeur, is in daily practice a great respecter of the household that keeps good books.” He helped himself to more fish. “A family without records, Severus, is a family that has already lost whatever case it hasn't yet been accused of. I've said it for forty years. Nobody writes it down.”

Across the table, Livia's eyes met her husband's for one precise moment, and moved away, and Marcus understood the dinner completely: the dormice, the cousin, the jurist, all of it arranged so that the family could be told, in respectable company and at no one's instigation, to begin keeping the kind of books that survive a prosecution. He thought of the wax tablets on his desk, the diary entries from Praeneste, the steward's movements, and he made a private note to have Phaon's daily rolls sealed at each month's end from now on, in duplicate, the second copy lodged away from the house.

At the children's table, Gaius was listening with the fixed, silent attention his father had learned to recognize, and Marcus saw with a slight shock that the boy was following the law as other boys followed the races, storing the rules of the adult world the way he stored the names of charioteers. Some of it, Marcus thought, would be needed sooner than anyone at this table wished.

Celer, for his part, ate through the jurist's lecture with the cheerfulness of a man who assumed laws were for other people, and drank two cups of the Alban, and asked no questions, which was, Marcus reflected, precisely the disposition that had filled his brother-in-law's life with creditors and would, before the winter ended, fill it with worse.

For an hour it held. Celer was at his most charming, telling a long story about a mule auction in the Forum that made even the jurist laugh, and Gaius, across the room, tried manfully not to laugh with his mouth full, and Vibia succeeded in teaching the cat — brought down from the nursery against all precedent — to sit beneath her couch like a small, bad-tempered guardian spirit.

It was Gaius, halfway through the dormice, who produced the evening's first difficulty, with the devastating timing of the young.

“Father,” he said, across the room, in the clear carrying voice of a boy trained in rhetoric. “Quintus's father says all the fathers go to a house in the Carinae where the women sing and no wives are allowed. Is that true?”

The jurist choked slightly on his wine. Livia's cousin became fascinated by her napkin. Celer burst out laughing and then remembered himself.

Marcus set down his cup. “Quintus's father should teach his son discretion before he teaches him anything else. The house exists. Women there are paid for their music and conversation. Men go because they enjoy both, and because Rome allows men pleasures it does not allow women, which is an injustice you are too young to debate and I am too tired to defend.”

“Are they wicked women?”

“There are no wicked women, Gaius. There are women with different misfortunes, and men who profit from describing them as wicked, which is cheaper than paying them honestly. Eat your dinner.”

Livia said nothing, but her eyes met her husband's across the couches with an expression he could not read, and later, when the children went up, Vibia paused at his couch to whisper that the cat had eaten three dormice and was now, in her professional opinion, the most distinguished person present, and the room breathed again.

But Livia's household ran on undercurrents, and when the women withdrew and the wine came round and the slaves had taken the second table, the undercurrent surfaced.

“You were magnificent in the house today,” Celer said, raising his cup. “‘In my own hand before anyone else's.' The whole Forum's quoting it. You could be consul twice with a tongue like that.”

“Consul once is sufficient for a man who intends to sleep at night.” Marcus set his cup down untouched. “What I require from you is not praise. Perperna all but named you in the Curia. Tell me plainly, before this goes further: how deep are you in the eastern subscriptions?”

The charm held; the colour did not. “Marcus, really, before our guest—”

“The jurist has heard worse, and probably ruled on it. How deep?”

“Eleven thousand.” Celer said it fast, the way a man tears off a plaster. “In the first subscription, and another four on a second note, most of it advanced by Perperna. There. I've said it. It's not a crime. Half the back benches—”

“It is a rope. Perperna doesn't lend money, Decimus; he distributes ropes, and he waits for the state to build the gallows. Why did you need fifteen thousand sesterces?”

The younger man stared into his wine. “The house on the Palatine. The games I gave as aedile — a man has to give games, Marcus, you know the rules, the crowd remembers a cheap aedile for twenty years. And the horses, and the dinners, and — oh, what does it matter. A gentleman's expenses are a gentleman's honour, and I have the expenses without the fortune.” He looked up, and for once the handsome face was open and wretched. “I'm sorry. There. I'm your little brother and I'm sorry, and I've been dreading this dinner since the audit was named.”

Marcus felt the anger drain out of him, leaving the older, heavier thing beneath it, the thing that had sat in his chest since the Tibur villa: responsibility.

“Can you clear the debt? Honestly, without the subscription profits?”

“Not before spring. If the contracts pay on the first instalment, I'm whole and Perperna has his principal and the thing quietly dies. If they don't—” He spread his hands. “If they don't, he owns me, and through me he gets the pleasure of watching you audit your own brother.”

Across the room, Gaius had gone very still over his dessert, listening with the entire attention of a boy who adored his father and worshipped his uncle and was beginning to understand that the two were not the same thing. Marcus saw him see it, and said, more gently, “We'll speak of this in the tablinum. Not at table.”

But Livia, returning with the cousin at that moment, had caught the tail of it, and her face did not change, and that was how Marcus knew she had heard everything and intended to discuss none of it until the guests were gone.

The guests were gone by the tenth hour. The household settled into its night routines, the lamps were trimmed, the children sent up with kisses, and Marcus and Livia faced each other across the empty dining room like the commanders of two allied states whose alliance had outlived its purpose.

“You knew,” he said.

“Of course I knew. I keep this household's accounts; I know the price of everything in it, including your brother's debts.” Livia removed her earrings and set them in a small dish, one by one, the movements precise. “I've been moving our private funds for two months to keep the worst of his creditors quiet. Do not look at me like that; it was your name they'd have dragged through the market, not mine.”

“You should have told me.”

“I should have been consulted, you mean. We are not in the habit of consulting one another, Marcus. We are in the habit of coexisting. It has worked very well for fifteen years, until your supper-house woman and your brother's debts arrived in the same season.” She met his eyes, and hers were cool and direct. “I heard about the Carinae. All the wives heard. You came home like a man who'd seen a ghost. Then Perperna stands up in the Senate and talks about names close to home, and now this. Is she part of it? The woman?”

“Antonia Cassia is a witness to more conversations than any of us like, but she is not part of any fraud, if that's what you're asking.”

“I'm asking whether she's part of _you_. There's a difference.” When he said nothing, she gave a short, dry laugh. “You see? Even the honest man has his silences. Very well. I will not pry into the ghost, because ghosts are a wife's least dangerous rival; at least she's old. But I will say this about Decimus. Do not let sentiment for your brother bury your audit. The Aelii have survived worse than one ruined spendthrift. We have never survived a name for dishonesty. Choose the name, Marcus. The name outlives all of us.”

“We were married at eighteen,” Marcus said, more to the room than to her. “My father arranged it on the day I came of age. I don't think either of us was asked.”

“Asked? I was shown your family tree and told to be grateful, which I was, because the alternative was an old man with gout in Pompeii.” Livia's voice was dry, but not unkind. “You were the handsomest bridegroom of the year, and you treated me with perfect, unbearable courtesy for the whole wedding. Courtesy, Marcus. Not warmth. I've had a great deal of time to consider the difference.”

“I tried to be a good husband.”

“You were a good husband, by every standard the law measures. You didn't beat me, you didn't spend my dowry, you didn't bring mistresses into my house, and the children are legitimate and healthy. Rome would give you a wreath.” She paused at the door. “What Rome cannot measure is that for fifteen years I've been the manager of your life rather than anyone's beloved, and I made my peace with it because the alternative for a woman of my family was not freedom but another house with another manager. I am not complaining. I am explaining why I will not allow your brother's appetites, or your ghost, or anything else, to bring down the structure I've spent half my life maintaining. The name, Marcus. Always the name.”

She picked up the earring dish and walked out, carrying herself with the erect dignity of a woman who had said exactly what she intended and regretted none of it, and Marcus stood alone among the dismantled couches and thought that his wife, in her way, was the most formidable person he knew, and that he had never once in fifteen years succeeded in being a husband to her rather than a colleague.

It was at that exact moment that Stichus appeared, softly, with a sealed letter that had been left at the door by a runner already gone, and Marcus broke the wax and read, in an unfamiliar clerk's hand, four lines.

_The auditor's name appears as witness on his brother's loan. Ask whether he wrote it. The grain is weighed at Ostia and rewritten at Rome. A dead clerk named Hector kept the true tally, and the Tiber has him now. Ask who profits by your silence._

No signature. No mark. He turned it over; nothing. He read it a second time, and a third, and carried it to the lamp and burned it, and stood watching the black flakes curl into the tray, with the unbidden, unwelcome image in his mind of a woman in a blue stola looking at him across a supper room as though he were a stain on her floor.

Earlier that evening, before the runner went out, Antonia had sat in the women's court while Phyllis combed out her hair, the old Roman ritual that ended every working day, and the household had gathered the way it always did at that hour, in a loose circle of women with mending and music and the day's small injuries.

Sabina was there, practising her letters on a slate, and Tyche, with the salve still faintly smelling on her wrist, and two of the musicians arguing about a tune, and for a while Antonia let the noise wash over her and said nothing, because one of the house's deepest rules was that the mistress sat with the women and not above them.

Then Phyllis said, without looking up from the comb, “I owe you a story. I said I'd tell you, and I'm a woman of my word. Not all of it tonight. The beginning.”

The court quieted by degrees, though no one had asked for quiet; the women of the house had learned that Phyllis's stories were worth any tune.

“I was a slave in the Aelius household before any of you were born,” Phyllis said. “I scrubbed their kitchen and I nursed their babies, and one of the babies I nursed was the boy Marcus, and one of the children who ran in and out of my kitchen was a little Greek girl called Anthe, whose mother sewed the family's linen. Anthe's mother was a quiet woman. She died of a winter fever when the girl was nine, and the girl would have gone to the fields or worse, except that she was bright, and bright children in a great house get used. She ran messages, she learned to read from the tutor's discarded scrolls, and later — much later — from the young master himself.”

Antonia felt the court's eyes turn toward her and did not move.

“I remember the year it began to change,” Phyllis went on, drawing the comb slowly. “I remember the mistress watching them. Not cruelly. Carefully. The way you watch a storm over the valley, deciding when to bring in the grain. The great houses don't have scandals, girls; they have solutions, and the solutions are always carried out by people like me, and paid for by people like her.” She laid the comb down. “That's the beginning. The rest is for another night, because the rest keeps me awake, and I'm old enough to choose my sleepless nights.”

“Did he love her?” Sabina asked, very low.

Phyllis looked at Antonia, and Antonia gave the smallest possible nod.

“Boys don't know what love is,” Phyllis said. “They learn it, or they don't, and the learning is the whole story. Now someone play something cheerful, before we all drown in our own reflections.”

The music started again, and later, when the court had emptied and the lamps burned low, Antonia sat alone a while with the remembered weight of the comb in her hair and the question hanging in the dark: whether the boy had become a man capable of learning, or merely another master of the house.

In the Carinae, the runner's report came back to the tablinum before midnight, delivered through Festus: letter accepted, read three times, burned. No alarm, no summons of the household, no message sent out.

“Self-contained,” Antonia said. “Good. A frightened man sends for help. A dangerous man thinks. He's thinking.”

Chloe sat across from her with Hector's tablets open, copying columns onto fresh parchment by lamplight, her eyes red but her hand steady. Beside her lay a wax tablet of her own, covered with the alphabet and the first principles of double-entry, the exercises Antonia had set her. The house was quiet around them, the night watch moving through the corridors with soft feet.

“Suppose he ignores it,” Chloe said.

“He won't. The letter doesn't ask him to do anything, which is why he can't leave it alone. It tells him his own seal may have been used without him. A man like Severus would investigate a stolen napkin; he'll investigate a stolen name.” Antonia leaned back in her chair. “What do the copies show?”

“That my brother was a genius with a column and a fool about danger.” Chloe touched the parchment gently. “The Ostia weights and the Roman tallies don't merely differ — they differ in a pattern. Every fourth shipment, the same gain: ninety modii. It's a system. Somebody's signature work.”

“Systems have authors, and authors have vanities, and vanities leave evidence.” Antonia took up her stylus. “Tomorrow we begin on the lenders. Every loan Perperna holds on the contractors' names. Find me the one he can't afford to have called. Hilarus's Ostia receipts by the end of the week. And Chloe—” she paused, choosing the words “—I want you to start carrying a knife. The little one, strapped to the inside of your arm. Festus will show you how.”

Chloe looked at her. “You think they'll come here.”

“I think they've already been here. The window bar. They know the house's layout, they move at night, and they kill clerks. Hope for the best and arm for the worst; it's the only prayer that ever works in Rome.”

Festus gave the knife lesson in the garden, before she dismissed them, and Antonia watched from the colonnade.

The blade was a small straight razor of Spanish steel, set in a bone handle, with a sheath that strapped inside the forearm beneath a wide bracelet. Festus had used similar knives in the arena, though for different reasons, and he taught Chloe without drama, the way he taught everything: slow repetition, no boasting, a professional's respect for the instrument.

“Up the forearm, under the ribs, or across the front of the thigh where the big blood runs,” he said. “You're not trying to win a fight. A woman your size doesn't win fights; she ends them, or she makes enough of a mess to reach the door. First lesson: drawing it. Again. Again. You're hiding the motion with your body, not your hand.”

Chloe practised until the draw came clean, and then they worked on the single upward stroke, again and again, into a sack of straw dressed in an old tunic, while the garden statues looked on and the fountain spilled its harmless water.

“Will it ever feel real?” Chloe asked, breathing hard.

“Everything feels real the first time,” said Festus. “After that it's just a tool, like a ladle. Don't make a romance of it. The men who make romances of knives are the ones who lose to the men who don't.”

Antonia said nothing from the shadows, but she made a note to herself: Tyche next, then Sabina when she was older, and the musicians if they were willing. The house's rule depended on Festus's fists; the women's own rule, the rule that lived inside their skins, depended on steel they could reach without asking.

She dismissed them then, and sat alone in the lamplit room with the ledgers and the soft sound of the fountain, and after a while she unlocked the small cedar box and took out the chipped wooden disc, and held it, and thought about a boy's hand over hers on a stylus, and about a man in a senator's tunic reading an unsigned letter three times before he burned it.

“Think,” she whispered to the empty room, to the disc, to the past. “Think, and be worth the trouble I'm taking. Because if you're not, Marcus Aelius Severus, I'll ruin you with the same care I'm using to save you.”

Outside, the watch called the fourth hour, and Rome, indifferent and enormous, went on about its night.
