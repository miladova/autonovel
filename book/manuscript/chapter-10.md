# CHAPTER TEN — Hilarus's Table

The ride out took an hour, and Chloe spent it coaching the evening's performance.

“Two Crassici, one philosopher, three Spaniards, two bankers, the brother, and the senator,” she said, counting on her fingers in the litter. “You're to be the cleverest person in the room and never look cleverer than the men. You're to let Hilarus lead the money talk until the second wine, when their guard's gone soft. And you're to keep your face absolutely neutral every time Marcus Severus speaks, because Porcia says the whole city's learning to watch that face.”

“Porcia talks too much.”

“Porcia loves you, which is the same thing in her case.” Chloe examined her profile in a little bronze mirror. “Are you going to be able to do this? Dinner across the couch from him, with the matrons counting your glances?”

“I've performed for harder audiences than a consular wife. The rule is the same as it's always been: the room is my house, wherever I'm sitting. Once I believe that, everyone else does too.” Antonia adjusted the blue stola's fold. “Besides, tonight's not about him. It's about the Spaniards' receipts and the banker on the Aventine. He's a witness to a business dinner; that's all the record will show.”

“You've an answer for everything.”

“I've had to. Girls without answers end in other people's ledgers.”

The villa lay a mile beyond the Capena gate, on the slope where the road turned south toward the Alban hills, and it was, Antonia observed as the litter climbed the cypress-lined drive, exactly the house a freedman built when he had decided to make the old families uncomfortable without giving them anything they could actually criticize.

The proportions were correct. The marble was Greek. The statuary was restrained. Only the scale, and the newness of the money, and the excellent Spanish staff, declared the owner's history. Hilarus met his guests at the door himself, in a flawless toga, with Porcia beside him in the blue that the matrons considered too bright for a woman of her station, and the two of them greeted their company with the particular confidence of people who had discovered that the best revenge was a better kitchen.

There were fourteen covers: three Spanish factors from Corduba and Gades, brown, capable men with oil under their fingernails; two Roman bankers; Marcus, alone, on the excuse of public business; a Stoic philosopher kept for ornament; the inevitable Celer, who had been unable to resist any table with a reputation; and a consular couple, the Crassici, who had accepted because Hilarus's wine was famous and spent their time making sure everyone knew the sacrifice involved.

Antonia arrived last, in the blue stola, with Chloe and a single footman, and the greeting line produced the exact small storm Porcia had promised. Crassicia, the consular wife, took in the dress, the companion, the unmistakable face, and produced a smile like a door closing.

“Hilarus, you _dog_. You didn't tell us the entertainment would be joining the table.”

“She isn't entertainment, Crassicia,” Hilarus said, beaming. “She's my business partner, and the cleverest person in Rome with a ledger. You'll like her; she's richer than your husband.”

Crassicus, the consular, flushed in patches. “There are places for women of — for women in business. The dinner table of one's peers is perhaps not—”

“Then it's fortunate you're not my peer,” Antonia said sweetly, “or I should have to mind. Hilarus, the drive was lovely; Porcia, the blue was exactly right.”

She took her place without being shown it, beside Marcus's couch, and the party settled into its food with the determined brightness of people determined not to be awkward, which is the most awkward mood in the world.

Before the dinner, the company walked the villa's terraces in the dusk, the Roman prelude that let everyone inspect everyone else's standing, and Hilarus used the tour to make his one political statement, quietly, through objects. The olive press in the farmyard was new and German; the granary was stone, fireproofed at absurd cost; the household slaves wore good tunics and answered him without flinching. A freedman, Antonia knew, was permitted everything except the appearance of taste; Hilarus had solved the problem by making the whole estate a lecture on competence, and even Crassicus, who had arrived prepared to condescend, was reduced to asking questions about the irrigation, which Hilarus answered in sentences too plain to show off.

“Remarkable fellow,” the consular muttered to Marcus, within Antonia's hearing. “Thirty years ago he was loading amphorae.”

“Thirty years ago a great many men were loading amphorae,” Marcus said. “He's the one who kept count.”

The dinner was magnificent. Hilarus's Spanish cooks produced the dishes of his homeland: excellent ham from the Sierra, olives from his own groves, a garum so refined that even the philosopher asked twice for more, and a whole tunny in pepper wine. The wine came from his own Campanian estate, chilled in the old snow, and the conversation moved, as Roman dinner conversation always did, from food to money to politics to gossip and back to food.

The factors were the evening's real prize. After the second course, when the women of the household had withdrawn and the wine had rounded every edge, the senior factor, a weather-beaten man named Secundus, began to talk trade with the freedom of a man three hundred miles from the customs officers.

“—the oil contracts for the eastern army are being bundled the same way the grain was,” he told Marcus, across the table. “A single firm wins the whole schedule, then sub-lets the actual supply at half the price and pockets the difference. The firm's registered to a freedman who can't write his own name. We know; we've met him. He's a door, Senator. Someone stands behind the door.”

“Did you bring the papers?” Marcus asked.

Hilarus answered: “Copies, sealed, in my strong room. The firm changes name each quarter, but the witness seals repeat. The engraver's mark is the same; Syrus on the Tiber bank identified it for me himself before he understood what he was identifying. After that he stopped answering his door.”

“Frightened?”

“Wise. There's a difference in our trade too.” Hilarus ate an olive. “He'll testify if the court's open and the guard's visible. He won't testify in a magistrate's back room, and I don't blame him; the Greek clerk who tried that is in the Tiber.”

Celer, two couches away, had gone very quiet over his wine.

Secundus had more to say, and the factors' Greek, a sharp-eyed man called Phoebus, took up the thread. “The army's leather is the next scandal, if anyone cares to look. Tents and boots, contracted for hides that were never delivered; the tally says twenty thousand hides, the tanners delivered twelve, and the difference was paid to a name that dissolves when you ask for its owner. My cousin tans at Pompeii. He has the workshop receipts. Nobody in Rome wants them; the quartermasters want their tents, the senators want their dividends, and the only man who wants the truth is the soldier marching in boots that split at the first river.”

“Bring me the receipts,” Marcus said. “Through Hilarus, with your cousin's mark witnessed. Every paper makes the case heavier; a prosecutor can be disbelieved, but a tanner's receipt is a stubborn thing.”

The philosopher, who had been drinking in disapproving silence, could not resist. “You speak as though the truth will simply prevail, Senator. In my experience, truth is a commodity like any other, priced by the market of power.”

“Then we corner the market,” Antonia said, from her couch, and the table turned toward her. “The truth doesn't win by being true. It wins by being better organized than the lie. We hold the receipts, the weights, the seals, and the witnesses; Perperna holds pamphlets and frightened men. Organization is the whole art.”

“Spoken like a merchant,” Crassicia sniffed.

“Spoken like a survivor,” Porcia corrected her, smiling. “Try the ham.”

Crassicus, to his credit, laughed, and even his wife's mouth twitched, and the table exhaled, and the wine went round again, and Marcus, looking at Antonia across the lamps, thought that he had never sat at a table where the cleverest person present was a woman the law called disreputable, and that the Republic his ancestors had built was stranger and more various than their statues admitted.

---

Before the sweet wine, while the second roast was being cleared, Hilarus drew the table's merchants into the kind of conversation that Antonia arranged suppers to produce: trade talk, apparently aimless, in fact charted like a sea voyage.

The question of the eastern contracts came up by way of the pepper, which was late and dear, and Porcia said her husband's factors had been told to expect worse, because the annona's ships were taking the best moorings at Ostia and the merchant fleet waited its turn like dogs outside a kitchen. “Whatever the bureau is shipping in such a hurry,” she said, “it has priority over pepper, and pepper, in my experience, is only ever postponed for grain or guns.”

“Grain,” said Crassicus, who was three cups in and beyond caution. “The new levies for the eastern army. Huge contracts. A man could retire on one run if his name's on the right list.”

“Or his wife's cousin's name,” said the shipping merchant beside him, and guffawed, and then remembered the company and turned the laugh into a cough.

Antonia let none of it touch her face. She filed the phrase — _the right list_ — beside Cossus's _private re-weighing_, and asked the table, with the perfect hostess's gift for making inquiry sound like flattery, how a contractor even came to be on such a list. The answers, offered over figs and warmed wine, built themselves into a picture: the lists were subscriptions, nominally open, actually controlled by a handful of names that renewed themselves from contract to contract; entry required a sponsor inside the bureau and, it was understood, certain arrangements about the surplus; and the firms themselves appeared and dissolved with the regularity of seasons, their papers witnessed by the same small circle of signatories.

“Shells,” Beryllus said unexpectedly, from the end of the couch, where he had been silent through the fish. The table turned. “In Alexandria we called them paper boats. They carry one cargo and sink. You'll notice none of these firms survives two contracts; a firm that endures keeps books that can be compared, and comparison is the enemy of the arrangement.” He helped himself to a fig with the air of a man who had said less than he knew, which was his usual air.

Marcus, watching Antonia across the lamps, saw her acknowledge the banker's sentence with the smallest inclination of her head, and understood that the supper had already paid for itself a dozen times over, though the bill for it would arrive at his own accounts in the morning.

The snub came during the dessert, and it came, as these things do, from the woman rather than the man.

Crassicia had spent the evening nursing her grievance, and the sweet wine did what sweet wine always does, and loosened the bolt on her tongue. The talk had turned to the new emperor's forum and the public dedications, and she said, across the table, with the bright voice of a woman pretending to be charming, “It's a mercy these ceremonies have standards of admission. One meets such _interesting_ people everywhere else these days. Why, at the races I saw a senator's wife actually seated within greeting distance of the Carinae.”

A small, awful silence opened around the table. Porcia's smile became dangerous. Hilarus opened his mouth.

Marcus spoke first, quietly, in the level voice he used in the Curia, which was why every head turned.

“Mrs. Crassicia, the woman beside me has more arithmetic in her little finger than your husband's entire client list, and I include his accountant. She has built, with no name, no dowry, and no man's permission, an enterprise that pays more in taxes than your family's estates. Rome has a great many women of impeccable ancestry who have never done a useful thing in their lives, and it calls them respectable. If your standards of admission exclude competence and reward idleness, I'm content to be excluded from them myself.” He sipped his wine. “Pass the honey cakes.”

The philosopher's hand froze halfway to his plate. Celer stared at his brother with undisguised awe. Hilarus beamed like a sunrise. Crassicia turned the colour of the wine, and Crassicus developed a sudden absorbing interest in his napkin, and the conversation lurched forward again with the frantic energy of a cart righted after a wheel had come off.

Antonia said nothing at all. She ate a fig, slowly, and her face gave nothing away, and when the women rose at the end of the course she moved with the rest, and it was not until they were alone in the garden, under Hilarus's imported roses, that she turned on Marcus with her eyes like flint.

“Don't ever do that again.”

He blinked. “Defend you?”

“Humiliate a matron in public on my behalf. Do you understand what you've done? By tomorrow every dinner in Rome knows the consul-designate quarrelled with a consular wife over the Carinae woman. Perperna's writers don't need to invent jokes; you've given them the whole plot yourself.” Her voice was low and furious. “I don't need a champion, Marcus. I need a partner. Champions are expensive and partners are useful, and I have spent fifteen years being useful because it was the only kind of safe I was allowed.”

“I won't apologize.” He was standing very straight, the way he stood in the Senate. “She insulted you in a room where I was eating. A man who lets that pass isn't prudent; he's a coward.”

“And a woman who lets a man fight her battles isn't prudent, she's finished. We're not the same, and we won't be, and the sooner you stop treating me like a matron whose honour needs your sword, the longer we'll both survive.” She stopped, drew a breath, and some of the fire banked. “That's the whole of it. I know you meant well. Meaning well is the Romans' favourite luxury, and someone else always pays the bill.”

For a moment he looked as though he might argue; then, to his credit, he thought about it, and the thought changed his face.

“You're right. About the politics, at least. I'm not sorry for the words; I'm sorry for the cost to you. Will that do?”

“It'll have to.” She looked away, toward the roses, and the anger drained out of her leaving the older, harder thing beneath. “We keep having this conversation in different shapes. You keep reaching for the world as it ought to be, and I keep standing in the world as it is. In the world as it is, a man like you and a woman like me can be allies. We can even be friends. Nothing else is on offer, and I'd rather you knew it now than discovered it at the worst moment.”

“I know.” He said it simply. “I've known since the chapel. I'm not asking for anything, Antonia. I'm merely — trying to become the kind of man who'd be worth asking, if the world were different. It may be the only atonement available to me.”

The garden was very still. Somewhere inside, the water organ started a tune, thin and sweet through the colonnade.

“That's a dangerous ambition,” she said, more quietly. “The men worth asking generally lose the things the other men want.”

“Then perhaps I'm tired of wanting the other things.”

He had stepped closer as he said it, not with intention, the way a man steps toward a fire in a cold room, and she had not stepped back, and for a held breath the garden held its breath too — the roses, the lamps, the distant music — and she could feel the warmth of him and smell the clean wool and the oil and the particular scent of a man who had spent his life out of doors, and fifteen years rose up in her like a tide, and she thought, with perfect clarity, _if he touches me now I will let him, and that is the most dangerous thought I have had in Rome._

“You don't get to say that.” Her voice came out harder than she intended, and she reined it in. “You have a wife, a son, a daughter, a consulship. You can be tired of wanting things because you've got them. I built mine from nothing, and if I weaken, if I let some grand romantic gesture carry the house off course, eleven women lose the safest roof they've ever known. There's no version of this where my heart gets to decide.”

“I wasn't asking it to.”

“You were standing close enough to. That's how it starts.” She turned to face him fully, the roses behind her dark in the lamplight. “Let me tell you what I can't tell anyone else, because it might be the only honest thing I say all month. I have dreamed, for fifteen years, of a man who'd come through a door and see me — not the house, not the woman the city buys, me. And then you walked through my door, and it turned out you were the original, the first one, and the dream got complicated in ways I haven't finished calculating. That's not an invitation, Marcus. It's a warning. I'm not immune. I'm just in charge.”

He absorbed that with the same patience he gave the audit tablets. “In charge is acceptable. Immune was never a requirement. I told you in the chapel: I'm not asking. I'm trying to become worth asking. That includes respecting the wall.”

“Then respect it from over there. Another pace back. Thank you.”

He stepped back, gravely, and the formality of it made something flicker between them that might have been amusement, and the amusement was more dangerous than the near-kiss had been, because it assumed a shared world.

Chloe appeared in the garden doorway at that exact moment, with the practised timing of a woman who had been watching from inside, and announced that Hilarus was calling for the Spanish brandy and Secundus had a story about a tax farmer and a mule that required witnesses. The moment broke like a reflection in a pond, and they went back in together, at a careful distance, and neither of them mentioned the garden again.

Over the brandy, Celer drank too much, as Celer did whenever his brother was in the room and his debts were in the air, and the charm loosened into the slurred confessions of a man who has decided to be loved for his sins.

“You all think I'm a fool,” he announced to the table at large. “That's quite all right; I think most of you are bores, so we're square. But I'll tell you what no fool ever understands, and that's how expensive _dignitas_ is. My brother was born with it; it grows on him like moss. I had to _rent_ mine. The games, the house, the dinners, the four Syrians — every sesterce was a ticket to a room I was born into and couldn't afford. Perperna understood that before I did. He's not a villain, you know; villains want things. He's a surgeon. He found the place where the old families were bleeding and he put his mouth to it.”

“Decimus.” Marcus's voice was quiet, and it cut through the brandy fumes like a blade.

“Oh, don't _Decimus_ me. I'm being profound. The philosopher should take notes.” Celer lifted his cup with an unsteady hand. “To the bleeding families. May the surgeons be gentle.”

Hilarus, with the host's instinct for rescue, started the mule story, and the party laughed on cue, and Marcus watched his younger brother with an expression Antonia could not read from across the room, though she guessed its ingredients: shame, love, and the cold knowledge that the charming drunk at the table was the weakest wall in the city, and Perperna knew it.

---

The ride home, in Hilarus's litter with Chloe opposite her and the torches bobbing along the road, was where Antonia paid for the evening.

“Your face,” Chloe said.

“My face is none of your business.”

“Your face is my business; I read it for a living. You're furious and something else, and the something else is what's keeping you awake.” Chloe settled against the cushions. “He's good at that, the senator. The quiet defence, the old-fashioned honour. It would be easier if he were a hypocrite like the rest of them. Instead he's the genuine article in a city of copies, which is much more dangerous.”

“I know what he is. I taught him his letters, remember; I've known what he is since we were children. That's the problem. If he were merely handsome and useful I could handle him the way I handle the guests.” Antonia stared out through the curtains at the dark road. “The danger isn't him. It's the girl I was, who thinks she deserves someone honest. I killed that girl in the Subura. I can't afford to find she survived.”

“Maybe she didn't die. Maybe she just went into business.”

“Worse.” Antonia closed her eyes. “Watch the house tomorrow. Perperna will answer the dinner; he always answers. I want to know before the messenger reaches the door.”

The answer came sooner than the morning. The litter had barely turned into the Carinae when Festus met it at the door with his street face on, and behind him in the vestibule stood a public messenger in the aedile's livery, holding out a sealed tablet with the careful blankness of a man paid not to know what he carried.

“Came an hour ago,” Festus said. “He's been made to wait, and he doesn't like it. Two witnesses with him, official. Also a man across the street selling nuts who's never sold a nut in his life; I've seen his face at the fountain.”

Antonia broke the seal in the tablinum, with Chloe reading over her shoulder. It was not the custody hearing — that paper she already held. This was a summons to present the house's licensing rolls, enrollment tablets, and the contracts of every woman resident, for inspection by the aedile's officers at the third hour of the following day, under the general review of houses of entertainment. The penalties for irregularity were listed with bureaucratic thoroughness: fines, closure, the transfer of residents to licensed houses of the city's choosing.

“That's tomorrow,” Chloe said.

“He counted on the dinner keeping me late and the wine keeping me slow. He doesn't know I don't get drunk.” Antonia set the summons beside her plate and began, immediately, to issue orders in the flat voice she used for fires and deaths. “Phyllis: every enrollment tablet out of the strong room, checked against the women, tonight. Chloe: the contracts, all of them, and the tax receipts back three years; find every gap and tell me before midnight. Festus: the three new guards on the door at dawn, and tell Vitalis I want every bar he hasn't finished finished by the second hour. If the officers find a single loose hinge, it goes into the report as evidence of disorder.”

“Tyche?” Chloe asked.

“Tyche stays in view, smiling, with her contract in her hand. Every woman in this house is going to be so completely in order that the aedile's men will weep with boredom.” Antonia poured a cup of water and drank it standing. “He wants paper. We'll give him paper until it buries him.”

They worked until the third watch. The tablinum became a scriptorium: Phyllis with the enrollment tablets, checking each woman's name against the aedile's register; Chloe with the contracts, her new accountancy showing its worth as she matched every seal to a receipt and every receipt to a date; Sabina, pressed into service for her clear handwriting, copying the tax record; even the musicians sat folding documents, and Festus tramped in periodically with progress on the bars, trailing the smell of iron and honest sweat.

It was during the midnight pause, with bread and diluted wine passed round, that Tyche said the thing the room had been avoiding.

“What if they don't care whether the papers are in order? What if they just — take us anyway?”

No one answered at once. The lamps flickered.

“Then they take me first,” Antonia said, “and the documents are already in three other hands — Hilarus, the priestess, and a sealed packet at Beryllus's bank with instructions for its contents to be read aloud in the Forum if I'm ever held without a public charge. Perperna knows this; it's the one reason I'm still sleeping in my own bed. Rome can't disappear me the way it disappeared Hector, because my disappearance has a price attached, and the city always pays its debts when they're properly documented.” She looked round the room, at every face. “That's the lesson of tonight, and I want even the scullery girls to know it. Paper is the weapon they use on the helpless. We're not helpless, because we keep better paper.”

Sabina, copying the last tax line, set her stylus down with the small determination of a girl making up her mind. “Then I want to learn the law. Not just letters. The law. The actual words they use.”

“Start with the Twelve Tables in the library tomorrow,” Antonia said. “They're old, but everything Perperna does is built on top of them, and you can't break a wall you've never measured.”

By the second hour the work was done, the rolls sealed, the women sent to their rooms for what sleep they could get, and Antonia sat alone a moment in the cleared tablinum, listening to the house breathe. Somewhere beyond the walls a watchman called the hour, and the false nut-seller across the street had finally abandoned his stall, and Rome turned in its sleep like a great animal, and she set her own tablets in order, and prepared to meet its officers in the morning with the one weapon they could not forge: the truth, correctly filed.

On the Esquiline, Marcus reached home to find Livia in the tablinum with her accounts, which was where she always was when she intended a difficult conversation, and he sat down across from her and waited.

“You defended her at dinner,” she said, without looking up.

“News travels.”

“News gallops. Crassicia sent round a note before the second course was cleared; she's been composing it in her head since the insult. Marcus—” now she did look up “—I've asked nothing of you in fifteen years except the appearances that protect the children. I'm asking now. Be careful. Not for my sake; for Gaius's. A consulship fathers can pass to sons; a scandal fathers nothing.”

“There's been no scandal. There's been a business partnership over the audit, and one dinner-table argument with a rude woman.”

“And the races, and the temple, and the broadsheets, and the way you look when her name's said.” Livia set down her stylus. “I'm not a fool, and I'm not asking you to pretend you don't feel whatever you feel. I'm asking you to keep it out of the record, because the record is all Rome keeps. Do that, and I remain your ally; I've even met the woman, as you know, and she's more than her reputation. Fail to do it, and I'll defend the children's name against both of you, and you won't enjoy the experience.”

He looked at his wife with the respect he had never quite learned to turn into love, and said, “You have my word. Nothing will touch the children, or the name. Whatever else happens.”

“Good. Then we understand one another, which is more than most married couples achieve.” She picked up the stylus again. “Your brother, by the way, has been up to something. My steward caught him this afternoon trying to have the Palatine house valued by a private agent. He means to sell it, secretly, to clear Perperna. I've had the deeds moved to my own strong box. You'll deal with him in the morning, or I will, and my version involves more witnesses.”

“The house isn't his to sell. It's family property, entailed on Gaius.”

“Entail is a moral rule, not a wall; a man with a forged witness will climb it. That's why the deeds are in my box, not his reach.” Livia permitted herself a thin smile. “You see why I keep the accounts. The Aelii produce heroes and spendthrifts in alternation, like a law of nature; the women do the actual surviving.”

He almost laughed. It was, he reflected, the nearest thing to a joke she had made in his company in a year.

“Will you tell me one thing honestly?” he said. “At the baths. You met her. What did you make of her?”

Livia considered the question, and him, with her head slightly tilted, the way she considered bids at auction.

“I made a woman who would have run the Senate better than half its members, if the Senate admitted women, which is precisely why it doesn't. I also made a rival who refuses to behave like one, which is infuriating.” She returned to her columns. “She's in love with you, by the way. Not that she'll admit it; she's far too disciplined. I've managed households for fifteen years, Marcus; I know the difference between a woman using a man and a woman pretending not to feel one. Use that knowledge carefully, because I will not repeat it, and I will deny it at need.”

Marcus sat very still. “And you?”

“I am the mother of your children and the keeper of your name, and I will be both until I die. What I feel is my own property, and Roman wives are allowed at least that.” She did not look up. “Good night. Deal with Decimus at dawn; he's always weakest before breakfast.”

Marcus sighed, and rubbed his eyes, and thought that between his wife, his brother, and the woman in the Carinae, he was being managed in three directions at once, and that the oddest thing was that all three of them were, in their different ways, right.
