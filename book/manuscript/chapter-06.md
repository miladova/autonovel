# CHAPTER SIX — The Soft Voice

Sextus Perperna came to the gilded door at the sixth hour, in full daylight, dressed like a visiting philosopher, and Festus let him stand in the vestibule for the precise length of time required to make a man of his importance feel it, which was about four minutes.

Antonia received him in the small reception room off the atrium, the one with the bird-painting on the wall and no couch low enough for a man to relax on. She was working at her accounts when he came in, and she finished the line, set the stylus down with deliberate care, and rose only as far as courtesy required.

“Perperna. My house is honoured, though my door is usually opened to men with names their fathers gave them. To what do I owe the distinction?”

He smiled, the kindly smile, and seated himself without invitation, arranging his mantle over his knees. “Directness. I approve; it saves the city's time. I've come as a friend, though I know friendship between us requires some imagination. May I speak plainly?”

“You may speak briefly. I'll decide whether it's plain.”

“Very well.” He folded his hands. “You are, at this moment, the most vulnerable wealthy person in Rome. Your licenses are under review. A magistrate holds a complaint for the surrender of a girl in your keeping. A murdered man was sheltered under your roof the night before he was cut, and the vigiles know it. Your women are enrolled, your accounts are clean, your taxes are paid — admirable, all of it. But cleanliness is a defence against honest prosecutors. It is no defence at all against a determined one.”

Antonia's face did not change. “You speak as though the determined prosecutor were in the room.”

“He is. He has been for two minutes.” Perperna's smile deepened, gentle as a doctor's. “I could close this house within ninety days. The licensing review finds irregularities — and every house in Rome has irregularities, if one has the patience to count them. The Aviania girl's case makes you a kidnapper of free citizens. The Greek clerk makes you a harbourer of fugitives, perhaps an accomplice in whatever fraud he was killed for. The charge of lenocinium does the rest. You would lose the house, the freehold, the bank deposits, and the protection your wealth has bought you. The women would scatter to the Subura, where the houses are cheaper and the rules are whatever the doorman decides. I've seen it happen to better establishments than yours.”

“You've made it happen to better establishments than mine.”

“I have. It's my gift.” He inclined his head. “You think your walls keep secrets. Let me show you otherwise, as a courtesy between professionals. On the night of the eleventh, the praetor Gratus left his signet ring here for the second time; inside it, beneath the stone, is a miniature of a woman who is not his wife. The governor of Syria's agent gambles more than his salary permits and owes Hilarus four thousand on last month's dice. The procurator's cousin is afraid of his own father, which is why he performs so loudly in bed. And your newest girl, the debt-bond, cries into her pillow for the first hour of every night and stops only when she has recited the names of her family's farm animals.”

Antonia's blood went cold; her face did not. “You have someone inside the women's wing.”

“I have someone inside every street in Rome. Do not look for one traitor; you'd be hunting a hundred pairs of ears. The point is that your sealed room is sealed only against the honest, and I am not honest.” He gave her the gentle smile again. “Which is why I'm here instead of at the courts. Ruining you would be easy and, frankly, not especially profitable. I prefer arrangements. Three things. First: this house becomes my listening post. The men who dine here speak with remarkable freedom; I want to know what they say, particularly the men around the supply audit. You will pass me notes — names, sentiments, anything useful — through a messenger of my choosing. Second: the girl Aviania is surrendered to the magistrate's keeping, after which she will be sold, lawfully, to a buyer I designate. Third: the freehold of this house is transferred to me at the public valuation, generous under the circumstances, and you remain as its manager on a salary.”

The room was very still. The fountain made its small, cool sound.

Antonia said, “You want me to become your spy, give you a free girl to sell, and hand you the deeds to the house I built. In exchange for what, precisely?”

“Protection. The review vanishes, the complaint is withdrawn, the clerk's death never touches your name. You keep your money, your comforts, your women, your life. All you surrender is the thing no one ever needed anyway: the fiction that you are independent.” He leaned forward slightly, soft-voiced and earnest. “Antonia. You are a freedwoman. The law does not love you; it merely hasn't noticed you yet. Every woman who has ever stood where you stand has ended in one of two places: a rich man's kept woman, or a name on an auction list. I'm offering you a third door. Walk through it.”

She let the silence run. She had learned, long ago, that silence after an insult was more eloquent than any insult, and she watched him sit in it, the kindly face attentive, the clever eyes measuring, and she thought that she had never met a man so at ease inside his own corruption.

“No,” she said.

His expression did not alter. “No?”

“No to the listening, no to the girl, no to the deeds. My guests dine here because they believe the room is sealed. The day I sell what's said in it, the house dies; you'd be buying a corpse. The girl stays under a lawful contract that Vipsanius sealed himself, and if your magistrate wants her, he can say so in open court, where I'll be waiting with witnesses. And the house is not for sale at any price, because the house is the proof that a woman born with nothing can own something in Rome. Take it from me over my dead body, and make sure yours is the next will read, because I keep copies of every document I've ever touched, and my bankers and my partner hold sealed packets with instructions to open them if I die in mysterious circumstances. You know Hilarus. You know Beryllus. Ask them whether I'm bluffing.”

Perperna looked at her for a long moment. Then he sighed, a genuine-sounding sigh of regret, and rose.

“I was afraid you'd say that. The strong ones always do; it's what makes them worth the effort.” He adjusted his mantle. “Let me leave you with one thought, since you're so fond of documents. The Greek came through your postern at the tenth hour. I know the hour. I know the room he slept in. I know the window. When a house can be entered in the night and a man taken out of it dead, the house is not a fortress, Antonia. It's a stage, and I've been watching the play for some time. Good day.”

He walked out himself, without a slave to show him, and Festus appeared in the doorway a moment later with the particular expression he wore when he had restrained himself from killing a guest.

“I could have thrown him into the fishpond.”

“And he'd have sued me for the fish. No. Let him walk; walking makes men careless.” But her hand, on the table, was closed so tightly around her stylus that the point had scored the wax to the wood.

When the door was shut and the guest's litter had turned the corner, she sent for Festus and Phyllis and sat them down in the tablinum with the lamps trimmed, the way she held every council of war, with wine for them and none for herself and the door bolted on the household side.

“Say what you think, both of you. Start with the small things; the small things in this house are the whole of its safety.”

Festus went first, because his trade was doors. “He came at the dinner hour with two clients and no litter of his own — hired, from the stand by the fountain. A man with his money doesn't hire unless he doesn't want his liveried bearers to know the address. He watched the locks the whole way in; I watched him watch them. And he asked the kitchen boy where the strong room was, as a joke. The boy doesn't know anyway, but the question's not a joke coming from a man like that.”

“Phyllis?”

The old woman folded her hands. “He knows the women's names. Not the enrolled names — the birth names. He called Tyche by her island name, the one she hasn't answered to since she was fourteen. Someone has been talking to someone who knew them before this house, or paying the dealers for their old lists. That's not flattery, domina. That's a man showing you he can reach inside the walls.”

Antonia sat with that a moment. The birth names were the deepest lever the city had over women like hers; a delator who could produce a woman's slave name in open court could unmake her presentable self in a sentence. She thought of her own name, Anthe, and the price tag that went with it, and kept her face still.

“Then we do three things, starting tonight. First, every woman in the house writes down her history — the true one, the dealer's name, the sale price, every place she's been — one copy for the strong room, one for the temple coffer. If a name is going to be used in court, it will be used by us first, in context, with the documents beside it. A secret he knows is a weapon; a secret we've already entered in evidence is a damp squib. Second, Festus: change the strong-room lock and add the inner bolt. The locksmith works here, in the atrium, where everyone can see him, so the street knows the door has new teeth. Third.” She paused. “From tomorrow, no one leaves this house alone. The market, the temple, the music teacher — pairs, always, and the youngest never without one of the older women. He wants us frightened in our own rooms. Frightened women make mistakes. We'll make none, and he'll have to make the next move, and the next move is evidence.”

Phyllis nodded slowly. “You've been waiting for a man like him.”

“I've been preparing for a city like this. He's merely the latest shape of it.” She unclenched her hand and laid the stylus down straight, parallel with the table's edge, the small act of order with which she ended every crisis. “Now. The evening meal. Tyche's singing tonight, and we will all look as though nothing on earth could disturb us, because the street pays for the story of this house, and the story tonight is calm.”

The calm was deliberate and visible. Antonia changed into the blue stola, had her hair redone, and ordered the best Falernian opened for the women's own table rather than the clients', and the household understood the message without needing it explained: fear that changed no routine was fear that had been beaten. When Tyche sang, the shutters stood open onto the lane, and the song carried to the fountain where the street's ears were paid to listen; the music said, more clearly than any denial, that whatever had walked through the gilded door that afternoon, it had not walked back out with anything worth having.

Festus changed the strong-room lock himself after dark, working by lamplight with the new inner bolt laid ready, and dropped the old lock into the well of the impluvium, where it joined the small archaeological record of the house's retired defences. “New teeth,” he said to Chloe, testing the bolt. “Let the next man bring better tools.”

---

Tyche came to her before the evening meal, in the small court where the jasmine grew, and the girl's hands were twisting a corner of her mantle into a rope.

“Domina. Something happened at the market. I should have told you sooner; I didn't know if it mattered.”

“Everything matters. Sit. Start at the beginning.”

“A man. Twice now, by the cloth stalls. Not young, not old, very polite. He asks after me — my name, where I'm from, whether I'm happy in the house. Yesterday he gave me this.” She opened her hand: a small silver brooch, cheap but new, in the shape of a dove. “He said a friend of his admired me, and if ever I wanted to leave the house, the friend would pay for a room of my own and a servant. He said I was too fine to belong to a freedwoman.” Tyche's voice trembled. “I didn't take it. Well — I took it, but only to be polite, and I haven't worn it. Domina, I'm not disloyal. I swear it by the house gods.”

Antonia took the brooch and turned it in her fingers. The silver was thin, the workmanship Subura, and the dove's eye was a chip of glass. Nothing about it was dangerous, which was precisely what made it dangerous: a gift small enough to accept, a promise large enough to dream about.

“Listen carefully. The man is Perperna's, or he works for someone who works for Perperna; the style is his. Nothing you've done is wrong — they approached you, not the other way round, and you told me, which is the one thing they can't survive. Here's what you'll do.” She set the brooch on the stone bench between them. “At the next market, be friendly. Tell him you're frightened of me, which he'll want to believe. Take a second gift if he offers one. Report every word, exactly, to me and no one else. You're not betraying him and you're not betraying me; you're letting two proud people spend their money while you keep your hands clean. Can you do that?”

Tyche stared. “You want me to pretend?”

“I want you to survive, and I want his letters. Rome is full of women who became powerful by being underestimated, and he has just made the classic mistake of believing a lyre player has no mind.” Antonia softened. “But if it frightens you, say so, and Festus walks you to the market from now on and the man gets nothing. The choice is yours. Always, in this house, the choice is yours.”

The girl sat a moment, turning it over. Then she picked up the brooch and pinned it inside her mantle, hidden against her breast. “I'll do it. For Hector. He was kind to me once, when I first came. Nobody remembers that, but I do.”

When she had gone, Antonia sat on with the jasmine smell and the fountain sound, and made a mark in her mental ledger: Tyche, courage untested until now, testing well. It was the kind of small, private accounting on which empires, and houses, ultimately stood.

They gathered after dark in the strong room: Antonia, Chloe, Phyllis, Festus, the door bolted and a trusted slave outside it. The tablets lay on the table among the silver, and a lamp hung from a chain above them, and the four faces around the light looked, Antonia thought, like the faces of a council of war, which was exactly what they were.

“He knows about the postern and the room,” she said. “That means his people have watched the house from inside the street, or someone inside ours talked. The bargeman, the suppliers, the night soil cart — Festus, every person who crosses our threshold gets a name, a sponsor, and a reason. No exceptions.”

“Already started.” Festus's jaw worked. “Vitalis, the carpenter, is fitting new bars to every upper window tonight, iron this time, set from inside. And I want two more men for the night watch. I know three ex-gladiators from the old school; they're loyal because I am, and they're cheap because nobody else will employ men with their records.”

“Hire them. Cost doesn't enter it.”

Chloe looked up from the tablets. “He called us a stage. He's right, and it's our weakness and our weapon both. If the house can be watched, it can also perform. Whatever he expects us to do, we do the opposite, loudly, while the real work happens where he isn't looking.”

“Which is why the tablets stay here and the copies go somewhere else.” Antonia unrolled a fresh parchment. “Chloe, your copy goes to Hilarus's Ostia office, sealed in his accounts. Phyllis, I want a second copy in the temple of Isis, with the priestess, among the votive records. A goddess makes a better strongbox than a banker; even Perperna hesitates before sacrilege, if only because it's bad for business.”

Chloe drew the tablets toward her and spread the parchment copies beside them, the way a surgeon lays out instruments.

“Look here. My brother's private marks — he put a tiny theta beside every entry he'd verified against an Ostia weight note. The thetas run for the first six months. Then they stop. From March onward, the tallies are clean copy work, no verification at all. That's when they brought him onto the board properly; that's when he stopped being allowed near the grain.” Her finger moved down. “And here, every fourth shipment, the same ninety modii. A signature, as you said. Whoever runs the machine is vain enough to keep a rhythm. Men who keep rhythms keep habits, and habits are how they're caught.”

“Can you reconstruct the money?” Antonia asked.

“Given time. The loan side's the cleaner half.” Chloe turned a tablet. “Perperna lends to the subscribers at twenty-four per cent, more than twice a respectable rate, which means the subscribers were already desperate when he found them. Desperate men don't conspire; they're herded. The conspiracy is one man's, and the rest are cattle with signet rings.”

Festus, who had been following with the strained attention of a man for whom columns were a foreign country, said, “So why not just kill Perperna? Men like him get knifed in alleys. I know men.”

“Because a dead Perperna makes a martyr and leaves the contracts standing,” Antonia said. “We don't want his corpse. We want his paper, in a court, under the right magistrate, with the emperor's prefect watching. The knife is the last argument; we're still using the first ones.”

Phyllis nodded, but she did not write anything down, and after a moment she said the thing that had been waiting in the room.

“He means to break you the way they break every woman who stands alone. First the paper, then the fear, then — when the women start leaving because they're frightened — the house falls by itself. I've seen it. I saw it begin, once, a long time ago.”

No one spoke. The lamp flickered.

“Tell them,” Antonia said. “All of it. They deserve to know whose house they're standing in.”

Phyllis folded her hands. She was old, and her voice had the steadiness of water running along a course it had worn for years.

“I was there. In the Aelius kitchen, the year the young master went to Germany. Our Antonia was seventeen then, called Anthe, house-born, her mother dead, the quickest girl I ever raised. The young master taught her letters and fell in love with her, which is the oldest story in the world, and she loved him back, which is the part the world never forgives. She was carrying his child when he marched north. The elder mistress found out at the wash tubs. I remember it like yesterday; I can hear her voice. She didn't shout. The dangerous ones never do.”

“She sent for Dromo the steward,” Phyllis went on. “The master wouldn't see the girl; gentlemen leave these things to their stewards. Dromo sold her to Dinis the dealer, nine hundred and forty sesterces, money down. I was in the colonnade when they took her out by the service gate. She didn't scream. She asked, very politely, whether a letter could be sent to the young master, and Dromo said the family would decide what letters were sent. That was the last I saw of her for eleven years.”

Chloe's eyes were on Antonia's face. Festus had gone very still.

“The dealer sold me to a house in the Subura,” Antonia said. Her voice was flat, a voice reading a document. “I'll spare you the interior; you've all seen enough of the world to imagine it, and your imaginations will be kinder than the truth. I worked there four years. The child came early, a girl, small and dead, and I nearly followed her. When I came up from the fever, I was worth less, and cheap women in cheap houses get sold on. I was bought by a courtesan called Antonia Cassia Prima, who had grown too old to do the work and too clever ever to have enjoyed it. She taught me everything this house knows — how to read a man, how to keep accounts, how to make desire wait while money changed hands. She freed me in her will and left me her name and a little house with six couches. I've spent eleven years turning it into the door Perperna wants. That's the whole story. There's no more to tell, and no one in this room is ever to speak of it outside it.”

No one spoke for a while. The lamp made the silver glow.

“I'll tell you one thing more, since we're being truthful in a locked room,” Antonia said. “The four years in the Subura taught me every lesson I've ever needed, and none of them was the lesson they intended. I learned that a woman without a man's protection is treated as prey by precisely the men who claim to protect women. I learned that the law is a language, and that people who speak it fluently can do almost anything to people who can't. And I learned that the body is a room other people believe they own, and that the whole work of a life is getting the keys back. Prima gave me the keys. Everything I've built is a house with bolts on the inside, and I will burn it to the ground before I hand a single bolt to Sextus Perperna.”

Festus nodded slowly. Chloe's eyes were bright, Phyllis's wet, and none of them said anything, because there was nothing to add, and the silence itself was a kind of oath.

“Right,” Antonia said after a moment, in her ordinary voice. “Back to the tablets. We've mourned enough for one evening; the dead don't pay the guards.”

“The letters,” Phyllis said. “The ones from Germany. Three came, with money. The mistress sent them back unopened. He never knew. She never knew he'd written. That's the part I carry.”

“It explains the man at the supper,” Chloe said slowly. “He didn't know.”

“No.” Antonia's mouth tightened. “He believed I walked away free and happy and never looked back, while I believed he let his family sell me without a word. Two stories, one lie, and the people who built it are all safely dead, which is very convenient for everyone except us.”

Festus, who had been silent throughout, said in his slow, deep voice, “What do you want done about him, then? The senator. I've seen how you look when his name's mentioned. I've also seen how he looked at you at the door, that night. A man doesn't look like that at a stranger.”

“What I want is irrelevant.” She bound the tablets with their thongs, one loop, two loops, precise. “What matters is that his name is on Perperna's paper and his brother's neck is in the noose, and the only honest auditor in Rome is the only man who can make a court believe these accounts. I don't have to forgive him. I have to use him. There's a difference, and the difference has made me rich.”

---

Sabina found her alone later, in the garden, feeding the fish.

The girl had been in the house nine days, and the change in her was visible already: she walked more steadily, she kept her hands still, she had learned to say “I'll consider it” instead of “yes.” She carried her slate now like a shield, and she held it out with the first line of her first full sentence written on it, the letters careful and even.

_The house is strong because the women are strong._

“One spelling mistake,” Antonia said. “And a split infinitive, which is forgivable in a beginner and unforgivable in a poet. Otherwise good. Who gave you the sentence?”

“Chloe. She said it was the house's motto, unofficially.” Sabina sat on the bench's far end, the careful space they had both learned to maintain. “Domina. The man who came today. The one Festus wouldn't let me serve. The girls say he's the reason the magistrate wants me. Is he going to take me?”

“No.”

“They said that in the village, too. ‘No one's going to take you,' the night before they came.”

Antonia turned to look at her. The fear in the wide, plain face was controlled now, managed rather than mastered, and she recognized in it the same thing she had built her own life out of: the refusal to be taken twice by the same terror.

“Listen to me, Sabina, and remember this as the second rule. The first was that no one touches you in this house against your will. The second is that no one leaves this house against their will while I have breath or money. Your debt paper is in my strong room, your contract is lawful, and if a magistrate orders otherwise in open court, I will spend every sesterce I own appealing it into the next emperor's reign. Perperna wants you because you're a wedge. He thinks if he can crack the door over one girl, the whole house splits. He's wrong, because the door isn't wood. It's me.” She tossed the last of the fish food into the impluvium. “Now go and correct the spelling.”

Sabina went, and Antonia stood listening to the household settle for the night — music practice from the east wing, Vitalis's hammering at the new bars, the fountain, the distant street cries — and felt, beneath her composure, the slow, cold exhilaration of a woman who has been cornered and has decided to turn and fight.

It was at that moment that Festus brought the second message of the day, this one on good parchment, sealed with a senator's ring.

She broke it. The hand was formal, the words few.

_I have received your letter, if the letter was yours. I require an hour of private conversation, on any ground you name, with witnesses of your choosing. I will come unarmed and alone, at any hour. The matter concerns the accounts, my brother, and a name I may have worn without deserving. — M. Aelius Severus._

She read it twice, as he had read hers, and smiled without humour.

“Send back word,” she said. “He will attend the races at the Circus Maximus on the day of the Saturnalian heats, as all Rome will. He may speak to me there, in public, where neither of us can be murdered for it. And Festus — make sure the word reaches Perperna's ears that the consul-designate and I have become sudden, visible friends. A friendship conducted in the open is the one kind he can't turn into a scandal.”

Festus grinned, the scar whitening. “You're going to make him dance.”

“I'm going to make us all dance,” Antonia said. “The trick is choosing the music.”

Phyllis, who had come in with the evening wine, waited until Festus had gone, and said, “You're careful with everyone else. Be careful with yourself where that man's concerned.”

“You mean Marcus.”

“I mean both of you, and the space between, which is the dangerous part. I've watched you carry that wooden disc for fifteen years. A woman doesn't keep a thing like that unless some part of her is still seventeen.” Phyllis set the wine down. “He may be everything you're hoping, now that he knows the lie. Or he may be a senator first and a man second, because that's what they make them into. Either way, don't give him the tablets until he's given you something that costs him. Proof isn't paper, Antonia. Proof is what a man loses by telling the truth.”

“Spoken like a woman who has survived the entire Julio-Claudian dynasty.”

“I survived worse than emperors. I survived being owned by people who thought they were kind.” Phyllis paused at the door. “The races will be loud, and crowded, and full of eyes. Good. Eyes keep men honest, at least while they're watching. Afterward is when you'll need your wits.”

When she was alone, Antonia poured the wine but did not drink it, and sat looking out through the colonnade at the garden's dark shapes. Somewhere beyond the rooftops lay the Esquiline, and the house of the Aelii, and a man reading her message with the same careful attention she was reading his. Fifteen years of silence, and now letters, and races, and a war of paper. She thought of the girl on the bench and the woman in the strong room and the child who had died in a dealer's house before she had drawn a proper breath, and she lifted the cup to each of them in turn, without drinking, and then she set it down and went to bed, because tomorrow's work required a clear head and Rome, as ever, would not wait.

Before she slept, she unlocked the cedar box, took out the chipped wooden disc, and placed it, wrapped in its old cloth, in the strong room drawer beneath the tablets — not because she had decided anything, but because she had learned that moving a thing was often the first step to deciding what it meant.
