# CHAPTER THIRTEEN — Terms

The two seals broke in the priestess's inner chamber, where the lamps burned before the image of Isis and the air smelled of myrrh and old stone, and Antonia laid Hector's original tablets on the cedar table between herself and Marcus with the care of a woman laying down weapons at a treaty.

Chloe stood against the wall with her arms folded, the priestess at the door, Festus outside it, and the room held the particular quiet of a place where history was being committed to wax.

“Four tablets,” Antonia said, “the duplicates the board didn't know existed. The theta marks are my clerk's verification against the Ostia weight notes; you'll find Dorion's notes match them tablet for tablet. The loan columns show Perperna's subscriptions. The witness seals repeat across the changing firm names. This is the machine, in the man's own hand.”

Marcus drew the first tablet toward him and read slowly, as he read everything, with his whole attention. The lamplight moved across the long, narrow face, and the two women watched him, and the only sound was the chanting of devotees somewhere beyond the wall.

“He was meticulous,” Marcus said at last.

“He was frightened,” Chloe said, from the wall. “Meticulous was how he stayed alive, until it didn't work. He thought if he wrote everything down, the writing would protect him.”

“He wasn't wrong. It protects him now. The dead can't be cross-examined, but their ledgers can, and these will survive it.” Marcus turned the second tablet. “The circular payment. My brother's loans. The Aventine bank. And Longinus names the bureau freedman—”

“Zosimus. A rationibus. The official who handles the emperor's supply correspondence.” Antonia placed Longinus's copied statement beside the tablets. “The fraud couldn't pass a single review without someone inside the bureau routing the questions. Zosimus is that someone. Perperna runs the street; Zosimus holds the door.”

Marcus sat back. “Then the case can't go to an ordinary magistrate. An imperial freedman answers to the Palace, not the praetor's court.”

“Which is why Ursus told you to take it to Attianus.” She had prepared the terms as carefully as the tablets, and she stated them now without flourish, the way she stated prices. “Here is my proposal. First: you carry the complete evidence to the praetorian prefect, under your name, with the praetor's standing already granted. I supply the documents, the witnesses — Dorion, Syrus, Longinus, and the tanner in Pompeii — and the house's records of what the contractors said over wine. Second: Celer turns state evidence at the moment you choose, early enough to earn relief, late enough that Perperna can't rearrange his affairs. Third: the house remains open and its women remain under their own contracts; the prosecution makes no use of their persons, only their testimony where they choose to give it.”

“Fourth,” Chloe said, stepping forward, “I testify. In open court, under my own name, to my brother's work and his murder. I'll not have him exist only as tablets.”

Marcus looked at her, and inclined his head. “Granted, and the court will be the better for it.” He returned to Antonia. “And in return?”

“Before we reach terms, the full witness list, so you know the shape of what you're carrying.” She counted on her fingers. “Dorion, the Ostia measurer, in Hilarus's warehouse; Syrus, the engraver, with him; Longinus, the Aventine banker, once his family's aboard a ship; Phoebus's cousin the tanner at Pompeii, with the hide receipts; the Hilarus factor's copies; and the statements of your own guests, the men who spoke over wine about phantom grain. Those last are delicate; I won't force a guest, but I'll tell you which ones said what, and you can decide whether Roman honour will make them repeat it.”

“Most won't.”

“Most won't. Some will, because the auditor asks them, and refusing an auditor is its own confession. We don't need all of them. We need the chain: Ostia to Rome, tally to gold, gold to seal, seal to Perperna, Perperna to Zosimus. Break any link and the case dies; keep them all and it can't.”

He nodded slowly. “You've built a prosecution. In a house of entertainment.”

“I've built an economy of trust. The prosecution is its side effect.”

“In return, the prosecution names the house as an honest source rather than a criminal scene, and your name stays between my doors and the praetor's office. No private bargain, no payment, no favour beyond the ordinary protection of a citizen's — a person's — lawful evidence.” She held his eyes. “I'm not buying you, Marcus. I'm hiring the only name in Rome Perperna can't purchase, and I'll have the terms clean, because clean terms are the only kind that survive.”

For a moment something moved behind his face, quickly controlled. “You've made this very easy to accept.”

“I make everything easy to accept. It's my trade.”

The priestess spoke from the door, calm as the Nile. “The goddess approves of clear contracts. Most human suffering arrives in the fine print.”

Marcus took the stylus Chloe offered and wrote his agreement on a fresh tablet, in his formal senatorial hand, and signed it, and passed it to Antonia, who signed beneath, and Chloe and the priestess witnessed. Then the tablets went back into the coffer and the two seals closed over them again, and the alliance that would occupy the rest of the winter existed, like everything powerful in Rome, as a document.

---

They walked in the sycamore grove afterward, while Chloe and the priestess walked behind at a respectful distance, and the winter sun made long shadows on the temple path.

Before the past, they settled the present, because both of them were people who did the practical thing first and the painful thing after. Marcus had questions about the coffer's mechanics, and Antonia answered them the way she answered everything, in terms of seals and copies.

“Nothing enters the coffer without two witnesses, and nothing leaves without both seals, mine and the priestess's. The temple holds the originals; Hilarus keeps a full copy in his warehouse strong room; the house keeps only the working tablets. If we're raided again, the officers find working papers, nothing more. If we're burned, the temple keeps the truth. If the temple is somehow reached, Hilarus keeps the second truth. Three doors, three keys, none of them in the same street.”

“Beryllus would approve. He keeps his own ledgers in three cities.”

“Beryllus taught me the principle, over figs. The city's honest men survive by being dull, and the city's dishonest men survive by being nowhere in particular. We're going to be both.” She tucked her hands into her sleeves against the shade of the grove. “For your part: every statement you take from here on, take it before two witnesses and seal it at once. Your household diary matters more than you think; Pacilius was right, and Livia has already begun the daily rolls. A prosecution is a story a delator tells about missing paper. We are going to be the paper that does not go missing.”

“Celer's subscriptions, the shell firms — I've begun matching them against the annona contract lists. The names rotate, but the witnesses don't. Half the firms share the same three signatories.”

“Then the signatories are the machine, and the firms are only its shadows.” She nodded slowly. “Find where the shadows meet. There will be a hand there — a steward, a freedman, a clerk with a taste for better wine than his salary allows. We don't need to prove the whole conspiracy in a week; we need to build it the way they built it, one witnessed name at a time, until the shape is unmistakable even to a praetor who'd rather not see it.”

“You speak like a general.”

“I run a house of eleven women in a city that would swallow eleven women before breakfast. It's the same trade, with better manners.” A pause. “One rule between us, while we're being practical. No protection I don't know about, no moves you make on the house's behalf without telling me first. The worst thing men in your position do to women in mine is help us in secret, because secret help is the kind that can be denied, and denied help becomes evidence against us. Everything in the open, or nothing at all.”

“Everything in the open,” he agreed, and the phrase, settled between them in the sycamore shade, would govern every harder decision of the winter, though neither of them yet knew it.

She watched him absorb it, and added the clause that mattered most to her. “And one more practical thing, while we're being exact. Whatever comes out in the inquiry, my house keeps its name and its licenses and its women's contracts. I am not to be rescued into respectability, Marcus. Rescued women become men's property in a softer vocabulary, and I've spent fifteen years refusing that bargain in every vocabulary Rome has. We cooperate as two free parties, or we don't cooperate; the alliance has no other form.”

“Two free parties,” he said, and the words came slowly, because he was measuring every one against the habits of his class. “I can agree to that. I'm not sure Rome can.”

“Rome isn't signing. We are.” She touched the coffer's iron band once, lightly. “There. Now that we've made the treaty, you may ask the difficult question. I saw it arrive on your face at the temple steps.”

The practical things said, the path narrowed, and the grove grew quieter, and the moment arrived that both had known was coming from the moment the coffer's seals had closed.

“You said once there was more to the past than the sale.” Marcus spoke to the path. “I've no right to ask, and you've every reason to refuse.”

“You've earned the question, which is rarer than the right. Ask.”

“The letters. Mine. Barto said three came, with money, and went back unopened. Did you never — was there nothing from me that reached you?”

“Nothing. Not a word. I believed you knew. I believed the family had paid you off with a clear conscience and a German commission, and that was the story I built my life on.” She walked a few paces. “It's easier, you know, to believe a man chose to betray you. Betrayal gives you anger, and anger is useful. The truth is worse: you were just absent, and absent people can't be blamed, and I've had to learn a new feeling at thirty-four, which is exhausting.”

“I wrote them in a frozen billet. The last one was mostly nonsense, I think; I was half delirious with a winter fever. I told you about the snow and the wolves and how I'd come home and we'd find a way, as if wishing were a plan.” He gave a short, bitter laugh. “It came back unopened, and I told myself that meant you'd chosen freedom. A man can build a whole philosophy on a letter he was too cowardly to investigate.”

“Your mother sent it back.”

“My mother. Dromo. The whole smooth machine of a great house, grinding on without anyone needing to give a cruel order aloud.” He stopped beneath a sycamore. “And the child. Laeta. You said — in the chapel, you said she was born before she could cry.”

Antonia stopped too, and the grove was quiet except for the doves.

“She was small. Six months, perhaps seven; the dealer's house had no midwife until it was nearly too late. I held her. She was perfectly formed, tiny and still, and I named her because no one else would bother. Then the fever took me, and when I woke she'd been buried somewhere in the common ground beyond the Esquiline, and I've never found the place.” Her voice did not waver; it had been used for this too many times in private. “That's the wound, if you've been imagining one. Not the men, not the house, not the work. The grave I can't visit. Everything else I could rebuild. A daughter doesn't rebuild.”

Marcus was very still. “I have a daughter. Vibia. Eleven years old, and she teaches the cat tricks and argues with her tutors. It never once occurred to me, when she was born, that there was another daughter, older than she is, who would have been mine.”

“The city prefers not to count the girls it kills. It keeps the ledgers clean.”

He said nothing for a long while. Then, with the formal care of a man performing an act he had rehearsed in fear, he took her hand — the left, the one without the rings — and kissed it, not as a lover, not yet, but as a man kisses the hand of someone he has wronged, in the old style, before the household gods.

They walked on, and after a while he asked, because he had made himself ask the harder things, “Tell me about the woman who freed you. Antonia Prima.”

“Small, sharp, older than any woman I'd met, and kinder than she could afford to be. She'd been the mistress of a wine merchant who left her money in a will his family contested for years; she won by being more patient than the lawyers. When she bought me I was eighteen, half dead of the fever, and worth very little, which is why she got me cheap. She said she could see the ledger-keeper in me. She kept a house of six couches, and she taught me everything: music, manners, the law's loopholes, and the one rule she said no courtesan ever survived forgetting — never confuse being wanted with being loved. They're close enough to fool the eye, she said, and as different as an investment and a gift.”

“Did she love anyone?”

“Her accounts, her cat, and, at the end, me, in her fashion. She used to say Rome would give a clever woman exactly two choices: be owned, or own. She chose to own, and left me the deed.” Antonia's step never faltered. “I've doubled the house twice. I've buried her in a proper columbarium with a marble plaque. I visit at the Saturnalia and tell her the figures. She'd approve.”

“And my marriage.” Marcus chose the words. “You should hear it from me, since the city will tell you louder and less true. Livia and I were married by our fathers; we have two children and a functioning estate and no love that either of us would recognize from the poets. She's the ablest person in my household, and I respect her entirely, and I've never once made her happy. There've been no mistresses — that's not virtue, it's temperament; I've no gift for casual things. I'm telling you this not as an excuse but as an inventory. You deserve the truth of what I am.”

“A husband, a father, a consul-designate. A man with a great name and a great deal to lose.”

“Yes.”

“Then we're well matched. I'm a freedwoman with a house, eleven dependents, and no name at all that the law respects. We're precisely the pair the law was written to keep apart.” She said it without self-pity, as a surveyor states a boundary. “Which is why the terms are what they are. Whatever this becomes, Marcus, it can't become respectable. Rome will never permit it, and I'll never ask Rome's permission. Do you understand what that means for a man like you?”

“It means I must decide what I want the rest of my life to mean. I've begun. The beginning is uncomfortable.”

“Good. Comfort's the luxury of people who've never had to choose.”

“I can't give you back the grave. I can give you the men who make the graves. That's the only atonement I know how to make.”

She let her hand stay in his a moment longer than the gesture required, then drew it away gently.

“Then make it well. We'll settle the rest of our accounts after the trial, if there's anything left to settle.”

Behind them, Chloe watched the doves and said nothing, and the priestess smiled the small smile of a goddess who had heard a great many vows and trusted very few of them.

---

That evening, on the Esquiline, Celer signed his statement.

Marcus had dictated it himself over three nights, in the tablinum, with Stichus writing and Livia present as the family's second witness, and it contained everything: the debts, the loans at twenty-four per cent, the bundle witnessing, the use of the family seal, and the names of every man who had guided him through the machine he had been too vain to examine. Celer read it twice, and for once did not reach for a joke.

“Once this is sealed, there's no road back.”

“There was never a road back; there was only the road forward in chains or the road forward as a witness.” Marcus pushed the wax toward him. “Sign. For Gaius, if not for yourself.”

Celer signed. Livia witnessed, Stichus witnessed, and the tablet went into the family strong box beside the deeds, and when it was done the younger brother sat looking at his hands, and said, without looking up, “I've spent my whole life being the charming one. It turns out the charming one was the easy one. The honest one costs something every hour.”

“Welcome to the rest of us,” Livia said, and poured him a cup of wine, which was, from her, roughly equivalent to an embrace.

Gaius, lingering in the colonnade where he had been sent to bed and had not gone, watched his uncle's back with an expression his father recognized from his own youth: the first sight of an adult being smaller, and more human, than the child had believed.

Sextus Perperna learned of the praetor's standing grant over lunch, in the house of a friend on the Caelian, and he did not stop chewing, which the friend later described as the most alarming thing he had ever seen a man do.

By the fourth hour he was in the upper room of a bathhouse behind the Palatine, in steam so thick the lamp was a red ghost, with the man the ledgers called Zosimus.

The imperial freedman was fifty, soft, and carefully dressed even for a bath, with the soft hands and hard eyes of a slave who had discovered that the emperor's correspondence was worth more than most men's fortunes. He spoke Greek-accented Latin in a low voice, and he was, Perperna had long known, braver about gold than about danger, which made him useful and required management.

“The praetor's office confirmed it this morning,” Zosimus said. “Standing over the Carinae house, under the audit. Ursus signed. Your prosecutor-turned-guardian has built himself a legal wall around the woman.”

“Walls are for people who intend to stay inside. I intend her in the dock.” Perperna poured water over the hot stones and watched it vanish in steam. “How much does he have?”

“More than is comfortable. The Aventine banker's gone quiet, which means he's talked. The seal-engraver's vanished, which means she has him. The Ostia measurer likewise. The Greek's duplicate tablets — I assume they exist, since everything else does.” Zosimus shifted in the steam. “If this reaches Attianus in one piece, the contracts unravel, and the contracts lead to me. I did not survive three emperors to be ruined by a whore.”

“Mind your language. She's a businesswoman now, according to the praetor's seal.” Perperna's voice stayed pleasant. “Listen carefully, because we have one season before the spring sailing, and the season must produce a trial that ends her before her evidence begins. Two instruments. The first is the lex Iulia de adulteriis. The auditor has been seen at her house, at her box, in her temple. Reputation plus opportunity is nine-tenths of that statute, and I'll provide the tenth with a willing witness.”

“From inside her house?”

“From near it. There's a girl — Tyche, the lyre player. My man's been working her for weeks. She hasn't turned; I didn't expect her to. But fear outlasts loyalty, and I mean to give her a reason to be afraid. A statement that the house profits from assigning women to named clients is lenocinium, and lenocinium closes the door and disbands the witnesses in one stroke.” Perperna drew a finger through the steam on the bench. “The second instrument is the auditor himself. A prosecution naming him as the adulterous senator doesn't need to convict. It needs to make him choose: denounce her and walk away, or stand beside her and lose the consulship and the name. Men like Severus can resist money and threats. They can't resist the destruction of the thing they love most, which in his case is his own honour.”

Zosimus was quiet, watching him through the steam. “And if neither instrument works?”

“Then there's the third, which has never failed me. Fire.” Perperna stood, reaching for his towel. “But fire announces itself, and I prefer the law. Arrange the girl's summons through the aedile's office — a request for her testimony in the custody matter, nothing threatening. Bring her to a room where my people can speak to her alone. Fear, properly administered, is cheaper than bribery and leaves no accounts.”

“You've done this before.”

“I've done everything before. That's the advantage of my profession; the material is always the same.” He wrapped the towel and paused at the steam-room door. “One more thing. The praetorian prefect is honest, which means he's slow. Honest men need the evidence presented like a dinner course, in order, with clean hands. We'll make sure the first course he tastes is ours.”

Zosimus was not reassured. “If it fails, remember that my name appears nowhere. I route correspondence; I sign nothing.”

“Everyone signs something. The art is arranging whose name sits above the line.” Perperna's voice came gently through the steam. “Don't trouble yourself with failure. I've prosecuted a Vestal's lover, a governor, and two consulars; the city provided me all of them wrapped in their own certainty. A freedwoman and an honest senator are merely the latest course. The trick, always, is to move first. By the time their evidence reaches the prefect, the prefect will have heard ten times that it's fabricated by a jealous brothel-keeper. Even truth, repeated against often enough, begins to sound like gossip.”

“And the emperor? Trajan dislikes informers; everyone knows it.”

“Trajan is preparing a war and trusts his household. I am part of his household's convenience, and inconvenient people are dealt with by convenient methods.” Perperna opened the door. “Relax, Zosimus. Rome has never once lost a war against a woman with no legal standing. It would scarcely know how.”

The cold air hit them, and the slaves came forward with oils and towels, and the two men dressed and parted in the street with no visible sign of acquaintance, the way conspirators do in a city of watchers, and each went home to his own dinner and his own calculations.

He went out into the cold air of the changing room, and the slaves brought his clothes, and he dressed with the unhurried neatness of a man completely at peace with his own methods, and by the time he reached the street his smile was back in place, the kindly smile, and no one watching him pass would have guessed that he was composing, in his head, the ruin of a woman he had never once seen afraid.

---

The summons reached the gilded door at the eighth hour, polite, sealed, apparently harmless: the lyre player Tyche was requested as a character witness in the custody hearing of Sabina Aviania, to attend at the aedile's offices on the following market day, with the option of a private preliminary interview to refresh her recollection.

Antonia read it, handed it to Chloe, and watched her read it, and neither of them needed to say what it was.

“He's changed tactics on the weakest wall,” Chloe said.

“Then we make the wall stronger. She doesn't go alone, she doesn't answer questions without me in the room, and if the interview turns, she asks for the adjournment and leaves. Tell her tonight.” Antonia took the summons back and placed it with the others in the strong box, in the growing file marked with the name of the man who had decided to make her winter interesting.

Outside, the street vendors cried their wares, the water clocks dripped, and Rome went about its thousand appetites, and somewhere in a bathhouse behind the Palatine two men were deciding the price of fear, and in a house in the Carinae three women were deciding not to pay it.

Tyche took the news standing up, in the women's court, with the lyre silent in her hands.

“It's not a witness summons,” Chloe said, going over it again. “It's a request, dressed up as one. They want you alone in a room where a clerk can write whatever the man with the soft voice tells him to write. You're not going alone.”

“What will they ask?”

“First, gentle things. Whether you're happy, whether you're paid, whether anyone's ever frightened you. Then the turn: whether the house tells you which men to sit with, whether gifts change hands, whether you've ever felt you couldn't refuse. Every question is a door; you answer only the one asked, you never volunteer, and if they touch the contract or the house's rules, you ask for me and for the adjournment.” Antonia had a wax tablet of her own now, with the questions written on it and the answers rehearsed beneath. “Read these tonight. Tomorrow we run it like a play, twice, until you're bored. Boredom is what brave looks like from the outside.”

Tyche read, and looked up. “What if I fail?”

“Then you fail, and we deal with it, and you still sleep here with the bolt on your door. That's the other thing they don't know about this house.” Antonia's voice softened. “You're not alone in the room because you're weak. You're never alone because none of us is. The whole point of the house is that the door has eleven women behind it.”

The lyre player held the tablet against her chest like a shield, and nodded, and went off to memorize her lines, and in the garden the fountain spilled its cool water, and somewhere in the house a musician practiced a scale, and the ordinary, stubborn life of the place went on, which was, Antonia thought, the deepest answer she knew to men like Perperna: to keep making music while they sharpened their knives.

The preliminary interview was held in a bare room at the aedile's offices, on market day, with Tyche on a stool between Chloe and a house advocate and two clerks across a table, and the polite man from the market — she recognized his voice before she saw his face, the careful friendliness of it — sitting in the corner with his hands folded.

The first questions were exactly as Antonia had predicted. Her name, her origin, her enrollment, the terms of her contract. Tyche answered in the rehearsed, bored voice, the voice of a girl who had been told to make bravery look tedious, and the clerks' wax gathered nothing worth having.

Then the turn came, as turns do, smoothly, without warning.

“And when a guest asks for your particular company,” the senior clerk said, “who decides the price and the terms?”

“The contract sets the prices. I decide the company. No one sits with me without my agreement, and nothing beyond conversation and music happens in the house at all, under any terms, without my written consent before witnesses. It's clause nine. You have the copy.”

“Has anyone ever — persuaded you? Offered you a better arrangement away from the house? Encouraged you to leave?”

She let her eyes move, just slightly, to the man in the corner. “Yes, as it happens. A gentleman at the cloth stalls has offered me gifts and a room of my own, on behalf of an unnamed friend. I reported each offer to my mistress, as the house rules require, and kept the gifts as evidence. A silver dove brooch and a glass bracelet. They're in the strong box with the dates.”

The polite man's hands tightened once, very slightly, and loosened.

The clerk changed direction twice more and got nothing but the same calm, documented answers, and at last the clerk with the soft voice in the corner leaned forward and said, in the warm tone of an uncle at a wedding, “My dear, you may speak freely here. Are you afraid of anyone?”

Tyche turned to look at him fully, the way Chloe had taught her, eye to eye, no flinch.

“Yes,” she said. “I'm afraid of men who give bracelets to girls they don't know and ask them to sign things without their advocates. But I'm told the law protects people who answer in front of witnesses, so I'm feeling much better.”

Chloe, behind her neutral face, wanted to cheer. The clerks looked at each other; the soft-voiced man said nothing at all; and the interview was closed with the thin-lipped record that the witness had nothing material to add.

Outside in the market, in the noise and the sun, Tyche's knees gave way for ten seconds on a bench, and Chloe held her wrist and said nothing until the shaking stopped.

“You were magnificent.”

“I was terrified.”

“That's what magnificent is, when anyone bothers to tell the truth about it.”

They went home through the crowded streets with the two guards a discreet distance behind, and when Tyche walked into the atrium Antonia was waiting, and said only, “I heard. The whole house heard, by now; news moves faster than the water. Go and eat something. You've earned the good bread.”

That night the lyre played later than usual in the women's court, and the tune was a defiant one, and even Sabina, struggling with the Twelve Tables, smiled over her scroll at the sound of it.
