# CHAPTER SEVEN — The Circus

The Saturnalian heats began at dawn, and by the fourth hour the Circus Maximus held a quarter of a million people, which was approximately a quarter of a million more than any structure had any sane reason to contain.

Antonia's party arrived in litters through the merchants' gate, Hilarus having hired one of the better wooden boxes above the turning posts, where the shade held until noon and the wine could be kept cool in snow. There were six of them: Antonia and Chloe, Hilarus and his wife Porcia, Festus in a clean tunic that made him look like an unhappy mountain, and Sabina, who had never seen the races and was doing her disciplined best not to gape, and failing endearingly at every turn.

“Breathe,” Antonia said as they climbed the wooden stairs. “You look like a girl being led to sacrifice.”

“I feel like one. Domina, there are _people everywhere_. Where do they all sleep?”

“Badly, on top of one another, which is why they scream so loudly here; the Circus is Rome's only room with space in it.” She handed the tickets to the attendant and stepped into the box, and the full roar of the place hit her like a warm wave: the long oval of sand, the spina with its obelisks and statues, the coloured cushions of the senatorial seats opposite, the flags of the four factions snapping in the wind — Reds, Whites, Blues, Greens — and above it all the baking white sky and the smell of two hundred and fifty thousand sweating bodies eating sausages.

Porcia, a stout, shrewd woman who had sold oil with her husband before either of them could afford litters, laughed at Sabina's face. “First time? Mine too, four years ago. I cried. The noise, you know. Then someone won me ten sesterces and I've come every race since. Sit by me, child; I'll explain the criminals.”

She explained them with the precision of a woman who had made her own money. The four factions were not merely racing colours; they were companies, with investors, stables, spies, and the loyalty of neighbourhoods that had nothing else to be loyal to. The Blues drew the merchants and the ship owners, the Greens the market traders and most of the Subura, the Reds the old families who did things because their grandfathers had, and the Whites whoever was winning. Drivers were slaves or freedmen, adored like gods and buried like boys if they misjudged a turn, and the fastest of them earned sums that made senators' wives bitter.

“It's the one place in Rome the rich and the poor scream the same scream,” Hilarus said, handing round the dates. “An emperor ignores the Circus at his peril. The bread keeps them alive; this keeps them governable.”

“And the betting?” Chloe asked.

“The betting keeps them poor, which keeps them governable too.” He winked. “I own a share in two Blues. Don't tell the customers; they think I'm neutral.”

Sabina drank it in, the factions and the flags and the vast democratic roar, and Antonia watched her relax by degrees, the way a stray animal relaxes when it discovers the warmth is genuine. It struck her, not for the first time, that the house's deepest work had nothing to do with men and everything to do with this: the slow rebuilding of a person's expectation of safety.

The first heats were already running. Seven laps per race, eggs dropped from a frame to count them down, the chariots tearing around the spina at speeds that looked impossible and often were, the crowd rising as one at every turn. Sabina was on her feet by the second lap, shouting with the rest, her reserve gone, and even Festus permitted himself a low rumble of appreciation when a Blue driver took the inside post with a hand's width to spare.

“Your man's late,” Hilarus murmured, leaning over the rail with his dates.

“He isn't my man, and he won't be late; senators arrive after the consul's procession so that the crowd can see them arrive.” Antonia did not look toward the senatorial seats. “There. Watch the opposite rail.”

The consular procession was making its slow way along the front row: lictors with their fasces, the magistrates in purple-bordered togas, the senators in their brilliant white, and among them Marcus Aelius Severus, walking with his son beside him, his head held in that unmistakeable profile. The crowd recognized the name; there was a small, warm round of applause from the middle seats, the applause Rome gave to men it believed honest, whether or not it intended to keep them so.

Beside him, Gaius was looking everywhere at once, in the transports of a boy at his first Saturnalian heats. Behind them, in the matrons' section, Antonia caught a glimpse of Livia Marcella, erect and elegant in dark red, acknowledging greetings with the minimum movement required.

“Wife's here,” Chloe said softly, at her elbow.

“I can see that. Wives attend races; it's one of the few outings permitted them, which is why they dress for it as though for battle.” Antonia lifted her cup. “Relax, all of you. We're simply respectable people at the games. Respectability is a costume like any other; wear it lightly.”

---

On the senatorial benches, Marcus was having a worse morning than he had expected.

Livia had chosen the races with the same care she gave all public appearances, and she sat now among the consular wives in the section reserved for rank, receiving greetings, commenting on the teams, performing the ancient, exhausting office of being a great man's wife. Gaius was beside himself with delight, and Vibia, who had been brought along under protest, was asleep against her nurse's shoulder before the third heat.

Marcus had come intending to watch the horses; instead he watched the men, because the Circus Maximus was the one place in Rome where every rank sat in the same roar, and a senator could observe without being observed observing. Below the senatorial benches the equestrian rows curved away in a sweep of good wool and careful grooming, and there, three sections down, he saw the plain dark tunic and the kindly, attentive face: Sextus Perperna, not among the equestrians of his own rank but seated one row behind them, in the cheaper seats, where he could watch the backs of the richer men's necks. He was not watching the races at all. He was making his harvest of the crowd, noting who greeted whom, which wives arrived with which escorts, which young men drank too deeply before noon; a delator at the games, Marcus realized, was a fisherman at a weir, and the Circus had been built for him.

Beside Perperna sat a younger man, expensively dressed, who talked with animation while the informer listened, and Marcus had Stichus identify him between heats: one Aquilius, an orator of the smaller courts, golden-voiced and expensive, whose practice ran to prosecutions bought by men who preferred not to show their own faces. The name went into Marcus's mental tablets beside the face, because the company a man kept at the races was the company he intended to use in the Forum.

Between the sixth and seventh heats, the crowd surged for the refreshment sellers, and Marcus found himself shoulder to shoulder at a wine stall with Cossus, the grain contractor, red-faced and garlanded and full of his own importance.

“Severus! The auditor himself. Come to see how Rome enjoys the money you're forever counting?” Cossus laughed at his own wit and waved a sausage. “You want a word of advice, man to man? Leave the annona contracts alone. There's a fellowship in the supply trade; we look after our own, and the bureau looks after us. A man can audit himself into a very quiet retirement if he doesn't understand the difference between what's written and what's done.”

“I've never been good at hearing that difference,” Marcus said. “My defect.”

“Defect?” Cossus grinned, but his eyes were small and assessing. “Call it what you like. The horses lose sometimes too, Senator, even the favourites. That's why they run the races.” He clapped Marcus's arm and moved off into the crowd, and Marcus stood with his weak wine and watched him go, and thought that threats at the Circus were the only kind Roman men made without lowering their voices, because the roar of two hundred thousand witnesses swallowed every word.

He looked across the great curved space, past the spina and its obelisk, to where the women's benches rose in their bright mosaic of parasols and veils, and found the dark, unadorned figure he was looking for, seated among the entertainers' section as though she belonged nowhere and everywhere. She was watching the track, or appearing to; and when the next heat burst from the starting gates and the whole Circus rose shouting around her, Antonia alone remained seated, turning her head slowly across the equestrian rows with the same fisherman's patience he had just recognized in Perperna. Two people, Marcus thought, hunting the same river from opposite banks; and somewhere between them, in the roaring sun, swam the truth about the grain.

“You've been watching the hired boxes more than the sand,” Livia observed, without taking her eyes off the teams.

“I've been watching the crowd. A senator should know his city.”

“A senator's wife knows he isn't watching the crowd when his eyes go to the same box every heat.” Her voice stayed light, perfectly social. “The Porcius box, isn't it? Hilarus the oil man. And his guest of honour. She does make an entrance; I'll give her that.”

Marcus said nothing. There was, he had learned, no answer to Livia in these moods that did not cost more than the silence.

“Go and speak to her, if you mean to,” she said. “Here, in daylight, with your son, it is merely politics. If you don't go, you'll think about it all afternoon, and I'd rather have your attention for the dinner with the prefect. Take Gaius; he needs the sausage stall, and the boy makes you respectable.”

He looked at her then, and she turned her face to him, and in it there was neither anger nor pleading, only the clear, weary intelligence of a woman who had made her peace with a disappointing marriage and refused to be made a fool of in public.

“You're remarkable,” he said.

“I'm practical. Remarkable women are burned as witches; practical women run Rome.” She waved a hand. “Go. And Marcus — whatever this is, remember the consulship. We have all spent too many years on it to waste them in a hired box.”

He took Gaius, and he went.

He came at the seventh heat, during the interval, when the crowd was occupied with food and a troupe of acrobats on the sand. Marcus crossed the long front promenade with Gaius, stopped to greet two senators, and then, with the unhurried certainty of a man who had known exactly where the box was since he entered the building, climbed the wooden stair and presented himself at its rail.

“Domina Antonia.” His voice was formal, pitched for the attendants. “A fine day for the Blues, though I'm told the Reds have a new driver from Africa. May I present my son, Gaius. Gaius, this is Antonia Cassia, whose hospitality your uncle enjoys so much.”

The boy bowed, correctly, and then looked up with the directness that the children of great houses either learned early or never learned. “You're the lady from the house in the Carinae. The one Quintus's father talks about.”

“Gaius,” Marcus said, in the voice he used for senatorial interruptions.

“It's quite all right; Quintus's father talks about a great many things, and few of them accurately.” Antonia smiled at the boy, the smile she kept for the young and the unarmoured. “Are you enjoying the races, Gaius?”

“Very much, domina. My father prefers the gladiators, because they're more serious, but I like the horses. They don't know about politics, so they run for their own reasons.”

“A philosopher already,” said Chloe. “Rome is doomed.”

Marcus's eyes moved to her, and Antonia made the introductions: “Chloe, of my household. Lucius Porcius Hilarus and his wife Porcia, whose box this is.”

The two men measured one another — the senator and the freedman merchant — and then Marcus bowed slightly, correctly, and Hilarus bowed back, and Antonia watched the old Roman machinery of rank groan and adjust and, because it was a public day at the games and rudeness would be observed, settle into a temporary truce.

“Your servant, Hilarus. I believe your oil contracts cross the annona offices; perhaps we'll speak of grain one day.”

“My door's always open, Senator. In my trade, a man's only as honest as his accounts, and mine are beautifully kept.”

Marcus gave the small nod that passed, in him, for amusement, and then he said the thing he had come to say, still in the public voice, still with the attendants listening: “Domina, the interval runs a half hour. My son has been begging for the sausage stalls at the upper level. Would you and your friend walk with us? The view of the spina is better from the promenade, and I've a question about a Greek poet your singer is said to perform.”

It was flawless: an outing, a chaperone, a literary excuse, the boy as witness. Antonia rose. “Chloe, keep Sabina out of the betting ring; she has the face of a girl about to lose her savings. Hilarus, hold the box. We'll take the air.”

They walked out into the promenade, four of them, Gaius ten paces ahead pretending not to be a chaperone, the crowd swirling past in a river of colour and noise, and for the first few moments they said nothing of substance, because there was no substance that could be said in such a river, and Marcus used the time to look at her properly, in daylight, the way he had not been able to across a lamplit supper room.

“You're older,” he said, low.

“Everyone is. It's the one reliable Roman institution.”

“You look well. More than well.”

“I am well. Wealth agrees with me; I recommend it.” She kept her face toward the sand. “Your letter was bold. A married consul-designate requesting an assignation.”

“It wasn't an assignation, and you know it. I need to know whether the unsigned letter was yours. The one about the witness line.”

“It was.”

He was quiet a moment, turning that over. “Then you know more about my brother's loans than I do. My own signature appears on Perperna's paper, and I have no memory of writing it. Explain that to me, if you can.”

“Easily, and without magic. At the contracts you witnessed as a family favour, did Celer present you with a single document, or a bundle?”

Marcus stopped. “A bundle. Three tablets, I think, sealed; he said the witnesses signed across the thongs, as usual, and I—”

“And you signed across the thongs without reading what lay beneath them, because he's your brother and the seals looked correct. Senators do it daily; it's how half the frauds in Rome are built. Your name witnessed a loan you never saw, at twenty-four per cent, to finance subscriptions that don't exist except as figures. You're not a villain, Marcus. You're a convenience.”

The noise of the Circus filled the space between them. Somewhere below, a roar went up as a chariot nearly overturned and saved it.

“I deserve that,” he said.

“You deserve a great deal more than that, but we're in public.” She glanced at him, and for one instant the mask slipped and he saw the seventeen-year-old in the woman, quick and fierce and wounded. “The point is that you're inside the trap whether you're guilty or not. Perperna doesn't require your guilt. He requires your silence, which your brother's shame will buy for him. The audit only works if you're free to name names. You're not free, because your own name is in the ledger.”

“Then what do you propose?”

“That you stop being an auditor and become a partner. Not mine — the truth's. There are documents. Real ones, duplicates of the board's tallies, kept by the clerk they've since murdered. I hold them. In return for what I know, I need your name where it matters: in a court, before a magistrate who can't be bought, with the prefect's office watching. And I need your brother kept breathing and in Rome, because a dead debtor closes the case and an exiled one takes the evidence with him.”

Gaius had turned at the sausage stall and was waving, holding up two enormous sausages like trophies. Marcus raised a hand in answer, and his face, Antonia saw, was the face of a man revising every map he owned.

“Give me a day,” he said. “I'll examine my own records. If what you say is true—”

“It is. Check the date of the witness signature against your diary of public appearances. You were dedicating a temple at Praeneste that morning. A man cannot witness a signing he wasn't in Rome for. They used your seal, not your hand. Ask Stichus; he keeps your diary.”

He looked at her then, fully, and the crowd might as well not have existed. “Why are you doing this? You could sell those tablets to Perperna for a fortune, or use them to blackmail half the Senate. Why hand me the means to prosecute?”

“Because the clerk who kept them was my friend's brother, and the Tiber is teaching Rome a lesson I refuse to learn. And because—” She stopped herself, chose again. “Because a city where men like Perperna buy houses with the proceeds of corpses is a city where my house doesn't survive either. Self-interest, Senator. I'm a very respectable woman.”

---

The crash came in the eleventh heat, and it came as catastrophes usually do in Rome: suddenly, completely, and in front of the largest available audience.

The African driver for the Reds took the turn too wide under pressure from the Blue, his wheel caught the stone base of the spina, and the chariot came apart in a long, tumbling explosion of timber and horses. The tangled team dragged across the path of the next two chariots, and within seconds the sand held a heap of screaming animals, splintered wood, and men, while above the crash the vast crowd surged to its feet with the sound of a single animal drawing breath.

The wooden barrier below the front boxes, old and half-rotted as half of Rome's public woodwork was, chose that moment to give way. A twenty-foot section dropped inward, and the spectators behind it spilled forward in a wave of tunics and cushions, and the wave hit the lower promenade where the four of them were standing.

Marcus moved before he thought. He had Gaius by the back of the tunic in one motion, lifting the boy off his feet and driving him backward against a pillar; his other arm caught the woman beside him — a stranger, a merchant's wife with two small children — and hauled her clear of the falling rail. Across the promenade, Antonia was knocked off balance by a fat man in a green cap, and would have gone down under the press if Festus had not materialized through the crowd like a ship through surf, one vast arm around her, another scooping up a fallen child. Chloe had Sabina by the wrist and her back against a wall, her eyes hard and clear.

“Hold still!” Marcus shouted over the screaming. “Hold the pillar — _hold it_ — don't run, the crush is behind you!”

It lasted perhaps five minutes. The vigiles and the stable hands waded in with staves, the injured were pulled clear of the broken barrier, the horses on the sand were cut loose or dispatched where they lay, and order returned the way it always returned in Rome: noisily, partially, and because enough disciplined men did their jobs.

When Antonia could breathe again, she found herself pressed close against the pillar beside Marcus, Gaius white and gripping his father's hand, the air thick with dust and the smell of frightened people.

“Everyone whole?” Marcus said, and his voice was steady, the officer's voice, and she understood suddenly how he had commanded men on the frontier.

“Hilarus's party is fine; Festus doesn't permit injury.” She looked down; the child Festus had saved was wailing in its mother's arms, unhurt. “You move quickly for a senator.”

“Germany. A winter of collapsing things. You don't survive it by standing still.” He released the merchant's wife to her husband, and then, in the dust and the noise, with the crowd still pressing around them, he did something uncharacteristic: he put his hand briefly over Antonia's where it gripped the pillar, not a caress, merely a steadiness, and said, low, “Are you hurt?”

The touch lasted two heartbeats. His hand was warm and calloused in the particular place a sword hilt makes. Fifteen years dissolved in the dust of the Circus, and she was back in the villa kitchen, and the hand over hers was the same hand, and the eyes were the same eyes, and she had to make a deliberate effort to remember that she was thirty-four years old and owned a bank.

“No,” she said. “I'm not hurt.”

He took his hand away. Neither of them pretended it had meant nothing.

A woman's scream from the broken barrier saved either of them from having to speak. A carter lay with a broken leg where the rail had fallen, white-faced and biting his belt, and the crowd was flowing around him the way a river flows around a stone. Marcus was already moving; after a moment's hesitation Antonia followed, and watched him take charge without raising his voice — sending Gaius for the vigiles' stretcher party, ordering the gawkers back with the flat authority of a magistrate, kneeling in the dust to set the man's leg against a board with the competent hands of an officer who had done such things in field hospitals. The carter, who had expected to be robbed where he lay, stared at the consul-designate binding his shin with strips of the man's own tunic.

“Why?” he kept saying. “Why, master?”

“Because Rome doesn't leave its people in the dust,” Marcus said, and Antonia, standing behind him, heard the words and could not tell whether he believed them or was merely determined to make them true, which she suspected was the only honest kind of goodness available to a Roman.

When the stretcher party came, he stood and brushed the dust from his toga, and they walked back through the crowd together, and for a while neither spoke.

“You're different from the men in my house,” she said at last.

“The men in your house are mostly trying to be someone. I've spent my life trying to be worthy of someone who's dead. My father. It's a different cage.”

“Is it? Cages are cages.”

“Then tell me how to open this one.” He stopped in the lee of a pillar, away from the stream of people, and his face in the slant light was tired and honest. “I've been to Tibur. I've read the sale entry. Nine hundred and forty sesterces. My mother's hand beside it. I've spent fifteen years believing you walked away with manumission silver. My father told me that, and I let him, because it was easier. I'm not asking you to forgive me; I've no right to it and you've no reason. I'm asking you to believe that I didn't know.”

She looked at him for a long time, and the Circus roared around them like the sea around a lighthouse.

“I believe you,” she said finally. “I checked; I have my own ways, and they all say the same thing. You were in Germany, and the letters came back unopened, and they lied to us both. Believing you didn't know is easy, Marcus. What I don't know is whether it changes anything. Knowing the truth costs a man like you something. I haven't seen the price yet.”

“Name it.”

“It names itself. At the temple, when you see the tablets.” She stepped back into the crowd's flow. “Until then, keep your brother close and your seal closer. Whoever used it once will use it again.”

---

They found Hilarus's box again during the lull, and it was Chloe, with the singer's gift for seeing the whole room, who leaned close and murmured, “Don't turn around yet. Two boxes to the right, the narrow one with the plain awning. Perperna's man — I've seen him at the market, the one who courts Tyche. And he's not watching the races.”

Antonia did not turn. “Hilarus. The box with the plain awning. Whose is it?”

Hilarus squinted across the way. “Rented, I'd say, by the day. The man in it I don't know. The older one behind him — yes, I do. That's one of Perperna's clerks. They're here for somebody, and it isn't the horses.”

“They're here for us,” Antonia said. “For the meeting. He wanted to know whether I'd take the auditor's hand, and now he knows.” She drank wine, her composure restored. “Good. Let him carry home the news that the whore and the censor are friends. A scandal he can't prove is worth less than a partnership he can't break.”

Marcus, who had followed the exchange, said, “He'll move faster now.”

“Then so shall we. The day after tomorrow, at the temple of Isis near the Esquiline gate, in the afternoon; the priestess owes me a favour and the precinct is neutral ground. Bring your diary and your witness's eye. I'll bring the truth.” She met his gaze levelly. “And Marcus — bring no one who answers to your brother. Not even family. Especially not family.”

He nodded once, gravely, and went back to his own seats with Gaius, and Antonia watched him cross the promenade through the thinning crowd, and beside her Chloe said very quietly, “His hand on yours. I saw.”

“It was the crush.”

“It was the crush. Of course. And I'm a vestal virgin.” Chloe ate an olive. “Just remember what Phyllis said. Proof is what a man loses by telling the truth. Watch what he loses at the temple, not what he says.”

The final heat ran in the lengthening afternoon, the Blues winning by a wheel, and the quarter-million poured out into the streets in a river of noise and colour, and somewhere in the narrow box with the plain awning a clerk made his careful note and slipped away through the crowd, and Rome, which had seen everything and forgotten nothing, rolled on toward another evening.

That night the house held its own council in the garden court, with the lamps floating in the fountain and the night watch doubled at the door.

Hilarus went first, counting on his fingers. “Perperna's clerk saw the meeting, so by tomorrow morning he'll know the auditor isn't isolated. That forces his hand; a man with a schedule moves faster when the schedule's threatened. Expect something before the temple meeting — a legal move, a bribe, or a fright. He likes fright; it's cheap.”

“Then we make sure the fright lands where it costs him nothing to give it,” Antonia said. “Tyche's market man comes back to her tomorrow, Festus following two streets behind. If he offers money, she takes it. If he offers threats, she remembers every word. Chloe, the temple precinct arrangements: speak to the priestess tonight, and make sure the side entrance is open. I'll not be followed into the presence of a goddess by Perperna's clerk.”

Sabina, who had been silent through the council, spoke up into the pause. “Domina. At the races, when the barrier fell — everyone pushed, and I thought I was going to die, and nobody in the crowd cared at all. And then Festus picked up a child like it weighed nothing, and the senator held up a whole family, and — and nobody paid them for it.” She frowned, working it out. “Why did they do it?”

“Because there are two Romes, Sabina.” Antonia chose the words carefully. “The one that pushes, and the one that holds. You've met mostly the first one. Don't let it make you doubt the second exists; just never assume it will arrive in time.”

On the Esquiline, Marcus reached home before the evening meal and went straight to his tablinum, where Stichus kept the master's diary of public appearances — a meticulous document, begun in the year of his quaestorship, recording where every day had been spent and who had witnessed it. He found the entry without difficulty, in Stichus's beautiful hand.

_The third day before the nones of March: dedication of the restored temple of Hercules at Praeneste; present the praetor, the town council, the contractor Cornelius; slept at Praeneste._

He sat with it a long time.

“Stichus.”

The nomenclator appeared. “Master?”

“Do you remember this day? The Praeneste dedication. We were gone two nights.”

“I remember it well, master. The road was flooded; we nearly lost the luggage mule. You delivered the dedication speech in the rain.”

“And Celer remained in Rome.”

“He did. He said his knee troubled him.” Stichus permitted himself the smallest pause. “Is something wrong, master?”

“Nothing you've done. Something that was done in my name while I was in the rain at Praeneste.” He closed the diary. “Bring me the family seal ring from the strong room. And don't mention this to anyone. Not my wife, not my brother, especially not my brother.”

When the ring lay in his palm, he turned it slowly, examining the intaglio of his ancestors' device, and thought of his brother's charming smile and the borrowed fifteen thousand and a signature that had never happened. The seal was kept in the strong room, to which Celer had access as family. It would have taken the steward ten minutes and a pot of wax.

He wrote no letter tonight. Instead he sat late, reading the audit tablets he had brought from the treasury, the columns of grain and gold, and for the first time he read them not as an official reviewing figures but as a man reading the map of an ambush, and by midnight he had begun to see, between the neat lines, the shape of the machine that meant to swallow his family and his name together.

In the litter going home from the races, Sabina fell asleep against Porcia's shoulder with the taste of her first sausage on her lips and ten sesterces of winnings tied in her belt, and Antonia sat awake in the dark, and did not look at her own hand, which still remembered the weight and warmth of his.
