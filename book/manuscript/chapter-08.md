# CHAPTER EIGHT — A Father at the Door

Quintus Avianius came to the postern at the ninth hour of the morning, and Festus, who had been a slave and a gladiator and therefore knew every species of desperate man, recognized the species before the man spoke: the smallholder at the end of his resources, washed and combed for the city, his one good tunic belted tight against the loss of weight, his farmer's hands hanging at his sides like tools he had forgotten how to use.

“I've no appointment,” he said. “I know I've no appointment. But I'll not go to the front door, not with what I am. Tell the domina it's Sabina's father. Tell her I won't stay past my welcome.”

Festus looked him over once, the slow professional glance, and said, “Wait in the passage. There's a bench. Don't touch the wall; it's wet paint.”

Antonia received him in the garden court, with Phyllis present as chaperone and a slave set to bring bread and wine, and she let the man stand until he had been given something to eat, because a starving man cannot negotiate and she needed him able to think. He took the bread with the careful dignity of the proud poor, broke it, ate half, and stopped, saving the rest.

“For my wife,” he said, catching her look. “She's worse. The ague. We've a neighbour woman with her.”

“Say what you came to say, Avianius. Farmers don't walk four miles to Rome for the bread.”

He set down the cup. “A man came to the farm. Two days ago. Fine cloak, soft hands, a clerk with him. Said he represented a gentleman who took an interest in my case. He said my daughter was being held in your house against the law — that she was tricked, that the debt paper wasn't properly transferred, that a magistrate would give her back to me if I made a complaint in writing. He had the complaint already written. I was to sign it, in front of his witnesses, and my debt would be cancelled into the bargain. Three thousand sesterces gone, just like that, and my girl home.”

The court was quiet. The fountain spilled its water.

“Did he give a name?”

“No name. The gentleman was ‘a friend of the family.' But the clerk talked when the soft one was busy with the mule. He said something about a prosecutor. Per—Perperna.”

Antonia's face did not change. “And you came here instead of signing. Why?”

For the first time something like anger moved across the weathered face, the anger of a slow man whose patience has been mistaken for stupidity. “Because I may be poor, domina, but I'm not a fool, and I've one thing left, which is my daughter's good opinion. I sold her once. I signed the paper that put her in chains because there was nothing else to do and her mother needed a doctor. I'll not sell her twice, with a lie, to men who'd drag her through a court and then sell her on to whatever they've got planned. Whatever this house is, the girl's own letters say no one's touched her and she's learning to read. My Sabina, reading.” His voice shook; he mastered it. “I came to ask your terms. The truth of them. Not his.”

Antonia regarded him. She had dealt with thousands of men in fifteen years — liars, lords, bankers, buyers of women — and the rare honest ones always cost her more, because they made her feel the things she had trained herself not to feel.

“My terms are these. The debt paper is mine, at four thousand. I'll tear it up today, in front of you and my household, and Vipsanius gets nothing further from you. The farm stays yours; I'll have Hilarus's factor advance seed money for the spring sowing, at no interest, against next harvest's oil, and you'll pay it in olives, not grief. In exchange, when Perperna's man comes again, you'll tell him you consulted your daughter and refuse to sign, and you'll note the day, the witnesses, and every word, and bring them to me. Not to the courts. To me.”

He stared. “You'd cancel the debt. Just so.”

“Just so. I've made worse investments. An honest farmer who owes me a favour is worth four thousand in any market; Beryllus the banker will tell you the same, in better Latin.” She paused. “There's a second matter. Your daughter wants to stay. Not because she's held — the door has no bolt on her side, she could walk out with you this afternoon and no one would stop her — but because she wants the education. I'll keep her, clothed and fed and paid, and you may visit once a month, at this postern, and she may write to you weekly. At any point, if she changes her mind, she leaves, with the wages she's earned. Those are the terms. Take a day; discuss it with her.”

“Tell me about the farm,” Antonia said, before he could answer. “All of it. The land first, then the money.”

He told her in the careful way of men who work the land, beginning with the soil — fifteen iugera by the fourth milestone, vines on the south slope, olives along the boundary wall, the lower field given to wheat in rotation. Two bad grape harvests, the olive fly in the third, a mule gone lame, the wife's ague and the doctor's bills stacked behind it. He had borrowed against the next two harvests at twelve per cent a quarter, and the compounding had done what droughts could not: turned a competent farmer into a debtor before he noticed the turning.

“Vipsanius didn't lend you money,” Antonia said. “He sold you a foreclosure in instalments. The interest is designed to outrun the harvest; men like him don't want repayment, they want the land. Your farm touches the road, doesn't it? Three hundred paces of road frontage.”

“It does.” He looked surprised. “How—”

“Because that's what it's always about in Rome. Ground. The debt was the method; the road frontage was the object. Someone wants your land for a depot or a tomb garden, and Vipsanius is the instrument.” She made a note. “Hilarus's factor will find out who. Meanwhile, no more signatures. Not for anyone, however kind, however clean the parchment looks. The literate classes rule the world with paper, Avianius; your protection is to make your mark nowhere except before my witnesses.”

“I don't need a day.” He rose, stiffly, and did the last thing she expected: he bowed to her, deeply, the bow of a client to a patron. “I'll take them now, and I'll bring the olive oil myself, and you can dock the jars until I'm square. Domina — they told me you were a whore. In the village, they said—”

“I know what they said. They're partly right, and the part that's right is my business and the state's, not the village's.” She stood. “Phyllis, bring Sabina. And Avianius — when your wife's well enough, bring her to the temple of Isis near the Esquiline gate. The priestess has medicines for the ague that don't cost a farmer's winter. Say my name.”

Sabina came through the colonnade in her house dress, saw her father, and stopped dead; then she ran, and the dignity she had spent nine days building dissolved in the garden, and she was a girl in her father's arms again, and both of them wept without shame, and Phyllis turned away to the roses and even Festus, passing with a new hinge for the postern, developed a sudden interest in the sky.

When they could speak, the father told her the terms, and the offer from the soft-handed man, and the choice that was hers.

He told it the way farmers tell things, in the order of the work rather than the order of importance, and Antonia, who had taken a seat a little apart where she could hear without intruding, made herself attend to every word. The soft-handed man had come to the village in the autumn, with two litters and a clerk, and rented the best room at the inn for three days. He had spoken to no one about his business, but the clerk had walked the fields and the debt-rolls, and within a week the landlord's agents knew exactly which families held their land on the sharpest terms. Avianius's own debt, a modest thing contracted for his wife's fever and a dead ox, had been bought from the local lender at full value, which no one ever did, and then the visits had begun: polite, unhurried, always ending with the name of the house in Rome where the daughter could earn her family's freedom in a single season.

“He knew the children's names,” Avianius said, marvelling still. “All four. He knew the boy's lame foot and the ague's pattern and what the rent came to at the next kalends. A Roman gentleman, with rings, and he knew our kalends. That's when I understood it wasn't chance. Somebody makes a living knowing villages like ours, domina, the way a wolf knows the folds.”

“Somebody does,” Antonia said. “It's called a subscription agent. The gentleman doesn't buy debts; he buys maps of desperation. Tell me the rest.”

The rest was the part the old man could hardly speak: the second visit, when the politeness thinned and the clerk had explained, with the door shut, what became of debt-bond girls sold to the public houses at the port, the places without contracts or lamps or witnesses; and then, by contrast, the warmth of the offer from the Carinae, where a girl could learn music and letters and keep her name, and where the debt, by the terms of the very tablet the soft-handed man had himself produced, would be worked off in four years with a signed release. The trap was beautiful, Antonia thought, in the cold way she admired well-made instruments: the lupanar threat was real enough to terrify, the Carinae offer generous enough to feel like salvation, and neither the father nor the girl was ever meant to notice that the same hand held both doors.

“What decided you?” she asked.

“The wife. My girl's mother.” He looked at Sabina, and his face worked. “She said any house that lets a woman keep her name and learn her letters is a house built by a woman who was once inside the same wall. She said it twice. She's never been to Rome in her life, domina, and she read your whole house from one clause in a contract.”

“Your wife is wiser than half the senate.” Antonia rose. “Stay the night; the kitchen will feed you and there's a clean room by the stable. In the morning we'll draw the copy of the contract you should have been given in the first place — witnessed, sealed, one for your village and one for the temple. And Avianius: when you go home, tell every family on the sharp terms what the soft-handed man's clerk is doing with their names. Wolves hunt best in the dark folds. Let there be lamps in yours.”

Sabina wiped her cheeks with the heel of her hand, the old gesture, and thought about it with the new slowness the house had taught her.

“If I come home,” she said, “the debt comes back the moment someone changes his mind, and I'm sold again by winter, somewhere worse. If I stay, I learn to read and reckon and walk, and I earn wages, and the farm is safe and Mother gets a doctor.” She looked at Antonia. “Is it true? I could leave later? Truly?”

“Truly. Ask any of the women. Ask Chloe, who came here as a debt-bond and now keeps the house's accounts. The contract's witnessed by the priestess of Isis, if that makes it safer in your eyes.”

“Then I'm staying.” The girl's chin lifted. “Not because I don't love you, Father. Because I'll be more use to all of us as a woman with a trade than as a girl with a price. And when I come home, I'll come home on my own feet, with money in my belt, and nobody will ever carry me out of our yard again.”

Avianius looked at his daughter the way a man looks at a field he has planted and found, in his absence, grown tall. “Your mother said you'd be the strong one. She said it when you were five and you fought the goat for the turnips.”

“The goat started it.”

They laughed, shakily, and he finished his bread and wrapped the rest carefully for his wife, and Festus walked him to the postern with a jar of good wine and a packet of the priestess's herbs that Phyllis had produced from somewhere, and the farmer went back down the Carinae through the morning crowds a free man with an unbroken daughter, which was more than Rome gave most men in his position.

When he had gone, Antonia found Sabina alone at the impluvium, feeding the fish with concentrated ferocity.

“Thank you,” the girl said, without looking round. “For the terms. For not buying me like he did.”

“I did buy you. I'm simply trying to be a better owner than the alternative, until you can own yourself. Never mistake kindness for absence of profit, Sabina; the women who do are the ones who get sold twice.”

“I won't.” A pause. “Domina — the men who came to my father. They'll come to the other women's families, won't they? To Tyche's, to the musicians'.”

“Yes. It's how Perperna works. He doesn't storm the wall. He pays the people inside to open the gate.”

“Then I'll learn faster,” Sabina said, and dropped the last of the fish food with a splash. “So I can be a wall too.”

Gaius Aelius had been planning the expedition for a week, in the meticulous fashion of boys with too many tutors.

His father's household believed him to be at the grammar school beside the Forum of Augustus, where he was meant to spend the afternoon on Greek composition. Instead, having persuaded the school's elderly doorkeeper with a silver coin and a story of a family errand, he slipped out with his writing tablets under his arm and walked into the Subura, the great crowded quarter his father's litters always hurried through with the curtains closed.

It was magnificent and terrifying. The street was barely wide enough for two laden mules, and it held, in the space of a hundred paces, a fishmonger shouting his wares, a barber shaving a man in the open air, a money-lender's stall, a shrine to the Lares with a smoky fire, a seller of hot sausages, and a crowd that moved like water around every obstacle. Gaius had read descriptions of the Subura in poets; none of the poets had mentioned the smell, which was rich and total and alive, or the noise, which had a rhythm, like an enormous argument set to music.

He had been there perhaps an hour, and was beginning to feel rather pleased with his own daring, when the argument beside the wine shop turned into a knife.

It was nothing dramatic by Subura standards — two carters over a loading space, a drawn blade, a shove — but Gaius, who had never seen a blade drawn except at the soldiers' drills, froze in the press, and the crowd flowed around him and pushed him off balance, and his tablets fell under the feet of a mule, and when he scrambled after them a hand closed on his arm and lifted him clear of the hooves as easily as he himself lifted the cat.

“Careful, little master. Mules don't know you're nobility; they'll step on anyone.”

It was the big man from the races — the one with the scarred eyebrow who had saved the child at the barrier. Gaius recognized him at once, and the big man recognized him, and the scarred eyebrow rose.

“You're the senator's boy. The one who likes horses better than gladiators. What in the world are you doing in the Subura without a keeper?”

“Learning the city,” Gaius said, with as much dignity as could be managed while being held at arm's length like a puppy.

Festus set him down and retrieved the tablets, wiping the mule marks off with his sleeve. “Learning's good. Dying of it's bad. Come on; I'm buying you something hot, and then I'm taking you somewhere your father's men can find you, because if anything happens to you in my street, my mistress will have my hide, and she keeps her promises.”

They sat on stools outside a sausage shop, and Gaius ate a hot sausage in a roll, which was the most delicious thing he had ever tasted and quite unlike anything served on the Esquiline, and Festus ate two, and answered the boy's questions with the unhurried patience of a man who had discovered that the children of the powerful were easier to talk to than the powerful.

“Were you really a gladiator?”

“Retiarius. Net and trident. Twelve years, forty-one fights, twenty-nine wins, the rest draws or men who let me live because I let them first.” He flexed the scarred arm. “The scar over the eye was a secutor who got past the net. I killed him in the rematch. He taught me more than any schoolmaster.”

Gaius's eyes were enormous. “Did you like it? The fighting?”

“No. I liked being good at something. There's a difference, and most people never learn it. I was born a slave in a school; I didn't choose the sand. But I chose to be the best man on it, because the best man eats and sleeps and sometimes gets given his wooden foil, and the worst man gets carried out through the gate of death. Your father's world has softer floors, little master, but it runs on the same rule.”

“Does my father know your mistress?”

The scarred face considered him. “Yes. And don't ask me what they are to each other, because I don't know, and she doesn't tell me, and that's the mark of a real question — the one even the doorman can't answer.” Festus stood, coins on the counter. “One thing I can show you, since you're learning the city.”

He walked the boy two streets over, to where a narrow lane gave a view of the Carinae and the gilded door, and pointed, very casually, at a man loitering by a fountain with a scroll he was not reading.

“See him? Third time this week. He watches our door, writes down who goes in. He works for the man who killed our clerk. Now watch what he does.”

The man at the fountain looked up, saw Festus looking at him, and with admirable composure turned and walked away.

“He knows you saw him,” Gaius said.

“Of course he does. That's the point. Now he'll tell his master the doorman's alert, and his master will change the plan, and the new plan is a thing we can prepare for. The best guards don't fight, boy. They make the other man waste his nights.” Festus clapped him gently on the shoulder. “Home with you. I'll send a runner to your father's door so you're not murdered for truancy as well. And Gaius — the sausage stays between us.”

Before the sausage, Festus had walked him through the market of the Macellum, and the boy from the Esquiline had seen his city as if for the first time. They watched the grain dole queue at the public granary, patient women and idle men with their tokens, and Festus explained the annona the way a soldier explains supply: not as the poets did, but as logistics. The city ate forty thousand modii of grain a day. Stop the ships from Ostia for a week, and the streets became a war. Emperors were loved or hated on the price of bread, and senators made fortunes from the difference between what arrived and what was written.

“Those contracts my father's auditing,” Gaius said slowly. “If the tallies are false, someone's stealing the grain itself.”

“Someone's selling it twice. My mistress says the theft isn't in the warehouses, it's in the counting houses.” Festus bought him a cup of watered wine from a stall. “You're quick. Most boys your age want to know about the fighting. You want to know about the grain.”

“Uncle Decimus says grain is dull.”

“Your uncle's a charming man with a talent for being wrong.” The big ex-gladiator said it without heat, as he might have observed the weather, and Gaius filed it beside everything else he was beginning to notice that adults did not say outright.

They passed a public fountain where two women were shouting at each other over a broken jug, a workshop full of noisy fullers stamping cloth in urine troughs, and a street shrine where a priest was selling little clay figures to sailors' wives; and at every step Gaius felt the vast, humming, unwritten Rome beneath the Rome of scrolls and tutors, and understood, with the sudden clarity of the young, that his education had been teaching him a map of a country he had never visited.

“Why does your mistress do it?” he asked. “The house. My father would say she could live respectably on her money.”

Festus laughed, a low rumble. “Respectable is what rich men call women who don't threaten them. She does it because a woman with money and no man has to be useful to the powerful or be devoured by them, and she'd rather be the one holding the door. Also — and this matters — the girls. Half of them would be dead in a ditch without the house. Rome's full of houses that take from women. Hers gives. That's a rarer thing than it should be.”

“The sausage stays between us,” Gaius agreed solemnly, and went up the Esquiline with his mule-marked tablets and his head full of nets and tridents and the peculiar discovery that the most honest man he had met all week carried a knife for a living.

Tyche's report came after dark, brought to the tablinum with the market baskets, and it was short and precise: the polite man had asked, twice, about her mistress's appointments outside the house, and specifically whether she ever visited the temples near the Esquiline gate.

“He knows about the Isis meeting,” Chloe said.

“Of course he does; he knows everything except what we'll do about it.” Antonia turned the silver dove brooch over in her fingers. “Send word to the priestess: change nothing. The meeting happens, in the precinct, at the appointed hour. But tell Festus I want every man he trusts inside that temple by noon, dressed as pilgrims, and Hilarus's litter at the side entrance, and a second litter — mine, with the curtains closed — leaving the front door at the same hour and heading for the Aventine. Let Perperna watch the wrong door. He's spent money on eyes; give them something expensive to look at.”

“And the senator?”

“Messengers to the senator too. Tell him only: the goddess expects him, and the goddess has arranged her own protection.” Antonia set the brooch down. “If Perperna wants a war in a temple, he'll discover that Isis has more worshippers in Rome than he has clerks.”

The decoy went out at the third hour the next day, and it was a small masterpiece.

Antonia's own litter, the one half the Carinae knew by its blue curtains and matched bearers, left the gilded door with the curtains closed and an armed footman at each corner, turned uphill toward the Aventine, and was at once picked up by the loiterer at the fountain, who passed the signal to a boy, who ran. Inside the litter, wrapped in Antonia's own mantle and veil, sat the cook's wife, a woman of similar build and excellent nerve, who spent a comfortable afternoon at the temple of Diana, ate the offerings, and came home with a blessing and three sesterces in gratuities.

Meanwhile Antonia, in a plain borrowed mantle with Chloe at her side, went out through the new carpenter Vitalis's workshop, across a neighbouring courtyard whose tenant Hilarus owned, and through that house's postern into a grocer's cart, which carried them under a load of cabbages to within two streets of the Esquiline gate. Festus met them there in pilgrim grey.

“Followed?” he asked.

“Not unless cabbages have learned to walk. Our man at the fountain watched the blue litter all the way up the Aventine.”

“Good. The precinct's quiet. Four of ours inside, two at the gate, the priestess warned.” He offered his arm to Chloe with old-fashioned gravity. “Pilgrims, ladies. Remember to look devout.”

“I've looked devout for worse men than an Egyptian goddess,” Chloe said, and they went up the temple steps together, unhurried, anonymous, three more worshippers among the stream, and the great city, for once, did not know where Antonia Cassia was.

On the Esquiline, Marcus received the same day's news in pieces, as fathers do.

First Stichus, with the careful neutrality of a good slave, reported that the young master had been absent from the afternoon's Greek lesson and had been seen, by a household runner, eating sausage in the Subura in the company of “a very large scarred man known at the Carinae house.” Marcus had dismissed the man and sat with this for a while, torn between the senator's instinct to forbid and the officer's recognition that a fourteen-year-old who could arrange his own truancy was a fourteen-year-old with initiative.

He sent for Gaius after dinner, and the boy came with his chin up and his tablets behind his back, ready for the storm.

“The school wrote to me,” Marcus said. It was not true, but he had been a lawyer once, and lawyers are allowed their tools. “Where were you?”

Gaius weighed it, and chose the truth, the way Marcus had taught him to by rewarding it more often than he punished its absence. “In the Subura. With Festus, from the Carinae house. I wanted to see the city, not read about it. I'm sorry I lied to the school; I'm not sorry I went.”

Marcus looked at his son for a long moment. “What did you learn?”

“That the city eats forty thousand modii of grain a day, and the annona is logistics, and that men steal it twice, in the warehouses and on the tablets. That the best guard doesn't fight; he makes the other man waste his nights. And that a retiarius killed the man who scarred him, in a rematch.” Gaius straightened. “And that the women in the Carinae house would mostly be dead in ditches without it. Father — are you going to prosecute the mistress? The boys at school say she's a wicked woman.”

“The boys at school are repeating their mothers, who are repeating their husbands, who have never read a contract.” Marcus leaned forward. “Listen to me, because I'm going to say this once, and you're to remember it when you're old enough to be a magistrate. The law and the good are not the same thing. The law is a machine built by the powerful to make their comfort look like virtue. A woman may be technically what the law calls disreputable and be, in fact, a person of honour; a man may be technically blameless and be, in fact, a thief. Judge by what people do, Gaius. Not by what they're called. As for the mistress — I'm not prosecuting her. I'm working with her, on the audit, and tomorrow I meet her under the protection of a goddess, which is more protection than most witnesses get.”

Gaius considered this. “Is she the ghost, Father? The one Mother mentioned.”

Marcus did not ask how he knew; children in great houses knew everything, it was the only advantage they had. “She is a person I failed a long time ago. I'm trying not to fail her now. Go to bed. And Gaius — next time you visit the Subura, take Stichus. A boy with a nomenclator is less likely to end in a barrel, and I'd rather explain truancy to your mother than ransom.”

When the boy had gone, Marcus sat late over the audit tablets and the diary entry from Praeneste, and at the household's last watch he wrote out, in his own hand, the questions he intended to ask beneath the eyes of Isis: about the seal, about the subscriptions, about the grain that grew on the river. And at the bottom, in smaller letters, he wrote one question he did not intend to speak aloud: what it would cost him, in the end, to be worthy of the trust he was asking.

He set down the stylus and opened the private drawer where he kept the things that belonged to no ledger, and took out the chipped wooden disc he had kept since boyhood, the token of a kitchen friendship in a house by the sea. Beside it lay the three letters returned to him unopened, their seals still intact, and the last letter, the one he had never sent, that Barto had pressed into his hand in Tibur with the news of the extra payment made “for the training.” He had read that final letter so often that the creases were beginning to part. Thirty years of a man's life, reduced to four small objects in a drawer; and the woman they concerned now kept a gilded door and a strong room full of evidence that could unmake an imperial secretary.

He thought about what a consul's life was supposed to contain: the lictors, the fasces, the curule chair, the carefully counted honours; and he thought about what it actually contained when the counting was honest: a returned slave girl's silence, a dead clerk's tablets, and the question, written small at the bottom of a private list, of whether he was good for anything at all when no one was watching.

The audit was no longer merely an audit. He had understood that for some time. It was a test, administered by no censor and recorded in no register, of what the Aelian name was worth now that it had been left, at last, with no convenient door to slip through.

He blew out the lamp, and the Esquiline house settled into its Roman sleep, and across the roofs the gilded door stood closed and barred and watched, and the watchers were themselves watched, and the night was full of eyes.
