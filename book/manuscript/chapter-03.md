# CHAPTER THREE — The Price of Paper

The offices of the urban aediles stood at the corner of the Roman Forum, in a building whose stucco was always slightly stained by the smoke rising from the cookshops of the Argiletum. Antonia went there at the second hour, dressed not in the blue stola of last night's hostess but in the plain dark gown of a woman with ordinary business, her hair covered, her jewellery confined to a single gold ring. In Rome, a person who wished to hear the truth dressed below her station; the truth, like a cat, approached the quietest feet.

The scriba Palumbus kept the licensing rolls in a chamber full of shelves and pigeonholes, where the air tasted of old wax and lamp oil and the particular dry dust of documents that have outlived everyone who wrote them. He was a small man with a careful manner and ink under his fingernails, and he rose when she came in with the nervous speed of a functionary who dealt too often with people richer than himself.

“Domina Antonia. This is an honour. We don't often see you in person — your steward usually—”

“My steward is an ex-gladiator, and gentlemen in offices become defensive with ex-gladiators. I find I get further with a smile and a ring.” She sat, uninvited, on the stool across from his table, and placed a small purse on the wood between them. “The annual enrollment of my household's women. It's three months early, I know, but I want every name in order and witnessed. The licenses for music and entertainment likewise. Fresh copies, sealed, today if it can be managed.”

Palumbus did not touch the purse. That in itself told her a great deal. In eleven years, no clerk had ever failed to touch the purse.

“Well,” he said, and wiped his palms on his tunic. “There may be a difficulty. The aedile Macer has ordered a general review of houses of entertainment in three districts. Ours not to reason why, domina. A backlog. New enrollments are being held until the review concludes.”

“A review. On what grounds?”

“Public order. Morals. The usual language.” He looked miserable. “And there's a complaint. Lodged by the lender Vipsanius. He says a debt-bond was transferred to your house under — these are his words, not mine — under improper pressure, before the terms were fulfilled. A girl. Aviania, Sabina, by name. He wants her surrendered to the magistrate's keeping until the hearing. There's a gentleman of standing behind the complaint, though his name doesn't appear. These things are done quietly.”

“Of course they are.” Antonia kept her face pleasant. “And the gentleman of standing? You may tell me. I have never yet repeated a name given in this room, which is why you're all so comfortable giving them to me.”

Palumbus swallowed. He glanced at the door, leaned across the table, and breathed a name so quietly it might have been a sigh.

“Perperna.”

She had expected it. Expectation did not make the name taste any better. She sat with it for a moment, turning it like a coin, while Palumbus watched her with the anxious respect of a man watching a bomb decide whether to explode.

“Tell the aedile Macer,” she said at last, “that my licenses are in perfect order, that every woman in my house is enrolled by her own hand before witnesses, and that the debt-paper for Sabina Aviania bears Vipsanius's own seal, which is difficult work to call improper. Tell him also that I shall be making a donation to the festival fund this month — a generous one, given in the aedile's name. Aediles who give good festivals become praetors. He understands arithmetic; so do I.”

Palumbus permitted himself a thin smile. “And the girl?”

“The girl remains where she is, in lawful employment under a sealed contract, until a magistrate orders otherwise in open court. No one takes anyone from my house through a back corridor. That is the one rule I have never broken and never will.” She rose. “The purse is for your trouble. If the review concludes, as I'm sure it will, with every license confirmed, there'll be a second purse on the day. Good morning, Palumbus.”

The way home led through the Argiletum, where the booksellers' stalls gave way to tenement blocks so tall they seemed to lean together overhead, and here the morning's business found her in a different shape. A crowd had gathered outside a timber insula, the kind of crowd Rome produced every day and ignored every day: a creditor's men carrying furniture into the street while a woman sat on a mattress nursing a baby and wept without noise, the way the very poor weep, having learned that noise only draws more creditors. The husband stood against the wall with his wrists bound, not cruelly, merely formally, a man being sold into his own debt.

Antonia stopped. Festus, who had followed at a distance through the crowd, materialised at her shoulder.

“Trouble, domina?”

“Not yet.” She watched the creditor's agent checking items off a list, and she read the scene as she read everything: the man's calloused hands were a carpenter's, the tools in the street were good ones and well cared for, the children were clean. This was not fecklessness. This was the ordinary Roman arithmetic of a sick wife and a winter without work.

She crossed to the agent, a thin man in a stained tunic, and asked the debt.

“Fourteen hundred, domina, principal and costs. He's nexum-bound; the law's the law.”

“The law is an ass, but asses carry money.” She looked at the carpenter. “What's your name?”

“Vitalis, domina.” The man's voice was hoarse. “I'm a builder. Scaffolding, joinery, repairs. I'll work it off, by the gods I will, only don't put my name on the block — a man sold once is never hired again.”

She considered him. Her own house kept a carpenter on contract, an arrangement she had disliked for years; a man in the household, bound by gratitude rather than wages, was worth three contractors. And a man who had watched the street eat his family would have very particular opinions about who was allowed through a gilded door.

“Festus, pay him. Fourteen hundred, and take the debt paper, not a receipt. The tools go back upstairs, the cords come off, and the family stays.” To the agent: “Your fee is in the sum; don't imagine it isn't. To Vitalis: you and yours move to my house in the Carinae this afternoon. There's a porter's lodge going empty and a roof that leaks. You work off the paper at fair wages, and when it's cleared you stay on salary, if you're any good. Are you any good?”

The man stared at her. The baby had stopped crying. “Domina, I built the altar screen at the temple of Concord. I'm the best man you'll get for fourteen hundred.”

“Then we're both fortunate.” She turned away. “Festus, see to it. And Vitalis — in my house, the first thing you learn is that I keep every promise and forget every debt. The paper is to protect you from the next creditor, not to own you. Remember that, and remember who taught Rome how little its laws protect the honest.”

She walked on, leaving the crowd to its murmuring, and did not look back. Looking back cost time, and Antonia had long ago discovered that kindness, done quickly and without ceremony, was indistinguishable from business, and far less dangerous to the giver.

Outside, the Forum roared in full morning voice. Bankers' boys ran with sealed bags, lawyers argued on the tribunals, a soothsayer screamed about omens to a crowd of idlers, and beneath the rostra the auctioneer was selling the contents of an insolvent shipping merchant's warehouse to a knot of sharp-eyed men. Antonia crossed it with the unhurried step of a woman who had learned to move through crowds without ever being touched, and turned into the bankers' row between the old shops, where Beryllus the Alexandrian did his business in a marble-floored taberna guarded by two enormous Germans.

---

Beryllus was sixty, brown as a nut, with a shaved head and the eyes of a crocodile. He had begun as a money-changer in the harbour at Alexandria and now moved more money in a morning than the treasuries of client kings. He rose from behind his table and bowed with genuine warmth, because Antonia Cassia was among his most careful clients, and careful clients were the only kind Beryllus loved.

“Best customer. Wine? No, you never take it before noon. Water, then, and figs — the figs are from Syria, you'll be astonished.” He clapped for a slave, gestured her to a chair, and sat again with his steepled fingers. “You've come about the licenses. Word travels faster than horses in this street.”

While the water was brought, Antonia let her eyes run over the taberna the way she read a room at one of her own suppers. Two Germans at the door, armed with clubs rather than swords, which told her the banker feared thieves rather than soldiers; a wall of pigeonholes holding the wax tablets of deposit accounts, each in its named sleeve; a bronze assay balance on a side table, polished bright at the pans from daily handling; and, behind Beryllus himself, an iron-bound chest of the kind whose lock alone cost more than a clerk's yearly wage. Everything in the room said the same sentence: money is a serious animal and will not live where the fencing is weak.

“You've changed your guards,” she said. “The old pair were Gauls.”

“The old pair took a gratuity from a litigant to report who visited me. Litigants are sentimental; they believe knowing a man's banker is knowing the man.” Beryllus shrugged his brown, nut-like shoulders. “Germans don't speak enough Latin to be bribed in it. It's the nearest thing to honesty available at that price. Now.” He folded his hands. “The licenses. Tell me what frightened you, because something did; you don't enroll three months early in a calm season.”

She told him, in the level voice she used for figures, about Palumbus and the untouched purse, and Beryllus listened without interrupting, which was his highest form of attention.

“A clerk who won't take money is either a zealot or a man who's been paid already by the other side,” he said when she finished. “Zealots in the aediles' office average two per generation, and I've known both; they're thin and they smell of leeks. Your Palumbus, by your description, is neither thin nor fragrant. Therefore the second. Someone is funding an interest in the licensing rolls, and an interest in the rolls means an interest in the houses the rolls describe. Yours first, because yours is the best run.” He selected a fig and considered it. “This is why I keep my ledgers in three copies, domina. The first copy is for the tax farmer, the second is for my own hand, and the third is sealed with a banker in Alexandria who has never seen Rome and owes nothing to anyone in it. A woman in your position should do the same. Paper that exists in only one city is a wish, not a defence.”

“I have a temple coffer.”

“Good. Temples are better than bankers for women; no one expects a woman to have a banker, and everyone expects her to be pious. Use both. Piety and arithmetic, the two shields the city can't laugh off.” He bit the fig. “The figs, by the way. Syria. I'm always right.”

“I've come about everything. Start with my position.”

“Your position is excellent, which is precisely why people are studying it.” Beryllus opened a codex of parchment leaves — a new fashion, replacing the old rolls for accounts — and ran a finger down a column. “Deposits across three houses, mine included: sixty-two thousand sesterces in ready money. Loans out at good rates: a hundred and four thousand, secured on property and cargoes, no defaults worth the name. The house itself, freehold, worth perhaps two hundred thousand if sold, though only a fool would sell it. Plus your share in Hilarus's oil ships. You are, in plain terms, what the law calls a wealthy woman, and what the Senate calls an inconvenience.”

“Who's been asking?”

“The aedile's men, routinely. Two tax farmers, routinely. And one man who is not routine.” Beryllus closed the codex. “Sextus Perperna. Through an intermediary, then in person, very charming, very plain. He wanted to know your holdings, your debts, and — he made a point of this — whether any of your loans could be called in at once. I told him my clients' affairs were as sealed as a vestal's vow. I then increased the guards, because a man like Perperna doesn't ask a question unless he means to use the answer.”

Antonia said nothing. Outside, a banker's boy went past at a run, his coin-bag clinking.

“There's more,” said Beryllus, and allowed himself the small frown of a man forced to discuss unpleasantness before lunch. “A Greek came to me last week. A clerk on the supply board, the army contracts — Hector, freedman of some provincial. He was asking the same questions from the other side. Which firms hold the eastern supply subscriptions, where the money flows, who's behind the seals. I gave him tea and nothing else; he seemed frightened, and frightened men make bankers nervous. Then yesterday he didn't come as promised. I sent a boy round; his lodging's empty, rent unpaid, landlord complaining.” Beryllus looked at her directly. “A frightened clerk with questions, gone without his deposit. In Rome, that story has only two endings, and neither is good. If he's one of yours—”

“He's my friend's brother. Chloe's.” Antonia's voice was steady, but her mind was moving like a shuffled deck, cards falling into order: the contracts, Perperna's questions, the missing clerk, the complaint about Sabina, the review of the licenses. Separate stones, and beneath them all the same dark water. “Beryllus, if a man wanted to ruin this house, how would he begin?”

“With paper.” The answer came without hesitation. “Not fire, not knives — those things alarm the city and bring the vigiles. Paper. A challenge to your licenses. A charge of lenocinium, of profiting from vice without proper enrollment. A debt proved in a friendly court. A witness who remembers, suddenly, that she was coerced. Rome destroys people with documents, my dear, because documents don't weep and can't be cross-examined about their childhoods.” He pushed the figs toward her. “Buy witnesses. Seal everything twice. And if there's a document in this city that could hurt you, find it before Perperna does.”

Beryllus rose and walked to a shelf of tablets himself, an honour he accorded almost no one, and came back with a land register.

“Since you're asking about Perperna, look at this. I keep a private tally of his purchases, because a man's money tells the truth even when his mouth doesn't.” He opened it. “Seven houses in four years. Every one bought at a forced sale, every one sold by the treasury after a prosecution. A senator's villa at Praeneste. Two tenements in the Subura. A shipping merchant's warehouse — the one being auctioned under the rostra today, in fact. The man feeds on ruin. He doesn't merely prosecute, Antonia. He buys the corpses.”

“Then he'll need a steady supply of corpses.”

“Exactly. And here's what keeps me awake.” Beryllus tapped the register. “His prosecutions have grown bolder. Small debtors first, then merchants, then a governor. A man with that appetite must keep climbing, because the only thing more dangerous than succeeding at his trade is stopping; the families remember. He'll need a spectacular victim soon. Someone famous enough to make the city forget his methods. A consul-designate would do very well. Or a woman whose house half the Senate visits every night.”

The implication settled between them like dust.

“Hilarus sent word, too,” Beryllus continued, lowering his voice though the guards stood well out of hearing. “His Spanish oil ships are fine, but two of the grain vessels he's been asked to insure for the eastern schedule came in light — short-weighted at the warehouses, the tallies corrected upward at the counting house. He's putting the figures together. If the army's grain is being measured once in Ostia and written twice in Rome, then every name on those contracts is a rope waiting for its neck.”

“Advice worth double my usual fee.” She stood. “Send me the names of every firm in those eastern contracts, as much as you know. Through Festus, at the postern, at night.”

“At night, at the postern, through an ex-gladiator. My favourite kind of business.” He bowed again. “Antonia. Be careful. I've seen what Perperna does. He doesn't accuse the strong. He makes them weak first, and accuses them afterward.”

Marcus reached the villa at Tibur at midday, riding a sure-footed mule up the via Tiburtina with Stichus behind him and a single groom, through the olive groves and the grey stone walls where his childhood was buried.

The villa sat on a terrace above the Anio, its old tufa walls mellow in the sun, its cypresses tall as sentinels. His father was seven years dead, his mother three, and the place was kept now by a bailiff and a skeleton staff, a museum of the Aelii that the family visited twice a year and otherwise forgot. The bailiff Barto came out into the courtyard as they dismounted, an old man now, stooped and half-blind, squinting against the light; then recognition moved across his face like sunrise.

“Little Marcus. Begging your pardon — Senator. Master Marcus.” His voice cracked. “I'd know that walk in the dark. Your grandfather walked the same way, as if the stones owed him rent.”

“Barto. You're well?”

“Well enough for a man whose teeth have mostly deserted him. Come in, come in. There's wine, and the olives are good this year, though I say so who pruned them.”

Marcus let the old man fuss over him through the courtyard and into the shade of the colonnade, accepted wine and bread, asked the careful questions about the harvest, the slaves, the roof, that a good landlord asks. Then he sent Stichus and the groom to eat in the kitchen, and sat alone with Barto on the stone bench beside the fishpond, and said the name he had ridden twenty miles to say.

“Anthe. The house-born girl. My mother's sewing woman's daughter.”

Barto's hands went still around his cup. He was very old, and old men have their own relationship with time; the name seemed to reach him from a great distance, but it reached him clearly.

“I wondered if you'd come. Fifteen years, and I wondered. Your father made us swear on the household gods never to write of it. Gods and I have an arrangement now, at my age. I'll tell you.” Barto drank, set the cup down, and looked at the water. “She was a sweet child. Quick. You taught her to read in the kitchen, you and her against the world, and the whole household knew and said nothing because she was sweet and you were lonely, the way boys in great houses are lonely. Then you went off to Germany. And she was carrying your child.”

The fishpond made its small, constant sound. Marcus said nothing. There was nothing to say. He had known, in some deep room of himself, since the ledger entry last night; the old man's words merely opened the door and showed him the furniture.

“Your mother found out at the wash tubs. There was a scene. I've never heard a voice like hers, before or since — cold as a well. Your father wouldn't see the girl. Dromo the steward did the work. Sold her to Dinis, the dealer from the Subura, nine hundred and forty sesterces, money down, no questions. I remember the price because Dromo laughed about it; said he'd got a bargain on a girl with letters.”

“The child.”

“Born in Rome, in the dealer's house, so the story came back. A girl. Small, and early, and dead before she drew breath, or so they said. The mother nearly went with her. Fever. A midwife from the temple of Juno came, and she lived, and after that—” Barto spread his hands. “After that she vanished into the city. A girl like that, with no man and no name. Rome eats girls like figs.”

“Letters. Did letters come for her? From me.”

The old man's face worked. “They did. Three, I think, and money with them, for her manumission. Dromo brought them to your mother. She had them sent back unopened. All of it. ‘Let him forget her,' she said. ‘It is better for everyone concerned.' I heard her say it, standing in this very colonnade. Better for everyone. That's what the powerful call it, when someone else pays.”

Marcus stared across the valley, where the olive trees moved in the wind like a grey-green sea. He was a soldier; he had seen men die in the snow beyond the Rhine; he had kept his face through councils and courts and the death of his own father. He kept it now. Only his hands, clasped between his knees, were white.

“Did she know? That I wrote? That I sent money?”

“How could she? The letters went back to Germany unopened. You'd have been told she refused them. She'd have been told nothing at all, or told that you'd forgotten her. That's the genius of a thing like this, master. No one has to lie very much. You just make sure the two people never stand in the same room.”

Marcus laughed then, a short, terrible sound. “And last night,” he said, “we stood in the same room.”

Barto's milky eyes sharpened. “She's alive, then.”

“She owns a house in the Carinae. The most famous supper-house in Rome. She has money, and clients among the consulars, and she looked at me as though I were a stain on her floor.” He paused. “She thinks I knew. Or that I consented.”

“Did you?”

The question, put without flinching by a half-blind old slave on a stone bench, was the hardest thing anyone had asked him in his life. Marcus considered it honestly, as he considered all questions, and found the answer more shameful than any lie.

“I didn't know. But I didn't ask. I let my father tell me a story and I built my whole life on it because the story was convenient. The girl was gone, the honour of the family was saved, and my conscience was handed a clean tablet. I didn't dig because I was afraid of what I'd find. That's not innocence, Barto. That's just cowardice with good manners.”

The old man considered this, and nodded slowly, as a teacher nods when a slow pupil finally reaches the answer. From the pouch at his belt he drew, with some difficulty, a small oilskin packet, and unfolded it. Inside was a letter, yellowed, the wax broken long ago, the handwriting young and rushed and unmistakably Marcus's own.

“One of them. The last. I took it from Dromo's waste basket before he burned the lot. Kept it because it felt wrong to burn a man's words. I've carried it fifteen years, thinking you'd come or I'd die, and one of the two was bound to happen.”

Marcus took the letter. He knew its contents without reading; he had written it in a winter billet on the frontier, by the light of a smoking lamp, pouring into it everything a twenty-three-year-old could not say to anyone else. He had believed, writing it, that she would read it and understand. It had never crossed a threshold except to be refused.

He read it now, on the stone bench, while Barto looked politely at the cypresses. The young man who had written it was almost a stranger: so certain of his own honour, so busy being noble, so utterly unable to imagine the machinery of his own family grinding forward without him. When he finished, the sun had moved and the fishpond was in shadow.

He folded it with the same care he gave all things, and put it inside his tunic, against his chest.

The ride back to Rome took the afternoon, and he made it mostly in silence, Stichus wisely keeping his distance on the narrow road. At the city gates the traffic slowed behind a column of ox-carts bringing stone for the emperor's new buildings, and he sat the mule in the press of animals and men, and thought about the mathematics of guilt.

He had not given the order. He had not known of the sale. He had been four hundred miles away, freezing in a German winter, writing letters that came back unopened. A reasonable man, an advocate, a jury of priests, would all acquit him. And none of it changed the central fact, which was that the whole engine of his life — the family, the name, the slaves, the clean conscience — had acted in his interest, with his happiness as its excuse, and he had spent fifteen years spending the proceeds without asking where they came from. The slave girl's sale had bought him an untroubled marriage, a career without scandal, the luxury of being the honest one. He had not lit the fire. He had lived in the house it warmed.

At the temple of Fortuna on the Quirinal he dismounted, went in, and made an offering — not for success, he did not insult the gods with that, but for clarity. Then he rode down to the Esquiline, and in his own tablinum, while the household prepared the evening meal, he took a fresh sheet of parchment and wrote a single line, and crossed it out, and wrote it again, and crossed that out too. There were no words that could arrive ahead of him through that gilded door and do anything but harm.

He would have to go himself. And this time, he would ask before he assumed, and listen before he spoke, and accept whatever the cost turned out to be. It was, he reflected, the least that nine hundred and forty sesterces had ever bought a man.

“Barto. I'm grateful. There'll be money — a proper sum, enough to keep you in comfort, and your freedom papers if you'll take them, though I know you've refused twice.”

“Refused twice and will refuse a third. I'm too old to be free; freedom's a young man's burden. But I'll take the money, because I'm not too old to be vain, and I want a proper sarcophagus with my name on it. Marcus.” The old voice caught. “Be gentle with her, when you see her. Whatever she's become. She was sold for nine hundred and forty sesterces and buried a baby before she was eighteen. Whatever she's built since, she built it on that.”

In the Carinae, Sabina was learning the alphabet.

Chloe had her in the garden court, where the light was good, with a wax tablet and a stylus and a cup of watered wine as a reward system, and the lesson had progressed from letters to the more difficult art of carrying one's body.

“You walk like a goat,” Chloe said, not unkindly. “Not your fault — you've carried water jars since you were eight, and it builds the wrong muscles. Again. From the fountain. Slow. Head up, shoulders down, and imagine there's a book balanced on your hair. Romans believe a woman who walks steadily thinks steadily, and in Rome belief is nine-tenths of the truth.”

Sabina tried again, fists clenched with concentration, and reached the colonnade without dropping the imaginary book.

“Better. Again.”

They had been at it since noon, between the letters and a lesson in how to address men of different ranks, which Sabina thought was the strangest lesson she had ever received, because every rule was the opposite of the rules her mother had taught her. Don't look down first. Don't fill every silence. Let a man wait; he'll pay more for the answer. Say no with the same face you say yes.

“Why are you teaching me this?” she asked, during the rest. “I'm a debt-bond. In the village, debt-bonds don't learn to walk.”

Chloe bit into an apple. “Because Antonia says so, and Antonia is never wrong about investments, and you are an investment. Because a woman who can read and walk and reckon can't be made helpless again, whatever happens to her papers. And because I was you once, and nobody taught me, and I had to learn it all in places I won't describe while you're eating.”

The tablet lesson followed. Sabina's first R came out backwards, and she laughed — the first laugh Antonia had heard from her, catching it as she crossed the courtyard with the morning's correspondence in her hand.

She stopped, watching. The sound reminded her of something she had no intention of remembering, and she let it pass, and went to her tablinum, and had barely sat down to the accounts when Festus appeared in the doorway, and his face told her everything before he spoke.

“Officer at the front. From the aedile's office, with a sealed tablet and two witnesses. I made him wait in the vestibule. He didn't like it.”

She broke the seal. The wax was official, the language careful, the meaning plain: on the complaint of Vipsanius, supported by a citizen of standing, the magistrate required the person of Sabina Aviania to be surrendered into safe keeping pending a hearing on the lawfulness of her service; any hindrance would be treated as contempt, and the licensing review of the house would proceed on the assumption that the house held free citizens against their will.

She read it twice. Then she laid it flat and weighted its corners, as she weighted all paper that might otherwise blow away.

“Festus. The women's wing is locked from now on, and you keep the keys yourself. No girl leaves it without me. Sabina sleeps where I can hear her. Send a boy to Hilarus at the docks — tell him I need him tonight, after dark, through the postern. And send Chloe to me.”

He went. She looked at the tablet a long moment, and thought of Beryllus in his counting house, and his crocodile smile, and his one cold truth.

_Rome destroys people with documents._

“Paper,” she said aloud, to the empty room and the fish in the impluvium. “He wants a war of paper.” She picked up her stylus. “Paper I understand.”
