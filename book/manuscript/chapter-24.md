# CHAPTER TWENTY-FOUR — The Consul's Choice

Rome took the verdict the way Rome takes everything, through the medium of talk, and for three days the story ran through the forums and the baths and the dinner tables in two versions. The serious version, among the men who knew the prefect's mind, was that the army supply audit had uncovered something large and that the courts had been used to bury it. The popular version, among everyone else, was much better: a beautiful courtesan with a head for ledgers had beaten the most feared informer in the city, and a consul-designate had stood beside her in open court and refused to say he was sorry.

Both versions were true, and both were dangerous, and the men who governed the empire spent the week deciding which truth to act on.

Marcus was summoned to the Palatine on the fourth morning, not to an audience with the emperor, who was at his villa and took no visible part, but to a long room where Publius Attianus waited with two senior councillors and a table of tablets, and where the conversation was conducted entirely in the careful language of men whose words would be written down.

Attianus began with the inquiry. The praetor's record had been received and verified. The finance bureau's offices had been quietly sealed and searched; Zosimus was in custody, protesting, with the seal-maker's forgeries and the disbursement ledgers forming a wall of paper against him. The Ostia granaries were under military audit, and the true extent of the shortfall was being measured for the first time. The emperor, when the case was complete, would decide on prosecutions; but the machinery was broken and the men at the top of it were no longer free.

“That is the good work of the past two months,” Attianus said, “and the state owes the thanks for it, in part, to your audit and to the witnesses your friend's house preserved.” He paused, in the manner of a man approaching the difficult paragraph. “Now we must speak of the cost.”

The two councillors took up the rest, with the practised gentleness that surrounds unpleasant bargains. The consulship was the issue. A consul-designate who had been named, however falsely, in an adultery case; who had publicly associated himself with an infamis woman, however legitimate her evidence; who had been removed from his audit amid fire and murder and informers' pamphlets — such a man could not, in the present mood, take the curule chair without making the office itself a subject of broadsheets and jokes. There were senators, men of the old families, who would not serve under him. There were equestrians who had lost money in Perperna's prosecutions and would not forgive the role of the house. The matter could be handled quietly: a withdrawal for reasons of health, the standard phrase, the lictors never taken, the fasces never carried. His rank retained, his property untouched, his career suspended rather than ended; in a few years, when the story was old, another office could be found.

“In return?” Marcus asked.

“In return, the inquiry proceeds without a political martyr at its centre.” The senior councillor did not flinch. “And you make no further public appearances with the woman. A quiet end to the association. The state cannot have a consul, or even an ex-consul, turning the Carinae into an emblem. The verdict settled the law. The rest is weather, and weather changes.”

Marcus sat with the tablets in front of him and thought, while the three men waited, about all the bargains of this kind he had watched other men accept. The safe phrase. The quiet withdrawal. The small, civilized abandonment dressed up as prudence. He thought of the words he had spoken in the basilica. He thought of a woman in a dark gown who had not wept. He thought of his father's ring on a forger's wax, and of a clerk in the Tiber, and of a stone niche beyond the Esquiline gate.

Then he said, “I'll give up the consulship.”

The room relaxed fractionally.

“But not for health,” he continued, “and not in the form of a bargain. I'll write to the senate that I withdraw because I've concluded I cannot take the chair without the authority the office requires, and because I choose not to pretend otherwise. And I will not end the association, publicly or otherwise, on anyone's instruction. The state doesn't own my friendships, and if the prefect's inquiry is sound, it doesn't need my silence to protect it. What it needs is the truth in its records, which it has.”

The silence that followed was of a different quality.

Attianus regarded him for a long moment, and then, to the visible discomfort of the councillors, the corner of the prefect's mouth moved. “You understand this may end your career.”

“My career began as a service to the state. It's ended, if it's ended, the same way. A man could do worse.” Marcus rose. “One thing more. The inquiry's witnesses. The house and its women, the engraver and his family, the measurer, the banker, the warehouse families. I want their continued protection in writing. The state has used their evidence; I won't have them inherit my enemies after I leave the building.”

“That can be done,” Attianus said. “It will be done. And Marcus — the letter to the senate. Write it the way you said. The honest version. Rome has too few of them; I'd like one in the record.”

He walked home through the Forum on foot, in the winter sun, a man who had just given away the office every man in his family for four centuries had been born to want, and he discovered, stepping around a vegetable stall and nodding to a client who stared, that the weight that left his shoulders was greater than the one that remained.

Attianus sent for him again three days later, not to bargain but to report, and the report was delivered over plain wine in the prefect's office with the guards shut outside.

Zosimus had been taken in the night, at the bureau itself, with the seals still warm on the day's false tallies. The search of his rooms produced the instruments of his trade: the signet impression of the Aelian ring set in its wooden block, the subscription lists of the shell firms in Celer's name, and a strong box of the loan contracts whose twenty-four per cent rates Beryllus had already entered in the basilica. Confronted with the row of his own documents, the imperial secretary had wept, which Marcus said did not surprise him, and offered to remember everything, which Attianus said did not surprise him either.

“He's giving us the clerks,” the prefect said, “and the harbour supervisors, and the two men in the annona office who corrected the tallies after Hector's death. He's not giving us anyone above him, because there is no one above him but your informer, and the informer holds no office, which is why this was always so difficult to reach. The machine can be taken apart. The man who sold the machine has to be reached through the senate, not the bureau.”

“Then reach him through the senate.”

“We intend to. The hearing opens next week. Your praetor's record goes to the fathers with the imperial backing, and the confiscated estates do the rest; nothing persuades a senate like the chance to redistribute an unpopular man's property.” Attianus poured the wine himself, a gesture of unusual informality. “One thing I'll say to you, since you've paid for the right to hear it. This city survives because every few years someone like her keeps better records than the men robbing it, and someone like you refuses to be reasonable about her. I've put both your names in the report to the emperor. Nothing will come of it publicly; it never does. But the right men will know. That's the coin of my trade, and I've paid it in full.”

Marcus drank the plain wine and said, “Then we're even. I gave them a chair; you gave them the truth. Rome will call both of us fools, probably.”

“Rome calls anything it can't price a fool.” The prefect shrugged. “It's a reliable system; it leaves the useful work to us.”

---

Perperna fell the way informers fall, which is to say quickly, completely, and without friends.

The inquiry's sealed record went to the emperor's court, and came back in the form of a senate hearing on the conduct of delators, a hearing at which the praetor's findings, the banker's figures, the arson report, and the names of the murdered clerk and the dead housekeeper were arranged in a single, damning sequence. The men Perperna had ruined over twenty years found, suddenly, that their surviving relatives had voices. The men whose property he had acquired found that property documents could be re-examined. The men who had laughed at his dinners discovered urgent business in the country.

He tried, as Antonia had predicted, to move first. Hilarus's watch at Ostia caught the attempt: a modest cart, a hired ship bound for the East, a chest of the kind that carries portable fortunes, and Sextus Perperna in a travelling cloak that had seen better days, with two servants and none of the old confidence. The harbour guard, paid better than the state paid them, detained him on the prefect's warrant with polite efficiency, and he was brought back to Rome in the same kind of custody he had arranged for so many others, though without the irons, which seemed to him, on the journey, a refinement of cruelty.

At the dock he made one last attempt at the old voice, asking for the names of the men who presumed to delay a Roman citizen, and the harbour officer, a veteran with twenty years of salt in him, had answered, “Not men. Paper. There's a warrant with your name on it, and paper's the one thing in this harbour that doesn't take bribes.” Hilarus, told the story at supper, repeated it at the Carinae for a week.

The aftermath of the audit ran on through the spring, quieter than the courts and more important than any of them. The military auditors finished the Ostia measurements; the granaries' true stocks were established, the supervisors replaced, and the shortfall, recovered where it could be from the confiscated estates, was entered against the army's accounts with the army's own witnesses. New rules came out of the finance bureau: double witnessing for every shipment weight, sealed duplicate tallies held outside the bureau, and a public posting of the monthly grain totals, the last of these an idea that had begun, Attianus's secretary let it be known, with a courtesan's question about why a farmer's dole could be weighed twice. Dorion's tattooed arm, measured and copied into the record, became the standard against which the new tallies were checked, and the measurer himself, freed from his contract by way of thanks, took employment in the Ostia harbour office at a state salary, which he considered a dull outcome much improved by its safety.

The senate hearing itself was a study in the speed with which Rome disassembles a man.

Perperna was allowed to speak, and he took the full measure of the allowance, because speaking was the only power he had left. He defended the delator's trade as the empire's immune system, the office by which treason and corruption were exposed when ordinary men feared to name them; he invoked the ancient statutes, the names of emperors, the gratitude of the treasury; he warned the fathers that to punish an informer was to silence every future warning, and he did it all with the same kindly, reasonable face, the same plain tunic, the same carefully sorrowful voice.

It had worked for twenty years. It worked for no one now, because the room contained too many men who had seen the figures, and because among the spectators sat the relatives of three of his victims, in mourning, visible at every bench.

The turncoats did the rest. Clerks who had drafted his statements testified to their preparation. Agents who had served the summonses admitted to threats. The banker's records showed the acquisition of property at a fraction of value after each prosecution, a rhythm as regular as the Ostia fraud itself. Zosimus, brought from custody, confirmed the route of the new silver and named, in his own defence, every name above his own; and a junior senator rose to read aloud, into the record, the arson report's account of the pitch-soaked rags and the dead housekeeper, which was the moment even Perperna's few remaining supporters stopped meeting his eyes.

The senate's judgement was pronounced in the first week of March. His property was confiscated to the treasury, his contracts and prosecutions were ordered reviewed, and he was interdicted from fire and water within four hundred miles of Rome — the old, terrible formula that meant no community might shelter him, and that his exile had no road back. Zosimus, in custody and now cooperating with the energy of a man who had suddenly remembered his own innocence, confirmed the bureau's part and named the remaining clerks, and the machinery that had eaten its way through the army's grain for years was disassembled, name by name, in the careful, vengeful way Rome cleans an office.

The restitution took longer than the condemnation, as restitution always does. A special board sat through the spring reopening Perperna's old prosecutions, and one by one the families he had ruined came forward with their copies of the documents he had assumed would burn with their owners. Some were restored to property that had changed hands three times; some received only a formal note that the original judgement had been unjust, the paper worth more than it seemed to men whose children had inherited infamia. The board's clerks noticed that an unusual number of these documents were clean, witnessed copies in a fine Greek hand, and asked no questions; the Carinae house's scriptorium did not advertise, and its clients did not explain.

One case Antonia followed personally, because it taught her what the restitution actually cost. A provincial knight named Octavius had been condemned three years earlier on a delation for maiestas, the broad, elastic charge of treason-as-attitude; his estate had gone to the treasury at a fraction of value, his widow had been reduced to a single room in a relation's house, and his son, barred from office by the infamia, had been working as a clerk in a shipping office. The evidence against Octavius had been a single letter, read aloud in court and then misquoted in the record, and two witnesses whose names appeared on the payrolls of three other Perperna prosecutions.

The widow arrived at the gilded door by the side entrance, in the hour Antonia kept for such callers, with a bundle of her husband's papers held together with kitchen string and the expression of a woman who had been refused at eleven doors and expected refusal at the twelfth.

Sabina took the bundle first, because the scrivener's eye was the fastest in the house, and within an hour she had found the thread: the original letter, kept by the widow against every advice, did not say what the court record claimed it said, and the two witnesses' payroll entries were in the same Beryllus-copied bank statements already before the board. Antonia had the whole comparison written into a clean memorial that evening, witnessed by Chloe and sealed at the temple, and sent it to the board with no covering note from herself, because a courtesan's name on the cover could poison a cause that the documents could carry alone.

The board reversed the condemnation within the month. The son's infamia was lifted, the estate's remains were traced through their three sales and restored where they could be, and the widow came to the door again, this time by the front, with a small bronze lamp for the household shrine and nothing to say that her face did not say for her. Antonia accepted the lamp and refused the rest — there was no rest offered, only gratitude, and gratitude she took in the Roman way, as a debt now marked paid — and afterward said to Chloe, in the tablinum, “That's the work the court scenes leave out. The court clears a name in an afternoon; the name has to be learned again by a family over ten years. We can't give them the ten years. We can give them the paper that makes the years possible.”

“One house at a time,” Chloe said.

“One house at a time. It's how Rome itself got built, according to the poets, and the poets are right about exactly one thing: no one ever did it all at once.”

The scriptorium that did the work occupied what had been the old music storeroom off the women's court, and it ran to a discipline Antonia had borrowed from Beryllus and sharpened for her own people: every memorial in three copies, one for the client, one for the temple coffer, one sealed against the future in the vault; every statement witnessed by two hands and dated against the festival calendar; every name cross-entered in the roll she kept of the delator's victims, so that a widow arriving with kitchen string and a dead husband's letters could be told, within the hour, which other widows held the pieces of the same false prosecution.

Sabina came up from the Forum three afternoons a week to run it, and the women of the house learned the copying in rotation, because Antonia held that a hand trained to form a clear testamentary hand could never again be told its work was merely decorative. “The pamphlet shops call this place a house of pleasure,” she told the newest copyist, a girl who had arrived in the spring with no letters at all. “They're half right. My own view is that there is no pleasure in Rome more durable than making the city's paper tell the truth about somebody it wanted to forget. Copy the next line slowly. A witness may flinch; a clean copy cannot.”

Antonia followed the board's lists the way other women followed the races, marking each restored name in a private roll she kept beside the strong room's key. “Why keep count?” Chloe asked her, near the end. “They're strangers. Most of them.”

“Because he counted them as income. Somebody should count them as justice, and the ledgers ought to balance in the end.” She closed the roll. “Twenty-three houses ruined that we can prove, eleven men dead or exiled, four widows restored this month alone. I want the number in writing where he can't reach it. That's how you kill a man like Perperna properly. Not with a knife. With an audit.”

Antonia was not present for the judgement, by her own choice. She spent the day at the Carinae, going over the quarterly accounts with Chloe, and when Festus came with the word, she set down the stylus and was quiet a while.

“Did he say anything?”

“At the end? He said the city would regret losing its best watchdog. He said it twice.” Festus's scarred face was impassive. “Then the lictors took him out the side door. Nobody watched the street. That's the part that'll kill him, if anything does; nobody watched.”

“Rome loves a winner and forgets a loser, and the difference is one good scandal.” She picked up the stylus again. “I'm not going to enjoy it, Festus. I'd have preferred a man who could be ashamed. But I'll take the city being safer for the women behind me, and I won't pretend the justice is less real because it feels ordinary. Send word to the temple coffer; the originals can come home now. And tell the kitchen a proper supper tonight. Not a celebration. A completion.”

That night, alone in her strong room, she took out the wooden disc, chipped and brown, that she had kept for thirty years, and held it, and thought of a boy's letter returning unopened and a girl sold for 940 sesterces and a door that had been gilded twice. Then she put it back in its compartment, closed the vault, and turned the key, because the keeping of the past, she had learned, was for the strong room, while the living was for the house above it.

Perperna left Rome on a grey morning with no escort and no spectators, which wounded him more than the judgement. The carter who took him as far as the coast reported, later, that the exiled man had talked for two days about the ungrateful city and the quality of the men who replaced him, and had only once fallen silent, at the crest of the hill where the road gives the last view of the rooftops. He settled, in the end, in a provincial town in the East, among strangers who did not know his name, which was the cruellest sentence the empire could have devised, though none of the judges had needed to write it. He lived some years, gave bad advice to lesser men, and died in his bed; Rome did not record the date, and the Carinae house did not mark it, because Antonia had decided that the best revenge available to a woman of her profession was to outlive a man so completely that he became a chore to remember.

Other debts were paid in the same weeks, with less noise. Hilarus's contacts in Sardinia secured the transfer of Tyche's brother from the mines to a road gang and then, through the proper bribes and the proper papers, his manumission; he arrived at the Carinae thin and blinking in the spring light, and Tyche, who had sung through her own ordeal without breaking, broke completely at the sight of him and was found an hour later in the garden, teaching him the names of the herbs. Sabina's debt contract was cancelled outright, the four thousand sesterces paid from the house's own funds, and Antonia set her up as a scrivener of legal documents with a small shop near the Forum, where she proceeded to make a ferocious living correcting the work of advocates twice her age, and where young men with hopeless cases soon learned to seek the girl who knew the summons law by heart. Lalla won her own hearing with the letters-first method, and came to the house afterward with a jar of preserves and an application to be enrolled as a house musician, which Antonia accepted with the gravity due to a fellow veteran.

He told the family that evening, in the tablinum, with the letter to the senate lying sealed on the table between them.

Gaius read it twice, in the careful way he now read everything, and said, “You could have taken the quiet version. Health, and all of it. Nobody would have known.”

“Nobody except us. I've spent my life being the man who takes the quiet version; I've discovered there's a tax on it, and the tax is paid in what you think of yourself at night.” Marcus watched his son's face. “You're fourteen. In your life men will offer you the quiet version a dozen times, each time dressed up as wisdom. I wanted you to have at least one example of the other answer.”

Gaius considered this with the Aelian gravity that sat so oddly on his young face, and said, “Then I'll remember it. And if the school says things about the house, or about her—”

“They will. Say that your father chose the truth over the lictors, and that the Aelii have been consuls for four hundred years and can survive one year without. The name doesn't live in the chair. It lives in the letter.”

Livia said nothing until Gaius had gone, and then she picked up the sealed letter and weighed it in her hand.

“My father would have taken the bargain and then spent twenty years telling everyone about the bargain he didn't quite take.” She set it down. “You've made the name smaller and the man larger. I'm not sure Rome understands the exchange, but I do. Send it. I'll send my own to the chief Vestal, so that the women hear your version before the men invent theirs.”

“Livia. If the association continues — the open friendship — you understand that people will speak of you as well as me.”

“People have spoken of me since I was fourteen; I've decided the only mistake is giving them dull material.” She rose, and paused at the door. “Invite her to dinner. Here, properly, with my name on the invitation. Rome can either grow up or gossip, and I've exhausted my patience with gossip.”

Marcus came to the Carinae in the evening, by the front door, in daylight hours, because there was nothing left to hide and no office left to lose.

They sat in the small dining room, the two of them, with the wine between them and the lamp flame steady, and he told her about the room on the Palatine, the bargain, and the letter he had sent.

“You gave it away,” she said, when he finished. “The consulship. For not abandoning me.”

“For not abandoning myself.” He turned the cup in his hands. “I gave away a chair. I kept the thing that made me fit to sit in one, which I'd nearly lost by being too careful. Do you know what I've discovered? That the most expensive things in Rome are the small acts of courage men put off until tomorrow. I've paid at last. The accounts are clean.”

“Livia?”

“Livia said the letter was the best prose I'd ever written and that she'd have despised me if I'd taken the bargain. She's begun referring to the whole affair as her year of being useful, and the consular wives have started a fashion for public-spirited wives, which I'm told is extremely inconvenient to their husbands. Celer's debts are being settled from Perperna's confiscated estate where the fraud touched him, and he's been sent to manage a family property in Sicily, where the temptations are fewer and the olive merchants are dull.”

Antonia smiled, and then was serious. “And us. The councillors said no association. You refused it, in the room, for the record. What does that mean, Marcus, in the actual days? I won't be a private comfort at a back gate, and I won't be a scandal that costs you whatever remains. You know the terms. I gave them to you in custody.”

“I know the terms.” He did not look away. “It means friendship, in the open, in daylight, at your front door, under the gilded panel everyone in Rome can watch. It means dinners and letters and whatever the years make of us, without lies and without the marriage the law won't permit and you wouldn't accept. It means I'm no longer a consul, so my friendship is no longer an offence against an office. They took the chair; they can't take the company. I've made sure of it.”

“You understand what people will say.”

“People already say everything. Let them say it over excellent food; your table has always been Rome's most reliable way to improve a gossip.” He set down the cup. “I'm not asking you for anything, Antonia. I'm telling you what I've chosen, and then I'm leaving the choosing with you, where it has always belonged.”

She looked at him across the lamplit table, at the man who had kept his three returned letters in a strong box and his hands off a slave girl in a kitchen and his word through a whole winter of fire and courts, and she thought that love, when it finally arrived in a life as fortified as hers, did not arrive as a conquest but as a treaty between two people who had separately decided the price of things.

“Then choose with me,” she said. “Dinner, the first night of spring, in the garden court. In the open, where the household can see, and Rome can hear. And after that, we'll find out what two free people do with a door that isn't locked on either side.”

He lifted his cup, and she lifted hers, and the cups met with the small, dry sound of an agreement, and outside, somewhere beyond the Carinae roofs, Rome went about its enormous business, indifferent as ever, while inside the gilded house the lamps burned steady and the winter, at last, began to end.

The first night of spring came with the almond trees in blossom along the Esquiline, and the garden court was set for twelve, in the open, with the new lamps and the blue hangings and the fountain freshly cleared of its winter leaves. The household attended in its best, and the allies came — Hilarus and Porcia, Beryllus with his wife, Vibius Longus out of uniform, Rubrius looking surprised to be enjoying himself, and Festus at the door with his forearm newly healed — and Livia came, with Marcus, and walked through the gilded door on her own feet in full view of the street, and Antonia met her at the threshold, and the two women looked at each other for a held moment, and Antonia said, “Welcome to my house,” and Livia said, “Thank you. I've been admiring its accounts,” and the household, watching, exhaled.

They did not become friends, exactly; they became something more Roman, an alliance of two clear-sighted women who had decided the same men were worth more than the rules that separated them. The dinner was excellent, the wine was honest, the music avoided the sentimental, and Marcus was seated on Antonia's couch in the open position, with no secrets for the servants to overhear, and when the party ended he left by the front door at the proper hour, and the door closed behind him with the sound of a thing settled rather than concealed.

Festus bolted it, as he bolted it every night, and said to Chloe in the atrium, “A good winter, all counted. We're still here, and most of them aren't.”

“Most winters,” Chloe said, taking the keys, “the door only has to shut. This one it had to hold.”

And in the strong room beneath them, the tablets slept in their sealed coffer, and the bronze ring hung at its mistress's throat, and the chipped wooden disc lay in its compartment, and all of it kept, and all of it true.
