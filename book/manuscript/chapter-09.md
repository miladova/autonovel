# CHAPTER NINE — The Baths

The temple of Isis stood in a grove of sycamores near the Esquiline gate, its columns bright with painted plaster, its air thick with incense and the low music of rattles, and it was, Antonia had always thought, the most honest sanctuary in Rome: a goddess for the imported people — the Greeks, the Syrians, the Egyptians, the freedmen and the slaves and the women — for everyone the old Roman gods had never quite bothered to know.

Marcus was already there, in plain dress with Stichus left at the outer gate, standing before the painted image of the goddess with the respect a Roman senator gives any god who might be listening. The priestess glided away with Chloe, leaving the two of them alone in the side chapel where votive candles burned in rows and the walls were covered with painted scenes of the Nile.

“You came alone,” Antonia said.

“I said I would. Romans of my class put enormous faith in the keeping of one's word; it's nearly the only faith we have left.” He turned to her. “You were followed?”

“My litter was. I wasn't.” She sat on the stone bench, folded her hands, and came at once to the point, as she did everything. “Your diary.”

He produced it, open at the marker, and laid it between them. Stichus's hand, the Praeneste dedication, the flooded road, the two nights away. She read it carefully, though she already knew its contents from her own sources, and nodded.

“Then your witness signature is a forgery. The seal was taken from your strong room — your brother has access, and the steward keeps the ring for the family. They sealed a loan you never saw, at twenty-four per cent, and wrote your name across the thongs. If this ever reaches court, the diary is your defence: you were sixty miles away.”

“I've spoken to no one but Stichus. What do you want of me, exactly? Your letter said a partnership with the truth, which is a fine phrase. Give me the plain version.”

“The plain version is that Perperna means to own the eastern supply contracts and everyone attached to them, including your brother, and that the audit is the instrument he means to use — either you sign the false tallies and become his creature, or you expose the fraud and your family name is destroyed through Celer. I hold the documents that let you choose a third path: a real audit, with the true accounts, prosecuted against Perperna rather than your brother.”

“At what price?”

“At the price of your name on the prosecution. Not mine — I'm infamis, my word carries nothing in a Roman court; a meretrix doesn't prosecute equestrians. You bring the evidence before the prefect's office under your own authority. In return, I get Perperna removed before he closes my house, and my people get to keep their lives and their trade.” She met his eyes. “That's the whole bargain. There's no hidden clause involving your bed, your money, or your conscience, though I expect you to suspect all three; men always do.”

He almost smiled. “And my brother?”

“Celer is a fool, not a traitor by design. He testifies to the loans, turns state evidence, and takes exile or bankruptcy rather than the rope. I don't care which; I care that he's alive and talking when the case opens. Perperna dead is a martyr; Perperna ruined in open court is a lesson.”

Marcus was silent a while, watching the candles. “I need to see the documents. The true tablets, not copies.”

“No. Not yet. The originals are the only thing keeping me alive; you'll see them when the magistrate does.” She softened, fractionally. “It isn't trust I'm withholding. It's prudence, and if our positions were reversed you'd do the same, and recommend it to your son.”

“Probably. You've become a Roman, Antonia.”

“I've become what Rome made me. There's a difference.”

The chapel was quiet. Somewhere beyond the columns the rattles sounded, thin and bright.

He said, without looking at her, “I have to ask. After the sale. Dinis the dealer. I know the broad shape of it from Barto; I don't know the rest, and I won't ask you to tell me in detail unless you choose to. But I need to know whether there's anything — anyone — I should hold myself to account for, beyond the sale itself.”

She considered him, the long Roman profile, the grey at the temples, the hands clasped between his knees like a man at prayer.

“You want absolution,” she said. “I can't give it. I won't. The baby was a girl, born too early in the dealer's house, dead before she cried. I nearly died of the fever after, and there was no doctor, because doctors cost money and I was a purchase going bad. Then four years in the Subura, in a house I won't describe, because describing it would give you the comfort of horror and the truth is mostly boredom and shame, which are less dramatic. Then a woman called Antonia Prima bought me, taught me, freed me, and left me everything. That's the account. You didn't light any of it, Marcus. But you lived in the house it warmed, and you believed the lie because believing was easier. I don't hate you for it. Hate is expensive. I simply haven't decided what you are to me now.”

He had gone white. “The child. Mine.”

“Yours. She never had a name; the dealer didn't name dead slave girls. I named her once, alone, in the fever. I called her Laeta, because it means happy, and it seemed only fair to give her the one thing the gods hadn't.” Antonia rose, smooth and composed, the composure itself a kind of armour. “Now you know why the documents matter more than feelings. The men who run this city can sell girls and erase their daughters and call it law. Perperna is what that system looks like when it's hungry. I'm going to make him choke on his own paper. You can stand with me or stand aside. I'd prefer the first; I'll survive the second.”

“I'll stand with you.” He said it quietly, without rhetoric, as one states a fact. “Not because of the past, though the past has its claim. Because the audit I've been given is a lie unless it's real, and I've never put my name to a lie.”

“Then we begin. Hilarus has the Ostia weight notes. Meet him at the baths tomorrow, men's hour, where talk is cheap and no one remembers it. And Marcus—” she paused at the chapel's edge “—don't tell your wife the details. Not because she can't be trusted, but because the fewer houses that hold this, the fewer doors Perperna can break.”

“She already knows more than you think. Wives generally do.”

“Then tell her less than she knows. It's the traditional Roman solution.”

The priestess returned at that moment, her linen robes whispering, her dark face serene, and murmured that two men had asked at the outer gate after a senator visiting the shrine — not pressing, merely asking, the way clerks ask when someone has paid them to remember. Marcus's hand went to his knife; Antonia's did not move.

“They'll have seen Stichus at the gate and guessed the rest. Let them. A consul-designate praying to Isis is eccentric, not illegal; half the nobility consult foreign gods in private and thank Jupiter in public.” To the priestess she said, “My thanks, Mother. The offerings I promised will come tonight, through the usual door.”

When the woman had glided away, Marcus said, “They move quickly.”

“They move constantly; speed is the only advantage money gives a man without rank. Remember that when the documents frighten you.” She rose and walked to the painted wall, where the Nile flooded its banks in bright pigment and the crops grew beneath a foreign sun. “While we're alone and off the record, as the lawyers say — tell me about Celer's part, as far as you know it. Everything, including the shameful parts.”

He told her: the Palatine house, the aedile's games, the horses, the dinners, the slow slide from borrowing to subscribing to signing things he had not read. She listened without interrupting, her face still, and when he finished she said, “He's exactly the man Perperna hunts. Not the vicious, the vain. Vicious men understand consequences; vain men only understand audience. Your brother thought he was the hero of his own story. Perperna writes the stories; he only lets men think they're authors.”

“You don't pity him.”

“I pity him precisely as much as he'd pity me if our positions were reversed, which is none. But I'll use him, because used men can still be saved and ruined men cannot.” She turned from the painting. “One last thing. When the time comes, you'll be asked to choose between the name and the truth, publicly, in front of men who can end your career. Don't answer now. Just know the question's coming, and decide before it's asked, because in the Forum there's no time to think.”

“You speak as though I'll fail it.”

“I speak as though everyone fails it unless they've rehearsed. I've been rehearsing for fifteen years. You've had a month.”

The Baths of Trajan rose on the Oppian Hill above the ruins of Nero's golden house, the greatest bath complex the city had ever seen, and at the men's afternoon hour it was less a bath than a parliament, where senators and shopkeepers steamed side by side and half the politics of Rome were settled in water too hot for dignity.

Marcus came with Hilarus as his cover, the two of them moving through the palaestra where men exercised with balls and weights, past the libraries and the shops, into the warm steam of the tepidarium, where conversation acquired the echo and intimacy of a cave.

The rule of the baths was the rule of the cave: names were rarely used, business was never named outright, and everything worth knowing was said in the third person, as anecdote. Hilarus, whose fortune had been built on understanding precisely this dialect, led Marcus to a bench beside two merchants whose backs were turned and whose talk, in the way of bath talk, was perfectly audible across the steam.

“...fourth shipment, every season, like clockwork. My factor at Ostia says the harbour masters don't even weigh them anymore; the tally's made out before the ship ties up.” A splash, a sigh. “And the difference goes where such differences always go. Up. It's an old river, this one. It's been running since the Dacian war; nobody digs a new channel while the emperor's winning.”

“Dangerous talk.”

“Dangerous to write down. Steam doesn't keep records.” The man laughed, a wet, comfortable sound. “Besides, everyone with a contract knows it. The only people who don't know it are the ones who audit by the official tallies, which is like judging a woman's virtue by her veil. You'd need the harbour weights, the private ones, and those don't leave the granary. They'd have to fire every clerk in the annona to get at the truth, and who's going to do that? The bureau employs the clerks.”

Hilarus caught Marcus's eye, the smallest flicker, and raised his voice just enough to enter the conversation as a third voice, the oil merchant's easy geniality serving as its own passport. “Gentlemen. Hilarus of the Aventine, oil and amphorae. My friend here is a student of harbour accounting — don't hold it against him, it's a sickness. You were saying the private weights don't leave the granary. Never? Not even for the military audit?”

“Especially not for the military audit.” The merchant turned, red and amiable. “The audit gets the tallies. The tallies are made from the private weights. It's like asking a man to swear by his own reflection. Now, if you wanted the true numbers, you'd need a measurer with a memory — one of the Greeks who checks the loading and keeps his own count. They're the only honest thing on the wharf, because nobody's thought to bribe them all yet. But a measurer who talks? The river keeps those.” A shrug, vast and wet. “Enough. Talk like this ruins the heat. The cold pool, gentlemen, before our courage fails.”

The two men moved off, and Marcus sat in the steam with the water running down his face and the sentence settling into him: _a measurer with a memory_. Hector, the dead clerk, had been one kind of evidence, the kind that lived on tablets and could be taken from a room. A man who carried the figures on his own skin, in his own head, would be another kind entirely — the kind that could not be stolen, only killed, and killing a man at Ostia left questions that killing a clerk in a Roman alley did not.

“Write it nowhere yet,” Hilarus murmured, eyes closed against the steam. “But tell your Carinae friend. Her house collects people the river would otherwise keep. If such a measurer exists, she'll know where he works before the week is out; her musicians have brothers on every wharf in the harbour.”

“The weight notes.” Hilarus spoke into the steam, barely moving his lips, the technique of a man who had done business in harbours. “My Ostia factor's copies. Every fourth shipment, the same ninety modii appears between the public weighing and the Roman tally. It's so regular it's almost artistic. The measurer who first flagged it is a man called Dorion; he's in my pay now, for his own safety, sleeping above my warehouse with two guards.”

“Can he testify?”

“He'll testify if the court is open and the guard is real. He's Greek, freed, and frightened; frightened men need to see the wall before they'll stand against it. Your name is the wall.” Hilarus dipped his head under the hot water and came up blowing. “There's more. The short-weight grain doesn't vanish into thin air. It's sold a second time, privately, to the bakers and the marine contractors, and the gold goes to a private banker — not Beryllus, he's too careful — a smaller house on the Aventine. Follow the gold and you'll find the hand at the top.”

“Perperna's.”

“Or the hand above Perperna. Men like him don't invent systems; they're allowed to run them. Ask yourself who profits if the great prosecution fails, or succeeds, and remember that the emperor is raising an army for the East. A war on that scale needs supplies faster than the state can honestly provide. There are people very high up who'd rather not look too closely at how the miracle is achieved.”

Marcus turned this in the steam. It was the first time anyone had said aloud what he had suspected: that the fraud was not a conspiracy against the state but an economy tolerated by it, and that an honest auditor might find himself opposing not one villain but the convenience of the entire machine.

They were in the caldarium, sweating against the hot walls, when Celer appeared in the doorway with two young men of his own fast set, and the brothers saw each other through the steam with the particular awkwardness of relatives meeting naked in public.

“Marcus. Hilarus.” Celer recovered first, lowering himself into the pool with a hiss of pleasure. “The censor and the oil king. Should I leave, or are the times sufficiently degenerate that I may bathe in my own brother's company?”

“Bathe,” Marcus said. “The water doesn't care about your debts.”

The young men laughed a little too loudly, and Celer's smile thinned. “You know, then. I assumed you would by now; everyone else does. Let me save you the speech. I'm a fool, I've been a fool, and I will be a fool again, because our father gave me his charm and his appetites and none of his money. Perperna's been carrying me. There. Is the family honour satisfied?”

“Perperna has been _using_ you. There's a difference.” Marcus kept his voice low. “The subscriptions aren't an investment; they're a rope. When the audit reports, you'll be the first neck offered, because your name makes the scandal respectable. I can protect you if you tell me everything, now, in front of Hilarus, with no more charm.”

The steam hissed. Celer looked at his brother, and for once the handsome face was entirely without performance.

“You really mean to fight him. Everyone says you're too honest to be real, and you're actually going to do it.” He let out a breath. “I'll tell you everything. Not here — I won't say his name naked in a room with twenty ears. At the house. Tomorrow. And Marcus — I'm sorry. For the seal most of all. They said it was a formality, family witnessing family, and I let them. That's on me.”

When he had gone with his friends, Hilarus raised an eyebrow. “That went better than I feared.”

“Because he's frightened. Frightened charmers make honest brothers, briefly. We'll see what survives until tomorrow.”

They moved to the palaestra afterward, where Marcus threw a heavy ball at the wall with controlled, soldier's precision and Hilarus, who hated exercise, watched from a bench with a towel.

“There's something else,” Hilarus said. “The broadsheets. The old consular wasn't exaggerating. I bought every copy my factor could find this morning.” He produced a damp, folded sheet from beneath the towel — cheap paper, rough Greek-influenced Latin, no printer's name. Marcus read it standing with the ball in his hand: a mock love-letter from the “censor” to his “Carinae mistress,” full of innuendo about ledgers and bed-curtains, ending in a joke about the audit being conducted at night. It was funny, which made it worse; Rome memorized jokes and forgot denials.

“Perperna's writers?”

“His style, though he'd never print it himself. The point isn't the accusation; it's the association. Once the crowd sees you two as a pair, any prosecution he brings against her gets to touch you. Adultery law, first of all — the lex Iulia doesn't require proof of the act, merely of the opportunity and the reputation. A man seen at a courtesan's house is half condemned by the gossip.”

“Let him bring it. There's been no adultery; the law still needs an act.”

Hilarus looked at him with the patient expression of a merchant explaining the weather to a landsman. “Senator. With respect. The law needs what a judge can be made to believe. You're an honest man in a dishonest system, and the system's been running long enough to know its business. Guard your movements. Meet her in temples, in crowds, through me. Never alone, never at night, never behind a closed door without a witness.”

Marcus folded the sheet and put it inside his tunic, beside the old letter from Tibur. “I'll take the precautions. I won't abandon the audit.”

“Nobody asked you to abandon it. I asked you to survive it. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference between a martyr and a magistrate.”

An elderly consular, drying himself nearby, had watched the exchange over his oil flask, and now he drifted close with the unhurried purpose of a man who intended to be overheard.

“Severus. A word of advice, from a man who's watched three generations ruin themselves. Your name is being linked in the street with the Carinae woman. Supper houses, races, private temple meetings. I don't care what's true; I care what's repeated. Perperna's pamphleteers are already at work; I've seen one of the broadsheets. ‘The censor and the courtesan.' Very neat. The city forgives vice; it never forgives a joke. Be careful.”

“I appreciate the warning.”

“Do you? You've the look of a man who appreciates warnings and ignores them.” The old man wrapped his towel. “I had a son like you. He's governing Mauretania now, which is safer than Rome, and perhaps that tells you everything.”

The women's bath, at the earlier hour, was a different country — quieter, denser with its own hierarchies, full of the particular laughter of women temporarily outside the keeping of men.

Antonia went with Porcia, who knew every matron in the room and owed deference to none of them, and they had barely reached the warm pool when Livia Marcella entered with her own retinue, and the temperature of the room changed by a degree, as it did wherever Livia went.

The two women's eyes met across the water.

Porcia, who had arranged nothing and understood everything, murmured, “Shall I drown someone, or are we being civilized?”

“We're always civilized,” Antonia said. “It's our weapon.”

Livia came to the pool herself, dismissed her attendants to the far side with a glance, and slipped into the water with the unhurried grace of a woman confident in her own body, her dark hair pinned high, her back straight.

“Domina Antonia. Or may I say Antonia? We've been discussed together in enough drawing rooms to be on first-name terms.”

“Names are free. It's everything else in Rome that costs.”

“I'll come to the point, since the steam makes long speeches ridiculous.” Livia settled against the pool's edge, and her eyes, direct and assessing, met Antonia's without flinching. “What is your price? Every woman in the city has one, and I don't mean it as an insult; the city prices everything. I want you to leave Rome. Take your house, your women, your money — I'll even add to it, from my own dowry, which is mine to dispose of. A year in Alexandria, two in Neapolis. Enough time for the pamphlets to die and the consulship to be secured. Name the figure.”

The pool murmured with other women's distant talk.

Antonia let the offer sit a moment, turning it like a coin.

“You've done this before,” she said. “Offered women money to go away.”

“Three times. Two took it and were sensible; one refused and made my life miserable for a year before she ran off with a dancing master. I've a high opinion of the sensible ones.”

“Then I'm sorry to be the fourth. No figure, Livia. I can't be bought out, because what I'm doing isn't a liaison that ends when the money's paid; it's a war, and my house is the battlefield. Perperna wants my doors and my women and the things my guests have said. If I leave Rome, he takes them anyway, and the women end in the Subura. I don't run from men like him. I bury them.”

Livia studied her for a long, considering moment. “You actually mean that.”

“I don't say things I don't mean. It's another of my bad habits.”

“Most people who say that are lying. You're not.” The senator's wife dipped her fingers in the water, watching the ripples. “I came here prepared to despise you. My friends say it's my wifely duty. Instead I find a woman running an enterprise larger than half my husband's clients' estates, speaking like a jurisconsult, and refusing a dowry-sized bribe for the sake of people the law doesn't even count as people.” She gave a small, dry laugh. “It's extremely inconvenient. I dislike being made to revise my opinions; it ruins the bath.”

“Then prepare for more inconvenience. Your husband is an honest man, which makes him nearly unique in the Forum, and the fraud he's auditing is real, and it reaches into your own household through Decimus. If it succeeds as Perperna means it to, the consulship dies with the family name. My interests and yours point the same way, whether either of us likes it.”

Livia's face sharpened. “Decimus. What has he done?”

“Ask him yourself — better, ask your steward. He visited yours three weeks ago, didn't he, asking about moving family funds? A man drowning looks for every hand on the boat.”

The silence that followed was not the silence of surprise; it was the silence of a theory confirmed. Livia's eyes went very slightly wider, and then narrowed.

“You are remarkably well informed.”

“I pay well for the privilege. I'll tell you one more thing free, because a wife who knows is safer than a wife who guesses. Perperna has filed or will file within days: a licensing review against my house, a custody claim against a girl in my care, and something aimed at the audit. Watch your husband's correspondence; watch your brother-in-law's creditors; and if anyone in your household is suddenly generous with money or kindness, remember that Perperna buys small people because they cost less.”

Porcia, floating nearby with her eyes closed, said without opening them, “She's right, Livia. My Hilarus says the same, and Hilarus is never wrong about money, which is the same as never being wrong.”

Livia ignored her, the way one ignores a beloved but inconvenient aunt. “Tell me about the women. The ones in your house. The matrons' version is that you keep them in gilded cages.”

“The matrons' version is comfortable for the matrons. The truth is that every woman in my house came from somewhere worse, and most of them will leave with money and a trade. Chloe runs my accounts now; she'll have her own business in three years. Sabina, the debt girl Perperna wants, is learning to read at an age when your daughter's still on the alphabet. Tyche plays the lyre and has more courage than half the knights I've met.” Antonia let a beat pass. “They're not caged, Livia. They're employed, in the only trade a woman without a man can enter in this city without becoming a thief or a corpse. Don't mistake the house for the world. The world built the cage; I merely improved the locks, and put them on the inside.”

“That's a very neat answer.”

“It's a true one. Neatness is optional.”

For the first time, something like genuine curiosity moved in Livia's face, beneath the senator's wife. “And you? Did you choose it? Any of it?”

“No. I chose to survive it, and then I chose to own it. Those are different choices, made in a different order, and I'd be lying if I said I knew where one ended and the other began.” Antonia met her eyes in the steam. “Your husband once taught me my letters, in a kitchen, because no one else would bother. That's the whole romance. The rest is law, money, and two families' worth of lies. You can live with that, or not; but don't imagine I've spent fifteen years plotting to take your place. Your place requires a husband. I've worked too hard to need one.”

For a long moment Livia said nothing. Then she extended her hand across the water, not in friendship exactly, but in the older, colder Roman manner of a treaty between equals.

“I won't pretend to like this. I won't pretend my husband's — association — with you is anything but a wound to my pride and a danger to my children. But the Aelii do not lose, and it seems we've acquired an unlikely ally.” Her hand stayed extended. “Truce, then. Not affection. Truce.”

Antonia took the hand. It was firm and cool and held exactly as long as formality required.

“Truce. And Livia — if it's any comfort, I've no interest in your husband's name, his bed, or your place. I had my chance at those things fifteen years ago, in another life, and the world made its own arrangements. What I want from him is his signature on a prosecution, which is far less compromising than an affair and considerably more useful.”

“Somehow,” Livia said, releasing her hand, “that's the first thing you've said that I entirely believe.”

They bathed in a companionable, armed silence after that, two women at the centre of the city's gossip, and when they left at separate hours by separate doors, neither looked back, and the attendants who had been paid to notice things went home with very little to report, which was, for both of them, the evening's real victory.

Back at the house, the women's wing held its own small ceremony. Sabina, in her third week of lessons, read aloud from a simple scroll while the others mended linen and the lamps burned low, and she reached the end of a full paragraph without a single reversal of the letter R, and the room applauded with the genuine warmth of people who knew exactly what the achievement cost.

“Read it again,” Chloe said. “Slowly. The end is where beginners rush.”

Sabina read it again, slowly, and Antonia, listening from the doorway with her day's correspondence in her hand, felt the old tightness in her chest that she had long ago given up trying to name. It was the feeling of watching a person take possession of herself.

Tyche waited until the reading ended and the talk had scattered, then came to the tablinum with her report, delivered in the flat, rehearsed voice of a girl determined to be useful.

“He met me by the cloth stalls again. He knows I report to you — or he guesses, it's the same — because now he doesn't ask anything. He gives. Today it was a bracelet, glass, very pretty, and a story about a friend who protects girls from bad houses. He says his friend can help any of us, any time, no questions.” She laid the bracelet on the table. “I didn't wear it. I brought it.”

“Good. Keep the gifts; evidence is evidence, and glass doesn't frighten me.” Antonia turned the bracelet in the lamplight. “The man's changing tactics, which means his master's impatient. Fear first, gifts second; what comes third is the threat. If he mentions the magistrate, or names Perperna, or asks you to sign anything, you come to me before you sleep. Not the next morning. Before you sleep.”

“I will.” Tyche hesitated at the door. “Domina — he keeps saying I'm too fine to belong to a freedwoman. As if belonging to you were the shameful thing, and belonging to some man I'd never met were freedom.”

“Because shame is all they've got. They can't outbid us, so they try to make us ashamed of the house. Go to bed; you did well.”

When she was alone, Antonia opened the two dispatches that had arrived in her absence. The first, on good parchment, was Hilarus's invitation to his villa outside the Capena gate, a dinner three days hence in honour of his Spanish factors, with a postscript in Porcia's hand: _Wear the blue, it annoys the matrons._ The second, on coarse official paper sealed with the aedile's mark, set the hearing on the custody of Sabina Aviania for the first market day of the next month, before the praetor peregrinus, and required the girl's person and all relevant contracts to be produced in court.

She read the second dispatch twice, then placed it in the strong room drawer beneath the tablets, beside the chipped wooden disc, and said aloud, to the lamps and the fish, “There it is. He's named the day.”

She sat a while in the quiet, laying the timetable beside the investigation like a general matching maps: the dinner, where Marcus could meet Hilarus's factors and the money could be traced; the hearing, where Perperna meant to take Sabina and crack the house; and somewhere beyond them, unannounced, the audit report that would decide which of them walked away from the spring.

Then she put out the lamps and went to bed, and slept, as she had taught herself to sleep in worse houses than this, quickly and without dreams.
