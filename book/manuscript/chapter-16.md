# CHAPTER SIXTEEN — Ostia

They left Rome before dawn, two separate parties by the old arrangement, and met where the road met the river, and the road and the river ran together toward the sea.

Antonia travelled with Chloe and Festus in Hilarus's covered cart, among the oil jars and account tablets, while Marcus rode ahead with Stichus on the public business of the audit, and the two groups were careful to arrive at Ostia through different gates at different hours, because Perperna's eyes were everywhere in Rome but thinner at the harbour, where the money was too busy to gossip.

The sea announced itself before the city did: a change in the air, salt and fish and open space, and then the masts, a forest of them, rising beyond the tiled roofs of the greatest harbour in the world. Ostia lay spread along the mouth of the Tiber, its quays crowded with ships from every province, its granaries towering like temples of bread, its streets full of the accents of the empire — Egyptians, Spaniards, Africans, Syrians, Greeks — all of them shouting, bargaining, hauling, and watching the grain flow toward the insatiable city upriver.

The road gave them Rome's true scale. For miles outside the walls it was lined with tombs and villas and the camps of the night-soil carts, and then the city's noise faded behind them and the country opened in winter fields and olive grey, and the Tiber broadened beside the road with its endless traffic of barges, the grain and wine and oil and timber moving slow and steady as blood through the empire's veins. Chloe slept; Festus drove; Antonia watched the barges and thought, not for the first time, that Rome was less a city than a mouth, and that everything she and Hilarus and the honest merchants did was merely the work of keeping the mouth fed.

At the river mouth the mouth became a harbour. The new imperial port opened beyond the old town, a vast enclosed basin ringed with quays and warehouses, its lighthouse standing tall as a temple, and the sea beyond it stretched blue and enormous to the edge of the world. Even Festus, who was hard to impress, looked at the ships and said, “More wood than the whole of the Subura.”

Hilarus's factor met them at the warehouse gate, a lean, anxious Numidian named Iucundus, with ink on his hands and the look of a man who had not slept since the first threats.

“Dorion's inside, with the guards. And there's something else — you should see it before you settle. One of the wharf clerks. They found him in the alley behind the customs house at dawn.”

The clerk lay on a pallet in the warehouse office, a young man with a broken arm and a face that had been worked over by professionals, breathing through swollen lips. Dorion, the Greek measurer, crouched beside him with a damp cloth, and rose when they came in, hollow-eyed.

“He's called Felix. He kept the public weight notes for quay seven. Two men came to his room last night and asked him, politely, whether he'd spoken to anyone from Rome. He told them no, which was almost true, and they broke his arm to keep him honest, and said next time it would be his writing hand.” Dorion's voice was tight. “He came to me because I'm the only Greek in the harbour he trusts. They're closing the doors, domina. One by one.”

Marcus arrived an hour later, in his senatorial tunic with the audit tablets under his arm, and Antonia watched him take in the wounded clerk, the guarded warehouse, the rows of amphorae, with the same cold attention he gave the Curia. He knelt beside the pallet and spoke to Felix himself, gently, as an officer speaks to a casualty, and got the whole story in ten minutes: the men, their accents, the name of the wine shop where they waited, and — the thing that mattered — the admission that the wharf's master copy of the weights had been altered after each weighing, by an official who reported not to the harbour prefect but to a finance bureau clerk in Rome.

“Zosimus's hand at the harbour itself,” Marcus said, when they were alone among the oil jars. “It's worse than the tablets show. The fraud isn't hidden in Rome. It's committed here, on the quays, and the Roman tallies are merely the lie's second copy.”

“Which is why the witnesses keep ending up in alleys.” Antonia laid out Hilarus's factor's copies beside his tablets. “We'll take Felix's statement now, witnessed and sealed, while he's alive and frightened enough to be precise. Then he goes into the warehouse with Dorion and the guards, and I want a physician, the best in Ostia, on Hilarus's account. A dead witness is useless; a healed one can stand in court.”

Dorion himself told his story over a cup of hot wine in the factor's office, a small, precise man with a clerk's hands and a hunted man's eyes. He had measured grain for eleven years, first at the public scales, later for private shippers, and he had noticed the rhythm slowly, the way a man notices a leak, before understanding that the leak was the building's design.

“Every fourth shipment. Always the same gain. At first I thought I was mistaken; nobody falsifies the public grain, the grain is sacred, the annona feeds Rome. Then I made my own marks, here, on my arm.” He pushed up his sleeve; a row of tiny tattooed numbers ran up the forearm. “I kept them on my body because they'd find any tablet. Your Hector found out about the marks through a friend, months ago. That's why he came to Ostia. That's probably why he's dead.”

“Can you reproduce the marks as a statement?” Marcus asked.

“I can reproduce every weight I've taken in two years. The arm's my ledger; nothing can burn it.” He looked from Marcus to Antonia. “I'll testify. I've decided. A man can't hide in a warehouse forever, and my daughter's in Rome, and I won't have her grow up thinking her father let them steal the bread and said nothing. But I'll say this too: if your protection fails, I'm a dead man at the bottom of the harbour, and so is Felix. We're staking our lives on your case.”

“Then we'll make a case worth the stake,” Marcus said.

Antonia said nothing, but she made a private note: Dorion was the strongest witness they had, the man with the truth written into his skin, and the strongest witness required the strongest guard. She would double the warehouse men and have Hilarus move the family quietly before they left Ostia. The powerful could buy soldiers; she would have to buy loyalty in advance, which was cheaper and more durable.

The granary inspection came first, while the light was good, and Marcus went in his official capacity, with the harbour prefect's reluctant secretary and a clerk to make notes, and Antonia came as the representative of Hilarus's shipping interests, her own copy of the factor's weight notes in hand.

The public granary at quay seven was a vast, cool hall of arches and raised floors, full of the dry, warm smell of stored wheat, and the supervisor, a plump man named Capito, greeted the auditor with the particular smile of an official who has prepared for the visit. The tallies on his tablets were immaculate. The grain was stacked correctly, the ventilation was sound, everything was in perfect order on paper.

Antonia walked the stacks with Dorion at her heels, and said nothing until they reached the rear bins, where the factor's notes showed a shipment of three hundred and twenty modii. The bin was marked for four hundred and ten.

“Same grain?” she asked Dorion, low.

“Same shipment, same bin, same dust line. I watched it weighed.”

She said nothing aloud until they were back before Capito, and then she asked, in the polite voice of a woman asking a small favour, whether she might see the public scale. They weighed a sealed measure, and it was correct. They weighed a second, and it was correct. Then she asked the clerk to read aloud the entries for the last twelve shipments, and the room heard the rhythm Dorion had found in the tablets: every fourth entry, the same gain, ninety modii, as regular as a heartbeat.

“Remarkable consistency,” Marcus said, in the voice he used in the Curia. “One might almost think the grain grew on the river.”

Capito's smile did not leave his face, but it became a fixed thing, the smile of a mask. “The weighing has its variations, Senator; the harbour air, the moisture—”

“The moisture adds no ninety modii to every fourth shipment, in every season, for two years.” Marcus closed the tablet. “You'll preserve all your records, Capito, under seal, and the harbour prefect will receive a copy of this morning's notes. Nothing is to be moved, burned, or corrected. I'll have the clerk witness that instruction.”

Outside, in the salt wind, Antonia said, “He'll run before nightfall.”

“Let him. A running official is a confession; I've put a man at the gate to note which road.” Marcus looked back at the granary's arches. “I've audited ten years of accounts in Rome and found nothing but clean wax. The crime was here, in the dust, all along. The tablets were never meant to be read against the bins.”

“That's why they killed the one man who could read both,” Dorion said, very low.

The statement took the afternoon. The light went golden over the harbour, and the lamps were lit, and one by one the pieces of the chain were pressed into wax: the weighing, the alteration, the bureau clerk, the men with their polite violence. Felix, the beaten clerk, dictated slowly through his swollen mouth, and the physician arrived while the last line was written, a grey Greek who set the arm with competence and took Hilarus's coin without asking questions. Festus stood in the doorway the whole time, and the warehouse guards stood outside it, and when the last seal was pressed, Marcus sat back and said, quietly, “They'll know we were here by morning. The whole case now turns on speed. I'll take these to Attianus the moment we're back. No more waiting on perfect order; the prefect gets what exists, and we fill the gaps under his protection.”

“Then we leave tomorrow at first light,” Antonia said. “Tonight, nobody sleeps alone. Festus, the guards double.”

While the physician worked and the lamps were trimmed, Marcus spread the day's statements across the warehouse table and did what auditors do at the end of a long counting: he checked the arithmetic of treachery.

“Lay the dates in a line,” he said to Antonia, who had drawn up a stool with her own tablet. “Felix's beating, the altered tallies, the fourth-shipment gains, the shell subscriptions Celer signed. If this is one machine, the dates ought to move in one direction, like water toward a drain.”

They moved. Within a quarter of an hour the table showed what neither statement showed alone: the bureau's corrections always followed the shipments by precisely three days, the false tallies were always witnessed by the same two names, and the shell subscriptions, which Celer had been signing in Rome for a year, predated each Ostia contract by a matter of weeks, as though the paper boat were built in the harbour before the cargo existed to fill it.

“That's the shape of it,” Marcus said, sitting back. “The subscriptions create the firm; the firm takes the contract; the fourth shipment creates the surplus; the bureau corrects the tally to hide it; the surplus becomes silver; the silver walks back through the bank to the men who built the boat. Every step is legal-looking, and every step has a document, and the documents were meant never to lie on the same table.”

“Until now.” Antonia sanded her notes. “Hector's tablets are the keystone. He kept the pre-correction numbers, which means his figures and the official tallies can't be made to agree, and two ledgers that disagree are the one thing a fraudulent bureau can't survive in open court. That's why he's dead.” She looked at the sealed statements drying in their rows. “We carry these back like eggs. If we're stopped on the road—”

“We won't be. We travel as Hilarus's factor train, his seals on the wagons, his name on the passes; no one searches an oil merchant who supplies the palace.” Marcus rolled the tablets into their oilskin with the care of a man handling his own life, which, in a sense, he was. “But we also make copies tonight, before we sleep. One set with Hilarus here, sealed in his warehouse vault; one set travels; the originals stay in the temple coffer in Rome. Three doors. Your rule.”

“My rule, learned from a banker over figs.” She rose, stiff after the long day, and looked out through the warehouse doors at the harbour, where the riding lights of the grain fleet swung in the dark water and the lighthouse beam turned its slow, indifferent circle. “All that food out there, Marcus. An army's bread, a city's bread, and a handful of men weighing it twice and selling the difference. Hector died for ninety modii of wheat. It's such a small number to kill a man for.”

“Small numbers are the only ones men kill for. A fortune in one stroke leaves witnesses; ninety modii a shipment leaves nothing but arithmetic, and nobody weeps for arithmetic.” He fastened the oilskin's ties. “That's why we're going to make them weep for it. In the Forum, if we have to, one figure at a time.”

Felix, the beaten clerk, was awake when they checked on him before the watches changed, his arm bound, his face a map of dried blood and fear. Antonia sat with him a while herself, because frightened men spoke differently to a woman who did not threaten, and she had the scrivener's questions ready.

“Tell me about the day's order of work. Not the men — the work. When do the tallies leave the granary, who carries them, where do they change hands?”

He told her, haltingly, and the routine built itself into another small map: the morning weights, the midday copying, the evening corrections, and the single hour each week when the granary's own rolls travelled to the bureau for reconciliation. “That hour,” Felix said, wincing as he shifted, “is when the numbers stop being ours. Whatever we send up comes back different, and we're told to make our books match the returned ones, or find other work. Or” — he touched his bound arm — “other lessons.”

“Then the returned tallies are the damning documents,” Marcus said when they were outside again. “The corrections exist in two hands, and the bureau's hand is the one that changed.”

“Two hands, and now one of them has a statement and a physician's report of the lesson.” Antonia looked back at the shuttered room. “Keep him here until the prefect's protection covers him. A man who's been beaten once expects to be beaten again; the only cure is a door he believes will hold.”

The inn Hilarus used stood a street back from the quay, a respectable place for merchants with papers, and the factor had quietly taken the whole upper floor, so that the party had the run of it: rooms along a balcony above a courtyard where an olive tree grew and a fountain splashed, and below, the ordinary noise of a harbour inn — sailors, dice, a singer with a lyre, the rhythmic thump of the sea.

They ate late, in the upper room, because no one had eaten since dawn: grilled fish, bread, the sharp local wine, and for once no servants but the innkeeper's wife, who served and withdrew and knew better than to remember faces. Iucundus took the first watch with Festus, and Chloe, with one long, knowing look at her mistress, gathered up the tablets and her mantle and went to the room she was to share with the innkeeper's daughter, and closed the door with a soft, final click.

Marcus and Antonia were alone.

They stood it for perhaps a minute, the fountain below them, the singer's voice threading the dark, and then he said, with the careful formality that always returned to him when he was moved, “I should go to my room.”

Before that, they had talked. The fish had come and gone, and the wine, and the long, strange ease of being a day's journey from the city that defined them. He told her about the German winter, the frozen patrols, the day his unit had walked into an ambush and he had learned, at twenty-two, that courage was mostly the absence of any dignified way to run. She told him about the early years of the house, the six couches and the leaking roof, the first great banquet she had staged on credit and the senator's wife who had tried to have her expelled from the street.

“Prima used to say Rome forgives everything except a woman's success,” she said. “A man rising is a story; a woman rising is an accusation.”

“She sounds wise.”

“She was cynical, which is wisdom after its illusions have died.” Antonia turned the wine cup in her hands. “And you. The marriage. You've never said the whole of it.”

“The whole of it is that Livia and I were assigned each other like two accounts in the same ledger. She's magnificent, and I've never once made her laugh from anything except duty. We produced two excellent children and a functioning estate, which is what the contract required. The poets' version was never on offer.” He looked into his cup. “I've been faithful, if that matters. Mostly because I'm no good at dividing myself. Other men manage it; I've watched them with admiration.”

“It matters less than you think. Fidelity without feeling is just another kind of arrangement.” She said it gently. “I've been the other woman, Marcus, more times than I can count, though never in my own house, and not the way the broadsheets imagine. I know the architecture of Roman marriages from the outside. You're not the first unhappy husband at my table. You're the first one who knew it.”

“And now?”

“And now the wine's gone to my head and the sea is making me honest, which is a dangerous combination. Let's leave it there.”

But they did not leave it there, and the silence that followed was a different silence from any before it, full of the sounds of the inn and the night and two people listening to their own hearts.

“You could. The whole arrangement would remain exactly as it was.” She did not move from the balcony rail. “Or you could stay, and it would change, and neither of us would be able to pretend afterward. I've made a policy of knowing which thing I'm choosing. I'd like you to know as well.”

“I've known since the temple grove. Perhaps since the door. I'm merely trying to give you the chance to refuse me without a scene.”

She turned from the rail. “Have I ever struck you as a woman who needed chances to refuse?”

“No.” He said it almost sadly. “That's been the difficulty. You refuse the whole world so completely.”

“Not all of it.”

The distance between them was perhaps three paces, the length of a small room, and they crossed it in the end at the same moment, the way two people cross every distance that has been waited through too long. His hands came up to her face with the tentativeness of a man handling something he had broken once, and she put her own hands over them and pressed them harder, because she had no use for tentativeness, and said, against his mouth, “I am not glass. I have never been glass.”

Then they were kissing, fifteen years of silence breaking like a held breath, and it was not gentle at first, it was urgent and almost angry, the kiss of two people discovering how much of their lives had been spent not doing this. Her back met the wall of the room; his hand found the fastening of her stola and fumbled it, and she covered his hand with hers and undid it herself, quickly, competently, the way she did everything, and the dark fabric fell, and the lamplight moved across her shoulders.

He stopped then, and looked at her, and she saw the soldier's composure fail entirely, and said, “What?”

“I had forgotten. I had made myself forget.” His voice was rough. “The kitchen. The light through the olive press. You were seventeen, and I was a fool who thought the world would wait.”

“The world didn't wait. It sold me. But this—” she took his hand and laid it against her heart “—this one thing it never quite managed to take. I checked, over the years. It was still here. Annoyingly.”

He laughed, and it came out broken, and kissed her again, and this time it was slower.

What happened between them then had nothing of the theatrical about it, and that was its mercy and its power. There was no performance, because there was no audience; no transaction, because nothing was being bought; no rule but the rule of two bodies deciding, at last, to stop being careful. She had known a great many men's hands in her life, professionally and otherwise, and none of them had ever trembled, and his did, and the trembling undid her more than skill could have. He undressed her with the same thoroughness he gave documents, learning her, and when she in turn undressed him she found the old scar on his shoulder from the German frontier and put her mouth to it, and he made a sound she had never heard from him, low and undefended.

In the lamplight, on the narrow inn bed with the sea beating its rhythm below the window, they were awkward and then not awkward, and the years did the work that no technique can do: he knew her history, all of it, the Subura house and the stillborn daughter and the price of every wall she had built, and he did not flinch from any of it, and she knew his — the duty, the marriage, the father's lies, the wall of virtue he had hidden behind — and did not flinch either. There was nothing to pretend. Two people who had been made strangers by other people's lies recognized each other in the dark, and the recognition was the whole of the love-making.

Afterward he lay with his head against her breast, and she held him, and neither of them slept for a while.

“Say something honest,” she murmured into his hair.

He was quiet long enough that she thought he might not. Then: “I am afraid. Not of the prosecution, of this. Of how much I wanted it. I've spent my life being the disciplined man, and it turns out the discipline was mostly loneliness with good posture.”

“That's honest enough to hurt.”

“You asked.” He lifted his head. “And you? Your turn.”

She considered the dark ceiling, the beam of lamplight, the moving shadows. “I'm afraid that I'll use this. That I'll turn even this into a calculation, because it's the only way I know to be safe. I love my house more than I've ever loved any person, and I don't yet know whether what's happening to me is an exception or a weakness.”

“Perhaps it's neither. Perhaps it's the first thing you've allowed yourself that didn't have to earn its place.”

She looked down at him, and for the second time in their lives — the first had been a kitchen, in another century — she let someone see her entirely unguarded.

“Careful, Marcus. You're beginning to sound like the man who taught me my letters.”

“He sounds insufferable.”

“He was wonderful. The world ate him.”

“The world didn't.” He kissed the place beneath her jaw. “It merely tried. It tries everyone.”

Below them the inn sang and gambled and went about its business, and the Mediterranean came in against the quay in its long, patient waves, and somewhere in the warehouse the wounded clerk slept under guard, and the sealed statements waited for the praetorian prefect, and two people who should never have met again lay in each other's arms at the edge of an empire, and for one night the ledgers and the knives and the names could not reach them.

They were woken before dawn by Festus's knock, two measured beats, and they were already themselves again by the time the door opened — dressed, composed, the room restored, the only sign the dark circles under their eyes and a certain carefulness in the way they did not look at each other for the first few seconds. Chloe noticed everything and said nothing, which was her gift, and handed Antonia a cup with the expression of a priestess presiding over a rite.

Chloe said nothing in the cart, for which Antonia was grateful, and Festus drove with his eyes on the road and the particular blankness of a man who had decided not to know things. At the river crossing, while the ferry took on carts and mules, Antonia walked a little apart along the bank, and Marcus joined her, and for a while they simply watched the brown water run toward the sea.

“Whatever comes of the indictment,” he said, “last night wasn't a bargain. I want that said, because everything else between us has been bargains, and this one wasn't.”

“I know. That's what frightened me.” She kept her eyes on the water. “I've made my life out of knowing what things cost. I don't know the price of this one.”

“Perhaps some things aren't bought. Perhaps they're merely paid for later, in whatever coin the city demands.”

“You've become a philosopher in your old age.”

“I've become a man who's finally noticed the world's rules don't cover everything.” He paused. “When the hearing comes, I won't deny you. Whatever the advocates write into their speeches, I won't stand in a Roman court and call you what Perperna calls you. I thought you should know before they ask.”

“I know. It's why I let you stay.” She almost smiled. “Now be useful and help me plan the defence, since we've apparently decided to be ruined together.”

They talked strategy until the ferry came, of witnesses and timetables and the order of evidence, and the talk was steadying, because the work was the thing they both trusted; and when they boarded, and the cart resumed its climb toward Rome, Antonia felt the night settle into her like a foundation rather than a crack, which was, she supposed, what the poets called happiness, though she would never have used the word aloud.

The ride back to Rome was quiet, the two parties separate again, converging on the city as the gates opened with the morning traffic. At the Carinae, the household was intact, the guards were in place, and the only thing waiting was a sealed letter that had arrived the previous evening and been placed, as all such letters were, on the tablinum table.

Antonia opened it, read it once, and read it again, and her face went through no change at all, which was how Chloe knew it was serious.

It was from the office of the urban praetor, and it said that a formal indictment had been received and accepted for examination, under the lex Iulia de adulteriis and the statutes against lenocinium, naming the establishment in the Carinae and its mistress, and naming, as the married senator alleged to have consorted within it, Marcus Aelius Severus, consul-designate. A preliminary hearing was set for the third market day.

She set the letter down, and looked out through the colonnade at the morning roses, and thought of one night by the sea, and of a city that never let a person keep anything without demanding its price.

Then she called for Festus, and for Chloe, and for wax, and began to dictate the replies, because whatever the indictment said, the house was still hers, and the case was still theirs, and the only answer Rome had ever respected from a woman like her was the answer written down, in order, before the other side had finished speaking.

By the third hour the replies were written: a formal denial delivered to the praetor's office, a request for the names of the indictment's accusers and witnesses, and a separate application, under the standing order Marcus had obtained, asserting that the house and its records formed part of the auditor's evidence and could not be touched without the praetor's hand. Hilarus carried the last himself, in his best toga, because a freedman millionaire at the praetor's door was harder to ignore than a letter.

“He'll fight on two fronts now,” Antonia said, when the messengers had gone. “The criminal indictment against us, and the quieter campaign to keep the audit from Attianus. We have to be faster on both.”

“Then I'll take the Ostia statements to the prefect today, not tomorrow,” Marcus said. He had come to the house by the postern, still dusty from the road, and he stood in the tablinum with the parchment roll in his hand and the weight of the indictment visibly upon him. “Attianus hates informers and he hates inefficiency in equal measure. The Ostia evidence gives him both, in one case. If he opens an inquiry, Perperna's indictment becomes a counter-attack against a prefect's investigation, which even he can't afford.”

“Go. And Marcus—” she paused at the door, with Chloe and Festus carefully looking elsewhere “—whatever they ask about us in that hearing, don't lie. Lying gives them the lever. Tell the truth about the partnership, about the evidence, about nothing else, and let them make what they can of it.”

“Nothing else,” he agreed, and was gone, and the house turned again to its work, and the indictment joined the growing file, and Rome, which had seen a thousand such indictments and forgotten them all, went about its morning.

Chloe waited until his footsteps had faded, and said, “Nothing else. That's very disciplined of you both.”

“Discipline is all we have left,” Antonia said, and touched, once, the place beneath her stola where his hand had rested over her heart, and then she put her own hand firmly to the accounts, and did not look up until noon.
