# CHAPTER TWO — Fifteen Years

The gilded door was even more striking by torchlight.

Marcus saw it first from the litter as the bearers turned off the Subura lane into the Carinae: two long leaves of bronze overlaid with gold leaf, framed in white marble, flanked by footmen in blue who stood as still as the columns behind them. A small crowd had gathered on the opposite pavement to watch the litters arrive, the way Romans gathered to watch anything that cost more than they would earn in a year, and the crowd was being managed, he noticed, without roughness and without eye contact, by a pair of steady-looking slaves who guided the sightseers back as gently as shepherds with sheep.

“Observe,” said Celer, who had insisted on sharing the litter and was now arranging his garland. “No names are called at the door. No announcements. A man can come here without his distinction going all over the street by supper-time. That's the first thing she sells, before the wine or the women. Discretion.”

“She?” said Marcus.

“Antonia Cassia. The mistress. You'll see.”

The litter set them down. A footman opened the door before they reached it, and another slave knelt to unlace their outdoor shoes and offer soft dining slippers. Inside, the vestibule was lit by bronze lamps on tall stands, and the dolphin mosaic gleamed under a thin film of fresh oil. There were flowers somewhere, roses and something sharper underneath, and the sound of a water flute drifting from the inner rooms, played softly enough that a man could still hear himself think.

Marcus had entered the houses of the richest men in Rome. He knew luxury the way a farmer knows weather, by instinct and long habit, and he took the measure of this one with a professional's eye. The marble was Greek, not the local Luna stone that new men used. The statues in their niches were genuinely old, two of them signed by Athenian masters. The Corinthian capitals were correctly proportioned, which alone separated the house from ninety per cent of the ostentatious domus on the Palatine. Everything here said the same thing in the same calm voice: whoever owns this has nothing to prove.

It made him uneasy. He had expected vulgarity. Vulgarity he could despise and be done with.

Other guests were crossing the atrium in pairs and threes — a legionary prefect in a gold ring, a grain contractor whose barges Marcus had seen unloading at the Aemilian bridge, two junior senators he knew by sight, and, moving among them with the ease of a man in his own club, Sextus Perperna.

Marcus knew Perperna, as every senator knew him, the way every traveller knows the location of the swamp on the road. He was an equestrian of obscure family who had made himself one of the wealthiest men in Rome through the single Roman trade that required no goods, no ships and no skill except nerve: prosecution. Under the laws against treason, extortion and adultery, a successful accuser received a share of the condemned man's property, and Perperna had accused successfully for twenty years. His victims included two consulars, a governor, and a Vestal Virgin's lover, and the city's attitude toward him was the ancient Roman attitude toward venomous snakes — not admiration, exactly, but a respectful distance.

He was dressed, as always, like a schoolmaster: plain dark tunic, no rings, no perfume, his greying hair cut short. His face was narrow and kindly, with deep lines around the mouth that looked, at first glance, like the marks of laughter. He came forward smiling and clasped Marcus's forearm.

“Severus. The most honest man in the Senate, come to see how the other half dines. I'm delighted. I shall tell everyone I saw you smile, and no one will believe me.”

“Then tell them I did not,” said Marcus. “It will confirm their opinion of me, and I dislike disappointing expectations.”

Perperna laughed, a warm, easy laugh. “There it is. The famous austerity. Rome needs men like you, Severus. We who do the practical work require someone to disapprove of us. It gives the city its moral architecture.” His eyes moved past Marcus to Celer, and the smile changed by a fraction, becoming something with knowledge in it. “Aelius. Your servant. I've a word for you later, about that little subscription. Nothing unpleasant. Later.”

Celer's answering smile was bright, but Marcus saw the swallow move in his brother's throat. Then a slave was bowing them through to the dining wing, and the evening closed over them like water.

---

The great triclinium held three couches around a low table of citrus wood, with a second room opened beyond it for the larger party, so that twenty men could dine in sight of one another without crowding. The couches were dressed in Tyrian purple, and behind them the walls were painted with garden scenes so that the room seemed to open onto impossible groves of fruit trees and fountains. In the space between the couches, a girl was dancing with castanets, moving with a trained, unhurried grace to the music of a water organ and a pair of flutes.

Marcus took his place on the middle couch, Celer beside him, and accepted the first cup — a seasoned wine, honeyed and cooled with snow from the mountains, served in a glass so thin it bent slightly in his fingers. The gustus came round: eggs in anchovy sauce, olives steeped in wine, little sausages on silver skewers, oysters brought live from the Lucrine lake. He ate sparingly, as he did everything, and watched.

That was when he noticed the women.

There were perhaps eight of them in the two rooms, moving among the couches with wine jugs and conversation, and none of them behaved the way the women in the cheap houses off the Subura behaved. They wore fine dresses and modest jewellery; they laughed at the men's jokes without hanging on them; one was arguing Greek philosophy with the governor of Syria's agent, and from the expression on his face, winning. A dark woman with a musician's long fingers was tuning a lyre near the fountain. Another, older, moved quietly through the rooms checking cups and lamps like a housekeeper, though she wore a lady's rings.

“Chloe,” said Celer, following his glance as the dark woman looked up. “The Greek. The best conversationalist in Rome, they say, which is worth more than the face, and the face is considerable. But the one you want to watch is the mistress. She's here somewhere.”

The first main course arrived — a whole boar brought in on a vast silver tray with steam rising from its flanks, its tusks gilded, attended by four carvers who took it apart in a kind of dance. Marcus had seen the dish at a dozen aristocratic tables. Here, he noticed, the smaller dishes that came with it were the more telling thing: mullet seasoned with pepper from India, a pâté of songbirds, asparagus, a sauce of garum and wine so precisely balanced that even the epicure beside him, a man who prided himself on being impossible to please, ate it in silence.

The wine worked quickly, as it was meant to. These were men who guarded their tongues in the Forum and the Senate as carefully as vestals guarded the flame, and here, among the lamps and the roses and the women who knew exactly when to laugh, the guard came down seam by seam. The grain contractor was soon explaining to anyone who would listen that the coming eastern war would make the fortunes of everyone with a contract and a cool head, and dropping names of firms as a fisherman drops bait. The Syrian agent argued that the emperor's new column was vulgar. The two junior senators competed for the attention of a red-haired girl from Alexandria with stories of their own courage, each story growing by the telling.

Marcus watched it with the cold interest of a man examining a machine. He understood now what the house truly sold. Not women, though women were its brightest merchandise; not food, though the food was the best in Rome. It sold the moment when a man believed himself off the stage of Rome — when the masks slipped, and the things he would never say in daylight came swimming up through the wine. And the woman who owned the room collected every word.

Chloe rose with her lyre and the talk softened into attention. She tuned the strings without haste, lifted her chin, and began to sing a poem of Sappho's, in the old Greek, her voice clear and a little husky, trained to fill a room without ever seeming to raise it. The officers and contractors fell quiet one by one, the way loud men fall quiet when real skill enters the room. Even Celer stopped talking. Marcus, who had been taught Greek from the age of six by a grammaticus who beat him for accents, followed the words: the moon had set, the Pleiades had gone, midnight was passing, time was passing, and she lay alone.

It was a poem about desire and waste and the small, private griefs that public men were not permitted to have. Chloe sang it straight, without ornament, looking at no one, and when the last note died the room applauded with the slight embarrassment of people who had been touched more deeply than they had intended.

“Extraordinary,” said the epicure beside Marcus, mopping his eyes with his napkin and pretending he had not wept. “What a house. What a house.”

The dessert came: figs and apples and grapes, honey cakes, sweet wine from Chios, and a confection of crushed nuts and dates shaped into pyramids. The castanet dancer returned, and now a second girl joined her, and the two of them moved through a pattern of feigned pursuit and flight that left the older men breathing hard. Marcus noticed that the women of the house never performed the more explicit dances that the cheaper taverns offered. Antonia Cassia understood something that the tavern keepers did not: that a man's appetite, kept just short of satisfaction, paid better than appetite fulfilled.

It was during the second course that the music changed, and the women shifted, and the room's temperature altered in the way a room alters when its owner enters it.

She came through from the garden colonnade with the dark Greek woman a step behind her, and Marcus did not at once understand why every head turned. She was not the most beautiful woman in the room, though she was handsome enough in a strong-boned, composed way, dressed in a deep blue stola that covered her shoulders, with a single string of pearls at her throat and her dark hair coiled simply. She moved without hurry, greeting a guest here with a word, checking a servant there with a glance, and the whole room arranged itself around her the way iron filings arrange themselves around a magnet.

Then she turned toward his couch, and the floor seemed to move under Marcus Aelius Severus.

Fifteen years fell away in a single instant. He was twenty-three again, home on leave from the German frontier, and standing in the kitchen of his father's country villa with a wooden stylus in his hand; and a girl of sixteen with ink on her fingers was looking up at him from the table, laughing because she had written her first complete sentence, and the afternoon sun was coming through the olive press and making a golden bar across the floor.

His hand tightened on his cup. The glass bent further, and he set it down carefully because he was no longer certain of his fingers.

She had reached the couch. She saw him.

She did not stop. She did not blink. The composed hostess's smile did not alter by the width of a hair. Only the Greek woman beside her, watching with a professional's attention, saw the very slight change in the set of Antonia Cassia's shoulders, the way a hunting dog sees a twitch in the grass that no one else notices.

“Senator,” she said, in a low, clear voice pitched exactly for the couch and no further. “You are welcome to my house. The Aelii honour me.”

Marcus heard himself speak as though from a great distance. “You have the advantage of me, domina. Your face — forgive me. Have we met?”

It was the wrong thing, and he knew it. A flicker passed behind her eyes, something cold and ancient, gone before it could be named.

“We have not, Senator. I should remember. I remember everyone who comes through my door.” She inclined her head, perfectly polite, perfectly final. “Antonia Cassia. And you are Marcus Aelius Severus, though I hardly need tell a man his own name. They tell me you audit the army's accounts now. A grim trade for so distinguished a soldier. I shall have the cook send you something to compensate you for the evening.”

And with that she was moving on, greeting the grain contractor with the same smile, leaving Marcus holding a conversation that had ended before it began, while beside him Celer chattered about the boar and the water organ and the extraordinary quality of the snow.

He could not eat. He drank water and watched her across the room, and the longer he watched, the more certain he became, and the less he understood how he could ever have failed to know her at once. The girl's bones were under the woman's face. The way she held her head when she listened, the small gesture with which she tucked a thought behind before she spoke, the particular stillness of her attention — these were not things a person learned. They were the architecture of a soul, built in childhood, unchangeable.

Anthe. Her name had been Anthe. A house-born slave, her mother a Greek woman who did the sewing, her father unknown; the child who had run wild in the villa's colonnades while he was being tutored in the library, whom he had taught his letters because she watched the lessons through the door with such hunger that the tutor had finally shrugged and let her sit in the corner. The girl who, at sixteen, had become the entire secret life of his heart.

And who, his father had told him, had been manumitted and gone south with a merchant, of her own free will, taking the manumission money and never once looking back.

He had believed it for fifteen years. He had built a marriage and a career and a wall of Roman virtue on top of the belief. Looking now at the woman in the blue stola moving among her guests like a queen among petitioners, he felt the wall develop its first crack.

“Chloe,” said a voice at his elbow, and the dark Greek woman had seated herself on the edge of his couch with a wine jug, her eyes clever and friendly and entirely unreadable. “The mistress said the senator drinks water. A man who drinks water at a supper like this is either ill or dangerous. Which is it?”

“Thirsty,” said Marcus, recovering himself. “And in no danger that I can see.”

“That depends on what you came looking for.” She poured for him anyway, a thin measure, and for Celer, who beamed. “Greek, aren't you?” Marcus said.

“Athenian, though Athens has forgotten me, which is typical of cities. I was sold at Delos at thirteen, bought by a school of rhetoric at fourteen — they needed someone to read the scrolls aloud — and manumitted at nineteen for correcting my master's pronunciation of Demosthenes.” She said it without self-pity, the way another woman might have given a recipe. “Now I read scrolls aloud for pay, and men pay a great deal more for it than the school did. You're looking at the mistress.”

Marcus said nothing.

“Everyone looks at the mistress. It's her room. But you're looking differently.” Chloe's voice stayed light, but her eyes were measuring. “She came from nothing, you know. Not poor — nothing. A slave. She freed herself, learned everything, built all this. In Rome, where a man with four generations of consuls behind him still can't arrange his own fish sauce. You'd think the city would admire her. Instead it calls her the whore of the Carinae and comes to supper every night.”

“Is that what she is? A whore?”

Chloe smiled, and the smile had a edge like a knife. “She is the woman who decides which men in this room get what they want. I'd be careful what I called her, if I were you. In this house, the names have a way of following a man home.”

---

Perperna found him in the garden, during the pause before the dessert.

The older man walked slowly along the colonnade, eating a date, and stopped beside him at the fountain where the bronze boy wrestled the goose. In the dining room behind them the water organ had started a new tune, and laughter rose and fell like surf.

“A remarkable establishment,” said Perperna. “Remarkable woman. You seemed struck dumb when she greeted you. An old acquaintance?”

“A resemblance. Nothing more.”

“Of course.” Perperna ate the date and dropped the stone into the fishpond. “We are all collections of resemblances, Severus. The trick is knowing which ones matter.” He looked out at the roses, his kindly face serene. “You've taken on the supply contracts, I hear. The eastern schedule. Brave. The consuls love to put an honest name on a dishonest ledge, so that when it crumbles, honesty falls with it.”

“I don't fall.”

“No. You don't. I've made a study of you, if you'll forgive the impertinence. You're a man who actually believes in the Republic's virtues, which is rare enough to be almost a kind of genius. I find it useful to keep such men in view. They do the unexpected.” Perperna turned toward him. “Take my advice, as one practical man to another. Sign the tallies as they're given to you. The grain is weighed, the seals are witnessed, the money has flowed through respectable hands. Ask nothing about the hands behind those hands. There are interests in those contracts that would ruin even your name, and the war in the East hasn't even begun.”

“Is that a warning or a threat?”

Perperna looked genuinely pained. “My dear fellow. It's an act of friendship. I have so few opportunities for friendship. My profession makes people uneasy.” He smiled. “Besides, I have a proposition forming, and a man with a proposition needs friends in high places. Watch this house, by the way. Its mistress has ambitions beyond wine and music. Such women are interesting until they become inconvenient. Then they become opportunities.”

He drifted back toward the lamps, leaving Marcus with the sound of the fountain and a coldness along his spine that had nothing to do with the night air.

He got his private word with her only at the door, as the slaves brought the shoes. The party was breaking up; Celer had gone ahead in the company of the grain contractor, already deep in some negotiation of his own. Marcus lingered, and Antonia came to see the last guest out herself, as she always did, because a departure, in her trade, was as deliberate as an arrival.

“Domina.” He kept his voice low. “A moment. I know you. Whatever you call yourself now, I know you. The villa at Tibur. The kitchen. The stylus. You wrote _Roma_ on a wax tablet and the R came out backwards, and you laughed—”

“Senator.” Her voice was quiet too, and absolutely even, but the knuckles of the hand on the doorframe were white. “Whatever you think you remember, I advise you to forget it. That girl, if she ever existed, is as dead as the men who founded the Republic. I am Antonia Cassia. I own this house. I pay my taxes, I hold my licenses, and I choose my own company. You were a guest here tonight. Be satisfied with that.”

“He told me you were freed. My father. He said you took the money and went south willingly.”

For the first time, something cracked. Not the composure — the composure held — but behind it, in the eyes, he saw a flare of something so raw and so old that he stepped back.

“Your father,” she said, “told you a great many things, I'm sure. Sons usually believe their fathers. It costs them less than the truth.” She pulled the door wide. “Good night, Senator. Do not come back unless you can behave like a guest.”

The gilded door closed between them with a soft, heavy, final sound.

---

She stood on the inside of it for a long moment, her hand still raised where she had pushed it, while the household moved around her, clearing lamps, gathering cups, going through the nightly ritual of restoration. Festus appeared beside her, silent and solid, and after a moment Chloe came too, her mantle wrapped against the cold, her sharp face concerned.

“Phyllis says he was the last person you'd ever want at that door,” Chloe said.

“Phyllis is right, which is a habit of hers I find exhausting.” Antonia let her hand fall. Her voice was steady again. “He didn't know. He came in here tonight believing I walked away of my own free will, with my manumission silver in my hand. Can you imagine it? Fifteen years, and they told him I _chose_ it.”

“Perhaps he's lying.”

“No.” She said it with the certainty of stone. “That's the unbearable part. He isn't. He looked at me like a man seeing a ghost he'd been taught never to believe in. They lied to him as thoroughly as they used me.” She drew a breath. “It changes nothing.”

“It changes him.”

“He's a senator with a consulship in his hand and a wife on the Esquiline. Nothing changes for men like him. The world was built to spare them.”

Phyllis had come in from the garden wing with her ring of keys, and she stood now in the shadow of the colonnade, where she had been standing, Antonia suspected, for some minutes. The old woman's face was impossible to read, the way old stone is impossible to read until the lamp moves across it.

“You knew him,” Antonia said. It was not a question.

“I knew the house. I was a slave in it before I was ever yours, remember. The Aelii bought me at nineteen and sold me at thirty, and in between I scrubbed that kitchen floor and carried that boy's porridge.” Phyllis's voice was level, but her hands, folded at her waist, were tight. “He was a good child, as the children of that house went. Taught you your letters when he'd have been beaten if his tutor caught him at it. Used to give you half his figs.”

“I remember the figs.” Antonia's voice was dry. “I try not to remember the figs.”

“There's things I've never told you. About that year. The year they sold you.”

Antonia turned fully then. The household moved around them, slaves carrying out the last of the silver, and in the noise of it the two women might have been alone on an island. “Why now?”

“Because he walked through that door tonight, and I saw your face, and I'm sixty-two years old and my chest pains me in the cold, and there may not be a great many more right moments.” Phyllis met her eyes. “But not tonight. Tonight you need sleep more than you need the past. When the time comes, I'll tell you all of it. Every word. Every name. I owe you that much, and I've owed it a long while.”

Antonia studied her. In fifteen years Phyllis had never once broken a promise or failed to name a thing plainly, and Antonia had built half her empire on the reliability of this one woman.

“Tomorrow, then. After the morning accounts. And Phyllis — whatever it is, it won't change how I run this house.”

“Won't it?” said Phyllis softly, and went back to her keys, and would say no more.

Antonia turned from the door. “Double the watch tonight. And tomorrow I want every name on the supply contracts that Hector sent word of. If the Aelius name is anywhere near those tallies, I want to know before he does.”

Chloe studied her. “You're going to fight him.”

“I'm going to make sure he can't hurt me. There's a difference. The first is anger. The second is survival.” She walked away down the colonnade, and after a moment Chloe heard the sound of her chamber door closing, very softly, upstairs.

---

Celer was waiting at the corner of the Carinae, swaying gently beside the litter with a half-eaten honey cake in one hand, and insisted that his brother share the ride up the hill. The bearers set off through the narrow torchlit streets, where the taverns were still roaring and a pair of drunken sailors were singing a marching song off-key, and for a while the two men rode in silence.

Then Celer said, into the dark, “She's Greek, you know. The singer. Gods above, what a voice. I'd give a year's income to have that voice in my own house.”

“You haven't got a year's income,” said Marcus. “That's the subject I want.”

The drunken affability dimmed by a quarter. “Who says I haven't?”

“Perperna. He called it ‘that little subscription.' He looked at you as a man looks at property he has already paid for. Decimus, look at me.” The litter jolted over a rut. “What have you put your name to?”

“Nothing. Nothing that won't bear the light, I told you.” Celer rallied, reaching for the old charm and finding it slightly dented. “A group of us, some firms, supply contracts for the eastern army — grain, hides, transport. We subscribe, the firms do the work, the treasury pays, the profit comes round. Half the Senate has money in it. It isn't a crime to prosper, Marcus. Cato the Elder prospered.”

“Cato the Elder didn't borrow his subscriptions from Sextus Perperna.”

The silence that followed was answer enough. Celer stared out through the litter curtains at the passing torchlight, and when he spoke again his voice had lost all its music.

“He holds some paper of mine. That's all. A loan, properly sealed. The contracts will clear it — once the treasury pays the first instalment. Everyone's waiting on the treasury. You'll see when you audit the tallies; everything's in order, weights, seals, witnesses. By summer I'm square, and Perperna goes back to eating widows, and you and I never speak of it again.” He tried a laugh. “You see? Nothing for the great censor to trouble himself with. Now tell me about the mistress, since she looked at you as if she'd like to bury you in the garden. What was _that_?”

“A mistake,” said Marcus. “She mistook me for someone.”

“Did she? Lucky fellow. A woman like that, with a house like that — do you know what she's worth? They say she keeps a bank of her own now, lends to merchants, buys paper. A freedwoman. Rome's gone strange, brother. Our grandfathers would have had her flogged.”

“Our grandfathers,” said Marcus, “never had to balance her ledgers.”

He said nothing more, and Celer, taking silence for forgiveness, began to hum the Sappho tune and got it wrong, and so they climbed the Esquiline, the younger brother humming and the elder watching the street with the unblinking attention of a man who has begun to see, in every shadow, the shape of a trap.

On the Esquiline, Marcus dismissed the slaves and went down to the strong room himself, which he had not done in years.

It was a windowless vault off the atrium, smelling of cedar and dry stone, where the Aelii kept their silver, their title deeds, and the family archives in a row of cedar chests going back four generations. He lit a lamp, set it on the floor, and pulled out his father's private ledgers — the household books, not the public ones, the tallies of slaves bought and sold, rents collected, loans made. His hands were steady now, the way they had been in Germany before a battle.

He found it near the back of the volume for the year he had gone north to his tribunate. A line in his father's cramped hand, entered without explanation among the sale of three mules and a quantity of old wine:

_Anthe, vernula, sold to Dinis the dealer, nine hundred and forty sesterces._

No manumission. No merchant going south. No money taken freely. A sale, plain and cold, for nine hundred and forty sesterces, the price of a decent mule.

Beside it, in a different ink, in his mother's hand, were four words.

_Better for everyone concerned._

Marcus sat on the floor of the vault with the ledger open on his knees, and the lamp flame leaned in a draught he could not feel, and somewhere far above him in the great quiet house his wife slept, his children slept, his ancestors watched from their wax death-masks in the atrium, and every stone of the world he had built his life upon felt, for the first time, like a stone that might move.

His father had lied. His mother had known. And the girl in the kitchen, the quick laughing girl with the backwards R, had been sold like livestock while he was marching to the Rhine.

He closed the ledger very gently, as though it were a living thing he was afraid of waking, and sat in the dark a long time, holding the lamp, thinking about a gilded door and a woman's white-knuckled hand upon its frame.
