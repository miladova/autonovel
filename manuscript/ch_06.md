# VI. The Work

The first thing Harold saw when he came out of the officers' quarters was a man in a dress suit standing in the middle of the boat deck with a glass of whisky in his hand, laughing.

He would think about that man for the rest of his life. Not with contempt; he never managed contempt for him. The man was not drunk, or not very. He was laughing because another man had said something funny about the noise, and the two of them were standing in the dark in the cold with their collars up and their glasses in their hands, and behind them, forty feet away, the forward well deck was already beginning to go under.

"Get below and put your belts on," Harold said, and did not stop to see whether they did.

The steam went on roaring. It was coming out of the escape pipe aft of the fourth funnel with a noise like a train in a tunnel, and it had the whole after end of the ship in a fog of warm wet vapour, and men were shouting into it and being swallowed. Harold went forward along the starboard side and found Mr. Murdoch at number seven.

Murdoch had his hand up and his voice was going out flat and clear into the noise, and he was doing three things at once: telling the men on the falls to take the strain, telling the steward to get the women back from the rail, telling a man in a dinner jacket that he could not go. He had the face of a man on the last hole of a long game.

"Lowe. Get the covers off these boats. Then take the falls on five and seven and get them away. Where's the list?"

"There isn't one, sir."

"There's a list." Murdoch turned his head. "There's a list in the chart room, get it. I want the boat numbers and the capacity and the number of souls in each."

"There's no time for a list, sir."

Murdoch looked at him. In the light from the deck the man's eyes were perfectly steady.

"Then there's no time for a list," he said. "Get them away. Fill them from the deck below as well as this one; there's women on B deck who'll not come up. And Lowe—" he was already turning away "—fill them. Do you hear me? Fill them."

"Yes, sir."

He filled them as much as they would let him.

That was the thing. That was the whole of the thing, and it took him twenty years to be able to say it without his voice going. He filled them as much as they would let him.

At number five he put in a stewardess, and a woman with a baby, and two women who came up out of the crowd and climbed in without a word, and then he looked round for more and there were none. There was a rail with forty women behind it and he went along the rail saying, "Madam. Madam, will you come into the boat, please," and they looked at him and looked at the boat swinging out over the black water with two feet of fall under her and they said, "No."

Some of them laughed. He was to remember that: three women at the rail of the *Titanic* at twenty past midnight, laughing at him, kindly, the way you laugh at a nervous host.

"It's all right, sir. We'll go in the morning."

"There's no morning, madam."

"Oh, don't be silly."

Number five went away with forty-one in her against a capacity of sixty-five, and Pitman went with her, and Pitman was a man of thirty-eight with a wife and two children and had been at sea since he was eighteen, and he went because Murdoch told him to, and Harold watched him go down the falls with his hand on the tiller and his face turned up, and thought: *he thinks he's coming back.*

Number seven went with twenty-seven. Twenty-seven. He counted them himself, standing at the rail with the falls in his hands, and he counted again because he did not believe it, and there were twenty-seven, and among them three men who had got in on the far side when nobody was looking, and one of them was laughing.

"Open the gangway doors," a voice said at his elbow. It was a passenger, a big man, grey-faced, holding his hat. "You must open the gangway doors, officer. There are hundreds of people below who don't know what's happening. Open the doors and they can come up to the boats."

The gangway doors. It was not a bad idea. It was the first sensible thing anybody had said to him all night, and he turned it over in his mind for a second, and he thought: *there's no man to spare to work them, and no man to stand at them, and no officer to keep order at the other side.* He said, "I'll pass it up," which was a way of saying no, and the man said, "Will you?" and Harold said, "I will," and he did, later, or he believed he did, and nothing was ever done about it, and he would hear that man's voice in his head at three o'clock in the morning for the rest of his life.

---

At number one he lost his temper.

Number one was the emergency cutter on the starboard side forward, a wooden boat of twenty-five souls, and by the time he got to her she had been swung out and there were six people in her and one of them was Sir Cosmo Duff Gordon and another was Lady Duff Gordon and another was a woman in a kimono with a Pekingese under her arm, and a fireman was standing by the falls and there was nobody else.

"Who's in charge of this boat?"

"I am," said a man in a bowler hat, and Harold looked at him, at the bowler hat and the white flannels and the face of a man who had never in his life pulled an oar.

"You're not."

"I beg your pardon."

"You're not in charge of her, sir, you're in her, and there's a difference." Harold got his foot on the gunwale. "Where's your crew? Have you got a seaman?"

"He has." The man in the bowler hat pointed at the fireman. "He's a stoker."

"I don't care if he's the Archbishop of Canterbury, has he ever rowed a boat?" Harold turned to the fireman. "Have you?"

"I've rowed a bit, sir."

"How much is a bit?"

"Boat's crew on the *Oceanic*, sir, when I was a lad."

"Then you'll do." He was aware of his own voice going up and he could not stop it. "Get in. Get in the bow and take an oar and if anybody in this boat tells you to do a thing you don't do it unless they've a seaman's ticket in their pocket. D'you hear me?"

"Sir."

"And you," he said to the man in the bowler hat, "you'll take the other oar and you'll do as you're told by him, and if any of you in this boat has a complaint about it you can make it to the Board of Trade in the morning."

There was a silence. Somebody in the boat said something under their breath. Harold did not care. He was past caring about it in a way that frightened him a little, even then, even standing there. He had six people in a boat for twenty-five and a deck full of people behind him who would not come and a ship that was going down by the head at a rate he could now see with his own eyes, and something in him had snapped its collar and was running.

He walked away from number one without waiting to see her go.

---

It was when he was going aft along the starboard side that he heard it.

A woman's voice, twenty feet behind him, saying something to a steward. He could not hear the words. It was the shape of the voice, the rise in it, the way it went up on the last word like a question at the end of a statement, and it was a voice out of the wall behind the cemetery in Barmouth and out of the vestry at a concert and out of the flat black water off the boat deck five hours ago, and he turned round so fast he hit his shoulder on a davit.

There was a crowd. There were a hundred people between him and the top of the aft staircase: stewards, women with rugs, a man on his knees being sick over the rail, a group of firemen coming up the ladder from the well deck with their gear in their hands. He could see, over all of it, a grey coat.

He went three steps towards it and then he stopped, because Murdoch was at number nine and had his hand up and was calling his name.

"Lowe! Lowe, get these boats away. Nine and eleven. Get them away."

He looked back at the grey coat. It was moving away from him, forward, into the crowd, and he could not see the hat.

"Yes, sir," he said.

He got nine away. He got eleven away. He filled them as much as they would let him, and at eleven a woman with a fur coat refused three times and then climbed in and then climbed out again, and the boat went with sixty-three in her, which was a good boat, and he felt a moment's absurd pride in it, and then a wash of shame, because filling a boat is not an achievement; it is what a man does with a bucket.

It was the water that had changed. He noticed it while the falls were running on eleven: the water was not ten feet below the boat deck any more, it was six, and when she rolled she was rolling with the water on her, and the boats swinging out over her had a whole new angle to them and the men on the falls had to hold her hard against the ship's side to keep the sea from taking the boat.

And she was down by the head with her propellers out of the water, and every now and then, when she rolled, the whole ship rang like a bell from a propeller coming out and going under.

That was the moment Harold Lowe became certain. There had been an hour of not being certain. Now there was a great many lights still burning and there were people laughing two decks up and there was a band playing, and there was no longer any doubt in him anywhere.

He went across to the port side. There was nothing left for him on the starboard.

---

The port side was a different ship.

He came round the forward end of the superstructure and the first thing he saw was that the boats were going away, and the second thing was that they were going away half full, and the third was that the officer in charge of them intended to keep going that way.

Lightoller had his back to the bridge and his arm out over the rail and his voice going out into the roar of steam. He was not shouting, particularly. He was speaking in a voice that carried, and what he was saying was: "Women and children. No men. Women and children. Stand back, sir."

There were men in that crowd who had been told to stand back four times. There was a man holding his wife's hand, who would not be separated from her, and there was a woman with two children and a husband, and Harold watched the husband put his hands on his wife's shoulders and say something to her and then step back into the crowd, and the wife go down the falls without a sound, and it took three seconds and it was the bravest thing he saw that night.

Moody was at fourteen with a lantern. The Sixth Officer had his cap on straight and his boots on and his face, in the lantern light, was the face of a boy of twenty-four who has been given a job at last and intends to do it perfectly. He looked up and saw Harold and something in his shoulders let go.

"Thank God," he said. "Will you take her? Sixteen's away, and fourteen's half loaded and there's no officer for her, and Mr. Lightoller wants an officer in each of these boats back here and there's none to spare."

"Get in her, then."

"I'm not going in her. I'm going forward to the collapsibles."

"Moody—"

"Mr. Lowe, I've got the falls and the crew and I know what I'm doing, and you've been on this deck for an hour and a half and you've not stopped moving." Moody said it quite kindly. "Get in the boat. She's yours."

It was the last conversation Harold Lowe ever had with James Paul Moody.

"All right," he said. "Get the women down to me. And get me some men—I want four men in this boat that can pull, and I want them now, and I don't want volunteers, I want men who've been told."

He climbed into fourteen.

---

The boat was crowded with women and children and their belongings. A woman had a box on her knees. A child of about six was wedged under a thwart and would not come out. There was a man in the stern sheets—a passenger, a big fellow of fifty in a tweed coat—who had the tiller, and Harold said, "Give me that," and took it, and the man said, "I'm only trying to help," and Harold said, "You can help by sitting still and doing what I say, sir, and I'll thank you not to argue."

"Don't you speak to me—"

"Sir." Harold put his hand up. "Not tonight. I'm not going to argue with you tonight."

They filled her from the deck below and from the deck above, and it took a long time, and the whole time the ship was going down.

That was the thing nobody understood who had not been there: it took a long time, and it was the longest hour of his life, and it was made of small operations. Get the women to the rail. Get the child out from under the thwart. Take the box off the woman's knees and give it to a steward and tell her it will come later. Hold the boat in against the ship's side with an oar because the swell was beginning to come up the side of her now and the falls were slack and then snatching. Say "steady" forty times. Say "there's room, madam, there's plenty of room, come along."

And all the time, at the back of it, under the noise of the steam and the shouting and the crying of a child and the band—the band had stopped some time ago, he did not know when—at the back of it, the ship was making a new sound. It was a low grinding, groaning sound, and it was not the engines. It was the whole fabric of her, twelve decks of steel and wood and a hundred thousand rivets, being asked to hold a shape she had been built not to hold.

"Mr. Lowe." A seaman was at his elbow. "Mr. Lowe, they're coming over the rail aft of us. The men off the lower decks. They've seen the boat and they're coming across the well deck."

He turned.

They were coming. A dozen of them, more, dark shapes moving along the ship's side on the lower decks, and the water was up to the well deck now and they were wading, and the first of them had his hands on the rail above the boat.

"Get back," Harold shouted.

They did not get back.

He had a revolver in his pocket. He had put it in his pocket at a quarter to twelve and had not thought about it since, and now it was in his hand without his having decided anything about it, and he fired over their heads, into the dark, once, and then again, and then a third time at the second deck as the boat went down past it, and each shot made a flat crack that the night swallowed at once, and he heard himself shouting, "Get back, you fools, get back or I'll shoot you down like dogs."

They got back.

The boat went down past the third deck and there was nobody at the rail, and the water came up to meet them, and the people in the boat had gone silent.

Harold put the revolver back in his pocket. His hand was shaking, and he looked at it with a kind of detached interest, and then he looked up, and there, forty feet above him, at a rail on the flooded A deck, standing perfectly still with her hands on the rail, was a woman in a grey coat.

He knew her. It was too far, and there was no light, and there were two hundred people on the decks above her, but he knew her, and he could not have said how, and it was the way she stood: on her own, still, not hurrying, with her shoulders square, the way she had stood at the after rail of the boat deck at half past eight and looked at the stars.

He could not go back up. There was no way back up. The falls were running and the boat was in the water, and behind her the sea was coming across the deck, and she was standing there looking down.

He shouted her name.

He shouted it twice and the wind and the steam took it and the people in the boat turned their faces up to the ship, thinking he was shouting at someone below, and then the boat swung, and the rail, and the grey coat, and the whole of the *Titanic*'s side went behind him and there was nothing over his shoulder but black water and the lights of a ship dying.

"Sit down," he said to the boat. "Sit down, all of you. Ship your oars. Get her head round. Pull."

---

He got them a hundred and fifty yards off, and then two hundred, because the suction of her when she went would take a boat like fourteen down with her and there was no arguing with that.

She was still going, and she had begun to go faster, and she was going at an angle now that made the whole of her port side a wall of lights at an unnatural slant, and the lights were still burning. That was the thing that undid the people in the boat. She had been sinking for an hour and a half and the lights were still on and the windows were still warm, and a ship with her lights on is not a ship that is going to die.

A woman behind him said, "It's all right. She's not going. Look at her, she's not going."

"Ma'am," Harold said, "she's going."

"You don't know—"

"I know. Be quiet now, please. Be quiet and look at her, because in a minute she'll be gone and you'll want to have seen it."

It was an hour and a half of a night that he had spent going from one thing to the next as fast as a man can move, and now there was nothing to do but hold the tiller and look.

The band had stopped. The steam had stopped—the roar of it had cut out at some point, and in the silence the ship was making the other sound, the grinding, and it went on and on and it got louder, and then the lights went out.

Not all at once. Forward first, the bow going black and the blackness running aft along the decks the way a fuse runs, and the stern still lit, a great blaze of light up in the air at an angle, and then a shock through the water that Harold felt in the tiller, and the stern came up out of the sea.

It came up and it kept coming up, and it was all lit, all of it, every window, and there were people on it. He could see them. There were hundreds of people on the stern of the *Titanic*, on the well deck and the poop, and they were climbing, and the great thing went up and up and stood there, two hundred feet of it, black and gold against the stars, and it stayed.

It stayed for a long time. He would say afterwards, at the inquiry, that it was a quarter of an hour, and a man in a wig would tell him that could not be, and he would say, "Then I am mistaken, my Lord," and hold to it anyway. It stood there, roaring. The noise of it was the worst thing he ever heard: not a crash, not an explosion, but a long tearing, grinding, boiling roar, and underneath it, very faint, a sound like the crying of birds.

Then it went.

It went down and the water came over it, and the last thing he saw of her was the stern going under with the flagstaff still up, and then there was nothing there at all, and the sea was flat and grey and the stars were on it, and the whole of the middle of the night was empty.

The people in the boat were screaming. Some of them. Others were praying, and one man was saying over and over, "My God, my God, my God," in a conversational voice.

"Now," Harold said.

He said it to himself. He had not meant to say it out loud.

"Now what?" said the woman behind him.

And then they heard it. All of them, at once, from every part of the boat, the way you hear a thing that everybody has been waiting for: a sound coming across the water from the place where the ship had been.

It was not screaming, exactly. It was a long, high, unbroken wall of sound, made of hundreds of voices, and it rose and it fell and it went on, and it had no shape to it at all. It was the sound of a great many people in cold water.

Harold Lowe sat in the stern of number fourteen with his hand on the tiller and listened to it, and did not move.
