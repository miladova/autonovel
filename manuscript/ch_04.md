# IV. The Flat Calm

Sunday began with the clocks going back and a bell in the wrong place.

They put the ship's clocks back forty-seven minutes that night—the *Titanic* ran on a shifting noon, so that every day the clocks were set to the sun, which meant that every night the watches were a little longer or a little shorter than the last, and the men who had to stand them complained about it and would not have had it otherwise. Harold came off at eight bells into a dark that had a bite in it he had not felt before on this voyage, and he went down to the mess and ate cold beef and bread and drank a cup of tea with his cap still on, and read the printed sheet that was pinned by the door.

*Ice.* It was not a word he had expected to see in the first week of a westbound crossing in April, though it was not a word he was surprised by either. The Grand Banks in April is a place where a ship goes with her eyes open. He had been in ice before, off the cape, in a barque with a crew of nine and no light, and had spent six hours on the forecastle head in a snow squall listening for the sea to change its note against a berg. You hear it before you see it, sometimes. Sometimes you hear nothing.

The notice was a small thing, a chit with a position on it and a hand-drawn line, and a warning, and Harold read it twice and then did the sum in his head, the way he did sums.

He had the six to eight. The position on the chit was somewhere ahead of them and to the south, and when he had worked it through he made it nine or ten that night, and Murdoch would have the bridge then, and it would not be his concern. He worked it out mentally and satisfied himself that the ship would not be in the ice during his watch, and he pinned the thing back up and forgot about it. He thought afterwards that this was what everyone did, all of them, from the captain down: each man looked at the ice and worked out whether it was his problem, and every one of them was right, and it made not the slightest difference in the end.

---

The service was at half past ten in the first class dining saloon.

The captain read it himself. That was the custom on the White Star ships on a Sunday and it was also, Harold thought, standing at the back with three other officers and their caps under their arms, good management. It put the master of the ship in front of the first class passengers as a man in a black coat with a prayer book, reading the lesson in a broad Staffordshire voice, rather than as a rumour who ate in his own cabin.

Smith read well. He was a big grey-bearded man of sixty-two, near the end of forty years at sea, with the face of a country doctor and a manner that had been polished by forty years of carrying other people's nerves, and he read the bit from Job about the sea and said a few words afterwards about being thankful for a fair passage, and the whole thing took forty minutes and improved everybody.

Harold sang the hymns from memory without moving his lips much, which was his habit, and looked at the back of the room. He was not looking for her. He had told himself he was not looking for her. He found her four rows from the front on the port side, in a dark blue coat and a hat with no feather, sitting very straight, not singing, and it came to him with a small cold shock that she had not sung at the service yesterday either.

He had grown up in a chapel where a woman who could sing was expected to sing, and a woman who could not was expected to sing anyway, and Arwen Prys had had a voice that made the back of your neck go tight, and had been made to use it every Sunday of her childhood by a mother who taught music in a front room. He had heard her sing *Calon Lân* at a concert in the vestry in 1896 and had walked home afterwards by himself in the rain to think about it.

So she had stopped. Somewhere in fifteen years she had put it down, the way she had put down the half-sovereign.

---

He was free until six. He walked the ship for an hour because he could not sit.

Round the boat deck twice, and then down to the third class, because the third class was the part of a ship an officer ought to know and the part of a ship the first class never saw, and Harold had a private and unreasoning loyalty to the steerage of every vessel he had ever sailed in, on the grounds that he had come out of it.

It was a Sunday afternoon and the third class were making the best of it. There was a general room with long tables and a piano going and a great many people in their Sunday clothes who had come up from the deep decks at eight in the morning and would not go down again until eleven at night, because a berth with three other men in it and no air is not a place a man stays in when he can be in the daylight. Two girls were dancing together in the middle of the floor, badly. A baby was asleep on four chairs pushed together. There was a smell of cabbage and wet wool and cheap tobacco, and it was the smell of every ship he had ever been in, and it was, oddly, the smell of home.

A man by the door said, in Welsh, "You're Welsh."

Harold turned round. He was a small dark man of forty-odd in a collarless shirt and a good waistcoat, and he had the accent of the quarry country.

"Barmouth."

"Llanberis." They shook hands, which was absurd and entirely correct. "You're an officer."

"Fifth."

"Fifth." The man nodded, satisfied that this was as it should be. "And they've put the Welsh on the boats. Good. It's a boy's game, is it, this ship? All the brass?"

"It's a good ship."

"It's a very good ship." He said it the way the chapel said things, with the reservation folded inside the compliment. Then he said, "Do you know the hymn *Calon Lân*?"

And Harold laughed out loud, in the middle of the third class general room, on the fourteenth of April 1912.

---

At four he went on watch and stood at the after end of the bridge with the sixth officer and the quartermaster and the ship going west at twenty-two knots through water that had gone flat.

He had never seen it like that. He would talk about it afterwards, at the inquiries, at Barmouth, at odd times for the rest of his life, and he would always come back to the same thing: there was no sea. Not a swell, not a ruffle, not the small chop that even the calmest day will give you. It lay like a plate of grey glass and the ship went through it without leaving a proper wake, and the wind had dropped to nothing, and it turned bitter, cold enough by six o'clock for the men on the look-out to be relieved every half hour.

The other thing about a night like that—and he knew it in his bones, he had known it since he was fifteen and had spent nights on a schooner's deck wishing he was dead—is that it takes the sound away. A sea with a swell on it makes a noise against a ship. It hisses and slaps and mutters. Flat water is silent. A ship going twenty-two knots through flat water makes a noise of her own, engines and fans and the rush of her bow wave, and that noise covers everything else, and there is nothing underneath it, no small voice of the sea to tell you that something is near.

He said something of the sort to Moody, who was on the bridge waiting to take over at six.

"You don't want a wind," Moody said.

"You want a wind."

"I'd not have said so."

"You're young," Harold said. "A wind is cold and it's wet and it's a nuisance and it will tell you where the ice is. Flat water tells you nothing at all, and if there's a berg in it, it's the same colour as the sky, and it'll be on you before a man in the crow's nest has time to get his whistle up."

Moody considered this in the way that he considered things, which was thoroughly and slowly, and said, "Well, there'll be no moon either."

"There'll be stars."

"There's that."

The stars came out at seven and they were extraordinary. There was no moon at all, and the night was as black as a night can be, and in that blackness the stars stood out and down in a way Harold had only ever seen in the tropics. The whole sky was full of them. You could see the Milky Way like a road. The sea took no notice of them and stayed grey.

He went off watch at eight and stood at the rail of the boat deck and looked at it for a while, and he was still standing there at twenty-five minutes past eight when he heard her shoes on the deck behind him.

---

She had come up with a rug over her arm and her collar turned up and a hood over her hat, and she had the look of a woman who has made a decision and is going to go through with it whatever the weather does.

"I had some difficulty finding you," she said. "A steward told me the officers' part of the deck was for officers. I said I was looking for a doctor."

"Is there a doctor up here?"

"No. That is why I said it." She stood beside him and looked at the stars and did not say anything for a while. "Oh," she said. "Oh, look at that."

"It's a good night for it."

"It's the most beautiful thing I have ever seen in my life," she said, "and I am perfectly frozen."

"Put the rug round you."

She put the rug round her. He stood with his hands in his pockets and his shoulders up to his ears and the cold coming through the soles of his boots, and they looked at the sky together for a long time, and it was one of those moments which are nothing and which he would find later on had been laid down in him like a photograph.

"The third class have a piano," he said. "Do you know the hymn *Calon Lân*?"

Her face, in the starlight, changed.

"Of course I know it."

"There's a man from Llanberis down there," Harold said, "who asked me if I knew it, and I said I did, and he said, well then. As though that settled something." He took his hand out of his pocket and held it out. "I've something for you. It's a poor thing and I'd not have given it to you at fifteen, but I'm not fifteen, and it's what I've got."

It was a whistle.

Not a tin one off a stall. It had been a length of half-inch copper pipe that had no business surviving in an engineer's rag bag, and he had cut it, and filed the lip in, and stopped the far end with a plug of wood, and it had taken him most of two hours at the bench in the lamp room with the third engineer's tools and the third engineer's amused permission, and it made a note that was not quite a note.

She turned it over. "You made this."

"I did."

"Today."

"Over the last two days. When I should have been asleep." He said it as though it were a fault, and she made a small sound that was not quite a laugh.

He said, "There's not much of a tune in it. It's not a proper whistle. But it'll do this." And he took it back, and put it to his lips, and played the first eight bars of a tune that a woman in a front room in Barmouth had taught to a girl in 1889, and the note was thin and slightly flat at the top of the octave and it went out over the flat black water and came back off nothing at all.

He stopped.

"Sing it," he said.

"Harold."

"Sing the thing. There's nobody up here but the two of us and a man in the crow's nest a hundred yards off, and the third class have a piano, and it's Sunday, and I've made you a whistle." He said it roughly, because he was afraid of being gentle. "Sing it, mun. For the man from Llanberis."

And she sang.

She sang the first verse at half voice, the way you sing in a room where somebody is asleep upstairs, and the Welsh of it came out of her with the *ll* sound in it that no English voice ever makes, and it was a message in a language that only the two of them and a man from Llanberis and a quarter of a million other people had any right to understand. *(Calon lân yn llawn daioni, / Tecach yw na'r lili dlos.)* Then she stopped and put her hand over her mouth and said, "I have not sung in nine years."

"Why not?"

"Because I married a man who does not like music," she said.

He did not answer that. He wrapped the whistle in a bit of clean rag from his pocket and put it in her hand and closed her fingers over it.

---

They stayed an hour. He could not afterwards account for how it went, except in pieces.

She told him about Liverpool: the house on Gaerwen Road, the green drawing room, the four women in the parish who had decided she was a cold woman because she did not come to the sewing circle, the endless small business of being a wife in a city where mistrust was a form of good manners. She had gone to the docks once a week to look at the ships. She had never told anyone that. She would stand at the north end of the landing stage where the wind came off the river and look at the funnels with their colours on them and go home again.

She told him about the child. Not much. Three sentences, and then she said, "I will not say any more about it, and I would be obliged if you did not say anything comforting."

"I don't know anything comforting."

"I know. It's why I told you."

He told her about the coast: Grand Bassam and Lagos and Calabar, the fever, the old Dane who had died at Grand Bassam and whose name he could not now remember, which he found shameful. He told her about his father's death, and about the gold watch, and about the fact that he was now, at twenty-nine, the eldest son of a dead man and the man whose name people said when they said the family. He told her about the certificate in the tin box and how he had held it in his hands at Liverpool and thought, *and now what,* and about how he had been in three collisions and one fire and had never once been afraid, and that he did not think that was courage, he thought it was a defect.

"You're not afraid now," she said.

"I'm afraid now," he said.

She looked at him.

"It's nothing to do with—" He stopped. "It's New York. I'm not a man who knows how to be a passenger. On this ship I know where everything is and I know what I'm for, and on Thursday I step off and I am a man in a blue coat on a pier, and you will go to your house on the street with the other curtains, and I'll go to a boarding house off West Street and find out what a man with a fifth officer's pay does in a city for a month."

"What will you do?"

"Come back," he said. "On the first White Star boat that'll have me. They owe me the passage home and I mean to take it."

"And then?"

"And then the *Tropic*, or something like her, down the coast, and one day a ship of my own." He said it and heard it and found that it sounded thinner than it had on the twelfth.

She was quiet. Then she said, "I could write to the *Oceanic*."

"Don't."

"Don't?"

"Don't write to the ship." He turned to face her properly. "If you write to me on the *Oceanic* something a steward gives me over a rail, that's the end of it, and I'll stand on the deck of that ship and read your letter and I'll be a man going back to the coast with a piece of paper in his pocket. If it's to be nothing, let it be nothing, and I'll keep what I've had. But don't write to me a letter to be read at a rail."

"Then what?"

"Then I'll come to Barmouth in June," he said, "if my sister's kept the room. And I'll walk up to the shop, and I'll stand at that door, and I'll not be fifteen and I'll not be turned off it this time, and if you're there—" He stopped. "If you're there, Arwen."

"That's not a plan. It's a hope with a date on it."

"It's the only kind of plan I've ever had," he said, "and I'll thank you not to say so in that voice."

She laughed then, properly, and the sound of it went out over the water, and a long way up the deck a seaman on the look-out turned to see what it was.

---

She went in at half past nine. She was shaking with cold and her eyes were bright and her nose was red, and she took his hand, briefly, at the doorway, with her glove on—the only time they touched, the whole night, and afterwards he could not decide whether he was sorry or not.

"Half past nine," she said. "That is putting me to bed like a child."

"It's the cold."

"It is not the cold." She got the door open. Then she said, without turning round, "The whistle is in my pocket. If I am searched at New York I shall be in difficulties."

"There's worse contraband."

"Yes," she said, "there is," and went in.

---

He was not on watch again until four in the morning. He went to his cabin at ten and lay down in his clothes, which he had not done since he was a boy, and looked at the deckhead.

The ship was so steady that she seemed to be still. There was no creak anywhere in the cabin, no movement in the curtain of the bunk, nothing but the fans and the far small hammer of the engines and the faint, faint ring of a glass in the washstand where the vibration came up through the steel.

He had said a great deal up there on the deck. He did not regret it. That was the strange thing: he lay there and went over it and there was nothing he wanted to take back, not the whistle, not the singing, not the thing he had said about the letter and the door. He had been a boy who would not say a thing plainly because he was afraid of being told no. He had said it plainly tonight, and had been told neither yes nor no, and had found that he could live with that.

He thought: *I'll be at Barmouth in June.*

He thought: *Ada will have a fit. She's a chapel woman now, and she'll have something to say about a married woman, and she'll say it in Welsh so that it sounds like scripture, and then she'll put the kettle on and tell me to go up and see to the roof.*

He thought: *and if the husband's there, I'll take it. Whatever he is. He's been kind to her, and she said so, and she's not a woman who says a thing that isn't true.*

He thought: *I'll be forty before I have a command of my own.*

He thought: *that doesn't matter so much now.*

At some point he slept. The last thing he heard was the bell striking ten somewhere above his head, a long way off, two bells of the first watch, the ship's own small voice telling the time.

---

He woke because the ship had changed her note.

It was not noise that woke him. It was the opposite. He came up out of sleep with his heart going and lay still and listened, and the engines were still there, still thudding, and yet the whole ship had gone quiet in some way he could not have explained to anybody who had not spent their life on one: the vibration in the washstand glass had changed. The fans went on turning. A door banged somewhere forward.

He looked at his watch. He could not see it; he got up and struck a match and looked at it and it was twelve minutes to twelve.

He sat on the edge of the bunk with the match burning and thought: *no.*

A ship does not stop from twenty-two knots in a breath. Her engines come off her over a couple of minutes, and while they are coming off she goes on through the water and the whole of her changes her mind about everything, and here she was, with the engines still turning, and stopped.

He put his boots on.

Then the steam went.

It came out of the escape pipes in a roar like the end of the world. He had heard it once before, in the *Tropic*, when the main injection had failed and they had lost the condenser for six minutes and every man aboard had looked at the sky and made his peace with it in his own way. It was a scream of forty tons of pressure going up into the night, and it filled the ship from the engine room to the crow's nest, and Harold stood in his cabin with one boot on and said, out loud, in Welsh, "God help us, what has she done."

He got the boot on. He took his revolver out of the drawer, and it was in his hand before he had thought about it, and then he opened the door and went out into the passage, and the steam whistle went on and on above him, and he could hear the ship's people running—men's feet on the boat deck overhead, a woman's voice asking a question, another door banging, the bell of the engine room telegraph ringing and ringing and ringing, as though somebody far below were hammering on a locked door.
