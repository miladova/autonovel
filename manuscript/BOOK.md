# I. The Ocean Greyhound

The ship had been at sea two days before Arwen saw the sea.

That was not quite true. She had seen it from Cherbourg, a black chop of water between the tender and the hull, and she had seen it from the aft end of the promenade deck where the wake went out from under the stern like a long white road being unwound. But the sea itself, the thing her father had called *the old woman*, was kept from her by glass. First class walked behind glass on this ship. The windows along the enclosed promenade were taller than a man and polished twice a day, and beyond them the Atlantic went by like a wall in a moving picture show, entirely silent.

She had not slept. Three nights now, and she had given up pretending about it. At half past four she put on her coat, and her gloves, and let herself out of the cabin with the care of a woman who has spent nine years not waking a sleeping man.

The corridor smelled of new carpet and of the varnish that was still, two days out, drying in the corners. Everything on the ship smelled new. It was the strangest thing about her: not the size, not the staircases, not the electric lights burning in the middle of the day, but the newness, the way the whole vessel was like a house whose owners had not yet come. The brass was too bright. The linoleum had no worn path down its middle. You could put your hand on a rail and feel that nobody had ever put a hand there before.

A steward passed her, a thin man of fifty with the face of someone who had been awake since three. He stopped and asked whether madam required anything.

"Nothing, thank you. Is it permitted to go out?"

"The wind's cruel, madam. There's the lounge, if you'd like the fire."

"I'd like the air."

He opened the door for her, and the cold came in at once, not like weather but like a hand. She stepped out onto the boat deck and the door shut behind her and the ship's voice changed. Inside, she had been hearing it through a wall: the engines a long way down, the fans, the string quartet three rooms off. Out here the ship was enormous and loud and cheerful, the wind going over the davits with a noise like a rope being pulled through a fist, the sea hissing along the plates below, and somewhere aft the whistle of the engine room telegraph being tested, one flat note and then another.

She went to the rail. Two days out of Southampton, and the rail was already salted white.

The sky was not yet grey. It was that older colour that comes before grey, and low down in the east there was a pale seam where the light was going to come from. She held on with both hands and looked at the water until her eyes adjusted and she could see that it was not flat at all, that it was moving in long hills, and that the ship went through them with an ease that was somehow worse than labour. She did not pitch. She did not labour. She took twenty-two knots of ocean the way a woman takes a comb through her hair.

Arwen had been at sea four times before, all of them on the Irish Sea, all of them in weather. She had been sick on every one. She had a theory that the great ships were not really ships and that people who loved them were in love with something else: with the idea of a thing that could not be argued with.

"You'll freeze, Mrs. Meredith."

Louisa Penhallow came along the deck with a man's ulster over her nightgown and her hair in a net, and the sight of her was so unexpected and so entirely without vanity that Arwen laughed out loud, which she had not done in some weeks.

"I couldn't lie there," Mrs. Penhallow said. "The beds are too good. It's like sleeping in a shop window. Are you a bad sailor, or a bad sleeper?"

"Neither. A bad wife, possibly."

It was out before she had thought about it. The wind took it and carried it aft, and Mrs. Penhallow had the grace to treat it as a remark about the weather.

"Come down at seven," she said. "I'll have them put us at a small table. I cannot face another breakfast watching Mr. Thorne eat a kidney."

---

At seven the dining saloon was a third full and the stewards moved through it with the particular hush of men serving people who have not yet decided what kind of day they intend to have. Mrs. Penhallow had got them a table by the after bulkhead, away from the windows, which she said was the only place on the ship where you could hear yourself.

"You'll want the kedgeree," she said. "The kitchen's better than the dining room. It's true of every ship and every house."

Arwen took the kedgeree. She was not hungry, but she had learned, in nine years of marriage, that not eating is a kind of statement, and she was not ready to make statements.

Mrs. Penhallow ate with her elbows near the table and her newspaper folded flat under the salt. The *Atlantic Daily Bulletin* carried, that morning, the shipping news, a report of a fire in a Chicago hotel, the movements of the ship's clocks, and a paragraph about the vessel herself, in which she was described as the largest moving object ever made by the hand of man.

"They always say that," Mrs. Penhallow said. "They said it about the *Mauretania*. My husband took me across on her and I was sick in a manner I would not describe to you. He said it was because she was fast. He said you can't have fast and smooth, it's against nature. Then he died and I came over on the *Carmania*, which is slow and also not smooth, so I don't know what to think. Do you play cards, Mrs. Meredith?"

"Badly."

"Good. So do I. There's a bridge four at eleven and a woman in it who counts. You know the sort. She counts and then she tells you what she counted."

Arwen turned her cup. The coffee was excellent; the butter was cut into curls; a boy went past with a tray of rolls he had evidently been told to offer twice. Everything in the room had been arranged by somebody who had thought about it for a long time. Somewhere below, a hundred and sixty firemen were shovelling coal into furnaces so that she could have butter curls at seven in the morning, and none of them would ever come up these stairs.

She thought of her father, who had sold marline and pitch and lamp oil to men who went to sea, and who had stood at their door in his good coat on a Sunday and said that no daughter of his would be walking out with a Lowe.

"And you?" Mrs. Penhallow said. "A bad sleeper. Does your husband sleep well?"

"Extremely."

"There you are, then."

---

She wrote to him after breakfast, in the writing room, on the ship's paper, which had the ship's name on it in a blue that was almost black.

*My dear Iestyn,* she wrote. And then sat with the pen up for a long time.

The writing room had a fire and four men in it, all of them writing, all of them with the same furrow between the eyes. It occurred to her that a ship at sea is a place where people write letters they would not say aloud, and that the postbag of the *Titanic* would have been, if anyone had opened it, the most honest document in the world. She thought about that for a while, comfortably, because it kept her from thinking about the letter.

*My dear Iestyn. We are two days out and the weather has been fine. There is a great deal of wind but the ship does not move. You would like that. Everyone is very kind to me. Mrs. Penhallow, of Boston, has adopted me; she is sixty-four and says whatever comes into her head, which is restful.*

She stopped. Below that sentence there was nothing she could put that would not be a lie, and she had made a rule, years ago, that she would not put lies in writing. It was a small rule and it had kept her alive.

She folded the page in half and put it in her book and looked out of the window at the sea, which was being kept from her again, this time by the writing room's glass.

Two tables over, a man was writing a letter in a hand that went across the page like a fence. Mr. Basil Thorne, who had eaten the kidney. He had told her at dinner the night before that he dealt in pictures, that he had once sold a small Corot to a man who kept it in a bathroom, and that he was going to New York because a picture dealer who does not go to New York is a man who has decided to grow old in one place. He had said it as though it were a joke and then looked at her to see whether she had laughed.

She had. It was easier.

---

She spent the middle of the day in the lounge, where the piano was, and did not play it. She had not touched a piano in public since she was nineteen. Her mother had taught her, and taught her well, and then her mother had gone on teaching other people's daughters in a front room in Barmouth until her hands would not do it any more, and Arwen had married a man who had a piano in the drawing room in Liverpool that was tuned twice a year and played never.

At four she could not sit still. She went up.

The boat deck in the afternoon was a different country from the boat deck at dawn. There were people on it, in rugs, in steamer chairs, being energetic. Two boys were walking a small dog. A woman in a sable coat was reading a novel with her finger following the line. The wind had dropped to something merely cold, and the light had come up out of the east at last and lay flat and yellow along the water, so that the sea looked less like a wall and more like a field.

Arwen walked forward, past the gymnasium, where a man in shirtsleeves was being bucked gently on an electric horse while a young instructor in a white jacket spoke encouragingly. Past the entrance to the Grand Staircase, where a steward was polishing the brass plate of the lift. Past the first of the lifeboats, which were stacked in their davits with the canvas laced over them, and which she had looked at on the first day with the idle approval of a woman approving the fire buckets in a hotel.

Four officers stood by the second boat, and she would have gone past them, and she did go past them, and then one of them said something and the wind brought it to her.

"—she's not on the falls, mun, she's on the gripes. Look at it. You'll have her chafing by Tuesday."

It was not the words. Any sailor might have said them. It was the way the sentence bent in the middle, that small downward turn at *mun*, that she had heard ten thousand times at the harbour in Barmouth and not once in nine years in Liverpool, and it stopped her on the deck as surely as a hand on her arm.

She turned around.

He was standing with his back to her, one hand on the davit, the other holding a length of rope he was not looking at. A blue serge jacket with a narrow strip of gold lace on the cuff. A cap pushed back. He was not tall. She remembered that he had never been tall, and that it had been a grievance to him, and that he had grown in the way that short men grow, sideways and hard.

"Mr. Lowe," one of the others said, and she heard the name and something in her chest turned over and lay still.

The fifth officer of the *Titanic* turned around, and saw a woman in a grey coat and a hat with a pheasant's feather in it, and looked at her with the flat assessing courtesy of a man who has been interrupted at work. His face had weathered into something she did not know. The boy had had a soft mouth and a great deal of black hair. This man had a face like a fist that had learned to open.

"Yes, madam?" he said. "Can I help you?"

She had rehearsed nothing, in fifteen years, because she had never thought there would be anything to rehearse for.

"Harold," she said.

Nothing happened in his face. That was the thing she would remember afterwards: that for three full seconds nothing happened at all, and then something went through him, some small internal collapse, as though a rope he had been holding had gone slack.

"Arwen Prys," he said.

Not a question. He said it the way a man says a word in a language he has not spoken since he was a boy.

"Mrs. Meredith, now," she said.

"Mrs. Meredith," he agreed. He looked at the deck between them, and then at his own hands, which were black at the knuckles with something that was not dirt.

The other officers had stopped talking. One of them, a young man with a good open face and a schoolboy's untidy hair, was looking from Harold to her with frank interest.

"You'll excuse me," Harold said to him. "This lady and I are from the same town."

"Of course," the young man said, and took the others away with a lift of his chin, and she thought: he is a nice boy, that one, and he is going to be a good officer, and it is a pity about his hair.

They stood in the wind. It was very loud, suddenly.

"You're an officer," she said.

"Fifth."

"On this ship."

"Aye." He wiped his hands on a rag that had been hanging from the davit, and it did not do much good, and he gave up. "I'd not have—" he started, and then stopped, and then said, in a different voice, "I'd not have known you. You've gone thin."

"You've gone hard."

"That's the sea." He looked at her properly then, and his eyes were the same, which was somehow worse than if they had changed. "How long have you been aboard?"

"Since Southampton."

"Two days." He said it as though it were a sum he was checking. "And you're in the first class."

"Yes."

"Well," he said, and there was a thing in his voice she could not name and did not like, "you would be."

She felt the blood come up into her face. "Don't."

"I said nothing."

"You said a great deal, and you said it in one syllable." She heard her own voice go high and stopped, and put her hand on the rail, and made herself speak quietly. "I looked for you. In Liverpool. I asked at the shipping offices. I did not know that you had gone to—" She gestured at the ship, the sea, the whole enormous machine of it. "Africa. The coast. Somebody said Africa."

"Twelve years of it, one way and another."

"You could have written."

The wind took the words and gave them back. He looked at the sea, and then at the boats, and then at her.

"I did write," he said.

She waited. He did not go on. He had the look of a man who has put a foot through a rotten plank and is deciding whether to mention it.

"When?" she said.

"Before I sailed. I left it with our Ada." He said it flatly, without accusation, the way he might report a fouled hawser. "It doesn't matter now. Fifteen years."

"Fifteen years," she repeated, and thought how strange it was that a number could be so exact and so useless.

---

She had been fourteen the summer he drilled the hole.

Barmouth that year had been full of excursion steamers and the smell of frying, and the tide had come in over the bar twice a day the way it always did, and her father's shop had sold more paint in one August than in the whole of the winter before, because every man in the town who owned a boat had decided that summer to paint it.

Harold was fifteen by then, or said he was. He had left school at thirteen and gone to work for his father, who was foreman at the marine store on the quay and would have had Harold in it too, in an apron, weighing out nails, if Harold had let him. Harold did not let him. He was on the water before it was light and off it after dark, and when there was no work on the water he took work on anything else, and he had a way of standing with his hands behind his back, looking at a boat, that was not a boy's way and that her father had noticed.

They had known each other since they could walk. There is no first meeting in a town that size; there is only the day you notice. She had noticed in the June, at the regatta, when he had taken a dinghy out past the bar on a day when nobody sensible went past the bar, and had come back in an hour with the sail split and his arm cut and a story that the other boys told for a month.

He had come up to her at the wall behind the cemetery, where the ground falls away and you can see the whole estuary, the water going out in long brown ribs, the Aberamffra woods on the far side, the little grey town stacked up behind the harbour, and he had said, without preamble, "I'm going to sea."

"You're already at sea."

"I'm going properly. Deep water. I'll not be in this town at twenty."

"Nor will I," she said, which was a lie she did not know was a lie.

He had looked at her with his head on one side. "You'll be married," he said. "You'll marry a man with a house on the hill and a carriage and you'll be in chapel of a Sunday with gloves on."

"What would you know about gloves?"

That had made him laugh, and the laugh was the thing about him, because it came out of him all at once and made his whole face young. He was not a handsome boy in the way the magazines meant. He was too short, his ears stood out, his hair would not lie down. But when he laughed you wanted to be the cause of it again.

The half-sovereign had been a joke first. She had said she wanted something to remember him by, in the mocking voice, and he had said, "I've nothing worth remembering," and she had said, "Then give me a penny," and he had gone quiet for a moment and then said, "No. I'll give you gold. Gold's what they give for a woman's hand."

The drilling took him a fortnight, on and off, because he could only use the bench when his father was out, and he ruined three bits learning, and the coin came out with the hole slightly off centre, which he was bitter about for years. He threaded it on a bootlace. He put it round her neck behind the wall, and his hands were shaking, and he said, "There. Now you're engaged to a man worth half a sovereign."

"I'm engaged to a fool," she said, and kissed him, and that was the first time.

They had a year. She thought about that year more than she thought about anything else in her life, which she knew was a kind of sickness, and which she could not stop. Nothing happened in it. They walked. They sat on the wall. They went out in his father's dinghy on a Sunday when the tide served and she held the sheet and he told her what to do and then told her she had done it wrong, and then told her she had done it well, in that order, because he could not give a compliment without a correction first. He learned the tides. He could tell her, to the minute, when the water would be over the bar, and she used to test him, and he was never wrong.

He gave her a tune. That was his other thing, the thing nobody outside the town knew about him. He could not read music and would not be taught. But he could hear a thing once and have it, and he had a whistle on him that was almost a bird, and he would sit with his back against the wall and play whatever came into his head, and she would sing it back to him, because her mother had taught her properly, and that was the whole of their conversation, in a way: him making a thing up, her giving it back with the corners straightened.

In September her father found out.

It was not a scene; that was the strange part. She had been brought up to expect scenes, being a chapel man's daughter in a town where everyone had a temper on a Saturday. Her father simply came in from the shop, and hung up his coat, and sat down, and said, "The Lowe boy."

"Yes."

"His father weighs nails for me. The boy's been at sea since he was twelve on other men's boats and he'll be at sea till he drowns. He's got no certificate and no education past thirteen and a temper that half the harbour's had a taste of." He had looked at her. "You're not to see him."

She had said something, she could not remember what.

"You're not to see him," her father said again, "and if he comes to this door I'll put him off it, and I'll not be gentle."

He came to the door on the Sunday. She heard it from upstairs: her father's voice, level, and then Harold's, not level, and then her mother in the passage saying, "Rhys. Rhys." And then the door, and then nothing.

She was not allowed down. Her mother came up afterwards and sat on the bed and said, "He's gone to Liverpool. His sister says he's taken his things," and Arwen had said, "I don't believe you," and her mother had said, "It's for the best, cariad," and Arwen had not spoken to her for a month, which she had regretted for the rest of her mother's life and after it.

She had waited for a letter for two years. She wrote eleven and posted seven, to the address Ada gave her, and none of the seven was ever answered, and she had decided in the end—she could remember the exact afternoon, a Tuesday, the smell of a wet wool coat drying by the range—that he had simply gone, and that the going had been the point, and that she had been a thing he had needed to leave behind in order to go.

So she had married Iestyn Meredith in 1903, at twenty-one, and he had been kind, and careful, and fourteen years older, and he had never once raised his voice to her, and she had thought that this was what a life was: a long slow exchange of one thing for another, and the trick was to keep the ledger straight and not look back at what you had sold.

---

He was wanted at half past five. There was a boat drill for the crew, or an inspection of the falls, or something of the sort; she did not take it in. The young officer came back and said, "Mr. Lowe," in a tone that meant the captain, and Harold put his cap on properly and said to her, "Will you be on this deck tomorrow? At four?"

"I could be."

"Four, then." He hesitated. "Mrs. Meredith."

"Arwen."

"Arwen," he said, and the way he said it was the way they said it at home, the *w* round and full, not the flattened thing the English made of it, and she had to look away.

---

In her cabin, with the door locked and Bessie gone down to her own berth in second class, she took the ribbon out of the drawer.

The half-sovereign had a hole drilled through the top of the queen's head. He had done it himself, at the bench in the yard, with a hand drill, when he was fourteen and she was fourteen, and he had threaded it on a bootlace and put it round her neck behind the cemetery wall on a Sunday in July, and it had taken him an hour and a half to do the hole and he had broken two drills and the coin had cost him a week of wages from the schooner he was not supposed to be working on.

She had worn it for a year and then put it away in a box. She had taken it out perhaps a dozen times since. It had been in the box when she married, and in the box when she went to the hospital in 1906 and came back without the child, and in the box when the trunks were packed for this ship. She had put it in her own hand herself, on the morning of the tenth, and she had not asked herself why.

She held it in her palm until it was warm, and looked at the porthole, which showed a black square of nothing at all.

The engines were under her feet, very steady, very far down. Somewhere above her head, in a room she had never seen, a man with tar on his hands was looking at the sea and telling himself that he had written the letter.

The ship went on. Nothing about her stopped, not the engines, not the clocks, not the water going by.

At eight bells she put out the light.

# II. Dead Reckoning

Harold Lowe had been aboard since Belfast. On the morning of the tenth of April, in Southampton, up before four in a cold that got into the seams of his boots, he spent the first hour of the day counting.

He counted because there was nothing else to do with himself. The ship was a building with a river under it, and buildings make men stupid. Every steward and every fireman and every cook on the *Titanic* had come aboard over the last forty-eight hours in a fog of shouting and paperwork, and by the morning of sailing they were all somewhere inside her, and none of them knew where. That was the thing nobody wrote about in the papers. The papers wrote about the swimming bath and the squash court and the seventeen hundred miles of electric cable. Nobody wrote about the fact that on her maiden voyage a ship is a town that has never been lived in, with a police force of eight officers and a crew of nine hundred who have never worked together, and that the first thing an officer does is try to find out how many of them can be relied on to do what they are told in the dark.

At four he had been up. He did not sleep well on land and worse before a sailing, and he had learned to stop fighting it. He shaved in cold water, put on his uniform with the strip of gold lace on the cuff that still felt like something he had borrowed, and went out on the boat deck while it was still black, and stood at the forward end of it with his hands in his pockets and his cap under his arm like a man at a graveside.

Portsmouth and Southampton Water were full of lights. Tugs, lighters, a Danish tramp working up the channel, the arc lamps on the dock sheds, all of it doubled and broken in the water. The ship's own lights were on, her windows going up the side of her in rows like a liner in a picture, and somewhere forward a winch was running and a man was singing.

He thought, as he always thought before a ship moved: *This is the last hour when nobody has made a mistake yet.*

---

The mistakes started at noon.

He was on the bridge wing with the third officer, Pitman, and the pilot, when the tugs began to haul her off the berth. She was heavy in the water and she came round slowly, and the crowd on the quay made a noise like weather, and Harold was looking aft along her side to see how she took the turn, which was his job, when Pitman said, "Oh, my God."

The *New York* had been moored in the Test, a White Star tender alongside her, and she was a big ship, an old ship, sixteen thousand tons of her, and Harold watched her come off the quay as though somebody had tilted the world.

"Her moorings are parting," he said.

"Aye," said Pitman. "And so will ours."

The *New York*'s bow swung out into the fairway and then travel, that great heavy bow, came on across the fifty feet of water and up the *Titanic*'s flank, drawn by the suction of her as surely as a nail to a magnet, and Harold felt the ship beneath him take a breath.

Six tugs. He counted them because counting was what he did. They came in from all sides with their funnels down and their wash going white, and one of them got a line on the *New York*'s bow and took the strain, and the line parted and whipped, and men on the quay scattered. Harold stood on the wing of the bridge with his hands on the rail and felt almost nothing. That was the other thing nobody told you about the sea. The dangerous minutes were quiet. Your heart went slow. Later, on shore, in the telling, it was all noise.

They got her off. The *Titanic* was held for the best part of an hour while the *New York* was warped back to her berth, and the crowd on the quay had something to talk about at their dinners, and the sailing was an hour late, which meant Cherbourg was late, which meant that for the rest of the crossing somebody would be trying to catch up a piece of time that had been lost in a place where nothing could be done about it.

Harold went below and wrote the word *fenders* in his notebook and then crossed it out. It was not a word that belonged to him. He was the fifth officer. His job on this ship was to stand watch, inspect the boats, keep his mouth shut, and learn the Atlantic.

---

He had been to sea fifteen years and had never once crossed it.

That was the thing he did not say to anybody, and it sat in him like a stone in a boot. He had been on the West African coast, on the *Belgic* and on the *Tropic*, in ships old enough to have sins. He had a master's certificate in a tin box in his cabin, ordinary master, which meant that on paper he was fit to command a vessel of any tonnage anywhere in the world, and in fact he was a fifth officer on seventeen thousand tons of somebody else's ship earning seventeen pounds ten a month.

He had been down the coast for years, nine months a trip, the kind of run that kills men with fever and rum and pays a hundred a year and keeps a man on shore for two months eating through it. He had had the fever twice. He had buried a shipmate at Grand Bassam, an old Dane who had told him, at the end, a great deal about the sea and nothing about himself. He had watched the sun come up over a river mouth and thought: *I'll die in this latitude and no one will ever hear of me and it will not be because I was not good enough.*

He was a Welshman on an English ship. He had learned early that this was not a thing to mention unless it was useful. But when the *Titanic* had come—the new ship, the great ship, the biggest thing ever built and they were taking anybody with a certificate and a clean discharge book—he had gone to Liverpool in March and stood in a room and been looked at, and afterwards he had walked out into the street and up to the corner and stood there and sworn to himself out loud, quietly, in Welsh, that he would be a commander before he was forty.

It did not occur to him that the ship was any part of that. It was a step. When a man is climbing a ladder he does not spend his time admiring the rung.

---

The officers' quarters were on the boat deck, a little run of cabins off the officers' mess with white-painted bulkheads and a smell of new linoleum and paint that would not go until somebody had smoked two hundred cigarettes in it. Harold had a cabin to himself, which was a luxury he had not had in eleven years, and he could not settle in it. He had folded his shirts into the drawers four times and each time taken them out and refolded them differently.

There were two hundred and forty-two pictures in the first class accommodation. He knew this because a steward had told him, in the tone of a man naming the value of a horse. There were four hundred and twenty tons of coal a day. There were six ice-making machines. The forward well deck was painted white on the inside of the bulwarks to keep the crew from being able to see the sea, a piece of management he had looked at for a long time and had privately admired.

He knew all these numbers because a fifth officer spends his first week memorising the ship until she is a shape in his head that he can walk through with his eyes shut. What he knew about water he kept to himself, because that was the only part of him that did not require a certificate.

---

On the second day out, the eleventh, there was a drill.

There were always drills. Boat drill on a White Star liner on passage is a performance with a good deal of rope in it, and Harold had a part: check the boats in the morning, report the falls, report the gear, count the oars and the water breakers and the biscuits, and see that everything was where the Boat Deck officer's chart said it was, and if it was not, find out why, and if the why was a man, put the man right, and if the why was the ship, write it down.

Twenty boats. He went round them in a little over an hour because he knew what he was looking for.

Sixteen of them hung in the davits, eight a side, each of them a wooden cutter of a good enough pattern, sixty-five capacity by the plate on the thwart. Four collapsibles on the roofs, they would have to be got off the roofs and over the side by hand, which took men and time. Twenty boats, and if a man was of a mind to multiply sixty-five by twenty and then by twenty more he would know that the whole boat complement of the *Titanic* was made to hold eleven hundred and seventy-eight people.

The ship carried about two thousand two hundred.

He did the sum on the eleventh, in his head, standing under number seven with his notebook against the davit, and then he did it again because he did not believe himself, and then he looked up the number of souls aboard and did it a third time.

Then he put his notebook in his pocket and went to find Moody.

Sixth officer. Twenty-four years old, quieter than Harold, a Blackheath boy with a good hand and a better memory and an appetite that had already become an officers' mess joke. He was running a party of seamen through the gripes on the forward boats and he came over wiping his hands, and Harold said, without preamble:

"Seven and the eighth. Do you know the capacity of these boats?"

"Sixty-five. Sixty-five point five."

"Twenty of them. That's not twelve hundred for two thousand two hundred."

Moody looked at him for a moment. He had the sort of face that was going to be good at forty and was still, at twenty-four, faintly unfinished, and something in it closed politely.

"Board of Trade," he said.

"Board of Trade."

"It's not a question of souls, Mr. Lowe, it's a question of cubic feet. The Board's rule is cubic feet, not souls. It works out at so much boat for so much ship, and by that reckoning we carry half again what we're asked to carry." He said it perfectly, he had learned it perfectly, and he said it like a man holding a door open for a call he does not intend to take. "She's got more boats than the regulation demands. That's the fact of it."

"That's not the fact I asked about."

"No," Moody said, and for a moment they stood there in the cold wind while a seaman behind them scraped a buckle on a fall and a woman two decks up laughed at something. "No, it isn't."

"There's no passengers in the Board of Trade."

"There's a great many of them in the first class."

Harold looked along the boat deck, where the boats were stacked each in its own cradle with the canvas laced over it, eight on this side going away aft in a line, and he could not have told anyone why the sight of them put a cold hand on his stomach. He had been on ships with fewer, on ships with none, on ships where the boats were lashed down for a voyage and a man who tried to get one off in a hurry would have a job finding a knife.

"Well," he said. "If it happens in daylight we're all right."

"It won't happen at all," Moody said. "It's the *Titanic*."

There was no cleverness in it. He meant it, the way a man means it when he has been to sea for six years and nothing has happened yet, and Harold, who had been at sea for fifteen and had been in three things that could have killed him and had a scar on his forearm from one of them, looked at him and did not argue.

"Get the men to change that griping line on eleven," he said. "It's chafed."

---

The captain came round the boat deck on the morning of the eleventh, and Harold, who had never served in a White Star ship before and had been told a great deal about Captain Smith by men who had, stood at the head of the ladder with his cap on and his hands at his sides.

Edward Smith was a heavy grey man in a black coat with a beard like a magistrate, and he came along the deck at the pace of a man who has walked that deck a thousand times and intends to walk it a thousand more. He stopped at number five and looked at the falls, and then at the gripes, and then at Harold.

"Fifth officer."

"Sir."

"Lowe." He said the name as though he were confirming it against a list in his head. "You had the *Tropic*."

"The *Belgic* and the *Tropic*, sir."

"Captain Carter's report on you was good." Smith looked at the boat again. "He said you were a driver."

It was not quite a compliment and not quite a complaint, and Harold, who had been in the merchant service long enough to know that both were being delivered at once, said nothing at all.

"That's what we want on a ship like this for the first month," Smith said. "After that we want a man who can be quiet." He put his hand on the davit. "Are you a quiet man, Mr. Lowe?"

"Not naturally, sir."

"Then learn it." The captain moved on a step, and then turned back. "You'll take the six to eight."

"Sir."

"Mind you're on the bridge wing at five minutes to. Not at the bell."

He went on down the deck with his hands behind his back, and the petty officer beside Harold breathed out and said, "He's a good man, the old man," in the tone of a man repeating a thing that was true and also a matter of policy, and Harold said, "Aye," and thought: *he'll do. He's got the trick of making a man want to be quiet for him.*

He thought about that again three days later, and it did not make him angry. It made him tired in a way he could not describe.

The other thing about the eleventh is that he wrote a letter.

He had written one before, in 1897, in Liverpool, on a bench in a lodging house off Canning Street with a borrowed pen, and had given it to Ada to hand to Arwen Prys, and had got on a ship and gone.

He had thought about that letter at intervals for fifteen years, the way a man thinks about a debt. He had written it badly; he knew that. He had been fourteen, and had believed himself fifteen. He had written a great deal about the sea and about what he intended to do and rather less about her, and at the end he had written something so unlike himself that he had had to put his hand over the page: *I will come back for you when I am worth coming for.* He remembered the sentence exactly. It sat in him like a swallow of bad water. It was the sort of thing a boy wrote who had read about it somewhere, and if she had read it she would have laughed, and he had hoped she would read it and not laugh, and had posted himself to the coast on the strength of it.

He had waited for an answer at every port for two years. Then he had stopped, in the way that you stop, not all at once, and had decided that the letter had been a boy's letter and the boy had been a fool and that Arwen Prys, who had a laugh like a service bell, would by now be married to somebody in the town with a shop and a house on the hill, and that this was the correct and ordinary end of the matter.

He had not once considered that the letter had never arrived.

---

He met her on the twelfth at ten past four in the afternoon, and afterwards, in his cabin, he found he could not work. He tried to write up the boat report. He got as far as *No. 11 griping line*, and then he put down the pen and sat with his elbows on his knees.

Fifteen years, and the first thing he had done was look at her coat and think: *that cost more than I make in three months.*

He had seen it in himself, standing there with a bit of swab rag in his hand and the wind going through him, and it had been a mean thing and he had said it and he was ashamed of it. She was somebody's wife and she was in the first class and she had a maid in the second class and a hat that had come from a shop with a carpet in it, and he was a fifth officer with salt on his hands and a manifest to check before eight. That was what the world was. The world had arranged itself very carefully around that fact for fifteen years, and he had been the one who arranged himself around it, and had been glad to, because a man who is climbing keeps his eyes on the rung.

But she had said *you could have written*, and her face had gone white with it, and he had said that he had written, and then he had watched her take that in and hold it, and he had not said the rest of it.

He had not said: *and I have been a fool in a way that has nothing to do with you.*

There was a knock at the door. It was Moody, with the second officer's compliments and the forward boat covers were to be left unlaced tonight on account of the damp.

"Tell him it's done," Harold said. "I'll do the port side myself."

"You've not eaten."

"I'll eat."

Moody stood in the doorway a moment. There was a way the younger man had of looking at you that suggested he had already decided what he thought and would wait a few years before saying it.

"Was that the lady from Barmouth?" he asked. "This afternoon."

"The lady is from Barmouth."

"I've never been to Wales."

"Nobody has," Harold said. "You should go. It rains."

Moody went away. Harold sat with his door open on the corridor, listening to the ship: the fans, the distant beat of the engines turning at seventy-five revolutions, a steward whistling somewhere in the pantry, the deck under him shuddering with the steady small shudder of a great ship going fast through a long swell.

He thought: *four o'clock tomorrow. She said she could be there.*

He thought: *if I have any sense at all I will be on watch.*

Then he got his cap and went up to the port side and unlaced the boat covers, one by one, in the dark, with his hands, because that was a job that could be done with the hands and no mind at all.

---

There is a thing a sailor does when he cannot sleep and it is not his watch.

He does not go up to the bridge; that is a hanging matter or near it. He goes and stands somewhere and takes the ship's pulse. Harold went aft along the boat deck to the after end, where the second class promenade came up to a rail, and stood in the lee of the last boat, and looked at the sea.

It was the twelfth of April and the wind had come round into the north-west, and the ship had begun to move a little, a long slow lift and fall with a roll of perhaps five degrees on the top of it, which is nothing, which is the amount a ship moves when the sea is in a good humour. Above him the mast and its yards went up into a sky with a great many stars in it and no moon worth the name. Aft, the wake went out white and then grey and then nothing. Ahead, the foremast and the crow's nest light, and beyond that the dark going on for a very long way.

There were men in the crow's nest looking at that dark for the good of two thousand two hundred people. There were six men in the stokeholds shovelling coal into one furnace mouth. There was a woman in the first class, two hundred feet forward and four decks down and one deck up, asleep or not asleep, who had given him a half-sovereign with a hole in it when he was fifteen, if she had kept it, which she probably had not.

He was not sentimental by trade. Sentiment on a ship was a leak. But he stood at the after rail for a little while and let himself do the sum that he had never let himself do in fifteen years:

He had been a boy who could not stay. That was the whole of it. He had run at fourteen, not because of her, not because of his father, but because of the particular dread that comes on a boy of that age when he looks at a town and sees exactly how his life will go in it, the counter, the apron, the same nine streets, the same forty faces, the grave at the top of the hill with his name on the stone and a hundred years of rain. He would have gone at fourteen whether she had existed or not.

And then he had met her, and the tragedy was not that he had to choose. The tragedy was that he had been given a year of something that made staying seem worth it, and then he had gone anyway, on the strength of a letter, and the letter had been a boy's letter, and he had known it even as he wrote it.

The ship lifted. The wake went out and out.

*Two more days to New York,* he thought. *Then I am out of it. Fifty years from now nobody will have heard of the fifth officer of the Titanic, and I will be a man with a command and a house and this will be a thing I remember once a year.*

He went below and slept for three hours, and dreamed about the harbour at Barmouth at low water, the boats all leaning the same way, and the smell of tar, and a girl he could not see.

---

# III. Saturday

Bessie Corrigan brushed her hair for twenty minutes every morning, which was ten minutes longer than the hair required, and Arwen had never once told her so.

She had come to Liverpool at nineteen from a village outside Wexford with a cardboard case and a cousin's address, and she could not sew a gusset to save her life but she could press a shirt front like a man in a shop window, and she had a way of putting down a cup that made the cup feel wanted. She also had a running argument with the ship, which she conducted in a whisper.

"It's the water, madam. It comes out of the tap hot and then it goes cold in the middle of the jug."

"Complain to the stewardess."

"I did complain to the stewardess." Bessie's hands went on doing something complicated at the back of Arwen's head. "She said it was the same on the *Olympic* and I said, well, madam, that's not a compliment, is it."

Through the porthole there was nothing but a grey moving wall. Arwen watched it and thought about the fact that she had slept five hours and had not dreamed.

"Are you frightened of the ship?" she asked.

"Me?" Bessie's hands stopped. "No, madam."

"You may be. I am a little."

"There's nothing to be frightened of. It's like a hotel on the Liverpool landing stage except the landing stage has gone." The hands resumed. "I'm not frightened of the ship. I'm frightened of New York."

"Why?"

"Because they'll all be Americans," Bessie said, in the tone of a woman naming a fact she has thought about a great deal, "and they'll not know what I'm saying and I'll not know what they're saying and there'll be nobody to ask."

"You'll have me."

"You'll be in the house, madam." It was said without any edge at all, which was the worst of it, and then Bessie said, "Which brooch?"

"The cameo."

She held up the mirror. Arwen looked at herself in it, and saw what she always saw, which was a woman of thirty with good bones and a mouth that turned down at the left corner, and eyes that had lately begun to look like her mother's.

"Bessie," she said. "Did you ever lose something and then find it and wish you hadn't?"

"That'd be my sister's rosary."

"What happened to the rosary?"

"I found it down the back of the dresser and I give it back to her and she said I'd stolen it." Bessie pinned the cameo. "It's better to lose a thing and keep the losing of it, madam. That's what my mother used to say. She said it about men."

---

Breakfast was kedgeree again, because Mrs. Penhallow had decreed it, and Mr. Thorne had joined them, because Mrs. Penhallow had decreed that too.

"Two days of this and I shall have to be carried off at New York," he said. "They'll have to use a derrick. Have you seen the gymnasium? There's a machine in it that is positively medieval. A man was on it yesterday and I thought he was going to be sick into the hat of the instructor."

"You're a slanderer," Mrs. Penhallow said. "It's a fine ship and you're enjoying yourself."

"I am enjoying myself enormously. I am enjoying myself in the manner of a man who has decided, at forty-six, that the only sensible thing to do with money is to spend it on being comfortable in transit." He buttered a roll with care. "And what do you do with yourself, Mrs. Meredith? You don't play cards. You don't go to the smoke room. You don't walk with the athletic gentlemen at six in the morning."

"I sit."

"There's a great deal to be said for sitting."

"I sit and I watch the water and I think about how I am going to arrange a house in a country I have never seen, in a street I have never seen, with furniture that is on this ship and a husband who is already there and has bought curtains."

Mrs. Penhallow put down her fork. "He's bought curtains?"

"Green ones. He wrote to me about them. Three pages."

"Did he describe them?"

"He described them," Arwen said, "as serviceable."

Mr. Thorne made a small sound and turned it into a cough. Mrs. Penhallow did not bother.

"Men are not educated in the matter of curtains," she said. "My husband bought a house in Beacon Street and had the whole of the drawing room done in a red I can only call patriotic, and I lived in it for eleven years because I could not face the conversation. Then he died and I had it done in green in a fortnight and I have never regretted a penny of it, and I do not think he would have minded, poor man. He'd have liked the green." She folded her newspaper. "It's what I say to my nieces. Marry a man you can talk to about the wallpaper. Everything else follows."

"And if there isn't one?" Arwen said.

"Then don't buy wallpaper," said Mrs. Penhallow, and poured herself another cup of tea with the air of a woman closing a subject.

---

At eleven there was a practice in the dining saloon for the next morning's service, and Arwen went because there was nothing else to do with the morning, and because a steward had told her that the singing would want all the help it could get.

She sat at the back. A young clergyman read the lesson in a voice that had not yet settled into itself, and the purser read the list of the day's amusements, and two hundred people sang *O God, Our Help in Ages Past* slightly behind the piano. The room had a hundred and fifteen feet of length to it and a glass dome at the end of it, and the sound went up into the dome and stayed there and came down again a moment later, so that the last line of every verse arrived twice.

Arwen did not sing. She had a voice, and she had been told so by her mother and by two choir masters and once, humiliatingly, by a man who had come to the house to tune the piano; and she had a rule about not doing the thing she was good at in front of people who had not asked. It was a rule she had made at nineteen for reasons she no longer entirely believed.

The clergyman gave out a notice about the next day's service, and then a general confession, and Arwen looked at her gloves and thought about the fact that she had promised to meet a man on the boat deck at four o'clock, and that she was going to do it, and that in nine years of marriage she had never done anything that could not have been described in front of her husband.

It was not, she had decided at three in the morning, anything. It was a conversation with a man from her own town. She had a right to a conversation. She had a right to the whole of her life, the part before Iestyn, and if she had signed it away she had not signed it away quite so completely as all that.

She knew this was a speech she was making to herself. She made it anyway, thoroughly, twice, and by the end of the last verse she had it by heart.

---

At two she went down to the second class, which took some arranging, and stood in the companionway outside the second class library with her hands folded, feeling like a visitor in her own house.

Bessie came up the stairs in her good hat, out of breath, having been fetched by a steward who had been given a shilling and a very clear description.

"You shouldn't have, madam."

"I wanted to see it. Is this it?"

"This is the library." Bessie looked around it with the settled disapproval of a woman who has inspected a great many rooms and found them all wanting in the same way. "It's very nice. There's a man in the corner has been reading the same page of the *Times* since Cherbourg."

"Are they kind to you?"

"The girls are. There's a Miss Kelly in the next berth and she's from Dundalk and she says she can get me work in a house in Brooklyn if the other thing doesn't suit." Bessie said this and then looked horrified at herself. "Not that it won't suit."

"Take the address," Arwen said. "Take the address and keep it in your shoe."

She gave her the book she had brought, which was *The Broad Highway*, because Bessie had once confessed to liking a story with a road in it, and Bessie took it and turned it over and said, "Madam, it's the second class. They'll think I'm putting on airs."

"Then let them."

Going back up, Arwen met a woman in a sable coat on the staircase, who looked at her with the flat curiosity of one passenger examining another's hat, and said, "There's a boy in the second class who has been told there's a swimming bath and he's been looking for it since Thursday. I hope he finds it." And went on down.

---

At four she was on the boat deck, and the fifth officer was not.

There were four men in dungarees working at the second boat and a petty officer telling them what to do, and she stood by the rail for a quarter of an hour, and the wind came over the deck from the port bow and got into her collar, and she had just decided that she had been a fool and that she was going below when he came round the corner of the gymnasium at a jog, without his cap, with a length of canvas in his hand.

"I'm sorry." He was out of breath. "I'm sorry. There was a—" He gestured with the canvas. "There was a thing."

"What kind of thing?"

"A boat cover that has been letting water into the boat instead of out of it." He threw the canvas down by the rail and stood there with his hands on his hips. "Every one of those covers has a hole in the skirt for the water to run out of. That one had been laced on upside down, so the hole was at the top, and the boat had four inches of water in her this morning, and the man who did it is a Welshman from Cardiff who has been at sea for thirty years and cannot be told anything."

"Are you going to tell him?"

"I have told him." Harold looked at the canvas. "He told me that in Cardiff they lace them that way."

They stood there. In the daylight, in the ordinary wind, with a seaman hammering forty feet away, it was different from the first meeting and different from what she had been rehearsing all night. It was easier. It was, in some way she had not expected, funny.

"Show me the boats," she said.

"Show you—"

"Show me the boats. I have been walking past them for four days. I should like to know what I have been walking past."

He hesitated. Then he picked up the canvas and said, "Come on, then. Mind your hat."

---

He showed her the boats the way he did everything, which was in order, and beginning at the beginning.

"This is number three. She's a cutter, wooden, clinker built, sixty-five souls on the plate but you'd not want to put sixty-five souls in her. Forty's a boat. Fifty's a risk. Sixty-five is a man with a pencil." He put his hand flat on the lapstrake planking. "There's a plug in the bottom, there, for when she's on the chocks. There's her water breaker, that barrel aft. There's the biscuit tank in the bow. Six oars, four rowlocks, a mast, a sail, a compass in that box. A boat like this'll live in weather that would kill the ship."

"Would she?"

"Aye. She'd live in anything, if there was a man in her who knew what he was doing." He said it without pride, the way another man might say that a horse was sound. "That's the whole of the thing, Mrs. Meredith. The boat doesn't care. The boat is a hundredweight of wood and it will do exactly what it's told, and if there's nobody aboard who knows what to tell it, it'll do what the sea tells it instead. And the sea doesn't care either."

Arwen looked at the boat. It was a beautiful thing, in a way that surprised her: the curve of it, the pale new wood, the oars lying along the thwarts with their blades wrapped. It had been made by somebody in a yard somewhere, carefully, by a man who would never see it.

"How many of these are there?"

"Twenty."

"Twenty." She looked along the deck, at the line of them going aft, each one in its davits with the canvas laced over. "And how many people are on this ship?"

He did not answer.

"Harold."

"Don't."

"How many?"

"Two thousand two hundred and odd." He said it flatly, looking at the boat. "And the boats hold eleven hundred and eighty if you fill them to the plate, and you cannot fill them to the plate, and they will not be filled to the plate. Now you know, and I'd be obliged if you'd keep it, because it's not a thing that anybody in the first class needs to have in their head on a Saturday afternoon."

"Why not? It's in my head now."

"Because they'd panic," he said, and then, seeing her face: "Not you. You'd not panic. You'd get up and ask a question, which is worse." He almost smiled. "It's the same thing in the end. A frightened passenger is a load on the boat. It's not a question of courage."

She thought about it. The wind went past them. Somewhere forward, the two boys with the dog were having an argument about whether the dog could swim.

"So what happens," she said, "if something happens?"

"Nothing's going to happen."

"That's not an answer. It's a door being shut."

"It's not shut, it's—" He stopped, and rubbed his face with the flat of his hand, and she saw that he was tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "Here's the answer. If she were to be in trouble, she'd be in trouble slowly. She'd take hours to go, and in hours you can do a great deal. You'd get the first class off, and the women and children of the other classes, and the boats would go away and they'd be picked up, because there's a dozen ships within a hundred miles of us at any hour of the day and one of them would come. That's the truth and it's a bad truth and it's the best one there is."

"And the men?"

He looked at her.

"Some of the men," he said. "The ones who were in the right place."

---

They walked aft. It was a quarter to five, and the light was thickening, and the ship had begun to move in a way that was almost nothing but which she could feel through the soles of her shoes.

"You never told me," she said. "At the rail, the first day. What you wrote."

"I told you I wrote."

"Tell me what you wrote."

He was quiet for a while. They had come to the after end of the boat deck, where the second class promenade begins, and there was nobody there, and the wake was going out behind them in a long white line that narrowed and narrowed.

"I wrote that I was going to the coast," he said. "I wrote a great deal about ships, to be honest with you. I was fourteen and I'd been to Liverpool and I thought I was the only man in Wales who had seen a barque. And then at the end I wrote that I would come back for you when I was worth coming for."

"Those words."

"Those words." He said them without flinching and without any softening at all. "I've thought about that sentence for fifteen years, and I'll tell you what's wrong with it now. It's a boy's sentence. It puts the whole thing off until a day that will never come, and it makes it sound like patience when it's only cowardice with a ribbon on it. If I'd meant it I'd have written, wait for me, and I'd have given you a year and a month and a ship and a date. I'd have made it a thing you could hold me to."

"Then why didn't you?"

"Because I didn't know if I could have you," he said. "And a man doesn't like to be told no by post."

She had thought, in fifteen years, that the wound was that he had gone. Standing there, she found it was not. The wound was that he had gone and left it at that. He had not even given her the dignity of a refusal; he had put a boy's pretty sentence in a letter and gone to sea, and she had been left holding nothing at all, and had had to make a story out of it, and the story she had made was that she had not been worth the trouble.

"There's something I have to tell you," she said, "and I would like you to hear it without saying anything clever."

"All right."

"I never got the letter."

He turned his head.

"Ada gave it to me," she said. "I know that now, because you've told me. But I never had it. My father—" She stopped. "I don't know that it was my father. I know that Ada was fifteen years old and frightened of him, and that my father had told her family what he thought of her brother, and that the letter went somewhere and it did not come to me. I waited two years. I wrote seven letters to an address your sister gave me and none of them was answered, and I have spent fifteen years believing that you had simply gone and that I had been a thing you left behind."

He did not say anything for so long that she thought he had chosen not to.

"Seven letters," he said at last.

"Seven."

"To the house on Denbigh Street."

"Yes."

"My father died in 1904," he said, "and my mother went to live with Ada in Chester in 1906, and the house was let, and before that it was—" He stopped and started again, and his voice had gone strange and careful. "My father would not have forwarded a letter to me. He'd have burned it. He told me when I was fourteen that if I went I was not to come back and write to him for money, and I told him I would not, and I didn't, and he died without either of us taking it back." He looked at her. "I never had a letter. Not one. In fifteen years. I'd have—"

He stopped.

"You'd have what?"

"I'd have come," he said. "I'd have come at twenty-two in a bad coat with nothing but a certificate and I'd have stood in your father's shop and taken whatever he said to me and then I'd have asked you, and you'd have said yes or no, and either way I'd have known."

The ship went on. Ahead of them the sun had gone down into the water without any colour at all, the way it does at sea, and the sky in the west was a band of dull rose that was already fading from the top.

"Well," Arwen said. Her voice was not steady. "There it is."

"There it is."

"I am married," she said. "You should know that I am married, and that it is not a bad marriage, and that I am not a woman who is looking for a way out of anything. I want to be very plain about what I am and what I am not."

"I know what you are."

"You don't. You know what I was at fifteen." She turned to face him. "I am thirty years old. I have a house in Liverpool with a green drawing room. I lost a child in 1906, at seven months, and there was no reason for it and there will not be another, and my husband has never once said a word to me about it that was not kind, and I have been lonely in a way that has nothing to do with him and that I have never said out loud to anybody until this minute, and I would like you to say something ordinary now, because I have finished."

He looked at her for a moment. Then he said: "It's going to be cold tonight."

She laughed. It came out of her wrong, half a sob, and she put her hand over her mouth, and he stood there with his hands at his sides and did not touch her, and the fact that he did not touch her was the kindest thing anyone had done for her in years.

"There's a wind coming round into the north," he said. "You'll want your steamer rug and you'll want it doubled. And tomorrow I have the second dog watch, six till eight, so it'll have to be after. Eight bells. Half past eight by the clock, when they put it back tonight."

"Half past eight."

"If it's too cold for you, don't come, and I'll not think anything of it."

"I'll come."

"Half past eight, then."

He put his cap on and touched it and went away forward, and she stayed at the after rail until her hands were too cold to hold on.

---

At dinner the à la carte restaurant was full and the band played *The Tales of Hoffmann*, and Mrs. Penhallow had a second glass of wine and told a story about a cousin who had been engaged to a man for nine years and then married his brother. Mr. Thorne laughed at it immoderately. Arwen ate about a third of what was put in front of her and agreed with everything that was said to her, and at half past ten she excused herself and went out on the enclosed promenade and stood with her forehead against the glass.

The sea was going by. It had gone quite flat, so flat that the glass hardly showed any movement in it at all, and the ship's lights went out along the water in long shivering columns. It was the first night she had been able to look at it without wanting to turn away.

She thought: *I have told a man I have not spoken to in fifteen years that I have been lonely. I have said it out loud in the wind on the deck of a ship in the middle of the Atlantic, and tomorrow I will see him again at eight bells.*

She thought: *and on Thursday I will be in New York and I will never see him again in my life.*

She went to bed at eleven and lay in the dark with her hands folded on the counterpane, and the ship went on at twenty-two knots through a sea like a plate, and somewhere above her a bell struck the half hour, and she counted it, because there was nothing else to do, and then she slept.

The last thing she heard was the engines. They were so steady that she had stopped hearing them four days ago, and the fact that she heard them now, that she lay there and listened to them and thought *there, that's the ship, that's what she sounds like*, was the sort of thing she would remember afterwards and could not explain to anyone: that on her last night she had lain awake and listened to the engines as though she had never heard them before.

---

# IV. The Flat Calm

Sunday began with the clocks going back and a bell in the wrong place.

They put the ship's clocks back forty-seven minutes that night—the *Titanic* ran on a shifting noon, so that every day the clocks were set to the sun, which meant that every night the watches were a little longer or a little shorter than the last, and the men who had to stand them complained about it and would not have had it otherwise. Harold came off at eight bells into a dark that had a bite in it he had not felt before on this voyage, and he went down to the mess and ate cold beef and bread and drank a cup of tea with his cap still on, and read the printed sheet that was pinned by the door.

*Ice.* It was not a word he had expected to see in the first week of a westbound crossing in April, though it was not a word he was surprised by either. The Grand Banks in April is a place where a ship goes with her eyes open. He had been in ice before, off the cape, in a barque with a crew of nine and no light, and had spent six hours on the forecastle head in a snow squall listening for the sea to change its note against a berg. You hear it before you see it, sometimes. Sometimes you hear nothing.

The notice was a small thing, a chit with a position on it and a hand-drawn line, and a warning, and Harold read it twice and then did the sum in his head, the way he did sums.

He had the six to eight. The position on the chit was somewhere ahead of them and to the south, and when he had worked it through he made it nine or ten that night, and Murdoch would have the bridge then, and it would not be his concern. He worked it out mentally and satisfied himself that the ship would not be in the ice during his watch, and he pinned the thing back up and forgot about it. He thought afterwards that this was what everyone did, all of them, from the captain down: each man looked at the ice and worked out whether it was his problem, and every one of them was right, and it made not the slightest difference in the end.

---

The service was at half past ten in the first class dining saloon.

The captain read it himself. That was the custom on the White Star ships on a Sunday and it was also, Harold thought, standing at the back with three other officers and their caps under their arms, good management. It put the master of the ship in front of the first class passengers as a man in a black coat with a prayer book, reading the lesson in a broad Staffordshire voice, rather than as a rumour who ate in his own cabin.

Smith read well. He was a big grey-bearded man of sixty-two, near the end of forty years at sea, with the face of a country doctor and a manner that had been polished by forty years of carrying other people's nerves, and he read the bit from Job about the sea and said a few words afterwards about being thankful for a fair passage, and the whole thing took forty minutes and improved everybody.

Harold sang the hymns from memory without moving his lips much, which was his habit, and looked at the back of the room. He was not looking for her. He had told himself he was not looking for her. He found her four rows from the front on the port side, in a dark blue coat and a hat with no feather, sitting very straight, not singing, and it came to him with a small cold shock that she had not sung at the service yesterday either.

He had grown up in a chapel where a woman who could sing was expected to sing, and a woman who could not was expected to sing anyway, and Arwen Prys had had a voice that made the back of your neck go tight, and had been made to use it every Sunday of her childhood by a mother who taught music in a front room. He had heard her sing *Calon Lân* at a concert in the vestry in 1896 and had walked home afterwards by himself in the rain to think about it.

So she had stopped. Somewhere in fifteen years she had put it down, the way she had put down the half-sovereign.

---

He was free until six. He walked the ship for an hour because he could not sit.

Round the boat deck twice, and then down to the third class, because the third class was the part of a ship an officer ought to know and the part of a ship the first class never saw, and Harold had a private and unreasoning loyalty to the steerage of every vessel he had ever sailed in, on the grounds that he had come out of it.

It was a Sunday afternoon and the third class were making the best of it. There was a general room with long tables and a piano going and a great many people in their Sunday clothes who had come up from the deep decks at eight in the morning and would not go down again until eleven at night, because a berth with three other men in it and no air is not a place a man stays in when he can be in the daylight. Two girls were dancing together in the middle of the floor, badly. A baby was asleep on four chairs pushed together. There was a smell of cabbage and wet wool and cheap tobacco, and it was the smell of every ship he had ever been in, and it was, oddly, the smell of home.

A man by the door said, in Welsh, "You're Welsh."

Harold turned round. He was a small dark man of forty-odd in a collarless shirt and a good waistcoat, and he had the accent of the quarry country.

"Barmouth."

"Llanberis." They shook hands, which was absurd and entirely correct. "You're an officer."

"Fifth."

"Fifth." The man nodded, satisfied that this was as it should be. "And they've put the Welsh on the boats. Good. It's a boy's game, is it, this ship? All the brass?"

"It's a good ship."

"It's a very good ship." He said it the way the chapel said things, with the reservation folded inside the compliment. Then he said, "Do you know the hymn *Calon Lân*?"

And Harold laughed out loud, in the middle of the third class general room, on the fourteenth of April 1912.

---

At four he went on watch and stood at the after end of the bridge with the sixth officer and the quartermaster and the ship going west at twenty-two knots through water that had gone flat.

He had never seen it like that. He would talk about it afterwards, at the inquiries, at Barmouth, at odd times for the rest of his life, and he would always come back to the same thing: there was no sea. Not a swell, not a ruffle, not the small chop that even the calmest day will give you. It lay like a plate of grey glass and the ship went through it without leaving a proper wake, and the wind had dropped to nothing, and it turned bitter, cold enough by six o'clock for the men on the look-out to be relieved every half hour.

The other thing about a night like that—and he knew it in his bones, he had known it since he was fifteen and had spent nights on a schooner's deck wishing he was dead—is that it takes the sound away. A sea with a swell on it makes a noise against a ship. It hisses and slaps and mutters. Flat water is silent. A ship going twenty-two knots through flat water makes a noise of her own, engines and fans and the rush of her bow wave, and that noise covers everything else, and there is nothing underneath it, no small voice of the sea to tell you that something is near.

He said something of the sort to Moody, who was on the bridge waiting to take over at six.

"You don't want a wind," Moody said.

"You want a wind."

"I'd not have said so."

"You're young," Harold said. "A wind is cold and it's wet and it's a nuisance and it will tell you where the ice is. Flat water tells you nothing at all, and if there's a berg in it, it's the same colour as the sky, and it'll be on you before a man in the crow's nest has time to get his whistle up."

Moody considered this in the way that he considered things, which was thoroughly and slowly, and said, "Well, there'll be no moon either."

"There'll be stars."

"There's that."

The stars came out at seven and they were extraordinary. There was no moon at all, and the night was as black as a night can be, and in that blackness the stars stood out and down in a way Harold had only ever seen in the tropics. The whole sky was full of them. You could see the Milky Way like a road. The sea took no notice of them and stayed grey.

He went off watch at eight and stood at the rail of the boat deck and looked at it for a while, and he was still standing there at twenty-five minutes past eight when he heard her shoes on the deck behind him.

---

She had come up with a rug over her arm and her collar turned up and a hood over her hat, and she had the look of a woman who has made a decision and is going to go through with it whatever the weather does.

"I had some difficulty finding you," she said. "A steward told me the officers' part of the deck was for officers. I said I was looking for a doctor."

"Is there a doctor up here?"

"No. That is why I said it." She stood beside him and looked at the stars and did not say anything for a while. "Oh," she said. "Oh, look at that."

"It's a good night for it."

"It's the most beautiful thing I have ever seen in my life," she said, "and I am perfectly frozen."

"Put the rug round you."

She put the rug round her. He stood with his hands in his pockets and his shoulders up to his ears and the cold coming through the soles of his boots, and they looked at the sky together for a long time, and it was one of those moments which are nothing and which he would find later on had been laid down in him like a photograph.

"The third class have a piano," he said. "Do you know the hymn *Calon Lân*?"

Her face, in the starlight, changed.

"Of course I know it."

"There's a man from Llanberis down there," Harold said, "who asked me if I knew it, and I said I did, and he said, well then. As though that settled something." He took his hand out of his pocket and held it out. "I've something for you. It's a poor thing and I'd not have given it to you at fifteen, but I'm not fifteen, and it's what I've got."

It was a whistle.

Not a tin one off a stall. It had been a length of half-inch copper pipe that had no business surviving in an engineer's rag bag, and he had cut it, and filed the lip in, and stopped the far end with a plug of wood, and it had taken him most of two hours at the bench in the lamp room with the third engineer's tools and the third engineer's amused permission, and it made a note that was not quite a note.

She turned it over. "You made this."

"I did."

"Today."

"Over the last two days. When I should have been asleep." He said it as though it were a fault, and she made a small sound that was not quite a laugh.

He said, "There's not much of a tune in it. It's not a proper whistle. But it'll do this." And he took it back, and put it to his lips, and played the first eight bars of a tune that a woman in a front room in Barmouth had taught to a girl in 1889, and the note was thin and slightly flat at the top of the octave and it went out over the flat black water and came back off nothing at all.

He stopped.

"Sing it," he said.

"Harold."

"Sing the thing. There's nobody up here but the two of us and a man in the crow's nest a hundred yards off, and the third class have a piano, and it's Sunday, and I've made you a whistle." He said it roughly, because he was afraid of being gentle. "Sing it, mun. For the man from Llanberis."

And she sang.

She sang the first verse at half voice, the way you sing in a room where somebody is asleep upstairs, and the Welsh of it came out of her with the *ll* sound in it that no English voice ever makes, and it was a message in a language that only the two of them and a man from Llanberis and a quarter of a million other people had any right to understand. *(Calon lân yn llawn daioni, / Tecach yw na'r lili dlos.)* Then she stopped and put her hand over her mouth and said, "I have not sung in nine years."

"Why not?"

"Because I married a man who does not like music," she said.

He did not answer that. He wrapped the whistle in a bit of clean rag from his pocket and put it in her hand and closed her fingers over it.

---

They stayed an hour. He could not afterwards account for how it went, except in pieces.

She told him about Liverpool: the house on Gaerwen Road, the green drawing room, the four women in the parish who had decided she was a cold woman because she did not come to the sewing circle, the endless small business of being a wife in a city where mistrust was a form of good manners. She had gone to the docks once a week to look at the ships. She had never told anyone that. She would stand at the north end of the landing stage where the wind came off the river and look at the funnels with their colours on them and go home again.

She told him about the child. Not much. Three sentences, and then she said, "I will not say any more about it, and I would be obliged if you did not say anything comforting."

"I don't know anything comforting."

"I know. It's why I told you."

He told her about the coast: Grand Bassam and Lagos and Calabar, the fever, the old Dane who had died at Grand Bassam and whose name he could not now remember, which he found shameful. He told her about his father's death, and about the gold watch, and about the fact that he was now, at twenty-nine, the eldest son of a dead man and the man whose name people said when they said the family. He told her about the certificate in the tin box and how he had held it in his hands at Liverpool and thought, *and now what,* and about how he had been in three collisions and one fire and had never once been afraid, and that he did not think that was courage, he thought it was a defect.

"You're not afraid now," she said.

"I'm afraid now," he said.

She looked at him.

"It's nothing to do with—" He stopped. "It's New York. I'm not a man who knows how to be a passenger. On this ship I know where everything is and I know what I'm for, and on Thursday I step off and I am a man in a blue coat on a pier, and you will go to your house on the street with the other curtains, and I'll go to a boarding house off West Street and find out what a man with a fifth officer's pay does in a city for a month."

"What will you do?"

"Come back," he said. "On the first White Star boat that'll have me. They owe me the passage home and I mean to take it."

"And then?"

"And then the *Tropic*, or something like her, down the coast, and one day a ship of my own." He said it and heard it and found that it sounded thinner than it had on the twelfth.

She was quiet. Then she said, "I could write to the *Oceanic*."

"Don't."

"Don't?"

"Don't write to the ship." He turned to face her properly. "If you write to me on the *Oceanic* something a steward gives me over a rail, that's the end of it, and I'll stand on the deck of that ship and read your letter and I'll be a man going back to the coast with a piece of paper in his pocket. If it's to be nothing, let it be nothing, and I'll keep what I've had. But don't write to me a letter to be read at a rail."

"Then what?"

"Then I'll come to Barmouth in June," he said, "if my sister's kept the room. And I'll walk up to the shop, and I'll stand at that door, and I'll not be fifteen and I'll not be turned off it this time, and if you're there—" He stopped. "If you're there, Arwen."

"That's not a plan. It's a hope with a date on it."

"It's the only kind of plan I've ever had," he said, "and I'll thank you not to say so in that voice."

She laughed then, properly, and the sound of it went out over the water, and a long way up the deck a seaman on the look-out turned to see what it was.

---

She went in at half past nine. She was shaking with cold and her eyes were bright and her nose was red, and she took his hand, briefly, at the doorway, with her glove on—the only time they touched, the whole night, and afterwards he could not decide whether he was sorry or not.

"Half past nine," she said. "That is putting me to bed like a child."

"It's the cold."

"It is not the cold." She got the door open. Then she said, without turning round, "The whistle is in my pocket. If I am searched at New York I shall be in difficulties."

"There's worse contraband."

"Yes," she said, "there is," and went in.

---

He was not on watch again until four in the morning. He went to his cabin at ten and lay down in his clothes, which he had not done since he was a boy, and looked at the deckhead.

The ship was so steady that she seemed to be still. There was no creak anywhere in the cabin, no movement in the curtain of the bunk, nothing but the fans and the far small hammer of the engines and the faint, faint ring of a glass in the washstand where the vibration came up through the steel.

He had said a great deal up there on the deck. He did not regret it. That was the strange thing: he lay there and went over it and there was nothing he wanted to take back, not the whistle, not the singing, not the thing he had said about the letter and the door. He had been a boy who would not say a thing plainly because he was afraid of being told no. He had said it plainly tonight, and had been told neither yes nor no, and had found that he could live with that.

He thought: *I'll be at Barmouth in June.*

He thought: *Ada will have a fit. She's a chapel woman now, and she'll have something to say about a married woman, and she'll say it in Welsh so that it sounds like scripture, and then she'll put the kettle on and tell me to go up and see to the roof.*

He thought: *and if the husband's there, I'll take it. Whatever he is. He's been kind to her, and she said so, and she's not a woman who says a thing that isn't true.*

He thought: *I'll be forty before I have a command of my own.*

He thought: *that doesn't matter so much now.*

At some point he slept. The last thing he heard was the bell striking ten somewhere above his head, a long way off, two bells of the first watch, the ship's own small voice telling the time.

---

He woke because the ship had changed her note.

It was not noise that woke him. It was the opposite. He came up out of sleep with his heart going and lay still and listened, and the engines were still there, still thudding, and yet the whole ship had gone quiet in some way he could not have explained to anybody who had not spent their life on one: the vibration in the washstand glass had changed. The fans went on turning. A door banged somewhere forward.

He looked at his watch. He could not see it; he got up and struck a match and looked at it and it was twelve minutes to twelve.

He sat on the edge of the bunk with the match burning and thought: *no.*

A ship does not stop from twenty-two knots in a breath. Her engines come off her over a couple of minutes, and while they are coming off she goes on through the water and the whole of her changes her mind about everything, and here she was, with the engines still turning, and stopped.

He put his boots on.

Then the steam went.

It came out of the escape pipes in a roar like the end of the world. He had heard it once before, in the *Tropic*, when the main injection had failed and they had lost the condenser for six minutes and every man aboard had looked at the sky and made his peace with it in his own way. It was a scream of forty tons of pressure going up into the night, and it filled the ship from the engine room to the crow's nest, and Harold stood in his cabin with one boot on and said, out loud, in Welsh, "God help us, what has she done."

He got the boot on. He took his revolver out of the drawer, and it was in his hand before he had thought about it, and then he opened the door and went out into the passage, and the steam whistle went on and on above him, and he could hear the ship's people running—men's feet on the boat deck overhead, a woman's voice asking a question, another door banging, the bell of the engine room telegraph ringing and ringing and ringing, as though somebody far below were hammering on a locked door.

---

# V. The Ship That Could Not Sink

There is a particular sort of dinner that a great ship gives on a Sunday.

Arwen had been to a great many of them. Iestyn's business had put her at tables in Liverpool and in London and twice in Hamburg, and she had learned the shape of them: the fish, the soup, a bird, a saddle, a sweet, the little ices that the French chefs made to show the kitchen could do mathematics, the coffee, the cigars moving away to another room. Nine courses and two hours and a band playing at the far end, and the whole thing conducted in the manner of a service, with the stewards in a long line and the plates going out and coming back and the wine being poured in a small, exact, continuous ceremony.

The Sunday dinner on the *Titanic* was the best she had ever eaten.

She sat between Mr. Thorne and a Mr. Ryerson from Philadelphia who had a round face and a great deal to say about the price of copper, and opposite Mrs. Penhallow, in black, with her rings off because it was Sunday. There were four hundred people in the room in evening dress and the whole of it was lit from above by electric light that came out of the ceiling in a hundred small soft globes, and it was warm, and it smelled of lemon and hot metal and clean linen, and every part of it had been made for people exactly like her.

"I don't hold with this at all," Mrs. Penhallow said, over the potage. "Sunday dinner should be cold. A piece of cold beef and a baked potato and a row with one's sister. This is not a dinner, it's a pageant."

"You say that every Sunday," said her neighbour, a Mr. Ismay's assistant or cousin or something of the sort, whose name Arwen had not caught.

"I say it every Sunday because it's true every Sunday."

"You'll take the canvasback duck, though."

"I will take the canvasback duck," Mrs. Penhallow agreed, "because I am a hypocrite, and because it is the best thing on this ship, and because I am sixty-four."

Arwen ate. She found, to her surprise, that she was hungry. She had eaten badly for four days and now she was eating canvasback duck and drinking a glass of something Mr. Ryerson had recommended with an air of large experience, and listening to the band play a waltz from *The Merry Widow*, and she was aware of the fact—aware of it as she would have been aware of a stone in her shoe—that she was happy.

That was the thing she could never afterwards make anyone understand, and in the end she stopped trying. Everyone wanted the last night of the *Titanic* to have been an omen. They wanted it to have been cold and strange, they wanted the warnings and the lifeboats and the iceberg, they wanted some sort of premonition to have run around the dining saloon at eleven o'clock, and she had to tell them that it was a very good dinner, and that she had laughed twice, and that there had been a woman at the next table with an enormous diamond and a bad temper and it had been funny.

At a quarter past ten she went out to the enclosed promenade with her stole and stood at the glass. The ship's clocks had been put back at midnight, and then put back again; the night was going to be long, and everybody knew it, and there was a general feeling of generosity in the room behind her, of a voyage that had gone well and would be over on Thursday.

The sea was like a floor. She had been told by a steward that this was the finest passage he had ever made in April, and that they were making twenty-two and a half knots, which was almost the best she could do, and that they had come nearly five hundred miles since the noon.

She looked at the water for a while and thought about the whistle in her pocket. She had taken it out, in her cabin, at six o'clock, and turned it in her hands. It was not a beautiful object. It was a piece of pipe with a plug in it. It had been made, badly, by a man who had been too tired to sleep and who had done it in a lamp room with borrowed tools.

She had put it back in the pocket of the coat she had on.

---

At ten to eleven she was in the reception room outside the dining saloon, taking a cup of coffee she did not want in order not to go to bed, when the engines changed.

She did not know that was what it was. She knew only that a thing had happened, the way you know that a person in the room has stood up. There was no noise. The vibration that had been in the floor for four days, which she had stopped noticing and would have sworn was not there, was not there. The chandelier gave a small shiver and was still.

A man near her said, "What's that?" in the tone of a man who has heard a noise in his own house at night.

"It's nothing," said his wife. "It's the engines."

"That's what I said. It's the engines."

Arwen put the cup down. She went to the window, and she could see the sky, and the sea, and a great many stars, and nothing else at all.

She went out into the corridor and up the staircase to A deck, because there was a window there that opened, and she stood in the vestibule of the Grand Staircase under the carved clock with the two figures on either side of it, and there was a little crowd of people in evening dress doing the same thing.

"The ship's stopped," a man behind her said.

"Nonsense. She's stopped her engines for something."

"There's a lot of smoke coming off the back of her."

Arwen went up to the top of A deck and out on the forward part where there was a rail, and she was in the open air, and the cold was extraordinary, and there was indeed a great deal of smoke and steam coming off the after part of the ship and blowing away to the north-east, and there were people at the rail all around her in furs and evening cloaks, looking at it, and two women were laughing.

"Ice," somebody said. "It's ice. There's ice on the forward deck—you can see it in the light."

But she could not see it. She could see nothing forward but the dark, and a thing that might have been a shadow.

She stood there for perhaps five minutes. It was a curious feeling and she observed it with interest, because she was not frightened; she was alert. She had felt the same thing once in a doctor's waiting room in Liverpool, and once, in 1906, when she had woken at two in the morning with a pain and known before the nurse came that the child was gone. It was the feeling of a person who has stopped waiting for something to happen and is now waiting to be told.

Then the door behind her opened and a steward came out, and he was not lounging, he was walking fast, and he said, "Beg pardon, madam. The officers have asked for all passengers to go below and put on their lifebelts."

The rail went silent.

"Don't be absurd," a man said.

"I was told to say it, sir." The steward's voice was not steady, and his face—she would always remember this—his face was apologising. "It's a precaution only. It's the regulation. The officers have asked for all passengers to go below and put on their lifebelts and come up to the boat deck."

---

In the corridor on the way down, a woman in a pale dressing gown with a fur over it caught her arm.

"Mrs. Meredith, isn't it? We sat opposite at the concert. What is happening? You've been on ships. Do something."

"I've been on ships that sank twice a week for four days," Arwen said. "It's what happens when it stops."

The woman stared at her.

"I'm sorry," Arwen said. "That was a stupid thing to say. Put your boots on. Put on two pairs of stockings and your heaviest coat and take every blanket in your cabin, and don't let anyone talk you out of them."

"Oh God," the woman said, and went.

In B-deck, the corridor was full of people going into cabins and coming out again. A steward was going along knocking on doors and calling, "All passengers. All passengers on your lifebelts." A man was standing in the middle of the corridor with his collar off saying to anybody and to nobody, "It's a drill. It's got to be a drill."

Bessie was at the door of the cabin with her hair down and her boots on the wrong feet.

"Oh, madam," she said. "Oh, madam, I knew you'd come back."

"Where did you get that?"

"They were in the wardrobe." Bessie held up the cork jacket. "They're a great deal heavier than you think."

"Put it on properly. Under your arms, not over. Under. Then put your coat over it and button it and put your hat on." Arwen was already at work on her own. The jacket smelled of cork and dust and something faintly like new canvas. "Have you your shoes? Both of them?"

"Yes."

"Have you a blanket?"

"The one off my bed's still there."

"Get it." Arwen went down on her knees in front of Bessie, which she had not done for anyone but a child, and pulled the straps tight under her arms and made them fast, and then looked at her. "Now listen to me. You will not go to second class. You will stay with me. If we are separated, you go up to the boat deck and you stand by the first boat you see that has women in it and you say *I am a lady's maid, I am with a party,* and you do not go back down for anything. Not for the trunk, not for the jewel case, nothing. Do you hear me?"

"Madam, the jewel case is—"

"I have the jewel case in my coat. Do not make me say this again."

They went up.

---

The boat deck, at that time, was not yet the boat deck of the stories.

It was cold and it was busy and it was—this was the part that no one ever believed—hotchpotch. There were people in evening dress and in furs and in dressing gowns. There were men in pyjamas and overcoats. There was a woman in a white satin evening dress with a Pekingese under her arm, holding it up out of the way of people's feet. There was a lady being offered bread and butter by a steward, and taking it, and eating it. There were forty or fifty people standing about in the cold with their lifebelts on over their evening clothes, and there were a great many empty seats in the deck chairs, and the band was playing.

The band was playing. She would be asked about this by reporters, later, and she would tell them that the band was playing in the lounge, and that you could hear it through the windows if you were near enough, but that the steam was so loud that you could not really hear the tune. It was not "Nearer, My God, to Thee." It was something with a dance in it. She was certain of that, and she was certain of very little.

And the boats were being got ready.

She could see them better now. She had walked past these boats four days ago while a man from Barmouth explained the plug in the bottom to her, and now the canvas covers were off them and they hung over the side, and the falls had been overhauled and the boats swung out on their davits, and each one had an officer with it and a group of seamen, and the whole thing was being done with the awful unhurriedness of men working by a rule.

"Women and children to the port side," a steward was saying, over and over. "This way, madam. Women and children to the port side."

Port side. The port side was on the left as you faced forward, she thought, and then was not sure she was right, and turned round twice.

"Mrs. Meredith." Mrs. Penhallow was standing by number ten with an extraordinary hat on and no gloves. "You are to come with me. I have been told I am not allowed to wait in the palm court and I said to the man, I must wait somewhere, and he said, come this way, ma'am, and here we are."

"Have you your jewel—"

"Don't you dare." Mrs. Penhallow took her arm with a grip that was entirely of bone. "I was told, months ago, by a very good friend who was on the *Republic* when she went down in ninety-nine. She said, three things. She said, keep hold of the person next to you. She said, do the thing that is being done, and do not start a thing of your own. And she said, take off your skirt if you have to climb and do not worry about your pride, because pride weighs more than a skirt." She looked at Arwen. "Do you know, I have thought about that every day for thirteen years and this is the first time I have had a chance to pass it on."

"How old was your friend?"

"Seventy," said Mrs. Penhallow. "She died in her bed. It was not a drowning, I said to her, it was a moral collapse, and she hit me with a cushion."

---

They stood by number ten for a long time.

There was a great deal of shouting and none of it seemed to be making the boat go. The officer at the davits was a tall Englishman with a hatchet face and a voice that carried—she heard later that his name was Mr. Lightoller—and he had put four or five women into the boat, and each one of them came back out again. A woman would be led to the rail and she would look down at the boat swinging over the sea, ten feet across the dark, and she would say, "I'm not going. Nothing has happened. Why should I go?"

And the officer would say, "Madam, please." And the woman would say, "Nothing has happened."

The band played. The steam roared. It came out of the after end of the ship in a great white column and the noise of it was a sort of wall around the whole scene, so that everybody on the boat deck was shouting in this enormous silence, and the little groups of people stood behind the boats waiting to be told what to do.

At a little before half past twelve, a rocket went up.

Arwen saw it go. It went up from the forward part of the ship with a hiss and a small thump and then broke into five white stars that hung and sank, and the whole of the boat deck was lit for a second and every face on it turned up, and then the stars went out and the dark came back down like a lid, and the people stood there in the cold and looked at each other, and nobody said anything at all.

"Well," Mrs. Penhallow said. "That is that. That is what I have been waiting to hear."

---

The thing that surprised her most, afterwards, was how long it took.

She had thought—she had never thought about it, that was the truth of it—but if she had thought about it she would have thought that a ship going down was quick, like a lantern being blown out. It was not. It went on and on and it took two hours and a half, and in that time she stood on a boat deck in a cork jacket with her hands in her sleeves, and the coldest she had ever been, and watched the sea come to them.

It was the sound that told you.

At half past twelve she had been cold; at a quarter past one she had ice in her bones. And at some point she had begun to notice that when she stood at the rail and looked down, the boat deck was not so high above the water as it had been. First the lettering of the ship's name, up forward, was only a few feet above the sea. Then the starboard side of the forward well deck began to be covered; she could see it from where she stood, a black shine coming over the plates.

A man near her began to pray out loud, in a language she did not know.

"Go and take a boat, Mrs. Meredith," Mrs. Penhallow said.

"I'll wait."

"You will not wait. There is nothing to wait for."

"I am waiting for my maid."

"Your maid is a sensible girl and she is somewhere with a boat by now, being sensible, precisely because you told her to be, and if she comes up here and finds you and there is no boat left for her, she will not thank you for your loyalty. What she'll do is die in your company, which is not a favour." Mrs. Penhallow's voice was perfectly level. "I am sixty-four and widowed and childless and I have no one to answer to and I intend to sit in that boat with my hands folded and be a nuisance to nobody. You are thirty years old and you have an entire life in front of you, and there is a woman on this deck who cannot bring herself to move and needs to see somebody move. Go and be her."

Arwen did not answer. She could not.

"Go on," Mrs. Penhallow said. "I shall see you on the *Carpathia*. I've had the tickets sent ahead."

---

Number twelve had been swung out and the falls were being led, and the officer with the hatchet face was at the rail with his hand up, and there was a queue of women going down into it—a stewardess, two girls with their arms round each other, a woman with no hat at all and a servant behind her with a baby she would not hand over to anyone.

"Just one moment, madam." Lightoller put his hand out to stop a man in a tweed coat. "Not you."

"There's no women present."

"I said no women present, sir, and I meant at that particular instant. Stand back."

The boat was hanging at the davits with perhaps thirty people in it. There was room for more and nobody to put in it. An old woman was refusing to go down the falls; two men were trying to help her and she was saying, "I have never been carried in my life."

Arwen thought: *Iestyn will not know what happened to me for four days.*

She thought: *the whistle is in the pocket of my grey coat, and the grey coat is in my cabin.*

She thought: *I have not had a single thought in nine years that was not about myself.*

She went forward. The deck was beginning to slant and it was a strange feeling, like standing in a room in a house that was settling. She passed behind the boat and there was an officer running down the deck towards her with his cap gone and his face black, and she had half a second to step aside, and she did, and when he passed she caught the side of his face in the light from the open door, and it was not a face she knew at all. The face of a man who has been doing something terrible for an hour.

She got to the rail. The gap between the ship and the boat was three feet, and below it the black water came up and went away and came up again, and the boat rose and fell against the ship's side with the falls groaning.

"Now, madam," the officer said. "Now, please."

She put her foot on the gunwale and her hand on the shoulder of the man holding the falls, and she found that she was above the boat and looking down into it, and there were thirty faces turned up to her in the dark, and beyond them nothing but black water and the lights of the ship going down its side in rows.

"One thing," she said. "There is a girl in second class. Her name is Corrigan. If I am not—"

"You'll find her on the *Carpathia*," Lightoller said. "Into the boat, madam, if you please."

She went over. She caught her skirt on the way and it ripped and she did not care. She landed awkwardly on a thwart and hands were on her at once and she was pushed down into the bottom of the boat, and the boat was hard and wet and there was an inch of water in the bottom of it, and somebody's arm was against her face, and the whole thing stank of tar and of the sea.

She sat up and got her bearings. She was in a wooden boat, and the ship was above her, and she had never in her life seen anything as big as she was.

"Fend off," a voice above her shouted. "Fend off there, or you'll have the plugs out of her."

The falls began to run. There was a man in the boat with a white armband and a soft Irish voice saying, "Easy now, easy, hold her off with the oar, hold her off, that's it, that's the way," and the *Titanic* rose past them, and rose, and rose, and it went on rising for a very long time.

Arwen looked up. There were people at every rail on every deck, a crowd of them, hundreds of faces, and they were looking down at the boat, and every one of them was silent.

Somebody in the boat began to cry, and then stopped.

"Where's the officer?" a woman said. "Who's in charge? Where is the officer?"

"There's no officer in this boat, ma'am," the Irishman said, and his voice was apologetic, and Arwen's whole body went cold. "They're all forward. There's a quartermaster went down in twelve but he's not in her, he's gone up to the bridge. It's only me and this gentleman here with the oars."

"Then what are we to do?"

"We're to pull away," he said. "That's what we're to do. We're to pull away and wait, and there's a dozen ships coming, and it's only a matter of the getting away from her side before she—"

He stopped.

Before she sucks us down, was what he did not say.

"Give me an oar," said Arwen.

---

# VI. The Work

The first thing Harold saw when he came out of the officers' quarters was a man in a dress suit standing in the middle of the boat deck with a glass of whisky in his hand, laughing.

He would think about that man for the rest of his life. Not with contempt; he never managed contempt for him. The man was not drunk, or not very. He was laughing because another man had said something funny about the noise, and the two of them were standing in the dark in the cold with their collars up and their glasses in their hands, and behind them, forty feet away, the forward well deck was already beginning to go under.

"Get below and put your belts on," Harold said, and did not stop to see whether they did.

The steam went on roaring. It was coming out of the escape pipe aft of the fourth funnel with a noise like a train in a tunnel, and it had the whole after end of the ship in a fog of warm wet vapour, and men were shouting into it and being swallowed. Harold went forward along the starboard side and found Mr. Murdoch at number seven.

Murdoch had his hand up and his voice was going out flat and clear into the noise, and he was doing three things at once: telling the men on the falls to take the strain, telling the steward to get the women back from the rail, telling a man in a dinner jacket that he could not go. He had the face of a man on the last hole of a long game.

"Lowe. Get the covers off these boats. Then take the falls on five and seven and get them away. Where's the list?"

"There isn't one, sir."

"There's a list." Murdoch turned his head. "There's a list in the chart room, get it. I want the boat numbers and the capacity and the number of souls in each."

"There's no time for a list, sir."

Murdoch looked at him. In the light from the deck the man's eyes were perfectly steady.

"Then there's no time for a list," he said. "Get them away. Fill them from the deck below as well as this one; there's women on B deck who'll not come up. And Lowe—" he was already turning away "—fill them. Do you hear me? Fill them."

"Yes, sir."

He filled them as much as they would let him.

That was the thing. That was the whole of the thing, and it took him twenty years to be able to say it without his voice going. He filled them as much as they would let him.

At number five he put in a stewardess, and a woman with a baby, and two women who came up out of the crowd and climbed in without a word, and then he looked round for more and there were none. There was a rail with forty women behind it and he went along the rail saying, "Madam. Madam, will you come into the boat, please," and they looked at him and looked at the boat swinging out over the black water with two feet of fall under her and they said, "No."

Some of them laughed. He was to remember that: three women at the rail of the *Titanic* at twenty past midnight, laughing at him, kindly, the way you laugh at a nervous host.

"It's all right, sir. We'll go in the morning."

"There's no morning, madam."

"Oh, don't be silly."

Number five went away with forty-one in her against a capacity of sixty-five, and Pitman went with her, and Pitman was a man of thirty-eight with a wife and two children and had been at sea since he was eighteen, and he went because Murdoch told him to, and Harold watched him go down the falls with his hand on the tiller and his face turned up, and thought: *he thinks he's coming back.*

Number seven went with twenty-seven. Twenty-seven. He counted them himself, standing at the rail with the falls in his hands, and he counted again because he did not believe it, and there were twenty-seven, and among them three men who had got in on the far side when nobody was looking, and one of them was laughing.

"Open the gangway doors," a voice said at his elbow. It was a passenger, a big man, grey-faced, holding his hat. "You must open the gangway doors, officer. There are hundreds of people below who don't know what's happening. Open the doors and they can come up to the boats."

The gangway doors. It was not a bad idea. It was the first sensible thing anybody had said to him all night, and he turned it over in his mind for a second, and he thought: *there's no man to spare to work them, and no man to stand at them, and no officer to keep order at the other side.* He said, "I'll pass it up," which was a way of saying no, and the man said, "Will you?" and Harold said, "I will," and he did, later, or he believed he did, and nothing was ever done about it, and he would hear that man's voice in his head at three o'clock in the morning for the rest of his life.

---

At number one he lost his temper.

Number one was the emergency cutter on the starboard side forward, a wooden boat of twenty-five souls, and by the time he got to her she had been swung out and there were six people in her and one of them was Sir Cosmo Duff Gordon and another was Lady Duff Gordon and another was a woman in a kimono with a Pekingese under her arm, and a fireman was standing by the falls and there was nobody else.

"Who's in charge of this boat?"

"I am," said a man in a bowler hat, and Harold looked at him, at the bowler hat and the white flannels and the face of a man who had never in his life pulled an oar.

"You're not."

"I beg your pardon."

"You're not in charge of her, sir, you're in her, and there's a difference." Harold got his foot on the gunwale. "Where's your crew? Have you got a seaman?"

"He has." The man in the bowler hat pointed at the fireman. "He's a stoker."

"I don't care if he's the Archbishop of Canterbury, has he ever rowed a boat?" Harold turned to the fireman. "Have you?"

"I've rowed a bit, sir."

"How much is a bit?"

"Boat's crew on the *Oceanic*, sir, when I was a lad."

"Then you'll do." He was aware of his own voice going up and he could not stop it. "Get in. Get in the bow and take an oar and if anybody in this boat tells you to do a thing you don't do it unless they've a seaman's ticket in their pocket. D'you hear me?"

"Sir."

"And you," he said to the man in the bowler hat, "you'll take the other oar and you'll do as you're told by him, and if any of you in this boat has a complaint about it you can make it to the Board of Trade in the morning."

There was a silence. Somebody in the boat said something under their breath. Harold did not care. He was past caring about it in a way that frightened him a little, even then, even standing there. He had six people in a boat for twenty-five and a deck full of people behind him who would not come and a ship that was going down by the head at a rate he could now see with his own eyes, and something in him had snapped its collar and was running.

He walked away from number one without waiting to see her go.

---

It was when he was going aft along the starboard side that he heard it.

A woman's voice, twenty feet behind him, saying something to a steward. He could not hear the words. It was the shape of the voice, the rise in it, the way it went up on the last word like a question at the end of a statement, and it was a voice out of the wall behind the cemetery in Barmouth and out of the vestry at a concert and out of the flat black water off the boat deck five hours ago, and he turned round so fast he hit his shoulder on a davit.

There was a crowd. There were a hundred people between him and the top of the aft staircase: stewards, women with rugs, a man on his knees being sick over the rail, a group of firemen coming up the ladder from the well deck with their gear in their hands. He could see, over all of it, a grey coat.

He went three steps towards it and then he stopped, because Murdoch was at number nine and had his hand up and was calling his name.

"Lowe! Lowe, get these boats away. Nine and eleven. Get them away."

He looked back at the grey coat. It was moving away from him, forward, into the crowd, and he could not see the hat.

"Yes, sir," he said.

He got nine away. He got eleven away. He filled them as much as they would let him, and at eleven a woman with a fur coat refused three times and then climbed in and then climbed out again, and the boat went with sixty-three in her, which was a good boat, and he felt a moment's absurd pride in it, and then a wash of shame, because filling a boat is not an achievement; it is what a man does with a bucket.

It was the water that had changed. He noticed it while the falls were running on eleven: the water was not ten feet below the boat deck any more, it was six, and when she rolled she was rolling with the water on her, and the boats swinging out over her had a whole new angle to them and the men on the falls had to hold her hard against the ship's side to keep the sea from taking the boat.

And she was down by the head with her propellers out of the water, and every now and then, when she rolled, the whole ship rang like a bell from a propeller coming out and going under.

That was the moment Harold Lowe became certain. There had been an hour of not being certain. Now there was a great many lights still burning and there were people laughing two decks up and there was a band playing, and there was no longer any doubt in him anywhere.

He went across to the port side. There was nothing left for him on the starboard.

---

The port side was a different ship.

He came round the forward end of the superstructure and the first thing he saw was that the boats were going away, and the second thing was that they were going away half full, and the third was that the officer in charge of them intended to keep going that way.

Lightoller had his back to the bridge and his arm out over the rail and his voice going out into the roar of steam. He was not shouting, particularly. He was speaking in a voice that carried, and what he was saying was: "Women and children. No men. Women and children. Stand back, sir."

There were men in that crowd who had been told to stand back four times. There was a man holding his wife's hand, who would not be separated from her, and there was a woman with two children and a husband, and Harold watched the husband put his hands on his wife's shoulders and say something to her and then step back into the crowd, and the wife go down the falls without a sound, and it took three seconds and it was the bravest thing he saw that night.

Moody was at fourteen with a lantern. The Sixth Officer had his cap on straight and his boots on and his face, in the lantern light, was the face of a boy of twenty-four who has been given a job at last and intends to do it perfectly. He looked up and saw Harold and something in his shoulders let go.

"Thank God," he said. "Will you take her? Sixteen's away, and fourteen's half loaded and there's no officer for her, and Mr. Lightoller wants an officer in each of these boats back here and there's none to spare."

"Get in her, then."

"I'm not going in her. I'm going forward to the collapsibles."

"Moody—"

"Mr. Lowe, I've got the falls and the crew and I know what I'm doing, and you've been on this deck for an hour and a half and you've not stopped moving." Moody said it quite kindly. "Get in the boat. She's yours."

It was the last conversation Harold Lowe ever had with James Paul Moody.

"All right," he said. "Get the women down to me. And get me some men—I want four men in this boat that can pull, and I want them now, and I don't want volunteers, I want men who've been told."

He climbed into fourteen.

---

The boat was crowded with women and children and their belongings. A woman had a box on her knees. A child of about six was wedged under a thwart and would not come out. There was a man in the stern sheets—a passenger, a big fellow of fifty in a tweed coat—who had the tiller, and Harold said, "Give me that," and took it, and the man said, "I'm only trying to help," and Harold said, "You can help by sitting still and doing what I say, sir, and I'll thank you not to argue."

"Don't you speak to me—"

"Sir." Harold put his hand up. "Not tonight. I'm not going to argue with you tonight."

They filled her from the deck below and from the deck above, and it took a long time, and the whole time the ship was going down.

That was the thing nobody understood who had not been there: it took a long time, and it was the longest hour of his life, and it was made of small operations. Get the women to the rail. Get the child out from under the thwart. Take the box off the woman's knees and give it to a steward and tell her it will come later. Hold the boat in against the ship's side with an oar because the swell was beginning to come up the side of her now and the falls were slack and then snatching. Say "steady" forty times. Say "there's room, madam, there's plenty of room, come along."

And all the time, at the back of it, under the noise of the steam and the shouting and the crying of a child and the band—the band had stopped some time ago, he did not know when—at the back of it, the ship was making a new sound. It was a low grinding, groaning sound, and it was not the engines. It was the whole fabric of her, twelve decks of steel and wood and a hundred thousand rivets, being asked to hold a shape she had been built not to hold.

"Mr. Lowe." A seaman was at his elbow. "Mr. Lowe, they're coming over the rail aft of us. The men off the lower decks. They've seen the boat and they're coming across the well deck."

He turned.

They were coming. A dozen of them, more, dark shapes moving along the ship's side on the lower decks, and the water was up to the well deck now and they were wading, and the first of them had his hands on the rail above the boat.

"Get back," Harold shouted.

They did not get back.

He had a revolver in his pocket. He had put it in his pocket at a quarter to twelve and had not thought about it since, and now it was in his hand without his having decided anything about it, and he fired over their heads, into the dark, once, and then again, and then a third time at the second deck as the boat went down past it, and each shot made a flat crack that the night swallowed at once, and he heard himself shouting, "Get back, you fools, get back or I'll shoot you down like dogs."

They got back.

The boat went down past the third deck and there was nobody at the rail, and the water came up to meet them, and the people in the boat had gone silent.

Harold put the revolver back in his pocket. His hand was shaking, and he looked at it with a kind of detached interest, and then he looked up, and there, forty feet above him, at a rail on the flooded A deck, standing perfectly still with her hands on the rail, was a woman in a grey coat.

He knew her. It was too far, and there was no light, and there were two hundred people on the decks above her, but he knew her, and he could not have said how, and it was the way she stood: on her own, still, not hurrying, with her shoulders square, the way she had stood at the after rail of the boat deck at half past eight and looked at the stars.

He could not go back up. There was no way back up. The falls were running and the boat was in the water, and behind her the sea was coming across the deck, and she was standing there looking down.

He shouted her name.

He shouted it twice and the wind and the steam took it and the people in the boat turned their faces up to the ship, thinking he was shouting at someone below, and then the boat swung, and the rail, and the grey coat, and the whole of the *Titanic*'s side went behind him and there was nothing over his shoulder but black water and the lights of a ship dying.

"Sit down," he said to the boat. "Sit down, all of you. Ship your oars. Get her head round. Pull."

---

He got them a hundred and fifty yards off, and then two hundred, because the suction of her when she went would take a boat like fourteen down with her and there was no arguing with that.

She was still going, and she had begun to go faster, and she was going at an angle now that made the whole of her port side a wall of lights at an unnatural slant, and the lights were still burning. That was the thing that undid the people in the boat. She had been sinking for an hour and a half and the lights were still on and the windows were still warm, and a ship with her lights on is not a ship that is going to die.

A woman behind him said, "It's all right. She's not going. Look at her, she's not going."

"Ma'am," Harold said, "she's going."

"You don't know—"

"I know. Be quiet now, please. Be quiet and look at her, because in a minute she'll be gone and you'll want to have seen it."

It was an hour and a half of a night that he had spent going from one thing to the next as fast as a man can move, and now there was nothing to do but hold the tiller and look.

The band had stopped. The steam had stopped—the roar of it had cut out at some point, and in the silence the ship was making the other sound, the grinding, and it went on and on and it got louder, and then the lights went out.

Not all at once. Forward first, the bow going black and the blackness running aft along the decks the way a fuse runs, and the stern still lit, a great blaze of light up in the air at an angle, and then a shock through the water that Harold felt in the tiller, and the stern came up out of the sea.

It came up and it kept coming up, and it was all lit, all of it, every window, and there were people on it. He could see them. There were hundreds of people on the stern of the *Titanic*, on the well deck and the poop, and they were climbing, and the great thing went up and up and stood there, two hundred feet of it, black and gold against the stars, and it stayed.

It stayed for a long time. He would say afterwards, at the inquiry, that it was a quarter of an hour, and a man in a wig would tell him that could not be, and he would say, "Then I am mistaken, my Lord," and hold to it anyway. It stood there, roaring. The noise of it was the worst thing he ever heard: not a crash, not an explosion, but a long tearing, grinding, boiling roar, and underneath it, very faint, a sound like the crying of birds.

Then it went.

It went down and the water came over it, and the last thing he saw of her was the stern going under with the flagstaff still up, and then there was nothing there at all, and the sea was flat and grey and the stars were on it, and the whole of the middle of the night was empty.

The people in the boat were screaming. Some of them. Others were praying, and one man was saying over and over, "My God, my God, my God," in a conversational voice.

"Now," Harold said.

He said it to himself. He had not meant to say it out loud.

"Now what?" said the woman behind him.

And then they heard it. All of them, at once, from every part of the boat, the way you hear a thing that everybody has been waiting for: a sound coming across the water from the place where the ship had been.

It was not screaming, exactly. It was a long, high, unbroken wall of sound, made of hundreds of voices, and it rose and it fell and it went on, and it had no shape to it at all. It was the sound of a great many people in cold water.

Harold Lowe sat in the stern of number fourteen with his hand on the tiller and listened to it, and did not move.

---

# VII. The Cries

There were fifty-two people in number twelve and one of them had a bucket.

That was the arithmetic that governed the next four hours. Fifty-two people, one bucket, and a boat that had been in the water for a quarter of an hour before anybody thought to look for the plug.

Arwen knew about the plug. She had been told about the plug on Saturday afternoon by a man standing under number three with his hand flat on the planking, and she had listened to him the way one listens to a man who loves his subject. There was a plug in the bottom of every boat in the world, and when the boat was on the chocks it was out, to let the rain run through, and when the boat was in the water it was in, screwed home, and if it was not in, the sea came up through it in a thin steady pipe like a tap left running.

"Have you looked at the plug?" she said.

The quartermaster's name was Hodges. He was a small Southampton man of forty with a scar along his jaw, and he had one hand on the tiller and one on the sheet, and he was the only man in the boat who knew what he was doing, and he had been trying to keep the boat's head up into the sea and fend off the ship at the same time for twenty minutes.

"What?"

"The plug. In the bottom. Have you—"

"God Almighty." He said it quietly, and then, "Pass her round. Find me the plug and screw it home. Where's the bucket?"

There was no bucket. There was an old tin can that had held paint once, and a woman's hat with a wide brim, and after a minute a man produced his boot.

They bailed with the can and the hat and the boot. The water was over the bottom boards and rising, and the boat was so heavy with people that she sat down in the water like a loaded punt, and the sea came in over the gunwale every time she rolled.

Arwen bailed. She had never bailed anything in her life. She knelt in the bottom of the boat with her skirts soaked to the waist and the tin can in her hands and she scooped the water up and threw it over the side, and it went into the black and did not make a sound, and she did it again, four hundred times, and after a while the water in the bottom was only an inch deep and someone found the plug and screwed it home with a knife blade and the boat came up like something taking a breath.

"Right," said Hodges. "Now she'll swim."

She sat back on her heels. Her hands were shaking and her arms were shaking and she was so cold that she had stopped being able to feel her feet, and she was alive in a way she had not been in years, and she thought: *I have done one useful thing in my life and it was bailing a boat with a paint tin.*

---

They were perhaps three hundred yards off when the ship went.

Nobody in the boat was talking by then. The women were sitting in a long row with their rugs pulled up to their chins, and the children had gone quiet, and there was a man in the bow who had been praying aloud and then stopped, and a young woman behind Arwen who had been crying steadily since the boat was lowered, and then stopped.

The *Titanic* had gone strange. She had come up out of the water at the stern and she was showing her three propellers, and the whole of her was lit and at an angle of forty-five degrees, and the lights were on in the smoking room where Arwen had taken coffee four hours before.

Somebody in the boat said, "She's coming back up."

Somebody else said, "She's broken in half."

"Don't be stupid, she's the biggest—"

The lights went out, in a line, forward to aft, and there was a pause, and then the lights aft came back on again, and then there was a great soft thundering roar and the whole shape of her went down and there was nothing but the sea and the stars and the black space where she had been.

The sound of it was not the sound she expected. It was not an explosion. It was the sound of a very large building being torn down somewhere out of sight, and then a long settling, and then the water closing over.

And then the crying started.

At first Arwen thought it was the gulls. They had had gulls at Barmouth, in the harbour, in the early morning, and there was a quality in the sound that was exactly the crying of gulls over a mackerel shoal.

Then she understood, and everything in her that had been holding itself up went out of her like water out of a cracked jar.

They were hundreds of yards away and it was a flat calm night and every voice carried. It was a wall of sound, and it went on and it did not stop, and it had no words in it at all, and the worst of it was that in among the high crying there were other voices, deeper, calling one word over and over—*here, here, here*—and one voice, somewhere far off to the left of the sound, that called a woman's name three times and then stopped.

"Pull back," Arwen said.

She said it to Hodges. She did not know she was going to say it.

"Ma'am—"

"Turn the boat. There are people in the water."

"There's fifty-two people in this boat." Hodges's voice was terrible and level. "Look at her. She's got two foot of freeboard. You put me alongside a hundred men in the water tonight and they'll come over the gunwale and we'll all be in it, and I'll not do it."

"Then take some of us out and—"

"Put you where, ma'am? We're three hundred yards from a ship's grave in a wooden boat with no officer and no lamp and no oars to spare. This isn't a matter of being brave." He was crying. That was the thing; he was crying while he said it, in the mild, reasonable voice of a man explaining a fact. "I've a wife and four in Southampton. I've a daughter your age. Don't ask me to kill her for the sake of a stranger."

A woman at the tiller end said, "My husband is in that water."

"And if we go back we'll be in it with him," Hodges said, "and I'm sorry, ma'am. I'm sorry. I'm truly sorry."

Nobody argued with him. That was the thing Arwen could never explain afterwards: that nobody argued. Fifty-two people sat in a boat in the dark and listened to six or seven hundred human beings freeze to death in the sea, and no one stood up, and no one said let us go, and the reason was not cowardice, or not the ordinary sort. It was that every one of them had somebody beside them whose hand they were holding.

She held the hand of a woman she did not know. She had no idea who took her own.

---

There is a thing that happens to a person who listens to that sound for an hour and a half.

It stops being people. That is the horror of it, and it is a thing Arwen never afterwards found a way to say in company: that the crying became a feature of the night, like the cold or the dark, and there came a point when she was aware that it was quieter than it had been and felt, for a second, a flicker of something like relief, and then knew what the relief was about and had to put her fist in her mouth.

It thinned out. That was the word she used, later, in her own head. It thinned out the way a congregation thins out after a service, voices going away one by one, and by three o'clock there were only a few left, and they were not calling any more, they were singing.

She heard that. She was certain of it for the rest of her life and would not be argued out of it. Somewhere out in the dark to the west of them a long way off, a voice, and then more than one, singing a hymn. She could not hear what it was. It had the shape of a hymn, the slow rising line of it, and it went on for perhaps a minute and a half, and then it stopped, and then there was nothing at all.

She would be asked about it, years afterwards, by a young man who had come to the house to write about the disaster for a magazine. He wanted to know if it was "Nearer, My God, to Thee." She said she did not know. He said that several survivors said it was. She said that she had heard something that was sung by more than one voice and that she had not been able to hear the tune, and that she had thought at the time—and still thought—that it did not matter what it was, only that they had chosen to do it together.

---

At about a quarter past three, a voice came across the water saying, "Boat ahoy. Boat ahoy, there."

It was not Hodges who answered. It was a woman in the bow, who stood up so suddenly that the boat rocked and shouted, "Here! Here! We're here!" and then sat down and burst into tears.

"Pull over to me," the voice said. "Have you an officer in that boat?"

"There's no officer," Hodges called. "It's Hodges, quartermaster. There's fifty-odd in her and she's leaking and she's got no plug in her till we put one in her."

There was a pause, and then the voice said, in a different tone, a tone that Arwen knew so well that her whole body turned over, "Hodges, is that you? This is Lowe. Bring her alongside and make fast to my stern, and I'll take you in tow. Have you got oars?"

"Two."

"Then ship them and row, mun, and don't sit there arguing with me."

Somebody in the boat laughed. That was the other thing that Arwen could never explain: that in the middle of the worst night of the world, a voice out of Barmouth saying *mun* made four women laugh in a lifeboat.

---

He came alongside out of the dark with a lantern in the bow of his boat and the whole of him black against it, and he had a mast in her, and—

"You've got a sail up," Arwen said.

He turned the lantern. She saw his face, and then she saw him see her, and something went out of him; she watched it happen, the way his shoulders came down.

"Arwen."

"Hello, Harold."

"Are you—" He stopped. He looked at her, at the boat, at the fifty-two people in it. "Are you hurt?"

"I'm cold. There's a child here with no coat. There's a woman in the stern who has been unconscious for an hour and I don't know if she's—"

"Get her into my boat." He was already turning. "I've got brandy and I've got blankets and I've got a man who was a hospital orderly in Liverpool. Pass her over. Gently, now. Pass her over the gunwales, don't lift her."

They passed her over. It took four of them and it was like passing a bundle of laundry, and the woman did not wake, and Harold put his own coat over her and knelt down and put two fingers against her throat and stayed there for what seemed to Arwen a very long time, and then said, "She's alive. It's slow but it's there." And then, to nobody: "Get the brandy into her. A teaspoon. Not more, or she'll choke."

"Mr. Lowe." That was a woman in his boat, one of the ones he had taken in off the *Titanic*'s deck. "Mr. Lowe, are we going back?"

"Not yet."

"When?"

"When the cries have thinned out," he said. "If I go back now I'll come alongside a hundred and fifty men in the water and they'll swamp this boat and we'll lose the lot of us, and I'll have killed fifty people to save twenty."

"That's what I said," Hodges said.

"Aye," said Harold, "and you were right, and I'll not have it said of you that you were a coward, because you weren't." He straightened up. "But I'm going back. Only I'm going back when I can do something when I get there, and not before. Do you understand me?"

Nobody answered.

"I asked if you understood me."

"Yes," several people said, at once, like children.

"Then here's what we're going to do."

---

What they did took an hour and a half.

He had four boats in a line now, and then five: fourteen, and twelve, and ten, and four, and the collapsible that somebody had got off the roof and that was half full of water and had to be baled out with a hat before a woman could sit in it. He went from one to the next in his own boat, and he put his people into the others. Not all of them. That was the thing Arwen watched him do, and it taught her more about him than fifteen years of letters could have.

"Mrs. Meredith's boat has fifty-two in her and she's got no officer and no plug and a woman who's dying. Number ten has thirty-five and a good coxswain. I want every woman out of fourteen that can be got out, into ten and four, and I want it done in a manner that doesn't frighten the children." He was tying knots while he talked, and the knots were perfect, and his hands were not shaking any more. "And I want five men who can pull. Not five men who want to come. Five men who can pull. You—" he pointed "—can you row?"

"I've rowed a boat on the Serpentine."

"Then you can't row. Sit down." He pointed again. "You. Where are you from?"

"Glasgow."

"Have you been in a boat on salt water?"

"Aye. The Clutha."

"Get in the bow. You, sir, you're a fireman, aren't you? Get in. And you, and you, and you—can you take an oar without hitting the man next to you?"

"I can try."

"Then God help us all, get in."

They got the mast up. He did that himself with two seamen, and it was the strangest thing in that whole strange night to watch him do it: he became, for twenty minutes, entirely happy. He unstepped the mast from where it lay along the boat and set it in its step and got the halyard and the gaff and the boom and shook out the sail and looked up at it, and the sail filled with the small breeze that had come up out of the north-west and the boat began to move through the water on her own, quietly, with the sound of water going past a hull.

"There," he said. "Now we'll not kill ourselves getting there."

"Where did you learn to sail?" Arwen asked.

He looked at her. In the lantern light his face was black on one side and gold on the other, and he was so tired that his eyes had gone deep into his head.

"Barmouth," he said. "Where do you think?"

---

They waited until half past three. He sat in the stern with the tiller in the crook of his arm and the lantern hooded and did not speak, and once, when Arwen looked at him, she saw that he had his hand over his eyes.

She did not go to him. There were sixty people in the two boats and she was one of them, and there was a woman beside her whose child had stopped shivering, which is a thing that a mother knows about and pretends not to.

At twenty to four he stood up.

"Right. Now."

They went back. Twenty men at the oars—his Glasgow man, his fireman, three seamen, and a boy of about seventeen from the engine room who had come off the ship with nothing but his drawers and a jersey and who rowed like a machine. Harold stood in the stern and conning-ordered them—"Port. Port. Steady. Starboard a touch"—and the sail came down and was stowed and the boat went through the water at a speed that Arwen would not have believed of men with oars.

The cries had almost stopped. There were two voices somewhere to the left and one a long way off to the right, and then only the left, and then nothing.

Ahead of them, the sea was covered with things.

That was the thing nobody had told her. She had thought the sea would be empty. It was not empty. It was full of furniture. There were chairs floating, and a table with all four legs up, and a piano lid, and a great many cushions, and a hundred thousand pieces of cork and wood, and white things that were not cushions, and the whole of it going gently up and down on a sea like oil.

"Starboard," Harold said. "Starboard, there's a man."

They got him. He was alive, and he was naked to the waist and grey as a fish and he had been in the water for forty minutes, and he could not speak, and they got him into the boat and put blankets round him and somebody sat on his feet.

They got a second one, and he was alive too, and he had a lifebelt and a dinner jacket on and had lost one boot, and he said, over and over, "I'm all right, I'm all right, is there a doctor, my wife is in a boat, is there a doctor."

"Where's your wife?"

"In a boat."

"Then she's all right, sir. Sit down."

A third. This one was not alive, or seemed not to be, and Harold had him brought in anyway and spent a long time with him, and in the end the man sat up and was sick and said something in Italian.

"Four," Harold said. He said it flatly, to himself, like a man counting cargo. "Four."

They went on for another twenty minutes and got nobody. There was a boy in the water with his arms out and his eyes open who had been dead for some time, and a fireman, and a man in evening dress who had his hands folded, and the boat went past them all, and Harold stood up in the bow and looked at each one before he said, "Pull on."

And then, at a little after five, in the first grey of the morning that was not light yet but the promise of it, they found the collapsible.

It had been washed off the roof of the officers' quarters, or it had floated off, and it was half under water, and there were men in it, and some of them were not men any more. Arwen saw the shape of it from thirty yards and did not understand what she was seeing, and then she did.

"Pull," Harold said. "Pull, there's people in her. Pull!"

The collapsible had been in the water for two hours and a half with its sides collapsed and it had taken on water until it was a rubber tub half sunk, and there were fifteen men in it standing up to their knees, and three of them were dead, and they had been standing in the same place for two hours so as not to fall over.

"Come on," Harold shouted. "Come on, then, come aboard, we've got room."

Nobody moved.

"Come on!"

A man in the collapsible said, in a voice that had been worn down to nothing, "The bodies."

"What?"

"There's three dead in here and we can't—" The man stopped. "We can't move them. We've been standing round them for two hours."

There was a small silence. Then Harold said, in a voice that was quite quiet and that went across the water like a bell:

"Before you come aboard my boat you'll turn those bodies over and make sure they're dead."

---

They turned them over. It took a long time and it was done by two men and one of them was crying, and Arwen turned her face away, and the woman beside her took her hand and held it hard and said, "Don't look, madam. Look at me. Look at me."

Twelve men came off the collapsible alive.

They came in over the gunwale one at a time, and the last one could not stand and had to be lifted, and by then the sky in the east was grey and the sea was grey and everything in the world had a flat, clean, morning look to it which was unbearable.

Harold was standing in the stern, and the last man had just been got aboard, and there was nobody left in the collapsible but the dead, and he stood looking down at it.

Then he said, "Get the oars out. We'll pull for the other boats," and he turned round, and he saw Arwen, and she saw him remember.

He had been awake for twenty-five hours and in the water for none of it and had done more work than any man she had ever watched, and his face, in the grey light, was the face of a man coming up out of a long dive.

"You're alive," he said.

"I'm alive."

He sat down on the after thwart all at once, as though somebody had cut the strings of him, and put his face in his hands for about four seconds, and then took them away and said, "Right. Let's go and find the others."

---

They found the others. The boats had scattered in the night and now, in the dawn, they began to come together the way boats do, with people standing up and waving their coats, and by six o'clock there were perhaps a dozen of them in sight, and they lay on the water like a regatta.

The *Carpathia* came up out of the south-east at half past four and it took two hours for her to get to them, and Arwen watched her come with the flat, exhausted love of a woman watching a lamp being lit in a window. She was small. That was the first thing: she was a small ship, and she had one funnel of a dirty yellow, and she came on through the light with her derricks swinging out and her boats going into the water, and every boat in the ocean began to pull towards her.

Arwen sat in number twelve with her hands in her lap and looked at the *Carpathia*, and beside her the woman whose child had stopped shivering began, at last, to cry.

"Is he—" Arwen began.

"He's asleep," the woman said. "He's asleep, the little—" and then she could not speak.

They were picked up at half past seven. The *Carpathia*'s boat came alongside and there was a man in the bow of it with a flask and a broad Scots voice saying, "Come up, come up, there's hot blankets and there's beef tea and there's a doctor, come up one at a time, take your time, there's no hurry now."

There was no hurry now.

Arwen went up the rope ladder. She had to be helped, because her hands would not close properly, and half way up the side of the *Carpathia* she stopped, and looked down, because she had remembered something.

Number fourteen was lying under the *Carpathia*'s counter with her sail down and her mast still stepped. Harold was in her, still, with two men, stowing the gear. He had his coat off and his sleeves rolled up and he was folding a blanket with enormous care, and the sailor beside him was saying something and Harold was laughing—actually laughing, his head back, in the morning light, in the middle of the Atlantic, at twenty to eight in the morning on the fifteenth of April, with the boat full of dead men's cork and a hundred and fifty of his own passengers already aboard the ship above him.

Arwen hung on the ladder and watched him fold a blanket, and she thought: *I have known two men in my life, and one of them is the man I married and the other is the man who is folding that blanket, and I have four days to decide what I am going to do about it.*

Somebody above her said, "Careful, ma'am, take my hand."

She took the hand and went up into the light.

---

# VIII. The Count

Harold Lowe came up the *Carpathia*'s side at twenty past eight in the morning with his boat's gear stowed, and the first thing that happened to him was that a stranger put a cup of beef tea in his hand and told him to drink it.

He drank it. He was standing on the after deck of a Cunarder with a hundred and forty of his own boat's people and two hundred and forty more from the other boats milling about him, and the deck was covered in blankets and women in men's coats and children being carried and a woman screaming because she had found her sister and a man sitting on a hatch cover being sick into a bucket, and he drank the beef tea and it went into him like something being poured into a hole in the ground.

Then he went to find Mr. Rostron.

Rostron was on the bridge, and he had been on the bridge since half past four, and he had brought his ship through six miles of ice at twenty-five knots in the dark and stopped her in the middle of an icefield without putting a scratch on her, and when Harold came up he turned round and said, "Mr. Lowe. How many?"

"Four alive out of the water, sir, and twelve out of the collapsible. Fifteen in the collapsible, three dead."

"Four," Rostron said. He wrote it down. "What have you done with the collapsible?"

"Left her, sir. She had the dead in her."

"You'll have to make a report of that to your company." Rostron's face was grey with strain and lack of sleep, and there was something else in it too, something Harold recognised because he had it himself: the flat surface of a man who has been engaged in extremely difficult work for four hours and has not yet been given permission to stop. "Do you know the number of boats?"

"Thirteen boats and two collapsibles, sir. Twelve's still out but she's alongside."

"Very well. Two hundred and four in my boats to the eleven boats, and I've got—" he looked at the paper "—seven hundred and five aboard. Go and get some sleep, Mr. Lowe."

"I'd rather—"

"Go and get some sleep. That's not a courtesy, it's an order. You'll be wanted tonight, and I'll want you able to stand up."

---

He did not sleep. He lay down in a cabin that had been given up by a first class passenger, a Mr. and Mrs. somebody, who had gone down to the dining saloon to help and had not come back, and he lay on the bunk in his wet clothes and looked at the deckhead of another man's cabin, and after ten minutes he got up and went out.

The ship was in a state that he had seen twice before, after a wreck off the coast and after a fire at sea, and it is not a state that can be described in a report. Seven hundred people had come out of the water or off a boat into a ship that was built for a hundred and fifty in the second class and four hundred in the third; and every one of them had lost everything, and half of them had lost everybody, and the ship's company of the *Carpathia*—stewards, cooks, cabin boys, the lot, many of them nineteen years old—were doing what they had been trained to do and then going round the corner and being sick or weeping and then coming back.

A stewardess was going along the corridor with a tray of tea and a list, knocking on doors. Two men were carrying a mattress up the companionway with a woman on it. A woman in a sable coat—he remembered the coat, she had been on the *Titanic*, he had seen her at number six—was sitting on the floor with her back against the bulkhead holding a cup, and when she saw him she said, "Officer. Officer, my husband is not on the list."

"Which list, madam?"

"The purser's list. There are two lists. One in the dining saloon and one in the—" she lost the thread, and found it again. "They have him down as saved, but I know he is not saved, because I saw him on the deck, and I would like to know who is putting him down as saved."

"I'll find out, madam."

"You said that to me last night."

"Did I?" He looked at her. "Then I meant it, and I mean it now, and I'm sorry I've not done it yet."

She looked up at him for a moment, and her face did the thing that faces were doing all over that ship, and she said, "No. No, you didn't say it to me. I'm sorry. I've been asking everyone."

---

The number was fifteen hundred and twenty-three.

It came over the wireless and went round the ship in an hour, and it was the wrong number, it was less than half the truth, and it did more damage in ten minutes than the ice had done in two hours. Fifteen hundred and twenty-three lost. The ship had carried two thousand two hundred and eight. Two thousand two hundred and eight, and seven hundred and five in the boats, and the sum was done by every passenger on the *Carpathia* that day, on the backs of menus and in the margins of telegram forms and in their own heads, over and over, the way a man checks a lock.

Twelve hundred dead when the boats were counted properly, and fifteen hundred when the wireless said it, and Harold sat in the officers' mess with a cup of tea going cold and did the sum a fourth time and then a fifth, and then he put the pencil down.

"Mr. Lowe."

It was Boxhall. He had been in a boat since ten past twelve and he had been firing rockets off her all night, and he had a face on him like a man who had been to a funeral and had another one to go to.

"Boxhall."

"They're saying there's to be an inquiry. In New York. Senator somebody. They'll want statements."

"Then they'll want statements."

"What will you say?"

Harold looked at him.

"About the boats," Boxhall said. "About the filling of them. They'll ask why they went away light, and they'll ask the officers, and I don't know what I'm going to say, and I've been sitting in there for an hour trying to find a way to say it that doesn't make it sound like—"

"It was my side," Harold said. "The starboard side, the first five. I put forty-one in a boat for sixty-five and I counted them myself and I went along the rail and I asked women to come and they laughed at me."

"So did everyone."

"Aye. Everyone." He turned the cup round on the table. "And if there was one officer on that deck who could have filled those boats by main force, it was Murdoch, and Murdoch is dead, so we'll none of us ever know what he'd have said. What I'll say is what I did. I'll not say *the women would not come* as if it were weather. They'd not come because they did not believe it, and they did not believe it because—" he stopped.

"Because what?"

"Because nobody told them," Harold said. "Because a woman on that deck at twenty past midnight looked at a boat with sixty-five seats in it and at the sea, and she'd been told by a man in a uniform all her life that the ship was unsinkable, and had believed him, and had been right to believe him, and the man in the uniform was still saying it while he filled her boat." He pushed the cup away. "I don't know. That's not for me to say either. I was a fifth officer. I'll answer for my own boat and no one else's."

---

On the Tuesday a man from the White Star Line came aboard at the head of a small party and had the officers into the second class smoking room and told them that they were not to speak to the newspapers.

He was a decent man, a passenger agent named Franklin with a grey moustache and a bad conscience, and he said it badly. He said that the company wished to have one account and not forty, that there would be an inquiry, that anything said now would be said before the facts were known, and that the officers would be looked after. He said that last part twice.

"Are we to lie?" Harold said.

"Nobody is asking you to lie, Mr. Lowe."

"You're asking us to say nothing, which on a ship's deck amounts to the same thing, because the fellows from the papers will write what they're given and if they're given nothing they'll write what they're not given." Harold was aware that his voice had gone loud and did not care. "There's seven hundred people upstairs who've lost everything they own and half of them have lost everybody they love, and there's a hundred reporters on the water outside this ship, and if we say nothing they'll print the wreck of the *Titanic* as though it were a hotel fire."

"Mr. Lowe—"

"I'll say nothing to the papers," Harold said. "You have my word on that, and my word's worth something to me, and I'd be obliged if you'd not make me spend it cheaply. But when the inquiry comes I'll answer every question put to me and I'll answer it straight, and if the company doesn't like what I say it can say so in the newspapers and I'll not be reading them."

There was a silence in the second class smoking room. Boxhall was looking at the table. Pitman had his hands folded. Lightoller, who had a bad back and had been standing all morning, said, "I'm with Lowe," in a voice like a door closing.

Franklin said, "I'll put it to the company," which is what men say when they intend to do nothing, and went away.

"He's not a bad man either," Pitman said, afterwards.

"No," Harold said. "That's the trouble with the whole business. There's nobody in it that's a bad man. It's all been done by good men, one after another, each of them doing the sensible thing." He put his cap on. "It'd be a great deal easier if there was somebody to hang."

Two things he would remember about the *Carpathia*.

The first was the moment he saw Moody's name on the list.

It was not official and it was not a list; it was a piece of paper that a steward had made, going round the first class with the names of the officers who were saved, and Harold read it at the top of the grand staircase of somebody else's ship and saw: *Lightoller. Pitman. Boxhall. Lowe. Moody — ?*

The question mark. Somebody had put a question mark.

He found out at two o'clock. He found out from a fireman who had been on the collapsible, the one with the dead in it, and who had seen it.

"I seen him, sir. Mr. Moody. He was on the collapsible with us, and he'd been all over the deck getting men to the boats, and he was the last one off — he was standing on the top of it, on the roof of the bridge, and the water came up over the bridge and he was—" the man stopped. "He was there, sir. And then he wasn't."

Twenty-four years old. Blackheath. A mother and a father and a sister in a house with a small garden, and a career that was going to be a good one, and an appetite that the mess had laughed at, and a scheme for the boats that he had explained patiently to a Welshman on the eleventh of April while the wind blew.

*She's got more boats than the regulation demands. That's the fact of it.*

Harold went and stood in the alleyway by the engine room casing where nobody went, and put his back against the bulkhead, and stayed there for a while.

---

The second thing was the girl.

She was perhaps seven, and she was one of the ones who had come off number fourteen, and he did not know her name or who she belonged to, and by the second day he had begun to suspect that nobody did. She had attached herself to the boat rather than to any person in it. She would be found in the mornings near the door of the smoking room, and she had a doll made out of a napkin, and she did not cry, and she did not speak, and she would look at Harold with an expression of complete and unembarrassed interest whenever he came past.

On the second day she followed him to the door of the officers' mess and stood outside it, and a steward said, "Get along, you," and Harold said, "Leave her."

He crouched down. His knees hurt. Everything hurt.

"What's your name?"

She considered him.

"I'm the fifth officer," he said. "That means I'm the one who does the jobs nobody else wants."

The corner of her mouth moved.

"There's a woman in the second class," he said, "with a little girl's coat and a doll's dress and she's got a list of children, and she's going to find out who you belong to, and if she can't, she's going to find somebody who'll have you, and if she can't do that either—" He stopped, because he had been about to say something absurd.

She put her hand out and took hold of one of the buttons on his cuff, and stood there holding it, and would not let go, and after a moment he stood up carefully, so as not to pull, and stood in the alleyway outside the officers' mess with a child attached to his sleeve, and a steward went past and said, "Morning, sir," and did not smile, because nobody was smiling on that ship.

---

He found Arwen on the night of the fifteenth, on the after deck, at eleven o'clock, in a coat that was too big for her, standing at the rail.

The *Carpathia* was stopped. They had stopped at four in the morning in the middle of the ice and they had not moved since, because there was nothing to move for and because the ice was all round them, and a dozen bergs stood out of the water in the dark and in the moonlight, and it was the most beautiful thing Harold had ever seen and he could not look at it without wanting to be sick.

She had her hair down and her hands in her sleeves and she was looking at a berg about half a mile off, and he came and stood beside her and neither of them said anything for a while.

"I have been trying to write a telegram," she said at last. "To my husband. All day. The purser's office has a form and you may have twenty words and I have written twelve and crossed out eleven."

"What's left?"

"*Saved. Well. Nothing else lost.*" She said it like a line of scripture. "It's not true. Everything else is lost. I have one dress and a nightgown and a whistle. But everything else in the world is on the bottom of the sea, so it seemed to me that *nothing else lost* was the truer thing to say than the thing I actually meant, which was that I am not the woman who got on the ship at Southampton."

He thought about that.

"You're not the woman who got off at Southampton either," he said. "You're not off her yet."

She turned her head. "What is that supposed to mean?"

"It means that a person doesn't change on a boat deck at two in the morning. They find out something that was already there. If you're not the woman who got on at Southampton, you were never her, and you've been carrying the other one about with you for nine years waiting for a reason to let her out."

"That's a very comfortable thing for a man to say."

"It's not comfortable at all. It's the least comfortable thing I know." He put his hands on the rail. "I've been at sea since I was fourteen and I've been in three collisions and a fire and a fever that killed a shipmate of mine in nine days, and every time I've come out of one of those things there's been a week when I've thought I was a different man and a month when I've had to admit that I was the same one. It's what happens. You have a night that shows you what you are, and then you go back to being it."

"And if it was a bad thing you saw?"

"Then you go back to being that, too, unless you take a hand in it." He looked at her. "Which is the whole of the matter, and it's why I'll not say to you what I want to say."

"What do you want to say?"

"That I want you to leave him." He said it quite levelly. "There. I've said it. I'm not a subtle man and you knew that when you were fourteen."

The berg went away behind them as the ship swung very slowly at her anchor of drifting ice.

"You're a rich man's wife and I'm a fifth officer on seventeen pound ten a month," Harold said, "and if you came to me you'd be leaving a house in Liverpool and a name and a place in the world, and you'd be marrying a Welshman with no money and a temper and a mother in Chester, and you'd be at sea nine months of the year in a rented house in Barmouth, and every woman in that town would know your business and half of them would say it to your face. That's what it'd be. That's the whole of what it'd be, and I'll not dress it up in a song."

"And if I said I don't care about any of that?"

"Then I'd say you should care, because I do." He turned to face her. "I've been a man who took a thing because I wanted it. I took the sea at fourteen and I broke my father's heart doing it, and I'd do it again, and I know exactly what I am. But I'll not have you come to me and find out in a year that you've swapped one green drawing room for a smaller one with a leaking roof and a man who's away and who can't be spoken to before breakfast."

She was silent for a long moment.

"You are," she said, "the most infuriating person I have ever met."

"Aye."

"I have spent fifteen years thinking of you as—" She made a gesture with one hand, and dropped it. "As a thing that happened to me. A thing that was done and finished, that I had got over, that I did not think about except at Christmas and in the rain and when I heard a certain tune, which was often, which was daily, which was—" She stopped and started again. "And now you are standing here telling me the practical difficulties in a voice like a man reading out a bill of lading. Do you know what I have been through in the last twenty-four hours?"

"I do."

"No, you don't. You were doing a job." She said it without cruelty, which made it worse. "I sat in a boat and listened to them for an hour and a half and did nothing, and there is nothing in the world that you can say to me that will make that into a thing that is all right, and so I will not be made all right. I have decided that. I am going to be a woman who heard it for the rest of my life, and it will be in me in the way a stone is in a wall, and I am not going to try to take it out."

"Good," he said.

She looked up.

"Not good. I'm sorry. That came out wrong." He rubbed his face. "It's the only thing in you that I'd fight a man for. It's the fact that you'll not be comforted. If you'd come off that boat and been made all right in a week, I'd have been able to leave you on the pier and not think about you again. I've seen it happen. There's people on this ship now who are already telling the story as an adventure, and they'll be telling it as an adventure for fifty years and it'll never cost them another night's sleep, and God help me I don't blame them for it, I'd give a great deal to be one of them."

She laughed then, very quietly. "You would not."

"No," he admitted. "I would not."

---

They stood there. The ice moved on the water with a sound like a door being closed in another room.

"I'm not going to answer you," she said. "Not tonight, and not on this ship. If I answered you tonight it would be a woman on a raft answering a man who pulled her out of the water, and it would be a lie, and I would be ashamed of it, and so would you."

"I know."

"On Thursday I shall be in New York and my husband will be on the pier, and I will go with him to a house I have never seen with green curtains in it, and I will need to be there for a while. Do you understand? Weeks. Perhaps a month. I have to go and stand in that house and find out whether I am the woman you say I am or whether I am only a frightened woman a long way from home."

"And then?"

"And then," she said, "I am going to come to Barmouth on the train, and I am going to walk up to a door, and I am going to knock on it."

"That's my line."

"You had it fifteen years ago and you made a poor job of it," Arwen said. "It's my turn."

He put his hand out. She took it, and her hand was cold and thin and had a blister across the palm from an oar, and they stood at the rail of the *Carpathia* holding hands for perhaps a minute, in full view of anybody who cared to look, in the middle of an icefield, on the sixteenth of April 1912.

Then she said, "I am going to go and see whether the child is asleep," and let go of his hand, and went in.

Harold stayed at the rail a while longer, and looked at the ice.

At four in the morning they got the *Carpathia* under way again, and she turned her nose west and went on to New York with seven hundred and five extra people in her, and Harold Lowe stood on her bridge wing for a while and watched the last berg go by, and then went below and slept for eleven hours, which he had not done since he was a boy.

---

# IX. The Pier

The first thing Arwen saw in America was a woman holding up a bottle of brandy.

They were four miles down the bay, moving slowly, and the *Carpathia* had been met by a tug full of newspapermen who had come out past the Hook and were shouting up at the rails, and one of them had a bottle in each hand and was waving them, and another had a board with a piece of chalk on it and was writing questions and holding them up. *DID YOU SEE THE SHIP GO DOWN. ANY FIRST CLASS SURVIVORS. HOW MANY DEAD.*

The passengers lined the rails and looked down at them. Nobody answered. A man beside Arwen, a Mr. Beesley who had been in a boat and had not stopped talking about it for three days, said, "They have no idea. They have absolutely no idea," and Arwen said, "They have been told a number and they want a picture," and he looked at her with dislike and moved away.

She had one dress. Bessie had found it for her on the second day: a green woollen dress out of a trunk belonging to a woman in the second class who had not survived and whose trunk had been brought up and opened by the purser with two stewards to witness. The dress was a little too large and it had a darn in the left sleeve and Arwen had worn it for two days and would wear it for another week. She had a hairbrush that Mrs. Penhallow had given her, and a toothbrush from the ship's doctor, and a whistle in the pocket, and a wedding ring.

Bessie had been found on the second day. She had gone into number thirteen and had come off the *Carpathia* with a black eye and somebody else's baby.

"They put me in the boat and I said I was a lady's maid, madam, and they said get in and there's a woman handed me a baby and I've had him ever since." She had been crying, briefly, and then stopped. "He's called Patrick. The mother's not here. There's a list. I've been round the second class with the list."

"Let me see it."

"Madam, it's not—"

"Let me see it."

Bessie took the paper out of her cuff. It was a page out of a notebook with thirty-one names on it, and twenty-two of them were crossed out.

Arwen looked at it for a long time. Then she folded it and gave it back.

"Keep it," she said. "You'll want it when you're old and you'll think you won't, and you will."

---

The pier was a wall of people.

They came alongside at a quarter past nine in the evening of the eighteenth of April, into Cunard's pier at the foot of Fourteenth Street, and the *Carpathia*'s crew had put canvas screens up along the rail to keep the crowd from looking at the survivors, and it made no difference at all. There were ten thousand people on the street outside the pier. There were men with cameras standing on the roofs of the sheds. There were women in evening dress on the pier itself, having come from dinner parties, standing in the cold with their hands in their muffs and their faces turned up to the ship.

And there was a line of motor cars along the side of the shed, and a crowd of relations, and a man with a megaphone who was reading out names.

They took the first class down the gangway in a group. A steward came along the deck calling, "First class survivors, this way, please, have your names ready," and Arwen went down with the rest of them, and the crowd pressed against the barrier and made a sound that was not cheering and was not weeping, and every now and then a woman in the crowd screamed a name and a woman in the line would stop and turn and put her hands to her face.

There were men with notebooks on the pier. They came in under the arms of the policemen. One of them got to Arwen and said, "Mrs. Meredith? Mrs. Iestyn Meredith? Mrs. Meredith, is it true that the boats went off half full? Do you know why the boats went off half full?"

And Arwen said, "I would like to find my husband."

"He's not on the pier, ma'am, he's been at the office night and day, he's at the Waldorf, they've set up a—ma'am, one question—"

"I would like to find my husband," she said, and a policeman put his shoulder between them, and she went on down the pier.

---

She saw him from forty feet away, and she knew him by his hat.

Iestyn Meredith was not a tall man and he had a way of standing with his hands behind his back and his chin up that made him look like a man waiting for a train he did not expect to enjoy. He was forty-eight years old and grey at the temples and he had a neat beard, and he had come out in his dark grey overcoat and his good gloves with the stitching on the back, and he was standing at the end of the barrier in a small clear space of his own, because the crowd had understood, in the way crowd's do, that this man was waiting for someone.

He saw her. He came forward three steps and then stopped.

"Arwen."

"Iestyn."

He looked at her for a moment. Then he took off his hat.

That undid her. She had held together for four days, and she had bailed a boat with a paint tin, and she had watched a man turn three bodies over in a collapsible, and she had not cried once, and now her husband took his hat off in a crowd of ten thousand people and she had to put her hand out to the barrier.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I'm sorry, that was—I don't know why I did that." He put the hat back on. "You're thin."

"I'm cold."

"You're cold." He said it as though it were a problem to be solved, and took off his coat and put it round her shoulders without any ceremony at all, and it was warm and it smelled of the drawer it had been kept in. "There's a car. Can you walk as far as the car?"

"Yes."

"Then let's go."

He did not ask her anything. Not in the shed, not on the street, not in the car. He sat beside her with his hands on his knees and looked out of the window, and once he said, "There's a hot bath and there's a bed and there's a doctor coming at eleven," and then he was quiet.

It was only when they were in the hotel, in a suite on the eighth floor of the Waldorf with the curtains drawn and a fire lit and a tray of sandwiches she could not eat, that he said, "They gave me a list. On the second day. And then they changed it, and then they changed it again, and there was a boy from the office who came up every hour with whatever they had." He was standing with his back to her, looking at the fire. "I want you to know that I did not believe any of it either way. I did not believe you were dead and I did not believe you were alive. I sat in this room and I did not believe anything at all for six days, and I would not recommend it."

"Iestyn."

"They told me the boats went off half full," he said. "That's the thing that I keep—" He turned round. "Half full. There were empty seats. And you were in a boat."

"I was in number twelve. It went off with fifty-two in it and it was built for sixty-five." She heard her own voice, flat and careful. "There were thirteen empty seats in my boat, Iestyn. I have had four days to think about those thirteen seats."

He looked at her.

"I'm not asking you to explain anything," he said. "I want to be very clear about that. I'm not asking you to explain why you are alive and somebody's wife is not. I don't want to hear that. Do you understand?"

"Yes."

"Good." He sat down in the chair opposite her, heavily, like a man of sixty. "Then here is what I do want, and I want it now and not in a week, because I have been six days in this room and I have had time to think. I want to go home. Not to Liverpool. I want to go to the house in Morristown and shut the door and have nobody in it but us, and I want to sell the New York business and I want to give up the sea, which is to say I want to give up sending other people across it while I stay on the quay, and I want to be a man with an office in a building and a wife and a garden, and I am telling you this before you have had a bath because I have discovered in the last six days that I am not a man who can wait to say a thing."

Arwen sat with his coat round her shoulders and looked at him.

"Say something," he said.

"I don't know what to say."

"Then say nothing. It's not a thing that needs an answer tonight." He got up. "The doctor's coming at eleven and the bath is running and there's a woman from the hotel coming up to sit with you tonight, and I'll be in the next room, and in the morning we'll have breakfast and you can tell me as much or as little as you like."

At the door he stopped.

"There was a man," he said. "On the *Carpathia*. A Welshman. The one they're all talking about, the one who went back."

The room was very quiet.

"They're saying he went back for the people in the water and got four, and twelve out of a collapsible. There's a woman down in the lobby who has been telling it to anybody who'll stand still. She says he's the real hero of the thing." He had his hand on the door frame. "Was he in your boat?"

"No," Arwen said. "He took mine in tow."

Iestyn nodded slowly. "Then I'm in his debt," he said, "and I'd like to shake his hand," and went out and shut the door quietly behind him.

---

She went to the inquiry.

It was held in the East Room of the hotel, in the same building, three floors down, and it began on the nineteenth of April, the day after they landed, and it went on for weeks. There were men in morning dress and a great many reporters and a table at the front with a chairman on it, and the chairman was a Senator from Michigan named Smith, and he was a large mild man who began every day by saying that the committee was not there to fix blame and then spent the day doing exactly that.

She went to the first day and did not go again for a week.

A Mrs. Aubert, who had been lifted into a boat at the last minute and had cried the whole way down, was examined on the first day and made everybody cry. Mr. Ismay was examined for a whole day and was not believed. A quartermaster was asked whether the boats were filled and said no, and was asked why not, and said he did not know, and the Senator said, "You do not know," and the quartermaster said, "No, sir," and the room was silent for a while.

She went again on the twenty-fourth, in the afternoon, in a borrowed black hat, with Bessie beside her in the gallery. She had not told Iestyn.

Harold came in at half past two.

He was in his uniform, and he had had his hair cut, and he looked, from the gallery, about nineteen years old and extremely tired. He was sworn. He sat down in the chair with his hands on his knees and his eyes going round the room once, and then he answered questions for two hours.

He was not like the other witnesses. The others had been careful. They had said *I believe* and *it is my impression* and *I could not say*, and had deferred to the committee and to each other and to the dead. Harold Lowe said what he had done and did not qualify it.

"Mr. Lowe, how many people did you take into your boat?"

"Four out of the water, sir, and twelve out of the collapsible."

"And did you see any others?"

"I saw a great many others, sir."

"Did you attempt to rescue them?"

"I did not."

"Why not?"

"Because they were dead."

There was a stir in the room. The Senator leaned forward.

"Mr. Lowe, you have said that you waited before returning to the wreck. Will you explain to the committee why you did not return immediately?"

"Because it would have been suicide, sir."

"Will you expand on that?"

"I had thirty-odd women and children in my boat and there was a hundred and fifty men in the water where the ship had gone down, and if I had gone in among them they would have swarmed the boat and swamped her and we'd have lost every soul in her. I waited until the cries had thinned out and I had gathered five boats together and put my passengers into them, and then I went back with a volunteer crew."

"You waited an hour and a half."

"I waited as long as I had to, sir."

There was a noise in the room. A woman in the gallery behind Arwen said, out loud, "Oh, how dreadful," and Arwen turned round and looked at her, and the woman stopped.

Then the Senator said, "Mr. Lowe, you have been praised a great deal in the newspapers. I want to ask you whether you consider that you did everything you could have done."

And Harold Lowe said: "No, sir."

The room went absolutely still.

"I did everything I could think of at the time," he said, "and I've had two days to think about it since and there's things I'd do different. I'd not have wasted ten minutes on the starboard side when I knew the port boats wanted officers. I'd have put more men in the first boats and I'd have lied to them to do it, and I'd have opened the gangway doors myself with my own hands if it came to it. And if I'd known then what I know now about how long she had, I'd have gone back into that water at two o'clock and I'd have taken every man I could pull out, and I'd have lost my boat doing it, and I'd have been right to."

"Then why did you not?"

"Because I did not know," Harold said. "And I'll not sit here and tell you I did."

---

Afterwards she caught him in the corridor.

He was with two other officers and a lawyer, and he saw her over the lawyer's shoulder and stopped in the middle of a sentence, and then said something to the men with him and came across the corridor with his cap under his arm.

"You were in the gallery."

"I was."

"You heard all that."

"I heard every word." She looked up at him. "You told them you did not do everything you could have done."

"I did."

"Was that true?"

He considered it. He had a way of considering a question that made you feel he was taking it out and looking at it in the light.

"No," he said. "Not the way they took it. I did all that was possible in that boat, and I've told my own conscience that on the worst nights and it holds. But that's not the question they asked me, and if I'd have said *yes, sir, I did everything*, there's a woman in this hotel who'd read it in the paper tomorrow morning and it would put a knife in her. And there's nothing I can do for her. I can't bring him back and I can't say a word to her that isn't a lie. But I could stand in that room and say the one true thing, which is that a man who was there wishes he'd done better." He put his cap on. "It's not much. It's what I've got."

"You are a very strange man," Arwen said.

"So I'm told." He looked at her. "I sail on the second, on the *Adriatic*, and then the inquiry in London in May."

"Harold."

"I'll not ask you anything," he said. "I said I wouldn't and I won't. I'll be at Pennrallt in the first week of June, and the house is my sister's now and she'll have the room got up whether I want it or not, and there's a wall behind the cemetery where a boy made a fool of himself in 1897 and where I intend to make a fool of myself again, and after that I'll be at the door of the shop." He touched his cap. "Goodbye, Mrs. Meredith."

"Goodbye, Mr. Lowe."

He went away down the corridor with his cap under his arm. A reporter stepped out in front of him at the turn and said, "Officer—officer, one word for the *World*—" and Harold said, "Not one word, and I'll thank you to move," and went on.

---

She went back to the suite and sat by the window and looked down at Fifth Avenue, where the motor cars went along in the wet with their lamps on, and she thought about the house in Morristown with the green curtains, and about a man who had taken off his hat in a crowd.

At seven Iestyn came in with the evening paper under his arm.

He put it down on the table, folded, with a photograph on it: the fifth officer of the *Titanic* at the tiller of a lifeboat, taken from the *Carpathia*'s deck by a passenger, a grainy brown picture of a man in a cap looking over his shoulder.

"They've given him a whole column," Iestyn said. "They're calling him the only man who went back."

"I know."

"He's a Welshman, they say. From Barmouth." He took off his glasses and polished them. "You're from Barmouth."

"I am."

Iestyn put his glasses on again and looked at the newspaper, and then at her.

"Arwen," he said, "I have been thinking about something for three days and I would like to say it badly and get it over with, because if I wait until I can say it well I shall never say it at all."

She waited.

"When I married you," he said, "you were nineteen years old and there was something in you that I could not get at. I knew it. I told myself it was shyness, and then I told myself it was the child, and then I told myself it was my fault for being forty and dull and for talking about freight rates at the dinner table." He folded the paper carefully and put it down. "And it has come to me in the last week that I have spent nine years being a good husband to a woman who was not in the room."

"Don't."

"I'm not accusing you of anything." He said it quickly. "I want to be clear, because I have thought about this and I am not a jealous man and I will not become one at forty-eight. I am saying that if there is something in your life that is not this—" he gestured at the room, at the hotel, at Fifth Avenue "—then you had better tell me, and I will listen to it and I will not shout, and we will decide what is to be done in a manner that leaves us both with our dignity."

She looked at him.

And then, because he had taken off his hat in a crowd of ten thousand people, and because he was a careful man who had spent nine years not asking, and because she had decided on a boat deck in the dark that she would not be made all right—she told him.

All of it. From the wall behind the cemetery to the whistle. She talked for an hour and a half and he did not say one word, and when she had finished he got up and went to the window and stood there with his hands behind his back.

"Well," he said at last. "Thank you for telling me the truth."

"Iestyn—"

"No, let me—" He turned round. "I want to say one thing and then we'll have dinner. It is this. If you go to Barmouth in June, and you walk up to that door, and you find what you are looking for—" He stopped, and started again, and his voice was perfectly steady. "Then I hope you will be happy, and I hope he knows what he has, and I hope he is worth it. And if he is not worth it, I hope you will not come back to me out of a sense of duty, because I would rather have a wife who left me for a good reason than a wife who stayed for a kind one."

He picked up the newspaper and put it in the wastepaper basket.

"And now," he said, "I am going to have a bath, and then I am going to take you down to dinner, and we are not going to speak about this again until you bring it up, and I would be obliged if you would not bring it up in the restaurant."

# X. Calon Lân

The train came down the estuary at a quarter past four in the afternoon, and Arwen put her head out of the window like a girl.

She had not seen Barmouth by daylight since she was married. She had come for her mother's funeral in 1909, and had stood at the graveside in a black coat with Iestyn beside her, and had got back on the train the same evening because there was nowhere to stay and nothing to say to her father. The town had not changed, and the fact that it had not changed was the thing that made her cry, quietly, with her head out of the window and her hat in her lap.

There was the estuary, going out, brown and ribbed. There were the woods on the Aberamffra side, dark green and full. There was the bridge, the long wooden one, with the tide running under it. There was the town stacked up behind the harbour: grey stone, slate roofs, the chapels, the tall thin houses on the hill, the smoke going up straight in the still afternoon.

The guard put his head out of the next window and said, "Barmouth, ma'am. Barmouth next."

She got down onto the platform with one trunk and a hatbox and a green woollen dress.

She had come on the 4th of June, on the early train from Chester, and she had not written to anyone to say that she was coming.

---

He was not at Pennrallt.

That was the first thing she learned, from a woman at the station who recognised her at once—the town had not changed, and neither had its memory—and who told her, with the delight of a woman delivering good news, that Mr. Lowe was at the chapel with his sister and would be at the Picture Pavilion at seven for the presentation, and that there was a platform and a band and that the Mayor was to speak, and that they had had to print more tickets because everybody in the town wanted one and there had been a great deal of bad feeling about it.

"The whole town?" Arwen said.

"Thirteen hundred," the woman said. "Thirteen hundred, and that's not counting the ones who'll stand outside. There's a man coming from the *Cambrian News*." She looked at Arwen's trunk. "Are you stopping long, Mrs. Meredith?"

"I don't know yet," said Arwen.

---

She took a room at the Belle Vue, because she would not go to her father's house, and she paid for a week in advance, and then she went and stood at the window of it and looked at the harbour until she could breathe properly.

At six she went down and ate a plate of bread and cheese she did not want, and at half past six she walked up to the Picture Pavilion.

It was a new building, all white tile and a glass front, and there were people going into it in their good clothes in a long slow crowd that filled the street from the corner to the door. There were flags. Somebody had hung a flag over the entrance, and there was a boy selling programmes at a penny each, and he was selling them fast.

Arwen bought one. It had a photograph of the *Titanic* on the cover and a photograph of a man in a cap inside it, and under the photograph it said, *Fifth Officer H. G. Lowe, of this town, who went back.*

She stood at the back and waited.

---

There was a platform at the front with a red cloth on it and a table with a thing on the table under a cloth. There was a band in the corner—the town band, twelve of them, in uniform. There were thirteen hundred people in a hall built for nine hundred, and it was hot, and it smelled of raincoats and hair oil and wet boots, and every woman in the room had on the brooch she wore for funerals and weddings.

The Mayor spoke for eleven minutes and said that the town was proud. The minister spoke for nine minutes and said that the Lord had been merciful. A man from the Cambrian News read a telegram from the White Star Line, and then a letter, and the letter was from a woman in New York whose name Arwen did not catch and it said: *he put his boat about and came back, and there were four of us in the water, and I am alive today because a Welshman would not leave us.*

Then the Mayor said, "Mr. Harold Godfrey Lowe," and the whole of the Pavilion stood up.

He came up the side of the hall because there was no other way through the crowd, and he was in a dark suit that did not fit him well and had been bought in a hurry, and he was red in the face before he got to the platform. He stood behind the table and put his hands on it and looked out at thirteen hundred people, and the applause went on for a long time, and somebody at the back shouted, "Bravo, Harold bach!" and there was laughter, and he went an even deeper red.

"Friends," he said. And then stopped.

"Friends," he said again. "I've stood in front of a good many things in my life but never thirteen hundred of them, and I'll tell you now that I'd rather be in a boat."

They laughed. He waited for it to stop.

"I've been asked to say a few words and I've had a month to think what they should be, and I've not managed it, so you'll have what I've got, and it's this." He put his hands behind his back. "I did what I was trained to do, and the men who trained me were men off these boats and out of this harbour, some of them in this room, some of them not. I went back into that water because there was nothing else to be done and because I could not have stood up straight afterwards if I hadn't. There's four people alive that were in the water and fifteen out of a collapsible, and I'll not stand here and pretend I don't know their names, because I do, and I say them every night before I sleep, and I'll not say them to you."

The hall was silent.

"But there was fifteen hundred that did not come out," he said, "and there's a good many of them were Welsh, and there's not a family in this town that's not been touched. And I'd rather have had my boat filled to the gunwale with the living than have a gold watch for the dead." He stopped, and rubbed his face with the flat of his hand, exactly as she had seen him do on the boat deck. "That's all I've got. Thank you."

There was a moment of absolute stillness. Then the applause came up out of the floor of the hall, and it went on and on, and the band played, and the Mayor uncovered the thing on the table with a flourish and it was a gold watch on a chain, and he read the inscription out: *Presented to Harold Godfrey Lowe, 5th Officer R.M.S. Titanic, by his friends in Barmouth and elsewhere, in recognition and appreciation of his gallant services at the foundering of the Titanic, 15th April 1912.*

Harold took the watch and looked at it as though it had been handed to him by a stranger on a street.

Then a woman in the front row stood up and said, in the voice of a person who has decided that a thing ought to happen and has decided to be the one who makes it happen, "Mr. Lowe, will you give us a song?"

"I will not," he said. "I cannot sing a note and you all know it."

"Then we'll have somebody else sing something, and you'll stand there and listen to it, and it'll do you good." The woman turned round and looked at the hall. "There's a lady here that's come home," she said. "Mrs. Meredith that was Arwen Prys, and there's not one of you that's forgotten what she could do with a hymn, and I'll ask her now, in front of the whole of Barmouth, to sing *Calon Lân*."

---

The hall turned round.

Arwen stood at the back with her programme in her hand and her hat on and thirteen hundred faces coming round to look at her, and she thought, quite clearly and with no drama at all: *this is the thing you have been afraid of for nine years, and it is standing in front of you in a hot hall with a flag in it, and it is nothing.*

She walked up the side of the hall. It was a long way. Her boots made a noise on the boards and she could hear them. She came to the platform and did not go up onto it. She stood at the front with her hands loose at her sides and looked at the band, and the band looked back at her, and the cornet player said, "What key, ma'am?" and she said, "The one you know."

They played the introduction. It was not the right tempo and the cornet was flat and she did not care.

She sang.

She sang it the way her mother had taught her to sing it, in the front room at the shop with the piano that was always half a tone down, and the whole point of it, her mother had said, was that you did not make a performance of it, you made a statement of it; you stood up and you told a room of people the plain truth about what a clean heart was worth, and if you were thinking about your voice while you did it you had already failed.

*(Nid wy'n gofyn bywyd moethus, / Aur y byd na'i berlau mân.)*

She was not asking for a luxurious life. Not gold, nor fine pearls.

Halfway through the second verse she looked up and saw him.

He was standing behind the table with the watch in his hand, and he was not looking at her the way thirteen hundred people were looking at her. He was looking at her the way a man looks at a light in a window when he has been out on the water a long time. And she thought—she would always be slightly ashamed of thinking it, twenty seconds into a hymn, in front of the whole town—*there. That is what it is. That is the whole of it, and I have spent nine years pretending I did not know the address.*

She finished. The last line went up into the roof and came down again and she let it go.

Then the hall was on its feet, and the noise was not applause so much as a kind of release, and the woman in the front row was crying into a handkerchief with a border on it, and the minister was saying something to the Mayor, and Arwen stood there and thought, with enormous clarity: *my mother would have liked that.*

She looked at Harold. He had not moved. He lifted the watch, slightly, the way a man lifts a glass.

Then she went out of the side door into the street, because she had had as much as she could take of being looked at, and she stood there in the cool with her back against the white tile of the Pavilion and her hands over her face, and a boy came past and said, "Are you all right, missus?" and she said, "I am perfectly all right," and he went away satisfied.

---

She went to see her father the next morning.

The shop was the same. That was the thing that undid her: the same bell over the door, the same smell of tar and hemp and linseed, the same counter with the worn place in it where four generations of men had put their hands. There were two coils of rope in the window that had not been there in 1909 and a new tin of paint with a label she did not know.

Her father came through from the back with a ledger in his hand and stopped.

He was sixty-three and had got thin, and his coat hung on him, and he had the look of a man who has been in a room by himself for a long time.

"Arwen."

"Hello, Dad."

He put the ledger down. Then he came round the counter and stood in front of her, and did not touch her, and said, "You're stopping at the Belle Vue."

"Yes."

"Your room's made up. It's been made up since the spring." He said it as a fact, and turned away, and began to do something unnecessary with a row of hooks on the wall. "I heard you sing last night. I was at the back. I'd not have gone but I heard you were in the town and I thought—" He stopped. "You've your mother's way with the last line."

"Dad."

"Don't." He turned round. "I know what you've come for and I'd sooner you didn't. I'm sixty-three and I've a bad chest and I've not the words for it."

"Then I'll say it, and you can listen with your hands busy." She put her own hands flat on the counter. "In September 1897, Harold Lowe left a letter with his sister for me. Did you take it?"

His hands went still on the hooks.

"No," he said.

"Did you know about it?"

"No." He turned round then, and his face was not the face she remembered from the doorstep. It was older and it was frightened. "I never saw a letter, and I never knew there was one till this week, and I'll tell you what I said when I heard. I said, well, that's a pity, and then I went into the back and I sat down for a quarter of an hour." He came and stood at the counter. "And I'll tell you the other thing, because you've come all this way and you'll not be fobbed off. If it had come to me, I'd have burned it. I'd have put it in the stove and not thought twice, and I'd have slept the night through."

Arwen looked at him.

"Thank you for saying that," she said.

"It's not a thing to thank me for."

"It is. I've had a month of people telling me kind things." She picked up her gloves. "I came here to find out who to blame and I have found out, and it's nobody, and I have to say that I would rather have had somebody. It would be a great deal easier to be angry at you."

"Be angry at me anyway." He said it quickly. "I put that boy off my door in front of the whole street and I told your mother it was for the best, and she cried, and I told her to stop. And there's not a year since that I've not thought about it." He looked at the counter. "I was a man with a shop and a daughter and I thought I knew what a life was. I did not know."

She put her gloves on.

"There's a thing I'd like," she said. "Not today. When it's all settled, and whatever happens happens. I'd like to come and sit in the back with you on a Sunday and not talk. That's all. I'm not asking you to be sorry out loud and I'm not going to be."

Her father looked at her for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, and picked up the ledger and went into the back, and she heard him put it down on the desk, and then she heard nothing at all for a while.

The bell over the door went as she left.

---

She went up to Pennrallt at four in the afternoon and knocked on the door, and Ada Lowe opened it.

She was thirty-eight and she had her brother's face in a different key: the same flat brow, the same way of standing with her weight forward. She had an apron on and flour to the elbow.

"Mrs. Meredith," she said. "He's not here. He's on the hill with the surveyor, they're putting a wall up at the back and the man's been at him about it since Monday."

"I didn't come to see him."

Ada's face changed and closed.

"I came to see you," Arwen said. "May I come in?"

---

The kitchen at Pennrallt had a range and a window with a view of the estuary and a dresser with blue plates on it, and Ada made tea because there was nothing else to do with her hands, and put a plate of seed cake on the table that nobody touched.

"I was fifteen," she said.

"I know."

"I want to tell it." She sat down opposite, and she sat very straight, and she looked at the oilcloth. "He gave me the letter on the Saturday and he went on the Monday. He said, give it to her, and mind you give it, and I said I would. And I put it in my pocket and I went down the hill and I got as far as the corner by the chapel and I saw your father coming up with two other men from the harbour." She stopped. "And I turned round and went home."

"Why?"

"Because I was fifteen and he was a man with a shop and my father owed him money." Ada's voice did not change. "And because I was jealous, Mrs. Meredith, and I've not said that out loud to anybody, not to my brother, not to my minister, not to God, and I'm saying it to you because you've come to this house to hear it. He was my brother and he was the only one of us that was going anywhere and I was going to be in this kitchen, and when he talked about you he was—" She made a small gesture with one hand. "Happy. He was happy in a way he never was in this house."

"What did you do with it?"

"I kept it." Ada got up. She went to the dresser and opened the second drawer down and took out a tin box with a picture of the Conway bridge on it. She put it on the table. "I've kept it fifteen years in this drawer and I've taken it out perhaps four times and read it, which was a sin, and I've prayed about it, and I'll tell you what I've never done, which is put it in the stove, and I could have, and I did not."

Arwen put her hand on the lid of the tin.

"Give it to him," she said.

"I have. This morning. Before he went up the hill." Ada sat down again, heavily, and for the first time she looked her age. "He read it standing in the passage with his cap in his hand and he didn't say anything for a long while, and then he said, 'Well, that's a boy's letter,' and he folded it up and put it in his coat, and then he went out and did not say another word to me all day."

---

She walked down from Pennrallt at half past five along the road that goes above the town, and at the bottom of the hill, where the road bends and the wall begins, there was a man sitting on the wall with his coat off and his sleeves rolled up and a piece of paper in his hands.

He did not get up.

"Sit down," he said.

She sat down on the wall beside him. Below them the tide was a long way out and the boats in the harbour were all leaning the same way, and the light was going gold, and a dog was barking somewhere down by the landing stage.

He held out the paper without looking at her.

"Do you want me to read it?"

"Aye. I'd like to know what a woman hears in it." He said it roughly. "I've read it four times today and I cannot get past the second page. It's the worst letter I've ever read. I've wrote better in a log book."

She took it.

The paper was folded in four and soft at the creases, and the ink had gone brown. The hand was a boy's: large, careful, sloping more and more as the pages went on. There was a smudge where he had rested his hand.

*Dear Arwen,* it began. *I am going on Monday and I have not the words to say what I would like to say so you will have to take it as it comes.*

He wrote about the ship he was going in. He wrote about his uncle in Liverpool and the price of a berth and the fact that a boy could get two pounds ten a month on the coast if he had a good discharge. He wrote, at length, about a barque he had seen in the Mersey with the topsail yards braced sharp up, and about the whole rig of her, in a paragraph that went on for a page and a half and had no full stops in it.

And then, at the bottom of the third page, in a different ink, as though he had put the letter away and come back to it a day later:

*I will come back for you when I am worth coming for. Do not think I have forgot you because I have not and I will not. If you are married when I come back I will not make a fuss and I will wish you well and I will go away and you will not hear from me again. But if you are not married I will ask you. That is all I have got to say and it has taken me four days to say it. I am yours in Christ, H. G. Lowe.*

Arwen sat on the wall with the letter in her hands and cried.

Not the way she had cried on the *Carpathia*, in the dark, with her fist in her mouth. Properly, out loud, with her shoulders going, the way a woman cries who has been holding something for fifteen years and has just been given leave to put it down.

He sat beside her and did not touch her and did not say anything at all, and when it was over he took his handkerchief out of his pocket and gave it to her, and it was clean, which somehow made it worse.

"It's a good letter," she said, when she could.

"It's a boy's letter."

"It's a good letter." She folded it. "It says what it means and it makes a promise and it gives the other person a way out of it. There are men of fifty who could not write that."

"There's a page and a half about a barque."

"There is." She wiped her eyes. "I would like to have read about the barque at the time."

"Aye," he said. "So would I."

---

They sat on the wall until the light went.

"Your sister told me," she said. "About being jealous."

"I know. She told me that as well, in the passage, at seven o'clock this morning, with the flour still on her." He picked at the mortar of the wall with his thumbnail. "I've been angry with Ada since Thursday and I'll be angry with her for a while yet, and I'll get over it, because she's my sister and she's had a tin box in a drawer for fifteen years and there's no punishment I could give her that's worse than that." He looked at the town. "And she's not the one who went. I went. She was fifteen and frightened, and I was fifteen and I went, and if I'd been a man I'd have handed the letter to you myself at the wall behind the cemetery instead of giving it to a girl to carry, and I'd have waited a week to see what you said, and I'd have gone on the Monday after that. There's a great deal of the blame in this and most of it's mine."

"Harold."

"I'm not asking for comfort."

"I know. That's why I'm going to give you some." She turned on the wall to face him. "I have thought for a month about what I would say when I got here, and I have had four different speeches, and they were all bad, so here it is without the speech. I am not going back to Morristown."

He was very still.

"Iestyn knows," she said. "I told him in New York, on the twenty-sixth of April, in a hotel room, for an hour and a half, and he listened to the whole of it and then he said he would rather have a wife who left him for a good reason than a wife who stayed for a kind one. He is a better man than either of us has been fair to. It will be done quietly and it will take a year and there will be talk, and I will not be received in some houses, and my name will be in the mouths of every woman in Liverpool for a season, and I have thought about all of it and I am not going to pretend that I have not."

"Arwen—"

"You said in the boat that you would not dress it up in a song and you told me the worst of it and you told me true. So I'll tell you the worst of it from my side." She looked at him. "I have no money of my own. What I have is what my father settles on me and it is not much, and I cannot keep a house on it and I will not be a woman who is kept. I have a maid who has nowhere to go and I will not turn her off. And I have not slept a whole night since the fifteenth of April and I do not expect to for a long time, and there will be nights when I am not fit to live with."

"I've had those nights since I was twenty."

"Then you know what I'm bringing you."

"I know what you're bringing me," he said, "and I'll tell you what I've got. Seventeen pound ten a month and a master's certificate and a sister who'll not speak to you for a year and a town that'll talk about you in Welsh, which is worse, because it's more accurate. And a rented house by the harbour with a roof that wants doing." He turned to her. "And I'll be away nine months of the year, and the first time I go you'll be standing on that landing stage with every woman in the town watching you, and you'll have to be a great deal braver than you were on that boat deck, because there'll be nobody there to see it and no gold watch at the end of it."

"That's my line," she said. "I had it ready."

"Then we'll not say it at all," said Harold Lowe, and took her hand.

---

She went back to Morristown in July to do it properly.

That was the part that nobody sang about. She had said, on a wall above the harbour, that it would be done quietly and would take a year, and she had been a fool to say a year; it took two. Iestyn Meredith was a careful man and he went about it in a careful way, and there was a solicitor in Liverpool and a solicitor in New York and a great deal of paper, and there was a period when it was necessary for her to be at an hotel in Liverpool for six weeks and to be seen to be there alone. She was not received in three houses that winter. Mrs. Penhallow, who was in Boston, wrote to her every fortnight for two years and never once asked a question.

Ada Lowe came round. It took her a year and eleven months and she came round in the end with a ham and a bad grace and the observation that she had prayed about it and had been given no answer either way and had decided to take that as permission.

Iestyn did not come to the wedding. He wrote, in his small exact hand, that he wished her well, that she should keep the green curtains because they had been bought for her, and that he had taken a house in Morristown with a garden and had planted it with things that would not need him. She answered the letter. They wrote to each other twice a year after that, for the rest of his life, and the letters were the sort that people would be surprised by if they found them.

Bessie Corrigan stayed. She stayed through the two years in the lodging house and she stayed afterwards and she married a man off the *Cambrian Coast* in 1915 and had four children and was, at the age of sixty, still saying that the hot water on the *Titanic* had gone cold in the middle of a jug.

They were married in the spring of 1914, at the registry office in Chester, because the chapel at Barmouth would not have them and they had decided not to mind about it. There were nine people. Arwen's father came and sat in the back with his hands on his hat, and at the end of it he stood up and shook Harold's hand and said, "I put you off my door, boy," and Harold said, "You did, Mr. Prys," and her father said, "Well," and that was the whole of it, and they were both satisfied.

She was thirty-two. He was thirty-one, and had a second officer's berth on a boat to the Plate, and a rented house above the harbour with a roof that wanted doing, and no gold watch about his person, because the gold watch was in a box in the front room and he took it out about once a year and looked at it and put it back.

In August, when the war came, he went, and came back in 1919 with a stripe and a cough and a great dislike of crowds; and they had a daughter in 1920 and a son in 1922, and the son went to sea, and the daughter did not.

---

There was one more thing.

On the Sunday evening before she went back, when the tide was coming in over the bar and the whole estuary was full of the sound of it, they walked up past the cemetery to the wall.

The wall was the same. That was the thing about walls. There was a place in the mortar at the corner where a boy had gouged out a hollow with a knife in 1896 to keep a marble in, and it was still there.

Harold took something out of his waistcoat pocket and put it in her hand.

It was a half-sovereign with a hole drilled in it, off centre, and a bootlace through the hole.

"You had it," she said.

"I had it. I bought it off a man in Liverpool in 1898 for eleven and six, which is more than it's worth, and he thought I was a fool, and I did not care, because I'd made a hole in the other one with a hand drill when I was fourteen and I wanted to see whether I could do a better job of it." He turned it over in her palm with one finger. "I did not do a better job of it."

"It's a great deal worse than the other one."

"It is," he said, with enormous satisfaction. "There's a thing I've been meaning to say to you since 1897 and I'll say it now and then we'll go home. I'd nothing to give you then but a promise and a hole in a coin. I've got a gold watch in a box in the house that thirteen hundred people in this town paid for, and it's worth more money than anything I'll ever own, and I'd not give you that. I'm giving you the eleven and six."

The tide came in over the bar with a long sound like a door opening somewhere out of sight.

"Arwen Prys," he said, "you're engaged to a man worth half a sovereign."

She laughed, and the wind took it, and it went out over the water where the light was going down behind the Aberamffra woods.

"Harold Lowe," she said, "you are a fool, and I have waited fifteen years to tell you so, and you are worth a great deal more than that, and I am not going to argue about it in the dark, and I am very cold, and take me home."

And they went down the hill together in the last of the light, past the chapel and the harbour and the boats leaning the same way, and he left her at the door of the Belle Vue and did not kiss her, because half the town was watching from behind its curtains; and he walked back up to Pennrallt to tell his sister, who had the kettle on and a great deal to say.

She kept the whistle.

She kept it for thirty years, and it went with her to three houses and a war and a daughter and a son, and it was in the drawer of the bureau in the front room at Deganwy when Harold Godfrey Lowe died in May 1944, at sixty-one, of a thing that had nothing to do with the sea.

Her daughter found it, afterwards, going through the drawers: a length of copper pipe with the far end stopped with a plug of wood, and a hole drilled through the side of it, and a note in her mother's hand, folded small, that said: *Calon lân. Barmouth, 14 April 1912. He made this in the lamp room instead of sleeping. There was a flat calm and no moon and he said a wind would have told us where the ice was.*

The whistle would not make a note by then. The wood had gone and the lip was worn through, and the girl put it to her mouth and blew and got nothing but a breath of air, and she put it back in the drawer, and went out of the room, and shut the door.

Above the harbour the tide was coming in over the bar, and it made the long sound it makes, like a door opening somewhere out of sight; and the boats lay at their moorings, all leaning the same way; and the water between the town and the far woods was brown and ribbed and going out, and then it turned, and came back.

## A Note on the Book

Harold Godfrey Lowe was a real man. He was born at Llanrhos in 1882, raised at Barmouth, went to sea at fourteen, and was serving as the fifth officer of the *Titanic* on her maiden voyage — his first Atlantic crossing. In the early hours of 15 April 1912 he took charge of lifeboat No. 14, fired three warning shots to keep men from swamping her, gathered five boats together, put his own passengers into other boats, waited until the cries had thinned out, went back into the wreckage with a volunteer crew and pulled four people from the water and at least twelve from the swamped collapsible. He was the only officer to raise a sail. He was the only boat commander who went back. He testified at both inquiries, came home to Barmouth in June to an afternoon that thirteen hundred people attended, and was given a gold watch. He married Ellen Marion Whitehouse in 1913, had two children, served through the war, and died in 1944.

Arwen Prys is invented, and so are Iestyn Meredith, Louisa Penhallow, Bessie Corrigan and Ada's letter. The ship, her officers, her boats, her hours and her weather are as the record gives them, and much of the language I have put into Harold Lowe's mouth at the inquiries is his own. Where the record is silent, I have invented, and where it is not, I have tried not to argue with it.

The watch was given to him by his neighbours and its inscription is in the record. The whistle is a novelist's invention, and I am sorry it never existed.

*A.M.*
