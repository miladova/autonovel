# VIII. The Count

Harold Lowe came up the *Carpathia*'s side at twenty past eight in the morning with his boat's gear stowed, and the first thing that happened to him was that a stranger put a cup of beef tea in his hand and told him to drink it.

He drank it. He was standing on the after deck of a Cunarder with a hundred and forty of his own boat's people and two hundred and forty more from the other boats milling about him, and the deck was covered in blankets and women in men's coats and children being carried and a woman screaming because she had found her sister and a man sitting on a hatch cover being sick into a bucket, and he drank the beef tea and it went into him like something being poured into a hole in the ground.

Then he went to find Mr. Rostron.

Rostron was on the bridge, and he had been on the bridge since half past four, and he had brought his ship through six miles of ice at twenty-five knots in the dark and stopped her in the middle of an icefield without putting a scratch on her, and when Harold came up he turned round and said, "Mr. Lowe. How many?"

"Four alive out of the water, sir, and twelve out of the collapsible. Fifteen in the collapsible, three dead."

"Four," Rostron said. He wrote it down. "What have you done with the collapsible?"

"Left her, sir. She had the dead in her."

"You'll have to make a report of that to your company." Rostron's face was grey with strain and lack of sleep, and there was something else in it too, something Harold recognised because he had it himself: the flat surface of a man who has been engaged in extremely difficult work for four hours and has not yet been given permission to stop. "Do you know the number of boats?"

"Thirteen boats and two collapsibles, sir. Twelve's still out but she's alongside."

"Very well. Two hundred and four in my boats to the eleven boats, and I've got—" he looked at the paper "—seven hundred and five aboard. Go and get some sleep, Mr. Lowe."

"I'd rather—"

"Go and get some sleep. That's not a courtesy, it's an order. You'll be wanted tonight, and I'll want you able to stand up."

---

He did not sleep. He lay down in a cabin that had been given up by a first class passenger, a Mr. and Mrs. somebody, who had gone down to the dining saloon to help and had not come back, and he lay on the bunk in his wet clothes and looked at the deckhead of another man's cabin, and after ten minutes he got up and went out.

The ship was in a state that he had seen twice before, after a wreck off the coast and after a fire at sea, and it is not a state that can be described in a report. Seven hundred people had come out of the water or off a boat into a ship that was built for a hundred and fifty in the second class and four hundred in the third; and every one of them had lost everything, and half of them had lost everybody, and the ship's company of the *Carpathia*—stewards, cooks, cabin boys, the lot, many of them nineteen years old—were doing what they had been trained to do and then going round the corner and being sick or weeping and then coming back.

A stewardess was going along the corridor with a tray of tea and a list, knocking on doors. Two men were carrying a mattress up the companionway with a woman on it. A woman in a sable coat—he remembered the coat, she had been on the *Titanic*, he had seen her at number six—was sitting on the floor with her back against the bulkhead holding a cup, and when she saw him she said, "Officer. Officer, my husband is not on the list."

"Which list, madam?"

"The purser's list. There are two lists. One in the dining saloon and one in the—" she lost the thread, and found it again. "They have him down as saved, but I know he is not saved, because I saw him on the deck, and I would like to know who is putting him down as saved."

"I'll find out, madam."

"You said that to me last night."

"Did I?" He looked at her. "Then I meant it, and I mean it now, and I'm sorry I've not done it yet."

She looked up at him for a moment, and her face did the thing that faces were doing all over that ship, and she said, "No. No, you didn't say it to me. I'm sorry. I've been asking everyone."

---

The number was fifteen hundred and twenty-three.

It came over the wireless and went round the ship in an hour, and it was the wrong number, it was less than half the truth, and it did more damage in ten minutes than the ice had done in two hours. Fifteen hundred and twenty-three lost. The ship had carried two thousand two hundred and eight. Two thousand two hundred and eight, and seven hundred and five in the boats, and the sum was done by every passenger on the *Carpathia* that day, on the backs of menus and in the margins of telegram forms and in their own heads, over and over, the way a man checks a lock.

Twelve hundred dead when the boats were counted properly, and fifteen hundred when the wireless said it, and Harold sat in the officers' mess with a cup of tea going cold and did the sum a fourth time and then a fifth, and then he put the pencil down.

"Mr. Lowe."

It was Boxhall. He had been in a boat since ten past twelve and he had been firing rockets off her all night, and he had a face on him like a man who had been to a funeral and had another one to go to.

"Boxhall."

"They're saying there's to be an inquiry. In New York. Senator somebody. They'll want statements."

"Then they'll want statements."

"What will you say?"

Harold looked at him.

"About the boats," Boxhall said. "About the filling of them. They'll ask why they went away light, and they'll ask the officers, and I don't know what I'm going to say, and I've been sitting in there for an hour trying to find a way to say it that doesn't make it sound like—"

"It was my side," Harold said. "The starboard side, the first five. I put forty-one in a boat for sixty-five and I counted them myself and I went along the rail and I asked women to come and they laughed at me."

"So did everyone."

"Aye. Everyone." He turned the cup round on the table. "And if there was one officer on that deck who could have filled those boats by main force, it was Murdoch, and Murdoch is dead, so we'll none of us ever know what he'd have said. What I'll say is what I did. I'll not say *the women would not come* as if it were weather. They'd not come because they did not believe it, and they did not believe it because—" he stopped.

"Because what?"

"Because nobody told them," Harold said. "Because a woman on that deck at twenty past midnight looked at a boat with sixty-five seats in it and at the sea, and she'd been told by a man in a uniform all her life that the ship was unsinkable, and had believed him, and had been right to believe him, and the man in the uniform was still saying it while he filled her boat." He pushed the cup away. "I don't know. That's not for me to say either. I was a fifth officer. I'll answer for my own boat and no one else's."

---

On the Tuesday a man from the White Star Line came aboard at the head of a small party and had the officers into the second class smoking room and told them that they were not to speak to the newspapers.

He was a decent man, a passenger agent named Franklin with a grey moustache and a bad conscience, and he said it badly. He said that the company wished to have one account and not forty, that there would be an inquiry, that anything said now would be said before the facts were known, and that the officers would be looked after. He said that last part twice.

"Are we to lie?" Harold said.

"Nobody is asking you to lie, Mr. Lowe."

"You're asking us to say nothing, which on a ship's deck amounts to the same thing, because the fellows from the papers will write what they're given and if they're given nothing they'll write what they're not given." Harold was aware that his voice had gone loud and did not care. "There's seven hundred people upstairs who've lost everything they own and half of them have lost everybody they love, and there's a hundred reporters on the water outside this ship, and if we say nothing they'll print the wreck of the *Titanic* as though it were a hotel fire."

"Mr. Lowe—"

"I'll say nothing to the papers," Harold said. "You have my word on that, and my word's worth something to me, and I'd be obliged if you'd not make me spend it cheaply. But when the inquiry comes I'll answer every question put to me and I'll answer it straight, and if the company doesn't like what I say it can say so in the newspapers and I'll not be reading them."

There was a silence in the second class smoking room. Boxhall was looking at the table. Pitman had his hands folded. Lightoller, who had a bad back and had been standing all morning, said, "I'm with Lowe," in a voice like a door closing.

Franklin said, "I'll put it to the company," which is what men say when they intend to do nothing, and went away.

"He's not a bad man either," Pitman said, afterwards.

"No," Harold said. "That's the trouble with the whole business. There's nobody in it that's a bad man. It's all been done by good men, one after another, each of them doing the sensible thing." He put his cap on. "It'd be a great deal easier if there was somebody to hang."

Two things he would remember about the *Carpathia*.

The first was the moment he saw Moody's name on the list.

It was not official and it was not a list; it was a piece of paper that a steward had made, going round the first class with the names of the officers who were saved, and Harold read it at the top of the grand staircase of somebody else's ship and saw: *Lightoller. Pitman. Boxhall. Lowe. Moody — ?*

The question mark. Somebody had put a question mark.

He found out at two o'clock. He found out from a fireman who had been on the collapsible, the one with the dead in it, and who had seen it.

"I seen him, sir. Mr. Moody. He was on the collapsible with us, and he'd been all over the deck getting men to the boats, and he was the last one off — he was standing on the top of it, on the roof of the bridge, and the water came up over the bridge and he was—" the man stopped. "He was there, sir. And then he wasn't."

Twenty-four years old. Blackheath. A mother and a father and a sister in a house with a small garden, and a career that was going to be a good one, and an appetite that the mess had laughed at, and a scheme for the boats that he had explained patiently to a Welshman on the eleventh of April while the wind blew.

*She's got more boats than the regulation demands. That's the fact of it.*

Harold went and stood in the alleyway by the engine room casing where nobody went, and put his back against the bulkhead, and stayed there for a while.

---

The second thing was the girl.

She was perhaps seven, and she was one of the ones who had come off number fourteen, and he did not know her name or who she belonged to, and by the second day he had begun to suspect that nobody did. She had attached herself to the boat rather than to any person in it. She would be found in the mornings near the door of the smoking room, and she had a doll made out of a napkin, and she did not cry, and she did not speak, and she would look at Harold with an expression of complete and unembarrassed interest whenever he came past.

On the second day she followed him to the door of the officers' mess and stood outside it, and a steward said, "Get along, you," and Harold said, "Leave her."

He crouched down. His knees hurt. Everything hurt.

"What's your name?"

She considered him.

"I'm the fifth officer," he said. "That means I'm the one who does the jobs nobody else wants."

The corner of her mouth moved.

"There's a woman in the second class," he said, "with a little girl's coat and a doll's dress and she's got a list of children, and she's going to find out who you belong to, and if she can't, she's going to find somebody who'll have you, and if she can't do that either—" He stopped, because he had been about to say something absurd.

She put her hand out and took hold of one of the buttons on his cuff, and stood there holding it, and would not let go, and after a moment he stood up carefully, so as not to pull, and stood in the alleyway outside the officers' mess with a child attached to his sleeve, and a steward went past and said, "Morning, sir," and did not smile, because nobody was smiling on that ship.

---

He found Arwen on the night of the fifteenth, on the after deck, at eleven o'clock, in a coat that was too big for her, standing at the rail.

The *Carpathia* was stopped. They had stopped at four in the morning in the middle of the ice and they had not moved since, because there was nothing to move for and because the ice was all round them, and a dozen bergs stood out of the water in the dark and in the moonlight, and it was the most beautiful thing Harold had ever seen and he could not look at it without wanting to be sick.

She had her hair down and her hands in her sleeves and she was looking at a berg about half a mile off, and he came and stood beside her and neither of them said anything for a while.

"I have been trying to write a telegram," she said at last. "To my husband. All day. The purser's office has a form and you may have twenty words and I have written twelve and crossed out eleven."

"What's left?"

"*Saved. Well. Nothing else lost.*" She said it like a line of scripture. "It's not true. Everything else is lost. I have one dress and a nightgown and a whistle. But everything else in the world is on the bottom of the sea, so it seemed to me that *nothing else lost* was the truer thing to say than the thing I actually meant, which was that I am not the woman who got on the ship at Southampton."

He thought about that.

"You're not the woman who got off at Southampton either," he said. "You're not off her yet."

She turned her head. "What is that supposed to mean?"

"It means that a person doesn't change on a boat deck at two in the morning. They find out something that was already there. If you're not the woman who got on at Southampton, you were never her, and you've been carrying the other one about with you for nine years waiting for a reason to let her out."

"That's a very comfortable thing for a man to say."

"It's not comfortable at all. It's the least comfortable thing I know." He put his hands on the rail. "I've been at sea since I was fourteen and I've been in three collisions and a fire and a fever that killed a shipmate of mine in nine days, and every time I've come out of one of those things there's been a week when I've thought I was a different man and a month when I've had to admit that I was the same one. It's what happens. You have a night that shows you what you are, and then you go back to being it."

"And if it was a bad thing you saw?"

"Then you go back to being that, too, unless you take a hand in it." He looked at her. "Which is the whole of the matter, and it's why I'll not say to you what I want to say."

"What do you want to say?"

"That I want you to leave him." He said it quite levelly. "There. I've said it. I'm not a subtle man and you knew that when you were fourteen."

The berg went away behind them as the ship swung very slowly at her anchor of drifting ice.

"You're a rich man's wife and I'm a fifth officer on seventeen pound ten a month," Harold said, "and if you came to me you'd be leaving a house in Liverpool and a name and a place in the world, and you'd be marrying a Welshman with no money and a temper and a mother in Chester, and you'd be at sea nine months of the year in a rented house in Barmouth, and every woman in that town would know your business and half of them would say it to your face. That's what it'd be. That's the whole of what it'd be, and I'll not dress it up in a song."

"And if I said I don't care about any of that?"

"Then I'd say you should care, because I do." He turned to face her. "I've been a man who took a thing because I wanted it. I took the sea at fourteen and I broke my father's heart doing it, and I'd do it again, and I know exactly what I am. But I'll not have you come to me and find out in a year that you've swapped one green drawing room for a smaller one with a leaking roof and a man who's away and who can't be spoken to before breakfast."

She was silent for a long moment.

"You are," she said, "the most infuriating person I have ever met."

"Aye."

"I have spent fifteen years thinking of you as—" She made a gesture with one hand, and dropped it. "As a thing that happened to me. A thing that was done and finished, that I had got over, that I did not think about except at Christmas and in the rain and when I heard a certain tune, which was often, which was daily, which was—" She stopped and started again. "And now you are standing here telling me the practical difficulties in a voice like a man reading out a bill of lading. Do you know what I have been through in the last twenty-four hours?"

"I do."

"No, you don't. You were doing a job." She said it without cruelty, which made it worse. "I sat in a boat and listened to them for an hour and a half and did nothing, and there is nothing in the world that you can say to me that will make that into a thing that is all right, and so I will not be made all right. I have decided that. I am going to be a woman who heard it for the rest of my life, and it will be in me in the way a stone is in a wall, and I am not going to try to take it out."

"Good," he said.

She looked up.

"Not good. I'm sorry. That came out wrong." He rubbed his face. "It's the only thing in you that I'd fight a man for. It's the fact that you'll not be comforted. If you'd come off that boat and been made all right in a week, I'd have been able to leave you on the pier and not think about you again. I've seen it happen. There's people on this ship now who are already telling the story as an adventure, and they'll be telling it as an adventure for fifty years and it'll never cost them another night's sleep, and God help me I don't blame them for it, I'd give a great deal to be one of them."

She laughed then, very quietly. "You would not."

"No," he admitted. "I would not."

---

They stood there. The ice moved on the water with a sound like a door being closed in another room.

"I'm not going to answer you," she said. "Not tonight, and not on this ship. If I answered you tonight it would be a woman on a raft answering a man who pulled her out of the water, and it would be a lie, and I would be ashamed of it, and so would you."

"I know."

"On Thursday I shall be in New York and my husband will be on the pier, and I will go with him to a house I have never seen with green curtains in it, and I will need to be there for a while. Do you understand? Weeks. Perhaps a month. I have to go and stand in that house and find out whether I am the woman you say I am or whether I am only a frightened woman a long way from home."

"And then?"

"And then," she said, "I am going to come to Barmouth on the train, and I am going to walk up to a door, and I am going to knock on it."

"That's my line."

"You had it fifteen years ago and you made a poor job of it," Arwen said. "It's my turn."

He put his hand out. She took it, and her hand was cold and thin and had a blister across the palm from an oar, and they stood at the rail of the *Carpathia* holding hands for perhaps a minute, in full view of anybody who cared to look, in the middle of an icefield, on the sixteenth of April 1912.

Then she said, "I am going to go and see whether the child is asleep," and let go of his hand, and went in.

Harold stayed at the rail a while longer, and looked at the ice.

At four in the morning they got the *Carpathia* under way again, and she turned her nose west and went on to New York with seven hundred and five extra people in her, and Harold Lowe stood on her bridge wing for a while and watched the last berg go by, and then went below and slept for eleven hours, which he had not done since he was a boy.
