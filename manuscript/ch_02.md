# II. Dead Reckoning

Harold Lowe had been aboard since Belfast. On the morning of the tenth of April, in Southampton, up before four in a cold that got into the seams of his boots, he spent the first hour of the day counting.

He counted because there was nothing else to do with himself. The ship was a building with a river under it, and buildings make men stupid. Every steward and every fireman and every cook on the *Titanic* had come aboard over the last forty-eight hours in a fog of shouting and paperwork, and by the morning of sailing they were all somewhere inside her, and none of them knew where. That was the thing nobody wrote about in the papers. The papers wrote about the swimming bath and the squash court and the seventeen hundred miles of electric cable. Nobody wrote about the fact that on her maiden voyage a ship is a town that has never been lived in, with a police force of eight officers and a crew of nine hundred who have never worked together, and that the first thing an officer does is try to find out how many of them can be relied on to do what they are told in the dark.

At four he had been up. He did not sleep well on land and worse before a sailing, and he had learned to stop fighting it. He shaved in cold water, put on his uniform with the strip of gold lace on the cuff that still felt like something he had borrowed, and went out on the boat deck while it was still black, and stood at the forward end of it with his hands in his pockets and his cap under his arm like a man at a graveside.

Portsmouth and Southampton Water were full of lights. Tugs, lighters, a Danish tramp working up the channel, the arc lamps on the dock sheds, all of it doubled and broken in the water. The ship's own lights were on, her windows going up the side of her in rows like a liner in a picture, and somewhere forward a winch was running and a man was singing.

He thought, as he always thought before a ship moved: *This is the last hour when nobody has made a mistake yet.*

---

The mistakes started at noon.

He was on the bridge wing with the third officer, Pitman, and the pilot, when the tugs began to haul her off the berth. She was heavy in the water and she came round slowly, and the crowd on the quay made a noise like weather, and Harold was looking aft along her side to see how she took the turn, which was his job, when Pitman said, "Oh, my God."

The *New York* had been moored in the Test, a White Star tender alongside her, and she was a big ship, an old ship, sixteen thousand tons of her, and Harold watched her come off the quay as though somebody had tilted the world.

"Her moorings are parting," he said.

"Aye," said Pitman. "And so will ours."

The *New York*'s bow swung out into the fairway and then travel, that great heavy bow, came on across the fifty feet of water and up the *Titanic*'s flank, drawn by the suction of her as surely as a nail to a magnet, and Harold felt the ship beneath him take a breath.

Six tugs. He counted them because counting was what he did. They came in from all sides with their funnels down and their wash going white, and one of them got a line on the *New York*'s bow and took the strain, and the line parted and whipped, and men on the quay scattered. Harold stood on the wing of the bridge with his hands on the rail and felt almost nothing. That was the other thing nobody told you about the sea. The dangerous minutes were quiet. Your heart went slow. Later, on shore, in the telling, it was all noise.

They got her off. The *Titanic* was held for the best part of an hour while the *New York* was warped back to her berth, and the crowd on the quay had something to talk about at their dinners, and the sailing was an hour late, which meant Cherbourg was late, which meant that for the rest of the crossing somebody would be trying to catch up a piece of time that had been lost in a place where nothing could be done about it.

Harold went below and wrote the word *fenders* in his notebook and then crossed it out. It was not a word that belonged to him. He was the fifth officer. His job on this ship was to stand watch, inspect the boats, keep his mouth shut, and learn the Atlantic.

---

He had been to sea fifteen years and had never once crossed it.

That was the thing he did not say to anybody, and it sat in him like a stone in a boot. He had been on the West African coast, on the *Belgic* and on the *Tropic*, in ships old enough to have sins. He had a master's certificate in a tin box in his cabin, ordinary master, which meant that on paper he was fit to command a vessel of any tonnage anywhere in the world, and in fact he was a fifth officer on seventeen thousand tons of somebody else's ship earning seventeen pounds ten a month.

He had been down the coast for years, nine months a trip, the kind of run that kills men with fever and rum and pays a hundred a year and keeps a man on shore for two months eating through it. He had had the fever twice. He had buried a shipmate at Grand Bassam, an old Dane who had told him, at the end, a great deal about the sea and nothing about himself. He had watched the sun come up over a river mouth and thought: *I'll die in this latitude and no one will ever hear of me and it will not be because I was not good enough.*

He was a Welshman on an English ship. He had learned early that this was not a thing to mention unless it was useful. But when the *Titanic* had come—the new ship, the great ship, the biggest thing ever built and they were taking anybody with a certificate and a clean discharge book—he had gone to Liverpool in March and stood in a room and been looked at, and afterwards he had walked out into the street and up to the corner and stood there and sworn to himself out loud, quietly, in Welsh, that he would be a commander before he was forty.

It did not occur to him that the ship was any part of that. It was a step. When a man is climbing a ladder he does not spend his time admiring the rung.

---

The officers' quarters were on the boat deck, a little run of cabins off the officers' mess with white-painted bulkheads and a smell of new linoleum and paint that would not go until somebody had smoked two hundred cigarettes in it. Harold had a cabin to himself, which was a luxury he had not had in eleven years, and he could not settle in it. He had folded his shirts into the drawers four times and each time taken them out and refolded them differently.

There were two hundred and forty-two pictures in the first class accommodation. He knew this because a steward had told him, in the tone of a man naming the value of a horse. There were four hundred and twenty tons of coal a day. There were six ice-making machines. The forward well deck was painted white on the inside of the bulwarks to keep the crew from being able to see the sea, a piece of management he had looked at for a long time and had privately admired.

He knew all these numbers because a fifth officer spends his first week memorising the ship until she is a shape in his head that he can walk through with his eyes shut. What he knew about water he kept to himself, because that was the only part of him that did not require a certificate.

---

On the second day out, the eleventh, there was a drill.

There were always drills. Boat drill on a White Star liner on passage is a performance with a good deal of rope in it, and Harold had a part: check the boats in the morning, report the falls, report the gear, count the oars and the water breakers and the biscuits, and see that everything was where the Boat Deck officer's chart said it was, and if it was not, find out why, and if the why was a man, put the man right, and if the why was the ship, write it down.

Twenty boats. He went round them in a little over an hour because he knew what he was looking for.

Sixteen of them hung in the davits, eight a side, each of them a wooden cutter of a good enough pattern, sixty-five capacity by the plate on the thwart. Four collapsibles on the roofs, they would have to be got off the roofs and over the side by hand, which took men and time. Twenty boats, and if a man was of a mind to multiply sixty-five by twenty and then by twenty more he would know that the whole boat complement of the *Titanic* was made to hold eleven hundred and seventy-eight people.

The ship carried about two thousand two hundred.

He did the sum on the eleventh, in his head, standing under number seven with his notebook against the davit, and then he did it again because he did not believe himself, and then he looked up the number of souls aboard and did it a third time.

Then he put his notebook in his pocket and went to find Moody.

Sixth officer. Twenty-four years old, quieter than Harold, a Blackheath boy with a good hand and a better memory and an appetite that had already become an officers' mess joke. He was running a party of seamen through the gripes on the forward boats and he came over wiping his hands, and Harold said, without preamble:

"Seven and the eighth. Do you know the capacity of these boats?"

"Sixty-five. Sixty-five point five."

"Twenty of them. That's not twelve hundred for two thousand two hundred."

Moody looked at him for a moment. He had the sort of face that was going to be good at forty and was still, at twenty-four, faintly unfinished, and something in it closed politely.

"Board of Trade," he said.

"Board of Trade."

"It's not a question of souls, Mr. Lowe, it's a question of cubic feet. The Board's rule is cubic feet, not souls. It works out at so much boat for so much ship, and by that reckoning we carry half again what we're asked to carry." He said it perfectly, he had learned it perfectly, and he said it like a man holding a door open for a call he does not intend to take. "She's got more boats than the regulation demands. That's the fact of it."

"That's not the fact I asked about."

"No," Moody said, and for a moment they stood there in the cold wind while a seaman behind them scraped a buckle on a fall and a woman two decks up laughed at something. "No, it isn't."

"There's no passengers in the Board of Trade."

"There's a great many of them in the first class."

Harold looked along the boat deck, where the boats were stacked each in its own cradle with the canvas laced over it, eight on this side going away aft in a line, and he could not have told anyone why the sight of them put a cold hand on his stomach. He had been on ships with fewer, on ships with none, on ships where the boats were lashed down for a voyage and a man who tried to get one off in a hurry would have a job finding a knife.

"Well," he said. "If it happens in daylight we're all right."

"It won't happen at all," Moody said. "It's the *Titanic*."

There was no cleverness in it. He meant it, the way a man means it when he has been to sea for six years and nothing has happened yet, and Harold, who had been at sea for fifteen and had been in three things that could have killed him and had a scar on his forearm from one of them, looked at him and did not argue.

"Get the men to change that griping line on eleven," he said. "It's chafed."

---

The captain came round the boat deck on the morning of the eleventh, and Harold, who had never served in a White Star ship before and had been told a great deal about Captain Smith by men who had, stood at the head of the ladder with his cap on and his hands at his sides.

Edward Smith was a heavy grey man in a black coat with a beard like a magistrate, and he came along the deck at the pace of a man who has walked that deck a thousand times and intends to walk it a thousand more. He stopped at number five and looked at the falls, and then at the gripes, and then at Harold.

"Fifth officer."

"Sir."

"Lowe." He said the name as though he were confirming it against a list in his head. "You had the *Tropic*."

"The *Belgic* and the *Tropic*, sir."

"Captain Carter's report on you was good." Smith looked at the boat again. "He said you were a driver."

It was not quite a compliment and not quite a complaint, and Harold, who had been in the merchant service long enough to know that both were being delivered at once, said nothing at all.

"That's what we want on a ship like this for the first month," Smith said. "After that we want a man who can be quiet." He put his hand on the davit. "Are you a quiet man, Mr. Lowe?"

"Not naturally, sir."

"Then learn it." The captain moved on a step, and then turned back. "You'll take the six to eight."

"Sir."

"Mind you're on the bridge wing at five minutes to. Not at the bell."

He went on down the deck with his hands behind his back, and the petty officer beside Harold breathed out and said, "He's a good man, the old man," in the tone of a man repeating a thing that was true and also a matter of policy, and Harold said, "Aye," and thought: *he'll do. He's got the trick of making a man want to be quiet for him.*

He thought about that again three days later, and it did not make him angry. It made him tired in a way he could not describe.

The other thing about the eleventh is that he wrote a letter.

He had written one before, in 1897, in Liverpool, on a bench in a lodging house off Canning Street with a borrowed pen, and had given it to Ada to hand to Arwen Prys, and had got on a ship and gone.

He had thought about that letter at intervals for fifteen years, the way a man thinks about a debt. He had written it badly; he knew that. He had been fourteen, and had believed himself fifteen. He had written a great deal about the sea and about what he intended to do and rather less about her, and at the end he had written something so unlike himself that he had had to put his hand over the page: *I will come back for you when I am worth coming for.* He remembered the sentence exactly. It sat in him like a swallow of bad water. It was the sort of thing a boy wrote who had read about it somewhere, and if she had read it she would have laughed, and he had hoped she would read it and not laugh, and had posted himself to the coast on the strength of it.

He had waited for an answer at every port for two years. Then he had stopped, in the way that you stop, not all at once, and had decided that the letter had been a boy's letter and the boy had been a fool and that Arwen Prys, who had a laugh like a service bell, would by now be married to somebody in the town with a shop and a house on the hill, and that this was the correct and ordinary end of the matter.

He had not once considered that the letter had never arrived.

---

He met her on the twelfth at ten past four in the afternoon, and afterwards, in his cabin, he found he could not work. He tried to write up the boat report. He got as far as *No. 11 griping line*, and then he put down the pen and sat with his elbows on his knees.

Fifteen years, and the first thing he had done was look at her coat and think: *that cost more than I make in three months.*

He had seen it in himself, standing there with a bit of swab rag in his hand and the wind going through him, and it had been a mean thing and he had said it and he was ashamed of it. She was somebody's wife and she was in the first class and she had a maid in the second class and a hat that had come from a shop with a carpet in it, and he was a fifth officer with salt on his hands and a manifest to check before eight. That was what the world was. The world had arranged itself very carefully around that fact for fifteen years, and he had been the one who arranged himself around it, and had been glad to, because a man who is climbing keeps his eyes on the rung.

But she had said *you could have written*, and her face had gone white with it, and he had said that he had written, and then he had watched her take that in and hold it, and he had not said the rest of it.

He had not said: *and I have been a fool in a way that has nothing to do with you.*

There was a knock at the door. It was Moody, with the second officer's compliments and the forward boat covers were to be left unlaced tonight on account of the damp.

"Tell him it's done," Harold said. "I'll do the port side myself."

"You've not eaten."

"I'll eat."

Moody stood in the doorway a moment. There was a way the younger man had of looking at you that suggested he had already decided what he thought and would wait a few years before saying it.

"Was that the lady from Barmouth?" he asked. "This afternoon."

"The lady is from Barmouth."

"I've never been to Wales."

"Nobody has," Harold said. "You should go. It rains."

Moody went away. Harold sat with his door open on the corridor, listening to the ship: the fans, the distant beat of the engines turning at seventy-five revolutions, a steward whistling somewhere in the pantry, the deck under him shuddering with the steady small shudder of a great ship going fast through a long swell.

He thought: *four o'clock tomorrow. She said she could be there.*

He thought: *if I have any sense at all I will be on watch.*

Then he got his cap and went up to the port side and unlaced the boat covers, one by one, in the dark, with his hands, because that was a job that could be done with the hands and no mind at all.

---

There is a thing a sailor does when he cannot sleep and it is not his watch.

He does not go up to the bridge; that is a hanging matter or near it. He goes and stands somewhere and takes the ship's pulse. Harold went aft along the boat deck to the after end, where the second class promenade came up to a rail, and stood in the lee of the last boat, and looked at the sea.

It was the twelfth of April and the wind had come round into the north-west, and the ship had begun to move a little, a long slow lift and fall with a roll of perhaps five degrees on the top of it, which is nothing, which is the amount a ship moves when the sea is in a good humour. Above him the mast and its yards went up into a sky with a great many stars in it and no moon worth the name. Aft, the wake went out white and then grey and then nothing. Ahead, the foremast and the crow's nest light, and beyond that the dark going on for a very long way.

There were men in the crow's nest looking at that dark for the good of two thousand two hundred people. There were six men in the stokeholds shovelling coal into one furnace mouth. There was a woman in the first class, two hundred feet forward and four decks down and one deck up, asleep or not asleep, who had given him a half-sovereign with a hole in it when he was fifteen, if she had kept it, which she probably had not.

He was not sentimental by trade. Sentiment on a ship was a leak. But he stood at the after rail for a little while and let himself do the sum that he had never let himself do in fifteen years:

He had been a boy who could not stay. That was the whole of it. He had run at fourteen, not because of her, not because of his father, but because of the particular dread that comes on a boy of that age when he looks at a town and sees exactly how his life will go in it, the counter, the apron, the same nine streets, the same forty faces, the grave at the top of the hill with his name on the stone and a hundred years of rain. He would have gone at fourteen whether she had existed or not.

And then he had met her, and the tragedy was not that he had to choose. The tragedy was that he had been given a year of something that made staying seem worth it, and then he had gone anyway, on the strength of a letter, and the letter had been a boy's letter, and he had known it even as he wrote it.

The ship lifted. The wake went out and out.

*Two more days to New York,* he thought. *Then I am out of it. Fifty years from now nobody will have heard of the fifth officer of the Titanic, and I will be a man with a command and a house and this will be a thing I remember once a year.*

He went below and slept for three hours, and dreamed about the harbour at Barmouth at low water, the boats all leaning the same way, and the smell of tar, and a girl he could not see.
