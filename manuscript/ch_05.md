# V. The Ship That Could Not Sink

There is a particular sort of dinner that a great ship gives on a Sunday.

Arwen had been to a great many of them. Iestyn's business had put her at tables in Liverpool and in London and twice in Hamburg, and she had learned the shape of them: the fish, the soup, a bird, a saddle, a sweet, the little ices that the French chefs made to show the kitchen could do mathematics, the coffee, the cigars moving away to another room. Nine courses and two hours and a band playing at the far end, and the whole thing conducted in the manner of a service, with the stewards in a long line and the plates going out and coming back and the wine being poured in a small, exact, continuous ceremony.

The Sunday dinner on the *Titanic* was the best she had ever eaten.

She sat between Mr. Thorne and a Mr. Ryerson from Philadelphia who had a round face and a great deal to say about the price of copper, and opposite Mrs. Penhallow, in black, with her rings off because it was Sunday. There were four hundred people in the room in evening dress and the whole of it was lit from above by electric light that came out of the ceiling in a hundred small soft globes, and it was warm, and it smelled of lemon and hot metal and clean linen, and every part of it had been made for people exactly like her.

"I don't hold with this at all," Mrs. Penhallow said, over the potage. "Sunday dinner should be cold. A piece of cold beef and a baked potato and a row with one's sister. This is not a dinner, it's a pageant."

"You say that every Sunday," said her neighbour, a Mr. Ismay's assistant or cousin or something of the sort, whose name Arwen had not caught.

"I say it every Sunday because it's true every Sunday."

"You'll take the canvasback duck, though."

"I will take the canvasback duck," Mrs. Penhallow agreed, "because I am a hypocrite, and because it is the best thing on this ship, and because I am sixty-four."

Arwen ate. She found, to her surprise, that she was hungry. She had eaten badly for four days and now she was eating canvasback duck and drinking a glass of something Mr. Ryerson had recommended with an air of large experience, and listening to the band play a waltz from *The Merry Widow*, and she was aware of the fact—aware of it as she would have been aware of a stone in her shoe—that she was happy.

That was the thing she could never afterwards make anyone understand, and in the end she stopped trying. Everyone wanted the last night of the *Titanic* to have been an omen. They wanted it to have been cold and strange, they wanted the warnings and the lifeboats and the iceberg, they wanted some sort of premonition to have run around the dining saloon at eleven o'clock, and she had to tell them that it was a very good dinner, and that she had laughed twice, and that there had been a woman at the next table with an enormous diamond and a bad temper and it had been funny.

At a quarter past ten she went out to the enclosed promenade with her stole and stood at the glass. The ship's clocks had been put back at midnight, and then put back again; the night was going to be long, and everybody knew it, and there was a general feeling of generosity in the room behind her, of a voyage that had gone well and would be over on Thursday.

The sea was like a floor. She had been told by a steward that this was the finest passage he had ever made in April, and that they were making twenty-two and a half knots, which was almost the best she could do, and that they had come nearly five hundred miles since the noon.

She looked at the water for a while and thought about the whistle in her pocket. She had taken it out, in her cabin, at six o'clock, and turned it in her hands. It was not a beautiful object. It was a piece of pipe with a plug in it. It had been made, badly, by a man who had been too tired to sleep and who had done it in a lamp room with borrowed tools.

She had put it back in the pocket of the coat she had on.

---

At ten to eleven she was in the reception room outside the dining saloon, taking a cup of coffee she did not want in order not to go to bed, when the engines changed.

She did not know that was what it was. She knew only that a thing had happened, the way you know that a person in the room has stood up. There was no noise. The vibration that had been in the floor for four days, which she had stopped noticing and would have sworn was not there, was not there. The chandelier gave a small shiver and was still.

A man near her said, "What's that?" in the tone of a man who has heard a noise in his own house at night.

"It's nothing," said his wife. "It's the engines."

"That's what I said. It's the engines."

Arwen put the cup down. She went to the window, and she could see the sky, and the sea, and a great many stars, and nothing else at all.

She went out into the corridor and up the staircase to A deck, because there was a window there that opened, and she stood in the vestibule of the Grand Staircase under the carved clock with the two figures on either side of it, and there was a little crowd of people in evening dress doing the same thing.

"The ship's stopped," a man behind her said.

"Nonsense. She's stopped her engines for something."

"There's a lot of smoke coming off the back of her."

Arwen went up to the top of A deck and out on the forward part where there was a rail, and she was in the open air, and the cold was extraordinary, and there was indeed a great deal of smoke and steam coming off the after part of the ship and blowing away to the north-east, and there were people at the rail all around her in furs and evening cloaks, looking at it, and two women were laughing.

"Ice," somebody said. "It's ice. There's ice on the forward deck—you can see it in the light."

But she could not see it. She could see nothing forward but the dark, and a thing that might have been a shadow.

She stood there for perhaps five minutes. It was a curious feeling and she observed it with interest, because she was not frightened; she was alert. She had felt the same thing once in a doctor's waiting room in Liverpool, and once, in 1906, when she had woken at two in the morning with a pain and known before the nurse came that the child was gone. It was the feeling of a person who has stopped waiting for something to happen and is now waiting to be told.

Then the door behind her opened and a steward came out, and he was not lounging, he was walking fast, and he said, "Beg pardon, madam. The officers have asked for all passengers to go below and put on their lifebelts."

The rail went silent.

"Don't be absurd," a man said.

"I was told to say it, sir." The steward's voice was not steady, and his face—she would always remember this—his face was apologising. "It's a precaution only. It's the regulation. The officers have asked for all passengers to go below and put on their lifebelts and come up to the boat deck."

---

In the corridor on the way down, a woman in a pale dressing gown with a fur over it caught her arm.

"Mrs. Meredith, isn't it? We sat opposite at the concert. What is happening? You've been on ships. Do something."

"I've been on ships that sank twice a week for four days," Arwen said. "It's what happens when it stops."

The woman stared at her.

"I'm sorry," Arwen said. "That was a stupid thing to say. Put your boots on. Put on two pairs of stockings and your heaviest coat and take every blanket in your cabin, and don't let anyone talk you out of them."

"Oh God," the woman said, and went.

In B-deck, the corridor was full of people going into cabins and coming out again. A steward was going along knocking on doors and calling, "All passengers. All passengers on your lifebelts." A man was standing in the middle of the corridor with his collar off saying to anybody and to nobody, "It's a drill. It's got to be a drill."

Bessie was at the door of the cabin with her hair down and her boots on the wrong feet.

"Oh, madam," she said. "Oh, madam, I knew you'd come back."

"Where did you get that?"

"They were in the wardrobe." Bessie held up the cork jacket. "They're a great deal heavier than you think."

"Put it on properly. Under your arms, not over. Under. Then put your coat over it and button it and put your hat on." Arwen was already at work on her own. The jacket smelled of cork and dust and something faintly like new canvas. "Have you your shoes? Both of them?"

"Yes."

"Have you a blanket?"

"The one off my bed's still there."

"Get it." Arwen went down on her knees in front of Bessie, which she had not done for anyone but a child, and pulled the straps tight under her arms and made them fast, and then looked at her. "Now listen to me. You will not go to second class. You will stay with me. If we are separated, you go up to the boat deck and you stand by the first boat you see that has women in it and you say *I am a lady's maid, I am with a party,* and you do not go back down for anything. Not for the trunk, not for the jewel case, nothing. Do you hear me?"

"Madam, the jewel case is—"

"I have the jewel case in my coat. Do not make me say this again."

They went up.

---

The boat deck, at that time, was not yet the boat deck of the stories.

It was cold and it was busy and it was—this was the part that no one ever believed—hotchpotch. There were people in evening dress and in furs and in dressing gowns. There were men in pyjamas and overcoats. There was a woman in a white satin evening dress with a Pekingese under her arm, holding it up out of the way of people's feet. There was a lady being offered bread and butter by a steward, and taking it, and eating it. There were forty or fifty people standing about in the cold with their lifebelts on over their evening clothes, and there were a great many empty seats in the deck chairs, and the band was playing.

The band was playing. She would be asked about this by reporters, later, and she would tell them that the band was playing in the lounge, and that you could hear it through the windows if you were near enough, but that the steam was so loud that you could not really hear the tune. It was not "Nearer, My God, to Thee." It was something with a dance in it. She was certain of that, and she was certain of very little.

And the boats were being got ready.

She could see them better now. She had walked past these boats four days ago while a man from Barmouth explained the plug in the bottom to her, and now the canvas covers were off them and they hung over the side, and the falls had been overhauled and the boats swung out on their davits, and each one had an officer with it and a group of seamen, and the whole thing was being done with the awful unhurriedness of men working by a rule.

"Women and children to the port side," a steward was saying, over and over. "This way, madam. Women and children to the port side."

Port side. The port side was on the left as you faced forward, she thought, and then was not sure she was right, and turned round twice.

"Mrs. Meredith." Mrs. Penhallow was standing by number ten with an extraordinary hat on and no gloves. "You are to come with me. I have been told I am not allowed to wait in the palm court and I said to the man, I must wait somewhere, and he said, come this way, ma'am, and here we are."

"Have you your jewel—"

"Don't you dare." Mrs. Penhallow took her arm with a grip that was entirely of bone. "I was told, months ago, by a very good friend who was on the *Republic* when she went down in ninety-nine. She said, three things. She said, keep hold of the person next to you. She said, do the thing that is being done, and do not start a thing of your own. And she said, take off your skirt if you have to climb and do not worry about your pride, because pride weighs more than a skirt." She looked at Arwen. "Do you know, I have thought about that every day for thirteen years and this is the first time I have had a chance to pass it on."

"How old was your friend?"

"Seventy," said Mrs. Penhallow. "She died in her bed. It was not a drowning, I said to her, it was a moral collapse, and she hit me with a cushion."

---

They stood by number ten for a long time.

There was a great deal of shouting and none of it seemed to be making the boat go. The officer at the davits was a tall Englishman with a hatchet face and a voice that carried—she heard later that his name was Mr. Lightoller—and he had put four or five women into the boat, and each one of them came back out again. A woman would be led to the rail and she would look down at the boat swinging over the sea, ten feet across the dark, and she would say, "I'm not going. Nothing has happened. Why should I go?"

And the officer would say, "Madam, please." And the woman would say, "Nothing has happened."

The band played. The steam roared. It came out of the after end of the ship in a great white column and the noise of it was a sort of wall around the whole scene, so that everybody on the boat deck was shouting in this enormous silence, and the little groups of people stood behind the boats waiting to be told what to do.

At a little before half past twelve, a rocket went up.

Arwen saw it go. It went up from the forward part of the ship with a hiss and a small thump and then broke into five white stars that hung and sank, and the whole of the boat deck was lit for a second and every face on it turned up, and then the stars went out and the dark came back down like a lid, and the people stood there in the cold and looked at each other, and nobody said anything at all.

"Well," Mrs. Penhallow said. "That is that. That is what I have been waiting to hear."

---

The thing that surprised her most, afterwards, was how long it took.

She had thought—she had never thought about it, that was the truth of it—but if she had thought about it she would have thought that a ship going down was quick, like a lantern being blown out. It was not. It went on and on and it took two hours and a half, and in that time she stood on a boat deck in a cork jacket with her hands in her sleeves, and the coldest she had ever been, and watched the sea come to them.

It was the sound that told you.

At half past twelve she had been cold; at a quarter past one she had ice in her bones. And at some point she had begun to notice that when she stood at the rail and looked down, the boat deck was not so high above the water as it had been. First the lettering of the ship's name, up forward, was only a few feet above the sea. Then the starboard side of the forward well deck began to be covered; she could see it from where she stood, a black shine coming over the plates.

A man near her began to pray out loud, in a language she did not know.

"Go and take a boat, Mrs. Meredith," Mrs. Penhallow said.

"I'll wait."

"You will not wait. There is nothing to wait for."

"I am waiting for my maid."

"Your maid is a sensible girl and she is somewhere with a boat by now, being sensible, precisely because you told her to be, and if she comes up here and finds you and there is no boat left for her, she will not thank you for your loyalty. What she'll do is die in your company, which is not a favour." Mrs. Penhallow's voice was perfectly level. "I am sixty-four and widowed and childless and I have no one to answer to and I intend to sit in that boat with my hands folded and be a nuisance to nobody. You are thirty years old and you have an entire life in front of you, and there is a woman on this deck who cannot bring herself to move and needs to see somebody move. Go and be her."

Arwen did not answer. She could not.

"Go on," Mrs. Penhallow said. "I shall see you on the *Carpathia*. I've had the tickets sent ahead."

---

Number twelve had been swung out and the falls were being led, and the officer with the hatchet face was at the rail with his hand up, and there was a queue of women going down into it—a stewardess, two girls with their arms round each other, a woman with no hat at all and a servant behind her with a baby she would not hand over to anyone.

"Just one moment, madam." Lightoller put his hand out to stop a man in a tweed coat. "Not you."

"There's no women present."

"I said no women present, sir, and I meant at that particular instant. Stand back."

The boat was hanging at the davits with perhaps thirty people in it. There was room for more and nobody to put in it. An old woman was refusing to go down the falls; two men were trying to help her and she was saying, "I have never been carried in my life."

Arwen thought: *Iestyn will not know what happened to me for four days.*

She thought: *the whistle is in the pocket of my grey coat, and the grey coat is in my cabin.*

She thought: *I have not had a single thought in nine years that was not about myself.*

She went forward. The deck was beginning to slant and it was a strange feeling, like standing in a room in a house that was settling. She passed behind the boat and there was an officer running down the deck towards her with his cap gone and his face black, and she had half a second to step aside, and she did, and when he passed she caught the side of his face in the light from the open door, and it was not a face she knew at all. The face of a man who has been doing something terrible for an hour.

She got to the rail. The gap between the ship and the boat was three feet, and below it the black water came up and went away and came up again, and the boat rose and fell against the ship's side with the falls groaning.

"Now, madam," the officer said. "Now, please."

She put her foot on the gunwale and her hand on the shoulder of the man holding the falls, and she found that she was above the boat and looking down into it, and there were thirty faces turned up to her in the dark, and beyond them nothing but black water and the lights of the ship going down its side in rows.

"One thing," she said. "There is a girl in second class. Her name is Corrigan. If I am not—"

"You'll find her on the *Carpathia*," Lightoller said. "Into the boat, madam, if you please."

She went over. She caught her skirt on the way and it ripped and she did not care. She landed awkwardly on a thwart and hands were on her at once and she was pushed down into the bottom of the boat, and the boat was hard and wet and there was an inch of water in the bottom of it, and somebody's arm was against her face, and the whole thing stank of tar and of the sea.

She sat up and got her bearings. She was in a wooden boat, and the ship was above her, and she had never in her life seen anything as big as she was.

"Fend off," a voice above her shouted. "Fend off there, or you'll have the plugs out of her."

The falls began to run. There was a man in the boat with a white armband and a soft Irish voice saying, "Easy now, easy, hold her off with the oar, hold her off, that's it, that's the way," and the *Titanic* rose past them, and rose, and rose, and it went on rising for a very long time.

Arwen looked up. There were people at every rail on every deck, a crowd of them, hundreds of faces, and they were looking down at the boat, and every one of them was silent.

Somebody in the boat began to cry, and then stopped.

"Where's the officer?" a woman said. "Who's in charge? Where is the officer?"

"There's no officer in this boat, ma'am," the Irishman said, and his voice was apologetic, and Arwen's whole body went cold. "They're all forward. There's a quartermaster went down in twelve but he's not in her, he's gone up to the bridge. It's only me and this gentleman here with the oars."

"Then what are we to do?"

"We're to pull away," he said. "That's what we're to do. We're to pull away and wait, and there's a dozen ships coming, and it's only a matter of the getting away from her side before she—"

He stopped.

Before she sucks us down, was what he did not say.

"Give me an oar," said Arwen.
