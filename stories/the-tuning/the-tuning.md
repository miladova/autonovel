# The Tuning

THE GERMAN at the gate on Bagatela read their papers longer than the papers deserved. While he read, Marianna watched the ash come down into the lilacs.

It fell the way snow falls, without hurry and without choosing where, and it lay on the leaves in grey flakes and did not melt. The lilacs had come out on the third of May, the day Zosia turned fourteen, which was also the day of the Constitution, which nobody in that city had been allowed to say out loud for four years. On the sunny side of the courtyard they were the ordinary pale violet. Where the wind had been, they were grey.

"Beruf?" {{Occupation?}}

"Klavierstimmerin." She had practised the word in the stairwell. Piano tuner. It was not true. It was the only word on the slip that would get her through the gate.

He read it twice, then looked at her hands, which she had scrubbed that morning with a brush and the last of the soap, and which had made no difference at all. He gave the papers back and stepped aside.

Zosia went through first, the way they had arranged, so that if anything happened Marianna would be behind her, and could decide.

She had brought her because the school had closed in March, when they took two of the teachers out of the corridor in front of the children, and because a girl of fourteen alone behind a door in that city was not a thing she was prepared to leave.

Krysia Malanowska, who lived one floor down with her mother and a canary, cleaned at that villa three mornings a week, and it was Krysia who had come up on Sunday, out of breath, to say that there was a grand in the house on Bagatela that had not been tuned since the winter, and that the major wanted it done before the end of the week.

There was nobody else to do it. The two men who had tuned in that quarter were gone, one of them east in 1940 and the other off a pavement on Wolska in a roundup the winter after, and the men who were left did not walk into German houses any more, because a man who went in to tune a piano could come out of it onto a lorry. You played, Krysia said. She had heard Marianna play through the floor.

Four hundred złoty, and a slip with an office stamp on it, good for one afternoon. Marianna had said yes before Krysia had finished the sentence, and that was the part of the week she was least proud of, and it was only Wednesday.

Beyond the gate the courtyard had a chestnut tree in it, and a bicycle leaning against the chestnut, and gravel that had been raked. At the far end the villa stood with its shutters open, which no Polish house in that city had done since 1939. The steps had been scrubbed. Beside the door hung a brass plate with her uncle's name on it, polished almost through.

"Sit on the step," Marianna said. "Don't talk to the sentry. Don't look at him either."

"Which is it?"

"Neither. You sit and you look at your shoes and you count."

"I'm fourteen."

"Then count to a thousand and you'll be fifteen."

Zosia sat on the step with her knees together and her hands flat on her skirt and her chin lifted slightly, the way she did when she intended to disobey a small part of an instruction, and Marianna went in alone, because the slip had only one name on it.

Frau Kalisch was fifty, from Łódź, and had been in the house since 1940, and had opinions about the curtains. She took the slip, read it, and said, "Sie sind spät."[[You are late.]]

"The gate."

"Ja." She handed the slip back and turned and went up the stairs without checking that Marianna followed, which was itself a judgment, and at the turn she said, over her shoulder, that she had a brother in the police in Łódź, as though it were part of the directions. "Der Flügel ist im Salon. Nicht anfassen, was Sie nicht stimmen müssen."[[The grand is in the drawing room. Don't touch anything you don't have to tune.]]

The salon had been her aunt's morning room. The furniture had been moved into new arrangements that favoured the windows and left the middle of the floor empty, and where the sideboard had stood there was a lighter rectangle on the wallpaper, the shape of a picture that had hung there since 1911. The picture had been of her aunt and uncle at Zakopane, her uncle with his hat in his hands. The wallpaper had kept the shape of them.

The piano stood at an angle it had never stood at, its lid closed, a glass of something on a folded newspaper beside it. It was a Blüthner. Her aunt's, and before that her aunt's mother's, and it had come from Leipzig in 1904 in a crate that took four men and a horse to move, and it had a cigarette burn on the lid from a cousin's wedding in 1931, and the F in the fourth octave had a damper that stuck.

Marianna put her bag down. She opened the lid and the smell came up out of it: felt, and dust, and forty years of that particular room.

"There is a cloth," Frau Kalisch said from the door. "Use it, please. The Major does not like dust."

"Where is the Major?"

"At his office. He will not be back before four." She looked at the clock on the mantel, which had stopped, and then at her own watch, which had not. "You have two hours. Tune it, and go."

Marianna took the tuning fork out of its cloth roll. It had come from the Conservatory in 1921 and she had kept it through three flats and a fire, and she struck it against the heel of her hand and held it to the bone behind her ear, and it hummed, and it was right, and it was the first thing that had been right all day.

Then she struck A, and the piano answered a hair flat, and something in her chest let go, because it was exactly wrong where she had expected it to be wrong. She took the hammer out and set it on the pin and began.

She worked for a little under an hour. It was slow work, and it was work she liked, and she had not been near a piano since the winter the Conservatory's instruments were carried out through the front doors on other people's shoulders. The work did not need her. The wrist did the turning, and the ear did the judging, and the two of them got on with it while she thought about nothing at all, which was the first rest she had had in a month.

Frau Kalisch sat in the hall with the door open, knitting. Not to watch, exactly. It was not required that a housekeeper watch a Pole tune a piano. She sat where she could see, and her needles went, and once she said, without lifting her head, "Das ist ein guter Flügel."[[That is a good grand.]]

"Ja," Marianna said. "Er ist sehr gut." {{Yes. It is very good.}}

At ten past three, a car stopped in the courtyard, and a door, and boots on the gravel, and then a voice in the hall, quick, asking something about the car at four, and the housekeeper answering him.

The voice.

Marianna's hand was on the hammer. She heard the voice say the word *Wagen*[[car]] and then say that the driver was to wait and that the other thing could be dealt with in the morning, and she heard the stairs, and she heard the floor of the hall above them take his weight, and she heard him stop in the doorway of the room.

She did not turn round. She kept her hand on the pin. It was in the fourth octave, the F, the one with the damper.

"Marianna," he said.

There was a great deal of noise in her ears, which was her own blood, and under it the fork still humming where it lay on the cloth, and she did not turn because turning would make it true.

"Marianna," he said again, quietly, and that was worse, because the second time he said it he said it the way a man says a word in a language he had stopped speaking.

Then she turned.

He had come into the room. He was in the uniform and it fitted him, and there was dust on his boots, and the belt had made a line across him that would outlast the day. He had gone heavier through the shoulders, and his hair had gone back from his forehead, and there was a shaving cut on his jaw with a scrap of paper stuck to it. His eyes had not changed at all, which was the worst of it.

She had spent nineteen years making a story out of this man, and the story had a shape she could carry. He stood in the door of her aunt's morning room and did not fit it.

He looked at the housekeeper, then, and something went across his face quickly, like a hand over a table, and when he spoke he spoke loudly and in his officer's voice, and he spoke to the room. "Frau Frontczak. Es ist lange her."[[Mrs Frontczak. It has been a long time.]]

Then, to Frau Kalisch, without turning: "Bleiben Sie." {{Stay.}}

The knitting did not stop. The needles went on, and the woman's eyes came up over them and stayed there, and Marianna understood that she was going to be in this room for whatever happened next, and that she was going to remember it precisely, and that she had a brother in the police in Łódź, because she had mentioned him on the stairs.

"Sie sind also Klavierstimmerin," he said. {{So you are a piano tuner.}}

"Meine Tante hatte einen Stimmer. Ich habe von ihm gelernt." {{My aunt had a tuner. I learned from him.}}

"Ich erinnere mich."[[I remember.]] He said it flatly. He was standing with his weight on his heels, rocked back a little, which was a habit he had had at twenty-two because he had been proud of a new pair of shoes, and the habit was still there, in the boots, and it was the second thing that undid her.

He went to the window. He put his hands behind his back. "Ihr Mann?" {{Your husband?}}

"Er lebt." {{He is alive.}}

"Was macht er?" {{What does he do?}}

"Er arbeitet."[[He works.]] She did not say where. He did not ask again, and she understood that he had decided not to ask again, and that the deciding cost him something.

The girl was in the doorway.

Marianna had not heard her come up. She was standing just inside the room with her hands at her sides, and she was looking at the Major with an expression Marianna could not read, which was new, because she had always been able to read her daughter.

"Und das ist Ihre Tochter," he said. {{And this is your daughter.}}

"Zofia."

"Zofia." He said it correctly, with the stress on the right syllable, and it was more intimate than if he had touched her. "Wie alt bist du?"[[How old are you?]]

"Vierzehn," Zosia said. Her German was good. Her mother had taught her, in the evenings, out of a book with a broken spine. {{Fourteen.}}

"Seit neun Tagen," Marianna said, and then heard herself, and stopped. {{For nine days.}}

The clock on the mantel was stopped and the ash was coming down outside the window, and the Major stood looking at her daughter, and Marianna watched him do the arithmetic. There was nothing wrong with the arithmetic. The girl had come nine days ago, in a hospital on Mokotowska, nineteen years after the last time this man had put his hands on her mother. Any man in the room could do the sum in four seconds.

He took longer than four seconds. Then he looked away, and he said, "Es ist eine gute Zeit, vierzehn zu sein."[[Fourteen is a good age to be.]] It was not a joke and was not meant to be one. Zosia’s eyes came round to her mother and stayed there.

That was the moment, Marianna thought afterwards, when she should have taken her by the elbow and gone. Her papers were in order, the slip was in her pocket, and the piano was in tune except for the F. There was a door, and a sentry, and an hour of daylight. She could have gone. What she did was stand there with a tuning hammer in her hand and let the room go on happening, and she has never been able to say, since, which of them she was waiting for.

"Ich habe dir geschrieben," she said.[[I wrote to you.]] In German. Using the other word, the one for people who are close.

The knitting stopped.

Marianna felt it stop, the way you feel a door close in another room, and she knew what she had just done, and she did not take it back, because the thing she had just done was the only true thing she had said in nineteen years.

The Major looked at the housekeeper. Then at the floor. Then at Marianna. "Wann?" {{When?}}

"Three times. In the spring and twice in the summer."

He was quiet. His hand went up and touched the shaving cut on his jaw and came away with the paper on it, and he looked at the paper as if it had said something. "Ich habe keinen Brief bekommen," he said.[[I never received a letter.]]

"Nein."

"Keinen einzigen." {{Not one.}}

He turned his hand over and looked at the palm. Then he began to speak slowly, in German, in the way of a man translating himself out of an older language, and the sentences came to her one at a time and had to be held.

His father's workshop had been finished by then, he said. Nineteen twenty-four, and the mark was worth nothing, and the pianos had gone for flour. His mother had kept the post in that house; she had a cousin in Posen who had married a Jew, and she had begun to be afraid of the post, and she had taken what came for him and put it in a drawer and told him that the girl in Warsaw had stopped writing. And he had believed her, he said. He had believed her because it was easier than the other thing.

"Was war das andere?" Marianna said. {{What was the other thing?}}

The needles had begun again. Three stitches. Four.

"That you had gone on with your life," he said. "Which you had."

The telephone rang in the hall.

He went out to it, and Marianna stood in her aunt's morning room with her daughter and a knitting German, and put her hand flat on the lid of the piano, on the cigarette burn, and pressed down until the bones of her fingers hurt.

He talked for perhaps two minutes. The door was open. The words came through in pieces: *Donnerstag*[[Thursday]], and a number, and *mit den anderen*[[with the others]], and *nein, die gleiche Liste*[[no, the same list]], and then something about a truck that had not arrived, and a laugh, short and real, at something the other man said. Then the receiver went down and he came back into the room and stood at the window again with his hands behind his back, looking northwest, where the smoke was.

From the window, in that house, you could see it. You could see the colour of it if the day was clear, and the day was clear, and the ash had been falling on that windowsill since April.

"Mamo," Zosia said, in Polish, quietly. "Czy to prawda, że oni ich palą?" {{Mama, is it true they are burning them?}}

He understood. He must have learned some Polish that year, or learned it since and kept it quiet, because he answered her in German before Marianna could get a hand on her arm.

"Es wird aufgeräumt," he said. It is being tidied up.

Nobody spoke.

"Dort sind Partisanen," he said. "Bewaffnete Männer. Es ist eine militärische Angelegenheit, und sie ist fast zu Ende."[[There are partisans there. Armed men. It is a military matter, and it is nearly over.]] He said it evenly, in the voice he used for the car at four and the truck that had not arrived, and then he turned round and said, "Der Flügel ist noch nicht fertig,"[[The grand is not finished yet.]] and looked at the tuning hammer in Marianna's hand.

"They are not partisans," Zosia said. In Polish. To her mother. "The ones who are burning are not—"

"Zosia." Marianna's voice came out of her at a volume she had not intended, and the room got smaller, and the needles went on, and the Major looked at the two of them with an expression of mild, tired patience. It was worse than anger. She had never seen it on his face before, and she would see it there every night for the rest of her life.

"Spielen Sie etwas," he said. "Bitte." {{Play something. Please.}}

She could have said that the instrument was not finished. She could have said that she had to be home before the curfew, which was nine that month, which everyone in the city knew to the minute. She could have picked up her bag and her daughter and her papers and her slip and gone.

She sat down on the stool, which was her aunt's stool, and put her foot on the pedal, and played the Mazurka in A minor, the one with the falling line in it, the fourth of the Opus 17, which is a piece about a country that is not there.

It is forbidden. Everyone in that city knew it was forbidden, the way they knew the curfew hour, and she played it in a German house in front of a German officer and a German housekeeper with a brother in the police. If she had been asked why, afterwards, she would have said that she did not know. The truth is that he had asked her to play, and that there was a version of her, twenty-two years old, who would have played this for him at midnight in a flat on Wilcza with the windows open, and that the version of her sitting on the stool was not going to be told by anybody which woman she was.

She played it well. The instrument was still a hair flat in the middle octaves, and the F stuck, and she played around both of them the way you walk around a hole in a road, and her hands were cold and did not care.

Nobody moved. The housekeeper's needles had stopped. The Major stood at the window with his hands behind his back, not looking at her, looking at the smoke, and his shoulders were up around his ears, and she thought, with a clarity that felt like being struck: he is standing where he can be seen from the gate. He has chosen the window.

When it ended, the room held it for a moment, the way a room does, and then the ash came down and the clock did not strike four, because it had stopped.

"Sie spielen es langsamer," he said. {{You play it slower.}}

"Ja." {{Yes.}}

"Früher hast du es schneller gespielt." {{You used to play it faster.}}

"Ich war jünger."[[I was younger.]] She took her hands off the keys. "Everyone is slower now."

He did not answer that. He went to the desk, and sat down at it, and took a printed form out of a drawer, and unscrewed the cap of his pen.

"Name."

"Frontczak."

"Vorname." {{First name.}}

"Marianna."

"Geburtsname." {{Maiden name.}}

She looked at him. He had the pen over the line, and his hand was the same hand, with the flat ridged nail on the right thumb that came from a boyhood of pushing tuning pins, and he did not look up.

"Tarnowska," she said.

"Ich weiß, wie man das schreibt," he said. {{I know how to spell it.}}

The pen stopped. It stopped for the length of one breath, and then it wrote, in small upright letters, the way a tuner writes, and Marianna watched her own name come out of his hand onto a German form in her aunt's morning room.

He wrote for a while. He filled in the office of the household, and the hours, and he stamped it with a stamp he took out of the drawer, and blotted it, and blew on it, and held it up.

"Sie sind bei dem Haushalt des Stabes beschäftigt," he said. "Bis zum einunddreißigsten Dezember. Ihre Tochter ist Angehörige, sie steht mit darauf."[[You are employed by the staff household. Until the thirty-first of December. Your daughter is a dependent; she is covered on it.]] He put it on the desk and turned it round to face her. "Sie kommen zweimal in der Woche. Dienstag und Freitag. Sie stimmen den Flügel, und was sonst im Haus an Arbeit ist."[[You will come twice a week. Tuesday and Friday. You tune the grand, and whatever else there is to do in the house.]]

She looked at the paper. Her name on it, and her daughter's, and a stamp with an eagle on it, and a line at the bottom for a signature that was already there, small and upright, and the date: 12. V. 1943.

It was a piece of paper that would keep them out of a roundup on Krucza. It was also a rope tied to a desk in a German house, twice a week, indefinitely, with a man sitting at the desk who had put her name on it in his own handwriting.

"Ja," Marianna said.

She heard herself say it. That was the thing she would keep: not the room, not the mazurka, not the nineteen years. She heard her own voice say *Ja* to that man in that uniform in that house, and it was steady, and it was pleasant, and she had not known until that moment that she was a person who could do that.

She folded the paper and put it in the inside pocket of her coat, and picked up her bag, and took her daughter's wrist.

At the door he said, "Hast du noch das Metronom?" {{Do you still have the metronome?}}

"The walnut one?"

"Ja." {{Yes.}}

"I sold it," she said. "In 1940. For coal."

"Natürlich," he said. Of course. And then, as if it were the same sentence, "Es hat nie richtig getickt."[[It never kept proper time.]]

And she went out through the hall and down the scrubbed steps into the courtyard, where the ash was coming down into the lilacs, and Frau Kalisch held the door open for her with an expression of great politeness, and said, "Ihre Tochter spricht sehr gut Deutsch."[[Your daughter speaks very good German.]]

* * *

They walked the length of Bagatela without speaking, and turned up through the streets behind the Aleje, where the Germans had the pavements and the Poles had the gutters, and where a tram went by with a board on the front of it that said NUR FÜR DEUTSCHE[[Germans only]], and a soldier on the platform eating something out of a paper with his gloves off. At the corner of the square a boy of eleven was selling cigarettes out of a wooden box. The lilacs had been cut back here by a gardener who had gone away, and they were coming out anyway, out of the ruins of the hedge, and they were grey.

Zosia stopped.

"You told me he was dead."

Marianna looked at the pavement. "I told you that a man in a photograph died."

"You told me his name was Jan. You told me he was from Kraków and he played the violin and he died of typhus in 1925. You told me the whole thing. You told me twice."

"He did play the violin. Badly. That was the joke between us, that he tuned pianos and thought he could play." She heard her own voice doing it, doing it well, and stopped.

Zosia said, "You are lying to me right now."

"No. Only about the violin."

"That's the same thing."

They walked. A woman passed them with a sack of something over her shoulder and did not look at either of them, because in that city in that year you did not look at anybody, and Marianna felt the not-looking land on her the way it landed on everyone, and understood that she had joined a category of person on the pavement.

"He has the same thumb," Zosia said. "The right one. The nail is flat and it has a ridge in it. I have looked at that photograph about four hundred times. It now has a bend in the corner where I looked at it." She was walking fast, two steps to her mother's one, and her voice was not steady, and she hated it, the way she hated anything that gave her away. "Why did you tell me he was dead?"

Because you were seven, Marianna thought, and you asked who the man in the tin was, and I had a pot on the stove and no answer that fit in a kitchen.

"Because it was easier," she said.

"That's not a reason."

"It is the only reason anyone does anything," Marianna said, and heard how that sounded, and did not take it back either.

They came out onto the wide street and turned north, toward the smoke, because that was the way home. A German lorry went past with men standing in it, and the men were not looking at anything either.

"Next time," Zosia said, "tell me first."

"There isn't going to be a next time."

"You said yes. You said it twice. Tuesday, and Friday." The girl's chin came up. "I was standing behind the door and I heard the whole thing, and I am telling you that if there is a next time you tell me before we go in, so I know what to do with my face. Because I did not know what to do with my face. I had to stand there for an hour and decide what to do with my face, and I think I did it wrong."

Marianna stopped in the middle of the pavement.

She had spent fourteen years teaching this child to be careful, to be quiet, to be invisible and useful, to count to a thousand on a step. She had made a girl who could stand in a room with a Wehrmacht major for an hour and worry about her face. There was nothing in that moment she could be proud of and nothing she could be sorry for. She wanted to put her arms round her and she knew that the arms would be for herself.

"All right," she said. "Next time I tell you first."

"Good," Zosia said. And then, after half a street: "Would he have given it to us? The paper?"

"No."

"Not even to you?"

"Especially not to me," Marianna said. "If you had not been standing there, he would have had to decide what I was to him. With you in the room he knew. I was the mother of a child in a school coat. It was the cheapest thing he ever bought."

Zosia thought about that for a while. She had a way of thinking with her whole body, her shoulders coming forward, her eyes going down and to the left.

"Then it's good I was there," she said.

They walked the rest of the way without talking. The ash was on the window ledges and in the gutters and in the lines of the tram rails, and it was in the folds of the coat, and when they got to the door on Hoża Marianna beat the coat against the wall of the entry with the flat of her hand before she would let either of them go up four flights in it.

* * *

Tadeusz was on nights that week. He set type for a newspaper that came out in Polish and lied in Polish, and he had done it since 1941 because the alternative was worse for all three of them, and in 1931 he had asked her once who the man in the photograph was, and she had told him, and he had never asked again, and he came home at six with his hands black to the wrist and washed them for a long time at the sink with the door shut.

Marianna lit the stove. Zosia ate bread and did her German verbs at the table, out of the book with the broken spine, and did not look up, and at a quarter past eight she put her head down on her arms on the table and was asleep, all at once.

Marianna carried her to the bed. She was too big to carry and Marianna carried her anyway, badly, bumping the doorframe, and put her down on the bed with her shoes off and her skirt on, and covered her with the coat.

Then she took the tin down from the top of the wardrobe, where it had been since 1939, and opened it on the kitchen table under the light.

There were three things in it. A theatre programme from 1923 with a corner torn off. A key to a door in a building that had burned. And a photograph of a young man in his shirtsleeves standing at the end of a piano with his hands on the lid and his eyes half shut against the sun, with a walnut metronome on top of the piano beside his elbow.

On the back, in her own hand, in pencil: VIII. 1923.

She had been twenty-two. He had come to Warsaw in June with a letter from his father's firm to tune the Conservatory's instruments, and he had stayed until February, because the money had become worthless faster than he could buy a ticket home with it, and in the autumn she had been glad of the inflation and ashamed of being glad. He had a room on Wilcza. He played the violin badly, and he had a cold in October that lasted three weeks, and he called her *Marianna* in the Polish way and never once in the German one, and when her aunt's piano developed a sticking damper in the fourth octave he had cut a sliver of felt from the band of his own hat with his penknife and fixed it, and she had held the lamp.

He had written from Breslau for eleven months. She had written back three times and then four, and the four came back, and after that she had decided something about herself, and had been living inside that decision ever since, and had made a daughter inside it, and taught her a German lullaby inside it, because it was the only song she could sing in tune.

She took the Ausweis out of her coat and read it twice in the bad light, and folded it small, and then she got the needle and the grey thread and turned Zosia's coat inside out.

She was good at small stitches. Her aunt had taught her, and her aunt had taught her the piano, and it was the same hands for both, and the stitches went in along the seam at the back of the collar where nobody would ever look for anything.

When she had finished she sat for a while with the coat in her lap, and then she put the photograph back in the tin, and put the tin back on the wardrobe, and did not burn it.

From the other room, through the wall, in her sleep, her daughter was humming.

It was the lullaby. It was the one Marianna had sung to her before she could talk, and then in the bad winters, and then for no reason at all. Guten Abend, gut' Nacht. She had never told her where it came from. She had told her, once, that it came from a violinist, and had not said which one. {{Good evening, good night.}}

The humming went on, four notes and then the same four notes, and outside the blackout paper the sky over the north-west was orange behind the roofs, and the ash was still coming down, and the lilacs in the courtyard below could not be seen and were, she knew, grey.

She did not tell her to stop.

On Tuesday she would go back, and sit on her aunt's stool, and finish the tuning, and the F in the fourth octave would stick again, and she would take a hairpin out of her hair and lift the wire with it, the way he had done it once with a sliver of hatband felt and a lamp held steady by her hand, and go on.
