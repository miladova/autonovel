The wagon from Ostia took two days, because the oxen were old and the driver stopped at every inn, and she sat in the back with a sack of beans and a boy of about twelve who was being taken to Rome to be a cupbearer and who had been told that it was a great honor.

He talked the whole way. His name was Felix, which was a name he had been given the week before by a man with a burn on his hand, and he had been washed and oiled and told to be cheerful, and he was cheerful, and he was going to be a cupbearer in the house of a very great man, and if he was good he would be freed at eighteen and given a trade.

Dessa listened to him for two days.

On the second night, at the inn, she said, "Do not go to the house."

The boy looked at her. "Why not?"

"Because a boy who is taken to Rome to be a cupbearer is not taken for his hands." She looked at the fire. "If they free you at eighteen, it will be because they have finished with you, and you will be eighteen and you will have nothing, and the trade will be whatever they give you, and it will not be a trade you chose."

The boy thought about this for a while.

"You are a slave," he said.

"Yes."

"Then why are you telling me what to do?"

"Because I can count," Dessa said, "and I have counted, and it comes out badly, and nobody in six years has told me the arithmetic before it was too late."

The boy looked at the fire and did not answer, and in the morning he was cheerful again, and he talked the whole way into Rome about the house of the very great man.

"Have you been to Rome?" he said, somewhere past the ninth milestone.

"Yes."

"What is it like?"

Dessa thought about it. She had been in three houses in Rome and a cell in Ostia and a yard behind a fuller's shop, and she had carried water up three flights of stairs and stood in a garden at night with her shoes off.

"There is a door," she said, "at the top of a hill, and it is covered in gold, and behind it there is a garden, and behind the garden there is a room with a strongbox in it, and in the strongbox there is a book, and in the book there are four hundred names. If you are brought to a house in Rome, look for the book. Every house that matters has one. The ones that do not keep a book are the ones where they do not intend to remember anything they promise you."

The boy looked at her as though she had told him a story about a dragon.

She never saw him again after the gate.

* * *

They came for her at the second hour of the afternoon, and the man who had been sitting with her was asleep, and she had to say his name twice before he woke.

She had been in the cell four days. She had counted them by the light at the top of the wall, which came in through a grate and moved across the floor and went out, and by the bread, which came twice a day and was the same bread. She had counted four days and she had been wrong by one, and she knew that she had been wrong by one because the man who brought the bread said, on the morning of the questioning, that it was the day before the Nones, and she had thought that it was the third.

The cell had a stone bench and a drain and a bucket and a nail in the wall, and the nail was at the height of her shoulder when she sat on the bench, and she had spent a great deal of time looking at it.

She was not frightened. That was the thing she could not explain afterward, to the physician, or to the woman on the Clivus, or to herself. She had been frightened in the wagon, and frightened in the yard at Ostia, and frightened for six years in three houses. And on the fourth day, in the cell, with the light coming through the grate, she had discovered that she had come to the end of the fear, and that on the other side of it there was a kind of quiet.

She had done the arithmetic. That was what she had been doing in the cell, all four days, with the nail and the light and the bread. She had added up the things that were hers.

The mark on her wrist. The knife with the wooden handle, which they had taken from her in the yard and which was, she supposed, in a box somewhere in the prefect's office with a label on it. Eleven sesterces, which they had taken as well. A name. And the fact — the plain, arithmetic fact — that she could count, and read, and keep a column, and that there was no man in Rome who could take that out of her head with a rope, because a rope works on a body and the counting was somewhere else.

Four things. She had held on to them the way a person holds on to a wall in the dark.

* * *

The room was on the ground floor and it had a high window and a board and four men.

She had expected a dungeon. It was not a dungeon. It was a room with a mosaic floor, a bad mosaic, of a fish, and a table with a jug of water on it, and a clerk's stool in the corner. There was a brazier in the corner and it was not lit. The walls were whitewashed and there was a hook in the ceiling with a rope on it, and the rope was coiled and hung neatly, the way a man hangs a rope he uses every week.

The clerk was old and had ink on his fingers and he had set out his tablets on the stool in front of him the way a man sets out his tools, and he did not look at her at all.

The man in charge was about fifty and had the hands of a butcher and the voice of a man selling vegetables. "You are the slave Dessa," he said. "Of the household of Antonia Cassia, freedwoman, of the fourteenth region."

"I am."

"You are claimed under a bill of sale by the procurator of the estate of Cominius Asiaticus, deceased, and you are held under an order of the prefect's office, and you are to be questioned in the matter of a death in the fourteenth region on the last night of the Floralia."

"I know."

"You will answer what you are asked, and when you have answered, you will be asked again." He sat down on a stool. "That is how it works. It is not personal. I have done this for thirty years and I do not enjoy it, and the man who says he enjoys it is a liar, and the man who says it does not hurt the person is a liar too. What I can tell you is that it is shorter if you answer the first time. Do you understand?"

"Yes."

"Then we will begin."

* * *

It began with questions, and the questions were not the ones she had expected.

She had expected questions about the garden, and the man in the lane, and the ring. What she got first was her life.

Her name. Her age. Where she was born, and when she was taken, and by whom, and sold to whom, and for how much, and what the men looked like, and whether she had ever been in the house of Cominius Asiaticus, and whether she had known a woman there called Prima, and whether she had ever heard the name Chryseros.

She answered it all. She answered it truthfully and in order, because she had spent six years learning how to answer a man who asks questions, and because the truth was the one thing she was good at, and no one had ever paid her for it.

At the end of two hours the man in charge put down his stylus and rubbed his eyes.

"You are a very good witness," he said.

"I can count."

"So I am told. The office has a note on you: a girl who can count, worth twelve thousand. There is a clerk in the grain office who would give a year's pay for you and would treat you well." He looked at her. "That is not a threat. That is information. I have been in this office thirty years and I have found that a person answers better when they know what the alternative is."

"What is the alternative?"

"The alternative is the rope," he said, "and the alternative to the rope is a statement that you were in the kitchen all night and saw nothing, which is what you have already said, and which the office does not believe, because a girl who was in the kitchen all night does not have water on her hands and mud on her feet at the sixth hour of the morning."

She had not thought of that. She had washed her hands in the cellar, and Rufina had washed them again, and nobody had thought about her feet.

"You are a clever girl," the man said. "Clever girls make the worst witnesses, because they think they can keep the story straight. The stupid ones cry and say everything in the first hour. The clever ones hold on, and the holding on is what takes the time." He stood up. "I am going to ask you again. Were you in the garden on the night of the Floralia?"

"Yes," Dessa said.

The clerk's stylus began to move.

* * *

They put the cords on her wrists first, and then on her ankles, and then they ran the rope through the hook in the ceiling, and the man in charge said, "Lift," and two men pulled on the rope, and her heels came off the floor.

It was not the pain that she remembered afterward. It was the surprise. She had been lifted by the wrists a thousand times as a child; the surprise was that a body could be lifted by the wrists at all, that the shoulders would take it, that a person could hang there by two hands with their feet off the ground and still be alive.

The first lift lasted as long as it takes to say a prayer.

"Were you in the garden?"

"Yes."

"Did you see the man in the lane?"

"Yes."

"What did he look like?"

"Tall. Dark cloak. A gold ring on his hand with a leaf on it."

"Did you see his face?"

"No."

"Did you see anything else?"

"No."

They put her down. The man in charge wrote nothing; the clerk wrote everything. She stood on the floor with her arms hanging and her shoulders burning, and the man in charge said, "Again," and they lifted her again.

* * *

It went on for four days, in the way that the woman in the stone room in the year 93 had said it went on: not all at once, and with water in the intervals, and with the clerk sharpening his pen and the man in charge sitting down and reading what had been written and saying, "Again."

She learned the intervals. The first day was the shoulders. The second day they left the cords on longer and the wrists began to swell, and the man in charge said, "Look at that," with a kind of professional interest, and put her down and told the clerk to write that the swelling had begun at the wrist, as though it were a fact about the weather. The third day the pain went into her elbows and her hands and she could not hold the cup they gave her and the water went down her chin, and the man in charge said, "That is the sign," and wrote it down.

The fourth day was the worst, and the fourth day was the one she did not remember clearly, and afterward she would be glad of that.

What she remembered was the room. The fish in the mosaic. The jug of water on the table. The whitewash on the wall, which had a mark on it, a brown mark near the hook, and she had spent a great deal of time looking at the mark. The clerk's hands. The sound of the rope on the hook, which was a small creaking, like a door in a wind.

And the questions. Always the same questions, in the same order, and the truth going out of her answers one word at a time.

* * *

On the second day, in an interval, the man in charge sat down on the stool beside her and gave her water and said, "Do you know what I am doing?"

"Questioning me."

"I am not questioning you. I am writing a document. There was a death in a house in the Subura and the aedile's office has written it as an accident and somebody wants it written differently, and my job is to produce a document in which a slave says, in her own words, what happened. The document will be read by a magistrate and it will be filed and it will be the end of it." He looked at her. "The document will say what I am told to put in it. It does not matter what you say. That is the thing nobody tells the person on the board. Your answers are not evidence; they are material. I am a clerk with a rope, and the man in the office already knows what the document will say, and the only question is how long it takes to make it true."

"Then why do you ask?"

"Because it has to be true in the document," he said. "That is the whole of the law of Rome. A thing is not true until a clerk has written it in the form. If I write that you said it, and you sign it, and I sign it, then it is true, and no magistrate can say otherwise, and the man in the office is satisfied. If I write it without your mark, it is a forgery, and a forgery is worth nothing, and I would be dismissed." He gave her more water. "So I ask, and you answer, and after a while you will sign, and then it will be over."

"And if I do not sign?"

"Then it is not over." The man in charge looked at the jug of water. "There is another way, and I am going to tell you what it is, because I am fifty years old and I have four daughters and I have discovered that I am not able to do this to a girl of twenty without telling her what the door is. The other way is this. You sign the document, and the document is what the office wants, and the office is satisfied, and I write in the margin that the subject was cooperative and that the injuries are consistent with the questioning and not in excess of it. And then the office will sell you, because a slave who has been questioned is always sold, and the price will be in the record, and a man who buys a girl with a document like that will not treat her well, because a girl with a document like that is a girl with no one."

"And the first way?"

"The first way is that you do not sign, and it goes on for another four days, and then you sign." He got up. "That is the whole of the choice, and it is not much of a choice, and I am sorry for it, and I am going to do it anyway, because if I do not do it there is a man in the office who will do it in a worse way."

She looked at the mark on the wall.

"What do you want me to say?" she said.

He told her.

She listened. It took a quarter of an hour, and he said it in the flat voice of a man reading a list of goods, and when he had finished she asked him to say it again, and he said it again, and then she asked him about the part where she was to say that her mistress had paid the aedile's office, and he said that the amount was two hundred thousand sesterces and that it was to be said in her own words.

"Two hundred thousand," she said.

"That is the figure."

"It is a hundred thousand and the aedile's clerk asked for fifty and she gave two hundred, and the difference is the reason she gave it." Dessa looked at him. "If I am to sign a document, I will sign a true one. She paid two hundred, and she paid it to be remembered, and she did it in front of me in the station house of the seventh cohort, and if you put a hundred thousand in the document then it is your lie and not mine."

The man in charge looked at her for a long moment.

"Two hundred thousand," he said to the clerk, and the clerk wrote it, and Dessa sat on the stool with her arms hanging and thought: that is the only thing I have done today that was mine.

* * *

She signed it on the fourth day with her left hand, because her right hand would not close.

The document said that she had been in the garden on the night of the Floralia and had found the body of Decimus Iunius Gallus; that she had seen a man in the lane with a gold ring, and that she could not identify him; that she had been ordered by her mistress, Antonia Cassia, to say that she had been in the kitchen; and that her mistress had told her that a slave who spoke of the death would be sold, and that her mistress had paid money to the aedile's office and had said in her hearing that the death would be written as an accident.

She signed it, and the clerk signed it, and the man in charge signed it, and they put her back in the cell.

She lay on the bench with her arm strapped to a board and looked at the nail in the wall and did the arithmetic, and the arithmetic came out badly.

She had said it. She had said the thing they wanted, not because she was weak, or not only because she was weak, but because on the fourth day, in the interval, the man in charge had said a thing that had gone into her like a nail: *the document will say what I am told to put in it.* And she had understood, lying on the board with her arms out of their sockets, that the questions were not a door she could refuse to open; they were a machine that produced a document, and the only thing she could withhold was her mark, and the withholding of her mark would cost her the hand and would not stop the document.

So she had signed. And she had thought, at the time, that she was being practical.

She was not practical. She had given them the one thing they needed, which was her mark, and she had given it because she was in pain and because she was twenty years old and because a person whose arms have been out of their sockets for four days will sign anything that will make it stop.

That was the truth. She lay in the cell with it and did not look away from it, because she had learned in six years that the truth was the only thing that stayed.

* * *

She was awake when the woman came.

The cell was dark. There was a lamp in the passage and the door was open, and the physician was standing in the doorway with his hands folded and his face averted with the elaborate discretion of a man who has decided not to see anything.

"Domina," Dessa said.

The woman from the Clivus came in and sat down on the stool.

She was in a plain mantle and no jewelry, and she was grey with tiredness, and her hands were shaking, and she sat on the stool and looked at the splint on Dessa's arm for a long time without speaking.

"I am here," she said at last.

"You did not come."

"I did not know where you were until August, and by then you were in the cells and no one would tell me which cells, and I have been to the prefect's office four times and to the station twice and to the house of a man in the Via Nova who would not open the door."

"The man with the burn."

"Felix. Yes."

Dessa looked at the ceiling.

"He told me you had sold me," she said. "In the cell in Ostia. He told me there was a bill of sale with a seal on it, and that you had sold me to a procurator for four thousand, and that I had been put on a cart to Ostia thinking I was being hidden."

The woman was very still.

"And did you believe him?"

"For three days," Dessa said. "And then I remembered that you had told me the truth about the arithmetic, and that a woman who tells the truth about the arithmetic does not sell a girl in the night. And when he came back I told him no. I told him no four times." She turned her head. "And then they brought me to Rome."

"What did they ask you?"

"About the garden. About the man in the lane. About the ring." Dessa's voice was flat. "And then about you. And I told them you were a hard woman and a liar and that you had sold me, because I was on the floor and my arms were out of their sockets, and I have thought about it every day since, and I am telling you now, before you go into that senate house, that they already have a document with my mark on it that says you are worse than they are."

The lamp burned.

"Good," the woman said.

Dessa stared at her.

"Good?"

"Good. Because in an hour I am going to stand up in the senate house and say that a slave of my household was taken from Ostia with a forged bill of sale and a genuine seal and questioned for four days without a magistrate's order, and that she has a hand that will not close for the rest of her life. And the men who want to know why I am saying it will produce your document, and I will say: she signed it on the fourth day, with her hand strapped to a board, and a document taken that way is not a statement. It is a thing that was taken from her, the way the hand was taken." The woman leaned forward. "You have given me the best thing in the case, and you did it by telling me the truth about what you said. That is what I have been trying to tell you since May. The truth is not the thing that is convenient. It is the thing that is left when everything else has been taken."

Dessa looked at her.

"I want to say my name," she said.

"In the senate?"

"In the senate. Like the girl in the basilica. I heard about it in the cell; the man who sits with me told me. He said a slave girl stood up in the court and gave her name and the whole hall went quiet." Her chin came up. "I want to do that. If I stand up in that room and say my name, they cannot say afterward that I was a thing in a cell. They will have to write it down."

"Then I will bring you," the woman said, "and I will bring your splint, and I will bring the physician, and I will stand beside you while you say it."

"They will not let a slave into the senate house."

"They will let a slave into the senate house if a consular of Rome says that the slave is a witness in a matter before the court, and if the clerk writes it in the book, and if the empress has spoken to the consul." The woman smiled, and it was a terrible smile, and Dessa understood, looking at it, that this was what the woman had been building for eight months, and that Dessa was not a piece of material in it; she was one of the stones.

* * *

In the cell, on the last night, the man who sat with her was called Lupus, which was a common name, and he had been in the office for eleven years and had a wife and a bad leg.

He sat on a stool in the passage with the door open and talked, because he had learned that a person in a cell who is talked to does not scream, and because he was a lonely man and a captive audience was better than no audience at all.

He talked about the races. He talked about his wife's sister, who had married a man in the corn office and never let anyone forget it. He talked about the emperor, whom he had seen twice, and about the empress, whom he had seen once, and who had stopped in the street to look at a child's toy in a shop window, and he had thought at the time that it was a very great woman who could look at a toy in a window with a whole street watching.

And once, at about the second hour of the morning, he said, "My name is Lupus, and I have a wife called Marcia, and two boys, and I am telling you that so that you will know that the man sitting in the passage is a man with a name, and that what is happening to you is being done by men with names, and that one day you may be in a room with one of us and you will remember his name."

"Lupus," Dessa said.

"Lupus," he said. "It is a common name. There are a great many of us."

They came for her at dawn.

The physician was there, and a chair with a leather roof, and the woman from the Clivus was standing in the street with her hair up and a dark green dress and a face like a wall.

"You will not be able to walk up the steps," the physician said. "There are eleven steps and you will be carried. The men will carry you. You will not look at the crowd."

"I am not afraid of the crowd."

"You will be. It is a different crowd from the ones you know. It is not a market crowd and it is not a street crowd. It is three hundred men who have never in their lives been looked at by a slave, and they will look at you the way a man looks at a dog that has come into a room." The physician took her left arm. "Say your name and nothing else. Not one word more. They will ask you questions and the woman will answer them. Do you understand?"

"I understand."

They put her in the chair, and the bearers took it up, and the street went by, and the whole city was awake and going about its business, and nobody looked at a chair with a leather roof and a slave in it.

At the door of the senate house there was a crowd. There were the lictors and the clerks and the clients and the men who came to the door of the senate house every morning to see who went in, and the women behind the columns, and the boys on the steps.

The chair stopped. The bearers set it down. The physician opened the curtain, and the woman from the Clivus was standing there with her hand out.

"Cassia," she said.

Dessa looked at her.

"Cassia," she said.

"Not to me," the woman said. "In there. When they ask you. Say it in a voice that the whole room can hear, and do not look at any man in the eye, and if your hand hurts, say so, and if you are going to faint, sit down on the floor before you do it. There is no shame in sitting on the floor of the senate house. I have been told that several consuls have done it."

Dessa got out of the chair, and the physician took her elbow, and they went up the eleven steps.

At the top, at the door, a clerk with a tablet looked at her and then at the woman and then at the consular who was standing in the doorway with a cold, and the clerk said, "A slave cannot be brought into the house."

"She is a witness in a matter before the praetor," the consular said, "and the praetor has asked for her, and the consul has been informed, and the empress has been informed, and if you write in your book that the door was shut against a witness, you will be asked to explain it before the consul at the second hour."

The clerk looked at the tablet.

"Name?" he said.

"Cassia," Dessa said.

The clerk wrote it down, and stood aside, and they went into the senate house of Rome.
