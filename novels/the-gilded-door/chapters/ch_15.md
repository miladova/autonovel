The house of Neratius Priscus stood on the Quirinal in a street so quiet that the loudest thing in it was the fountain in the courtyard, and the fountain had been turned off because the old man said the noise made him lose his thread.

He was seventy and he had been consul twice and he had refused a third consulship and a province and, it was said, a marriage into the imperial family, on the grounds that he was happier with his books. He had written nine volumes on the law and was at work on a tenth, and he had a face like a tortoise and the manners of a man who has decided, at some point in his sixties, that he no longer had to be polite to anybody.

"Sit down," he said. "Not there. There. That chair has the light behind it and I want to see your face."

Antonia sat in the chair.

"You want a causa liberalis," he said.

"Yes."

"For a girl of eighteen in the house of Cassius Verus."

"Yes."

"And you are the mother."

"Yes."

"Then you are either a very brave woman or a very foolish one, and I have been in this profession for fifty years and I have never been able to tell the difference until the verdict." He poured wine with a steady hand, and did not offer her any. "Tell me what you have."

She told him. She told him about the manumission and the birth, the token, Bassa's page, the note in Verus's hand, the midwife at Ficulea, and the register that said Dessa was in Africa and Myrtale did not exist.

The old man listened with his eyes closed. When she had finished he did not open them.

"The law," he said, "is simple, and it is not simple. The simple part is this: a child born to a free mother is free. It does not matter that the mother was a slave when the child was conceived. It does not matter that the mother had been freed for one day. It does not matter that the father is a senator and the mother a freedwoman and the child was sold at three months like a lamb. If the mother was free at the birth, the child is freeborn, and any man who holds her is holding a free person, and that is not a private matter between him and his household; it is a public crime under the lex Fabia." He opened his eyes. "That is the simple part. Do you want the rest?"

"Yes."

"The rest is that the law does not come and find you. The law sits in a room and waits for someone to bring it a case. And to bring it, you need a man."

"The adsertor."

"The adsertor libertatis. A free man, of good standing, who stands up in court and says: this person is free, and I claim it, and I will answer for the claim. He is not her advocate. He is not her friend. He is a man who stakes himself. If the court finds against him, he pays a penalty to the master, and it is not a small penalty, and worse than the penalty is the fact that he has been publicly wrong about a question of status, which in this city is worse than being wrong about money."

"How much is the penalty?"

"The old law says fifty asses. The praetor's edict says the value of the person claimed, which in this case would be twelve thousand sesterces, and the value of the trouble, which would be whatever the praetor decides it is worth." Priscus turned his cup. "And there is a second risk, which nobody will tell you and which I will, because I am old and I have no career left to lose. The adsertor must be a man whose own status is beyond question. A freedman cannot do it. A man with a stain cannot do it. And a man who does it for a woman he is known to be connected with will be asked, in open court, why he is doing it, and the answer will be that he is doing it for her, and then the case is not about the girl anymore. It is about you."

"I have understood that."

"Have you? Let me be plainer." The old man set his cup down. "You are a freedwoman. You have been free for nineteen years and you have a fortune and a house and a name that the city uses, and none of it matters, because the one question a Roman court asks about a freedwoman is not whether she is rich. It is whether she is respectable. And respectability in a woman is not a thing she owns. It is a thing that other people lend her, and they can call in the loan at any time, for any reason, and once it is called in, it is not a matter of money. It is a matter of the street, and the street does not forget."

"You are telling me not to do it."

"I am telling you what it will cost. Whether it is worth the cost is not a question for a jurist." Priscus smiled, and his smile was surprisingly kind. "I will tell you what I would do, if I were you, and then I will tell you what you will do, and you may compare them."

"Very well."

"If I were you, I would do nothing. I would send the girl money, if I could send it without being seen. I would wait until Verus is in Asia, and then I would buy her from his steward, quietly, through three men, for fifteen thousand, and free her in my own house with two witnesses and no court. It would take two years and it would cost a little more and it would work, and nobody would ever know, and the girl would have her freedom and you would have your daughter and the world would be exactly as unjust as it was the day before."

"That is what you would do."

"That is what I would do." Priscus looked at her. "And you will not do it, because you are not a jurist, and because the girl has a token around her neck and a man has been looking at her across a tablinum fifty-one times, and because a thing that is done quietly is a thing that was never done at all."

Antonia looked at him.

"Will you be my adsertor?" she said.

The old man laughed. It was a dry, delighted, entirely unkind laugh.

"No," he said. "I am seventy years old and I have a book to finish and I have not stood in a court in eleven years. But I will tell you who will. There is a man called Lucius Iunius Gallus, who is a consular, and who has no debts, and no stains, and no fear of Verus, and who is in mourning for a son and has recently discovered that grief makes him reckless." He picked up his cup again. "I have known him since he was a boy. He is a good man with a shallow river in him, and he will say yes before you have finished the sentence, and you will spend the rest of the case wishing he had said no."

* * *

Gallus said yes before she had finished the sentence.

They were in the garden of the house on the Caelian, in the heat of the afternoon, with the bees in the roses and the aqueduct going over the wall like a row of arches built by giants. He had put on weight since the funeral, and his eyes had a flat look, and he drank more than he had.

"Say it again," he said.

"I need a free man of good standing to assert the freedom of a girl in the house of Cassius Verus. The case will be heard in November, before the praetor, with the decemvirs. If we win, she is free and the record is corrected. If we lose, you pay the value of the girl and you are wrong in public."

"I will do it."

"Lucius."

"I will do it," Gallus said, "and I will tell you why, and you will not like it." He turned and walked to the end of the garden and came back. "Because it is the house of Cassius Verus. Because a boy died in your garden and his son was there and his father came to my son's funeral in the correct clothes and said the correct words. Because a man came to me in June and told me that my son was killed by a push in a garden and that the man who did it was the son of a consular, and that the whole city knew it and nobody would say it."

Antonia stood very still.

"Who came to you?"

"Oppius Nepos."

"Nepos is a liar."

"Nepos is a liar and Nepos is a man who sells information, and a man who sells information sometimes sells true information, because a merchant who only sells false goods does not stay in business." Gallus stopped in front of her. "I have thought about it for two months. I have gone over it every night. I have written it out. And I have come to this: I do not know whether Manius Verus's son killed my son. But I know that the man who held the lamp in a room in the year 93 is the same man, and I know that a man who can stand in a passage for eleven hours and say nothing can stand in a garden and say nothing, and I have decided that I will spend the rest of my life making his life as difficult as it can be made, in every lawful way, in every court in Rome."

"That is not justice."

"No," Gallus said. "It is grief, wearing justice's coat. I am aware of the difference. I am a consular; I have been aware of the difference all my life." He took her hand. "I will be your adsertor. I will stand up in the praetor's court and say that a girl is free because her mother was free when she was born, and I will do it because her father is the man I intend to ruin. And I will do it honestly, because the law is the law and the girl is free, and I have read the papers."

Antonia looked at his hand on hers.

"There is a thing I have not told you," she said.

"There always is."

"The night of the Floralia. I have not told you all of it."

Gallus's face did not change.

"Then tell me now," he said, "before I stand up in a court and swear an oath on your behalf."

"Your son was in my garden at the sixth hour. He was not alone." Antonia's voice was steady. "There was a man in the lane with a ring on his hand. My slave saw him. He was tall, and he stood with his hands behind his back, and he went away down the hill when she screamed. And in the morning I found that Verianus had left the house at the fourth hour and had not come back, and that his father had given him a ring in May."

Gallus let go of her hand.

"You are telling me," he said slowly, "that the son of Manius Verus was in the lane outside your house on the night my son died."

"I am telling you that a tall man with a gold ring was in the lane. I do not know that it was Verianus. I know that Verianus had the ring, because his father told me, in his own garden, in May."

"His father told you."

"Yes."

"Why would he tell you that?"

Antonia looked at him.

"Because I asked him where his ring was," she said, "and because he is a man who has spent eighteen years discovering that the truth is easier to say to a woman who already knows it."

* * *

Acilia Marcella came to the house on the Clivus on the Ides of August, at the ninth hour, in a plain litter with one slave and no escort, and stood in the atrium looking at the mosaic with an expression Antonia had never seen on her before.

"It is a door within a door," she said.

"It is."

"Who chose it?"

"Nobody chose it. The mosaic was here when I bought the house, under three layers of dirt and a bad repair. I had it cleaned because a customer had slipped on the repair and threatened to sue me." Antonia came and stood beside her. "My man took off the top layer with a chisel, and there it was. I have never changed it. I have thought about changing it a hundred times and I have never done it."

"Why not?"

"Because a door within a door is a very good description of my life," Antonia said, "and because the man who laid it was probably a freedman himself, and it is the only thing in this house that I did not buy."

Marcella looked at the mosaic for a moment longer.

"I have brought you something," she said, and took from her sleeve a small purse and put it on the table. "There is twenty thousand sesterces in that. It is my own money; it is not my husband's, and it is not from the dowry. I have been saving it for eleven years against the day my husband's house fell down, and I find that I would rather spend it on this than on a house in Puteoli."

Antonia looked at the purse.

"Why?"

"Because a case costs money. There is the adsertor's fee, and the praetor's clerk, and the copies, and the witnesses' time, and the man at Ficulea who will want his roof repaired before he will come to Rome and stand up in a court. I have been the wife of a great house for nine years. I know what a case costs." Marcella pushed the purse across the table. "Take it. Do not thank me. If you thank me I shall cry, and I have not cried in nine years and I do not intend to begin in your atrium."

Antonia took the purse.

"There is a condition," Marcella said.

"There always is."

"When it is over, and the girl is free, I want her out of that house within a week. Not because I do not want her there. Because a freedwoman in the house of the man who kept her is a thing that the whole city will talk about for a year, and she will hear every word of it, and she will be a scandal before she is a person." Marcella's voice was steady. "She will need a place. Not this house. A respectable house, with a respectable woman in it, where she can learn a trade and be somebody's freedwoman and not a story."

"Kallisto wants a house on the Aventine."

"Kallisto is a singer with a temper and a patron in Puteoli."

"Kallisto is a singer with a temper, a patron in Puteoli, and four hundred thousand sesterces that I am going to give her in the spring, and she will need a respectable woman in her house to keep her respectable, and she will hate it, and it will work." Antonia put the purse in her own sleeve. "I will arrange it."

Marcella nodded. Then she said, "The wedding is on the fourth day before the Kalends of September."

"I know."

"You will come."

"I said I would."

"My husband has told the household that you are not to be spoken to. He has told my stepdaughter that you are a woman of no consequence. He has told his son that if you are seen at the wedding, there will be trouble." Marcella's mouth was thin. "He is afraid of you, and a frightened man makes rules, and the rules are the only power he has left. Do not break them. Come in at the side gate with the women, sit where I put you, and do not speak to him, and if he speaks to you, answer him in one sentence and then look away."

"That is not my way."

"I know it is not your way. It is my house, and it is my stepdaughter's wedding, and I am asking." Marcella looked at her. "Do you know what the slaves are calling you?"

"No."

"The mother of the house." Marcella's voice was strange. "They are calling you that. Not to your face. In the kitchen. The cook started it and the steward has not stopped it, and by September the whole household will be saying it, and my husband does not know."

Antonia stood in her own atrium with the purse of another woman's savings in her sleeve and found that she could not speak.

* * *

The old woman at the gate of the empress's garden was a freedwoman of sixty with a face like a nut and a stool and a stick, and she had been at that gate for nineteen years.

"A quarter of an hour," Antonia said. "That is all. Tell her that the woman who sent the petition in March is standing at the gate."

The old woman looked at her.

"The empress is at Antium," she said. "She will be back in October. There is a place made for petitions to her household at the beginning of November, in the garden, on the third day before the Nones, and it is not a quarter of an hour, it is the time it takes to walk from the gate to the fountain and back, and you must be brought by someone the household knows."

"I do not know anyone the household knows."

The old woman looked at her for a long moment.

"You are the woman from the Clivus," she said. "The one with the gilt door."

"Yes."

"My sister was in a house on the Aventine for six years." The old woman shifted on her stool. "She came out with a bad chest and four hundred sesterces, and she died in the year of the fire, and she used to say that the only person in that whole trade who ever gave her a thing without taking something was a woman with grey eyes who kept the accounts and never once pretended to be her friend."

Antonia did not move.

"Come back on the third day before the Nones," the old woman said. "Come to this gate at the second hour and ask for me. My name is Callirhoe. I will walk you to the fountain myself, and on the way you will say whatever it is into the air, and the empress will hear it if she is disposed to hear it, and if she is not, you will have had a walk in a garden and nobody will have lost anything."

"Thank you."

"Do not thank me. I have been at this gate nineteen years and I have never once let anyone in." Callirhoe settled back on her stool. "It is a small thing to be kind once."

* * *

The tablet came on the fifteenth day before the Kalends, by the hand of the fuller's boy, wrapped in a piece of sacking and addressed to nobody.

It was a wax tablet, and the wax had been smoothed and written on and smoothed again and written on again, so that the earlier writing showed through the new like bones through skin. The new writing was in a girl's careful hand, and it was a copy, and it said:

*In the tablinum of the house of M. Cassius Verus, in the third pigeonhole, a bundle tied with string and marked with the year. On the fourth tablet of the bundle: a note of a debt owed by Chryseros, slave, to his master, in the sum of ninety thousand sesterces, paid on his behalf to Bassa, dealer, for a child of three months. In the margin, in my father's hand: For the child, from Bassa, and no more to be said.*

And underneath, in the same hand: *I could not take the tablet. He has moved the bundle. He has moved me, too — I am to go to my aunt's at Tibur on the Kalends of September and stay until the wedding, and Myrtale is to stay in the house. I do not know why he has separated us. I am frightened. Do not write. P.*

Antonia read it three times, and then sat with it in her lap in the small room with the lamp and the strongbox and the smell of oil.

Ninety thousand sesterces. A debt owed by a slave to his master, paid on the slave's behalf; a fiction that turned a purchase into an accounting, because a Roman gentleman does not write down that he bought a baby. And then eleven words in the margin, in his own hand, in his own tablinum, in the house where he had held a lamp for eleven hours: *and no more to be said.*

She had waited eighteen years for this piece of wax.

She felt nothing at all, and then she felt everything at once, and she put her hand over her mouth and sat very still for a long time.

* * *

The petition was refused on the last day of August.

She had gone up to the office of petitions with the sealed endorsement of a consular in her hand and the fee paid and the witness's statement taken, and she had waited three hours on the bench in the corridor, and at the end of it she was shown into the room of the secretary who had told her in March that the office had no objection.

Haterius had aged in five months. He sat behind his table with the tablets in front of him, and he did not look at her, and that was how she knew.

"The petition is refused," he said.

"On what grounds?"

"The office has received a representation from the urban prefect's office, in the matter of certain irregularities in the manumission records of the fourteenth region, and in the matter of the character of the petitioner." He read from the tablet in front of him, in a flat voice, like a man reading a list of goods. "The petitioner is a woman of the freed class who keeps an establishment of entertainment in the fourteenth region, who has been the subject of a public inquiry by the aediles, and whose household has been named in a matter of concealment of a slave. The office does not say that the petition is false. The office says that the petition cannot be advanced while the matter of the house is unresolved."

Antonia stood in front of the table.

"There is no matter of the house," she said. "There is an inquiry by the aediles which has been closed, and a death which has been written as an accident, and a slave who was registered as sold. There is nothing unresolved."

"The prefect's office has written that there is."

"The prefect's office has been persuaded to write it by a man called Oppius Nepos, who wishes to buy my book."

Haterius's pen stopped moving.

"Domina," he said, and his voice was almost kind, "do not tell me that."

"Why not?"

"Because I am a clerk, and a clerk who is told a thing must write it down, and if I write it down it becomes a document, and a document is a thing that can be used." He put the pen down. "The petition is refused. It may be presented again in a year, with a fresh endorsement and a clean record and a statement from the prefect's office that the matter of the house is resolved. In a year. Not before."

"A year."

"A year." He looked at her, and then away. "The emperor is going east. The office goes with him in part. A petition that is presented again in a year may be presented to a man who is not the emperor, and a man who is not the emperor cannot grant a rescript of this kind." He picked up his pen. "I am sorry, domina. I have been in this office twenty years and I have refused four hundred petitions and I have never once been sorry, and I am sorry now."

She stood in the corridor for a moment with her hand against the wall.

The room behind her had a table and a pen and a man who had been sorry. In front of her the corridor went down to the stair, and the stair went down to the forum, and the forum was full of men going about the business of an empire that had been built on exactly this: on a table, and a pen, and a man who was sorry, and a woman who was told to come back in a year.

"Domina." The clerk who had called her name in March was standing at the door of the room with a tablet in his hand. "The secretary's clerk asked me to give you this."

It was a copy of the refusal. He had written it out himself, in his own hand, in the corridor, in the last quarter of an hour, and at the bottom, where the seal would go, there was nothing at all.

"It is not sealed," Antonia said.

"No, domina."

"An unsealed copy is not a refusal."

"No, domina." The clerk looked at the floor. "An unsealed copy is a piece of paper that a woman might have taken away by mistake, and there is no record of it in the office, and if a praetor's clerk were to come to this office in November and ask whether the petition had been refused, I would have to look in the book, and the book has not been written up yet." He cleared his throat. "The book is written up at the end of the quarter. There are four hundred petitions in it. It takes me three weeks."

Antonia looked at the young man.

"What is your name?" she said.

"Faustus, domina."

"Faustus. Do you know what you are doing?"

"I am giving a woman a piece of paper that has not been sealed," the clerk said, "and I am going back to my table, and I am going to write up the quarter, and if the refusal is in the book by the Kalends of October it will be because somebody in this office wrote it there, and I am not going to be that somebody, and I am not going to be able to explain why." He went back into the room and shut the door.

Antonia stood in the corridor with the unsealed copy in her hand and understood, for the first time in eleven years, that the city was not one thing. It was a hundred thousand people, and every one of them had a reason, and the reasons were not all the same, and a woman who had spent her life assuming that every door was locked because the men behind it were against her had been wrong about at least one clerk in an office on the Palatine.

She went out through the corridor where the woman in black had sat with the boy in March, and there was a different woman there now, with a different boy, and the letter in the boy's hand was addressed to a different dead man.

* * *

Tullia was waiting in the street with the litter.

"Well?" she said.

"Refused."

Tullia's face did not change. "On what grounds?"

"The company I keep." Antonia got into the litter. "Take me to the palace."

"The palace?"

"The house of the empress. Not the palace. The garden of the house of Pompeia Plotina, on the Palatine, at the fifth hour, and send a message ahead of me that the woman who sent the petition in March asks for a quarter of an hour."

Tullia stared at her. "You cannot simply go to the empress's house."

"I am not going to the empress's house. I am going to the garden of the empress's house, which is a different thing, and I am going to stand at the gate until someone comes out, and I am going to say one sentence, and then I am going to go home." Antonia pulled the curtain. "It is the only door in Rome I have never knocked on. I have been saving it."

"Why now?"

"Because my petition is refused for a year and the case is in November and the wedding is in September and a man in the prefect's office has my book, and because the only person in this city who can move an emperor's office with a word is a woman who has never been in an emperor's office."

She went home and put on the dark blue stola and the gold earrings and no other ornament at all, and then she sat in the small room with the two halves of a lead token in her hand and waited for the fifth hour.
