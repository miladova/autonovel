They went on the third night after the wedding, at the second hour, when the moon was down and the whole Subura was asleep except the bakers and the watch and the women who could not sleep.

There were four of them: Antonia, Hilarus, Cerdon, and the fuller's man with the boat, whose name was Vindex and who had a bad leg and a good memory for lanes. They went up the Viminal by the back ways, through the yard of a dyer's and over a wall that Vindex knew, and came into the lane behind the house of the Cassii from the east, where the wall was lower and where the fig tree had grown over it.

The plan was not a good plan. Antonia knew it was not a good plan, and had known it from the moment she had said it out loud, and had gone on with it because a bad plan that moves is worth more than a good plan that waits.

The plan was this: Hilarus and Cerdon would go over the wall into the garden and open the side door. Antonia would go in and up the back stairs to the room next to the steward's, and she would wake the girl, and she would say four sentences, and the girl would come or she would not. If she came, they would go out through the garden and down the lane to the boat and across the river to the fuller's cousin's house in the Transtiberim, and in the morning Antonia would send word to the praetor's office that the girl had been produced by her mother and was under the protection of a consular, and the case would begin in November with the girl alive and out of the house.

Four sentences. She had written them out on a tablet and burned the tablet.

*My name is Antonia Cassia and I was born in this house.*

*You are my daughter and you were born free.*

*There is a case in November and a man called Gallus who will stand for you.*

*Come with me now and you will not go back.*

She had said them to the wall of her own room a hundred times in the last three days. She had heard them in her own voice and in the voice of a stranger. She had thought about what the girl would do, and had made herself consider every answer, including the one she could not bear: that the girl would look at her and say, *you are the woman who keeps the house on the Clivus, and my master has told me what you are.*

The wall was cold and the fig tree was easy and the garden was dark.

They got over. They crossed the garden by the vine, where Antonia had sat in the evenings when she was nineteen, and where the bees had been in the spring and were not now, and where the bench was still the bench.

The side door was bolted from the inside. Hilarus had it open in a quarter of an hour with a knife and a piece of wire, and they went into the passage, and the passage smelled of the same things: stone dust and lamp oil and, faintly, the vinegar they used on the floors.

Antonia stood in the passage behind the kitchen of the house of the Cassii, nineteen years after she had been carried out of it, and listened.

The house was asleep. The kitchen at the end had a banked fire and a smell of bread. The door to her old room was shut, and behind it someone was snoring, and it was not the girl; it was a man's snore, deep and regular, and she stood outside the door of the room she had slept in for fourteen years and listened to a man she did not know snoring in it.

She went up the back stairs.

The room next to the steward's had a door with a crack of light under it.

She stood outside it for a moment, and her heart was going, and her hands were steady, and she thought: I have waited eighteen years for this door, and now I am going to knock on it, and the person on the other side is going to be my daughter, and she is going to look at me the way she looks at a stranger.

She knocked.

The door opened at once, as though someone had been standing behind it, waiting.

Myrtale was in her shift with a lamp in her hand, and her face was the face of a person who has not slept. Behind her, on the bed, there was a bundle tied with cord, and a cloak, and a pair of shoes.

"You are the lady," she said.

"Yes."

"You are the lady with the fan."

"I am." Antonia's voice was steady. "May I come in?"

The girl stepped back. Antonia went in and shut the door, and they stood in the small room with the lamp between them, and Antonia saw, on the table, a wax tablet with writing on it, and a knife with a wooden handle.

"You are leaving," Antonia said.

"Tonight." Myrtale's chin came up. "The cook is going to leave the gate unbarred at the third hour, and there is a man with a cart who takes the vegetables out at the Porta Esquilina at dawn, and he has been paid, and I have eleven sesterces and a cloak and the name of a woman in Tibur who knew my nurse."

"Who paid the man with the cart?"

"The cook." Myrtale looked at her. "She has been in this house thirty years and she has never once done anything that was not ordered, and she is doing this, and she is doing it because the master has told the steward to have the litter ready, and because a girl who is sent away in a litter does not come back."

Antonia stood in the small room and looked at her daughter.

"I have four sentences," she said.

"Domina?"

"I have four sentences that I wrote out and burned, and I have said them to a wall a hundred times, and now I am going to say them to you, and you are going to listen to all four before you say anything at all." She put her hands behind her back, because her hands were not steady anymore. "My name is Antonia Cassia, and I was born in this house. You are my daughter, and you were born free. There is a case in November, before the praetor, and a consular of Rome called Gallus has sworn to assert your freedom and to answer for it. Come with me now, tonight, and you will not go back to this house."

The lamp burned. The girl did not move.

"Say it again," she said.

"Which part?"

"The first part."

"My name is Antonia Cassia, and I was born in this house."

"Where?"

Antonia looked at the floor, and then at the wall, and then at the girl.

"At the end of the passage," she said, "behind the kitchen, on the right. There is a door, and behind the door a room with a pallet and a jug and a nail in the wall. I slept in it for fourteen years. There is a crack in the plaster above the nail in the shape of a leaf, and the third brick from the floor on the left of the door is loose, and I used to keep things behind it." She paused. "There was a lamp with a bronze door on the handle. I stole it when I left. I still have it."

Myrtale's face had gone strange.

"The crack is in the shape of a leaf," she said. "I have looked at it. I have looked at it for thirteen years."

The lamp burned.

"You are my mother," Myrtale said.

"Yes."

"The master said she was dead."

"The master lied."

"The master said she was a slave who died when I was small, and that he had bought me from a dealer because he was a kind man and my mother had been his servant and he had promised her." Myrtale's voice was very quiet. "He has said it twice. Once when I was nine and I asked him, and once in the tablinum in the spring, when he told me to take off the cord."

Antonia's hands were shaking.

"What did you say?"

"I said, yes, master. What else would I say?" Myrtale looked at the lamp. "And then I went out and sat on the step by the store-room door and I put my hands over my mouth, and I did not cry, because a slave who cries in a passage is a slave who has to be asked why."

Antonia took a step forward, and the girl took a step back, and Antonia stopped.

"I am not going to touch you," she said. "Not until you say I may. Do you understand? You may say no to everything I say tonight, and I will go out of this house and I will find another way. But I am going to tell you the rest of it, and it is not a nice story, and you are going to be angry with me, and that is your right."

"Tell me."

So she told her. She told it standing in a small room with a lamp between them, in the house where it had happened, and she did not spare herself, because she had spent eighteen years learning that the truth told badly is a kind of lie.

She told her about the garden and the bench and the poets, and about the man who read to her. She told her about the man with the folded paper and the passage and the promise she had made without understanding it. She told her about the stone room at the back of the tablinum and the clerk and the man with the rope, and about the young master standing by the door with the lamp in his hand. She told her about the eleven hours and the physician and the salve and the arm that would not lift above the shoulder. She told her about the manumission and the record and the old lady's purse. She told her about the room above the fuller's and the birth, and about the midwife from Ficulea and the crescent mark on a baby's wrist, and about the token that had been struck and snapped in two, and about the four days on the floor after the litter had gone up the lane.

She told her about the year and a half of washing, and the debt, and the house on the Aventine, and the two years. She did not spare herself there either, and she watched her daughter's face while she said it, and her daughter's face did not change, and that was worse than anything.

At the end she said, "That is all of it. There is nothing else."

Myrtale stood with her hands at her sides.

"You left me," she said.

"I did not leave you. He took you."

"You gave me to him."

"I let him take you, because I had eleven sesterces a month and a room above a fuller's shop and no work while I was nursing, and he had a house and a name and a promise." Antonia's voice was steady. "I have told myself for eighteen years that I had no choice. That is not true. I had a choice. I had a bad one and a worse one, and I chose the worse one, because I could not bear the thought of watching you grow up in a house where I could not touch you, and I told myself it was for you, and it was not for you. It was for me. I could not do it, so I let him do it, and I have been paying for it ever since."

Myrtale looked at the floor.

"The lady Marcella," she said. "She knows."

"Yes."

"The young mistress knows."

"Yes."

"Everybody knows," Myrtale said, "except the girl in the passage."

"Yes."

The lamp guttered, and Antonia reached out and turned the wick up, and her hand was shaking so badly that she could not do it, and Myrtale took the lamp from her and turned the wick and set it down.

"Come with me," Antonia said. "Not because I am your mother. Because there is a litter ready on the fifth day before the Kalends of October and a destination that is not written down, and because your cook has a cart at the third hour and a man who has been paid, and because a man who has been paid by a cook in a great house is a man who can be paid again by a steward."

Myrtale said, "What is the case?"

"It is a freedom suit. In November, before the praetor, with the decemvirs. A consular asserts that you are free. I testify to the birth. Your father produces you as his slave and gives no title. Bassa's page is produced, and the note in his hand, and the midwife, and the token. If we win, you are freeborn from the day of your birth, and it is written in the record, and no man in Rome can say otherwise."

"And if we lose?"

"Then you are a slave, and your father takes you, and Gallus pays the value." Antonia looked at her. "And I will be ruined in the street, and my house will close, and I will have nothing, and you will still be his."

Myrtale looked at the bundle on the bed.

"And if I get on the cart at dawn," she said, "there is no case, and you are not ruined, and I am in Tibur, and then I am somewhere else, and in a year I am a maid in a house in the country and nobody knows my name."

"Yes."

"Or I am caught," Myrtale said, "and I am sold, and the man in Formiae has his twelve thousand and I never see any of you again."

"Yes."

The girl stood in the small room for a long time. Then she went to the table and picked up the tablet with the writing on it and looked at it, and put it down.

"I do not know how to be a daughter," she said.

Antonia felt something go through her that she had not felt since the room above the fuller's, and she held it, and did not let it out.

"Neither do I know how to be a mother," she said. "I have had eighteen years to practice and I have not done it once. We shall have to be bad at it together."

Myrtale looked at her.

Then she picked up the cloak from the bed, and put it around her shoulders, and picked up the shoes, and the knife, and the tablet, and stood by the door.

"Domina," she said.

"Yes."

"You will have to tell me your name again. Every day, for a while. Because I shall forget it, and it will be a strange thing in my mouth, and I shall call you domina in front of people, and you will have to be patient with me."

Antonia's eyes were stinging.

"Yes," she said.

They went out through the passage, and down the back stairs, and the house was asleep, and the snoring man snored behind the door of the room she had slept in, and the garden was dark, and the vine was still the vine.

They were at the side door when the lamp came out of the tablinum.

"Stop," Verus said.

* * *

He was in his night tunic with a lamp in his hand, and he was barefoot, and he looked older than he had in the garden in May. Behind him, in the doorway of the tablinum, Callistus the steward was standing with a second lamp and a face like a closed door.

"You have come into my house," Verus said.

"I have come for my daughter."

"You have come into my house at night with four men and a boat."

"Yes."

"Do you know what I could do? I could have you taken up for housebreaking. I could have the men flogged and sold. I could have you in the cells by morning and the whole of Rome would say that the woman on the Clivus was a thief." His voice was shaking. "My son is in the house. My wife is in the house. My daughter's wedding was four days ago."

"Then do not wake them," Antonia said. "Do not wake your son, Manius. Do not wake him and ask him to help you with this, because he has been sleeping with a cup in his hand for four months and he is the last person in Rome you want standing in a passage tonight."

The lamp shook in Verus's hand.

"Let her go," he said.

"No."

"She is mine."

"She is not yours. You have known she is not yours since the day you signed the note that said *no more to be said*. You have known it every night you sat in your tablinum and sent for her and looked at her. You have known it every time you told her that her mother was dead." Antonia took a step forward. "There are two ways for this to end, and you are going to choose one of them tonight, in this passage, with a lamp in your hand, exactly as you chose in the year 93."

Verus stood in the passage of his own house.

"Which ways?" he said.

"The first way is that I take her out through that door and down the lane, and in the morning the whole city knows that the daughter of Manius Cassius Verus was carried out of his house at night by her mother, and the case in November is a public scandal, and you are finished in a week."

"And the second?"

"The second is that you open the door yourself," Antonia said, "and you say, in front of your steward, that the girl Myrtale is to go with the lady Antonia Cassia, and that she is to be produced before the praetor in November, and that you will answer the summons. And then you go back into your tablinum and you sit down and you do not sleep, and in the morning you are still a man who did one thing properly."

The lamp burned.

Callistus said, from the doorway, "Domine."

Verus did not turn.

"Go back to bed, Callistus," he said.

"Domine, the steward's duty —"

"I said go back to bed."

The steward went. His lamp went away down the passage and the light of it went out behind the corner, and the passage was dark, and there were three people in it and one lamp.

Verus looked at Myrtale.

"Girl," he said.

"Master."

He stood there for a long moment with the lamp in his hand, and Antonia saw him do it — saw him open his mouth to say the thing, and saw the thing go back down, and saw him put his shoulders back the way he did in the tablinum, and saw the man in the garden in May and the man in the passage in 93 and the man in the senate house all standing in the one body, and none of them able to say it.

Then he turned and went into the tablinum and shut the door.

The lock did not turn. He had not locked it. He stood on the other side of it, and Antonia could see the light under the door, and she could hear him breathing.

"Come," she said.

They went out through the side door, and across the garden, and over the wall by the fig tree, and down the lane, and the boat was at the foot of the steps with Vindex in it, and the river was black and moving, and the whole city was asleep above them.

Myrtale sat in the stern with her bundle on her knees and did not look back at the wall. Antonia sat in the bow with her hand on the gunwale, and the water went past, and neither of them spoke until the boat came out into the middle of the river and the current took it.

* * *

The boat came in at the landing below the Aventine and they went up the steps into the Emporium, where the warehouses stood in the dark and the smell of grain and rope and river water was so strong that it was almost a solid thing, and Hilarus went ahead with the lamp.

At the top of the steps there was a wineshop with its shutters down, and a baker's cart, and a group of men loading amphorae by torchlight, and a woman selling hot chickpeas from a brazier. It was the fourth hour of the morning, and the whole riverfront was awake, and the ordinary business of the largest port in the world was going on exactly as it went on every night, and Antonia stood in the middle of it with her daughter beside her and thought: this is the city, this is what it is, it is this and it is not the senate house.

"Domina." Myrtale had stopped at the corner. "What happens now?"

"Now we go up through the Emporium and across the Aventine, and I put you in a house where nobody will look for you, and in the morning Tullia brings you clothes and a comb and something to eat, and I go and find a man about a door."

"Your door."

"My door. It has a hole in the roof of the dining room the size of a cartwheel, and the mosaic in the atrium is full of ash, and there is a man in the passage who was with me for eleven years and is dead, and I am going to have to bury him." She started walking. "That is the whole of what happens now. It is not a story. It is a list."

They walked up the hill. The Aventine was quiet on the river side, and the lamps were few, and the streets were narrow and steep, and Myrtale kept her hand on Antonia's sleeve the way a blind person holds a guide. At the top, at the corner of a lane where a fig tree grew out of a wall, Antonia stopped.

"Turn around," she said.

"Why?"

"Because you have never seen the city from the top," Antonia said, "and because in a month I may not have a house to show you from, and because there is no better hour for it than this."

Myrtale turned around.

Below them Rome lay in the half-dark, and the fires of the bakers were pricked out in every quarter, and the great mass of the Palatine was black against a sky that had begun, at the edge, to be grey. There was the Circus, and the temples on the Capitoline with a light in one of them, and the masts of the river, and beyond all of it the dark that was the Campagna.

"It is not the end of the world," Myrtale said.

"No," Antonia said. "It is the world."

They were three streets from the house when they saw the light.

It was in the Subura, high up, in the block behind the fuller's yard, and it was not a lamp and it was not a cook fire. It was the color of the inside of a furnace, and it was moving, and there was a sound coming from it that Antonia had heard twice in her life and had never forgotten: the sound of a great many people all shouting the same word at once.

"Fire," Hilarus said.

They ran.

The lane to the house was full of people. The whole Subura was out in the street in their night clothes, with buckets and wet cloths and children on their shoulders, and the vigiles were there, the seventh cohort, with their pumps and their hooks and their axes, and the fire was in the insula at the end of the lane and it had already jumped the alley to the roof of the fuller's shed.

"The house," Antonia said. "The house!"

Cerdon was at the door before her. The house was dark and shut, and Cerdon had his club, and he was shouting at the crowd to get back, and the crowd was not getting back, and the sparks were coming down like a hot yellow snow.

"Where is the book?" Antonia said.

"In the room," Cerdon said. "It is in the room. I will get it."

"No."

"I will get it." He pushed her toward the far side of the lane. "Take the women to the fuller's yard. Take the girl and go. The house is stone at the front and the roof is tile and it will hold until the cohort gets the pump up, and if it does not hold it is a house and not a person."

"Cerdon."

"Domina," he said, and he was already going in through the door, and he turned for a moment in the doorway with the light behind him and said, "It is a good door. It will hold."

Then he was gone into the dark of the house.

Antonia got Myrtale to the fuller's yard, and Rufina was there with the girls and Beryllos and the two porters, and the pumps came up the lane and the vigiles got the water going, and the fire was in the insula and then it was in the roof of the house and then it was not a fire anymore, it was a fact.

Crescens found her at the corner of the lane at the fourth hour, with her mantle over her face against the smoke.

"The house," he said. "The roof is gone and the upper floor is gone. The stone is standing. The dining room is standing. The book room is —" he stopped.

"Where is Cerdon?"

Crescens did not answer.

"Where is Cerdon?"

"He went up to the book room," Crescens said. "He got the strongbox. He was coming down and the stair went. The box is in the yard. The book is in the box. He is in the passage."

Antonia stood in the lane with the smoke in her hair and looked at the front of her house, at the gilt on the door, at the iron studs, at the good door which had held.

"Take me to him," she said.

* * *

He was in the passage by the back stairs, under the beam.

They had got the beam off him and he was alive, and he was not going to be alive for long, and the physician was a vigiles surgeon with a bag and no illusions.

"Domina," Cerdon said.

"I am here."

"You did not sell me," he said.

"What?"

"In the first year. When I ran. You said you would give me a week's wages and a letter to a man in Ostia." His voice was a whisper and his eyes were going. "I have been thinking about it tonight. I never told you. I have been thinking about it for three years."

"I did not sell you," Antonia said.

"I know," Cerdon said. "That is what I have been thinking about."

He died a little after the fifth hour with his hand in hers, and the whole Subura smelled of smoke, and the gilt on the door had run in the heat and set in a puddle on the step like a spilled coin.

* * *

They took her out of the city four days later, at dawn, in a cart of wool, with a cloak and a purse and a letter.

The letter was to Iusta the midwife at Ficulea, on the road to Nomentum, and it said that the bearer was the girl, and that she was to be lodged in the midwife's house and taught whatever the midwife was willing to teach her, and that she was not to be seen in the town and not to be sent to Rome until a man came from the house of Antonia Cassia with a red cloth and a sealed tablet.

The house at Ficulea was small and had a pear tree and a goat and a room with a bed in it, and it was fifteen miles from Rome, and it was, as Myrtale said, the end of the world.

"I have never been outside the walls," she said, on the step, with the cart waiting.

"Then you will have something to tell me when I come," Antonia said. "In November. I will come for you myself, with the litter, and I will bring the record of the case if I win it, and if I lose it I will bring myself and nothing else."

"And what will we do if you lose?"

"We will find out what a freedwoman with no house can do," Antonia said, "and it will not be nothing."

Myrtale stood at the cart with her bundle.

"There is a thing I have not asked you," she said.

"Ask."

"That night. In the stone room. When the man with the rope had finished." She was looking at Antonia's shoulder, not her face. "Did he say anything to you afterward?"

Antonia was silent for a moment.

"He said that I was alive because I had been clever," she said. "And then he said that I would be freed because a freedwoman cannot be put to the question, and because a man who frees the witness has bought a silence that no one can accuse him of buying."

Myrtale looked at her.

"Then he told the truth," she said, "and he is still the man who did it."

"Yes."

"And that is what we are going to do to him," Myrtale said. "In November. We are going to tell the truth, and he will not be able to accuse us of anything, because the truth is the one thing that cannot be bought."

"It can be bought," Antonia said. "It can always be bought. But it is expensive, and there is not enough money in Rome to buy it twice."

Myrtale got up into the cart and sat on the wool with her bundle on her knees. The driver clicked his tongue, and the mule leaned into the harness, and the cart went down the lane and turned at the corner by the burned shed, and Myrtale did not look back.

Antonia stood in the ash of her own doorway with her hands at her sides and watched until the cart was out of sight.

Then she went to find the man with the pump and asked him how long it would take to make the house stand up again.
