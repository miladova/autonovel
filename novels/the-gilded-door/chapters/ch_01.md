The gilt on the door was renewed every spring, and every spring it fooled somebody. From the Clivus Suburanus it looked like the entrance to a magistrate's house: two leaves of oak, a row of iron studs, a bronze knocker worn smooth by hands that had no business being gentle. You had to stand close to see that the oak had been patched at the bottom, where a generation of the Subura had kicked it, and that the studs were iron painted to look like bronze.

Cerdon stood in the doorway with his club and his good cloak, and told the litter-bearers where to put the litter, and the litter-bearers told him where to put his club, and the matter was settled the way such matters were settled in that lane: with insults, and a coin, and everybody going where they had intended to go.

Behind the door the mosaic said what the door did not. A threshold shows a visitor what the house thinks of him. The house of Antonia Cassia showed a door within a door, worked in black and white tesserae, and beyond it a wreath, and beyond the wreath nothing at all.

The first guests came at the ninth hour, when the light on the Esquiline turns the color of old honey and the tanners down by the river begin to make their presence known.

Lucius Iunius Gallus arrived on foot, as he always did, with two clients trailing him and the air of a man who had once been consul and had not yet stopped mentioning it. He was fifty-eight and looked sixty, and he had the manners of a man who had been taught that manners were the only thing a gentleman truly owned. He kissed Antonia's cheek, which he had been doing for eleven years, and said, "I hear you have a turbot."

"I have two turbots," Antonia said. "One for you, and one for the man you will tell about it."

"Then I shall tell only one man."

"Then one turbot would have sufficed."

His son came behind him, and did not kiss her. Decimus Iunius Gallus was nineteen and had spent the winter in the company of people who told him he was remarkable. He had his father's nose and his mother's money and no occupation beyond the spending of both. He looked around the atrium as though he were pricing it.

"Decimus," Antonia said.

"Domina."

That was all. He had called her that since he was fourteen. It was the correct word from a young senator to a freedwoman, and he used it the way a man uses a knife at table: correctly, and to make a point.

Gaius Calpurnius Daphnus came next, off a boat that morning, still smelling faintly of the Tiber and strongly of money. He was fifty, a freedman like herself, with a shipper's hands and a banker's eyes, and he had brought a jar of wine from Crete which he presented with the gravity of a man handing over a treaty.

"From the estate of a man who died owing me four hundred thousand," he said. "I thought you might like to drink his cellar."

"I might like to drink his whole estate."

"Give me time."

Lucius Valerius Crescens came in his uniform because he had come from the station and had not had time to change, and because he knew that the uniform did something to the room. He was a tribune of the seventh cohort of the vigiles, thirty-four years old, and he had the particular tiredness of a man who spent his nights being shouted at by people whose houses were on fire. He was also in debt to three money-lenders, a fact known to Antonia and to no one else in the room.

"You are late," she said.

"I am on duty."

"You are on duty in a dining room."

"Somebody has to watch the wine."

Then Manius Cassius Verus the Younger came in, alone, twenty years old and already carrying his father's name like a coat that did not fit him. He had been back from the Danube six months, and he had not yet found anything in Rome worth doing. He drank too much, and he was clever, and those two things together had made him the sort of young man who is invited everywhere and trusted nowhere.

"Verianus," Antonia said.

"Antonia." He used her name, and only her name, and he did it with a small defiance that she had long since decided to find useful rather than rude.

The last guest was Oppius Nepos, and he was late on purpose.

He came in with two slaves behind him carrying a covered basket, and he made his apologies to the room rather than to his hostess, which was the sort of thing he did. He was forty-three, an equestrian, and he had the smooth, well-fed look of a man who had never carried anything heavier than a document. He had made his money in the last years of Domitian, when a man could make money by remembering what other men had said, and he had kept it in the better years by remembering what other men had said about the men who had remembered.

"Domina," he said. "A small thing. Thrushes from Baiae. Fattened on figs."

"Your generosity is famous."

"Only my generosity?"

"Among other things."

He smiled. He had a good smile. That was the trouble with him.

They went through into the dining room, and the dining room did what it was built to do. It was twelve feet by twenty, painted on three walls with the story of Danaë, who was shut in a tower by a father who had been told that her son would kill him, and who was visited by a god in the form of a shower of gold. The painter had been paid well, and had made the gold look like coins, and the coins look like rain.

"Your Danaë has been busy," Gallus said, settling onto the couch. "The tower looks well. The god looks like a tax collector."

"The god is a tax collector," Antonia said. "That is the joke."

"Your painter has a coarse mind."

"My painter has six children and a bad hip. He paints what he is paid for."

The first course came: oysters from Britain, eggs, thrushes, and a dish of mushrooms that Beryllos had been fretting over since noon. The wine was Caecuban, which was almost gone from the world and which Antonia served to men who would notice.

"Tell me about the forum," Daphnus said to Gallus. "They say the basilica is bigger than the Circus."

"Nothing is bigger than the Circus."

"The emperor's architects disagree."

"The emperor's architects are paid to disagree."

"The emperor," Nepos said, "is building a forum that will last a thousand years. I should like to build something that lasts a thousand years."

"You have built a reputation," Antonia said. "That lasts longer."

Nepos inclined his head, accepting it as a compliment, which it was not.

Verianus was drinking his wine too quickly and looking at the painted Danaë as though he had never seen her before. "My father says the column will be finished in the summer," he said. "He says the spiral will show the whole war. Every cohort, every bridge, every Dacian village. He says you will be able to read it like a book."

"Can you read?" Decimus asked.

"I can read well enough to know what my father says."

There was a small pause, the kind that costs a young man a dinner invitation. Gallus filled it by talking about the Dacian treasure, and how much of it had gone into the forum, and how much of it had gone into the soldiers' purses, and how much of it, in his opinion, had gone into the pockets of the men who arranged the contracts.

"Which is to say," he said, "the usual."

"The usual is what makes Rome," Daphnus said. "If the emperor built a bridge and nobody stole from it, we should have to invent a second river."

They laughed, and the wine went round, and Gallus began to talk about the east. There had been a letter from the governor of Cappadocia, he said, or a letter about a letter; the king of Armenia had done something, or had failed to do something, and the Parthians had done something in reply, and the emperor had been seen in conversation with the men who drew maps. Nothing had been decided. Everything had been prepared.

"War?" Daphnus said. "In his sixtieth year?"

"Alexander was dead at thirty-three," Gallus said. "The emperor is not Alexander. He is patient. He will go east, and he will take his architects with him, and when he has finished with the Parthians he will build a road where their capital used to be."

"And the cost?"

"The cost is always the same. The soldiers pay it first, and the provinces pay it second, and we pay it third, in the price of grain and the price of marble and the price of a good mason."

"And who profits?"

"Ah," Gallus said. "That is a different question, and the answer is always a man with a contract."

Nepos turned his cup slowly by the stem. "You speak as though the war were a misfortune. It is an opportunity. The east is full of men who have something to sell and no one to sell it to. A man with a ship, or a friend in the commissary, or a cousin who knows the roads —"

"A man with a cousin," Antonia said. "Rome is governed by cousins."

"Rome is governed by cousins and freedmen," Nepos said, "and the cousins need the freedmen to count."

"Then the freedmen should ask a better price."

Nepos smiled at her over the cup. "That is what I have always admired about you."

"Admiration is a cold room to keep a woman in."

The second course came: a boar, a turbot, a dish of peacock's tongues which were, Antonia knew, not peacock's tongues at all, because Beryllos had told her so and had threatened to leave if she told anyone else.

Kallisto sang after the second course. She came in with her lyre and stood where the light would find her, and she sang a song in Greek about a woman waiting at a door. She had sung it before, twice, and both times Antonia had told her not to sing it again. Tonight she had been permitted, and she knew why, and she sang it to the room as though the room were one man and she had a use for him.

When she had finished, Gallus wiped his eyes and said, "You have a treasure there, Antonia."

"I have a singer."

"You have a treasure."

"Treasures are kept in strongboxes. Singers are kept in houses."

"Some treasures are kept in houses," Nepos said. "Some houses are kept for treasure."

Antonia looked at him. "You are becoming philosophical, Oppius Nepos."

"Only after the second course."

There was a scrape of a stool. Decimus had turned on his couch to face Verianus, with the bright, unkind look of a young man who has found something to say.

"Speaking of treasures," he said. "Verianus was telling me about his father's house. The old man keeps a girl there. A slave. She sings like a nightingale, and she has the old man's eyes."

"Decimus," his father said.

"She does. He won't sell her, either. My sister says —"

"That is enough."

"She is said to have the old man's eyes," Decimus said, to the room now, "and if she has his eyes, I hope for her sake she does not have his nose."

The laughter came, and went, and left something behind. Verianus had gone the color of the linen. His cup was halfway to his mouth and had stopped there, and he was looking at Decimus with an expression that Antonia had seen before, on men in courtrooms, in the moment before they were told the verdict.

Antonia said, "Decimus, tell me about your mother's estate. Is it true that the olive trees are dying?"

The boy turned to her, pleased to be asked. He talked about the estate for a quarter of an hour, and the moment passed, and the room went back to being a room.

But Antonia had heard it. A girl in the Cassian house. A singing girl, with the old man's eyes.

She had heard such things before. Rome was full of men with bastards, and full of bastards with their father's eyes, and it meant nothing. It meant nothing at all. She reached for her cup and found that her hand had gone cold.

At the end of the evening, when the guests were collecting their cloaks, Nepos found her by the door.

"The Palatine has been slow with your little matter," he said.

"It is not a little matter."

"All matters are little to the men who decide them. That is why they take so long." He adjusted his cloak. "I know a man in the petitions office. A discreet man. He has done such things before."

"And what would he want?"

"A friend," Nepos said. "He is a man who likes to have friends. As I am."

"You have a great many friends."

"I have a great many friends," he agreed, "and very few of them know it."

He went out. Antonia stood in the doorway for a moment, and the cold came in off the lane, and Cerdon closed the door against it.

* * *

The house after a banquet was a different house. The lamps burned low; the slaves moved through the rooms with cloths and buckets; the smell of wine and perfume gave way to the smell of vinegar and wet wool. Beryllos could be heard in the kitchen, telling someone exactly what he thought of the turbot.

In the passage outside the dining room, Kallisto was waiting, wrapped in a bed-cloak with her hair down. She had taken the pins out and had not put them back, which meant she wanted to be seen being tired.

"You sang well," Antonia said.

"I sang what you told me not to sing."

"I noticed."

"Three times I have asked you for the papers." Kallisto fell into step beside her. "You said after the games. The games were in October."

"The games were in October. The papers are not."

"I have been in this house nine years. I have paid for the roof twice and the bath once and the Greek teacher for the little one, and I have kept my mouth shut about the governor of Baetica and the wife of the praetor and the thing with the incense. I am not asking for a gift. I am asking for an accounting."

Antonia stopped at the door of the small room. "You want a house of your own on the Aventine, with a garden and a cook and a door that locks from the inside. That is four hundred thousand, and you have sixty, and if you leave this house with sixty you will be back in a room above a fountain by the winter."

"I might do better than you think."

"You might. You are clever, and you are beautiful, and you have a voice that makes old men remember their fathers. None of those things keeps a house. A house is kept by a woman who can say no to a man with money, and you have never once said no to a man with money."

Kallisto's mouth tightened. "You taught me not to."

"I taught you to make him pay for the yes. That is not the same thing." Antonia put her hand on the door. "Stay until the summer. I will give you a share of the season, a real share, written down, and in the summer we will talk about the papers again."

"A written share."

"Written and sealed."

"Sealed by whom? You have no seal of your own. You have a ring with a leaf on it that you never use."

Antonia looked at her. "Go to bed, Kallisto."

The singer went, and her bare feet made no sound on the mosaic, and Antonia stood in the passage for a moment with the lamp in her hand.

Antonia went to the small room behind the tablinum, where she kept the strongbox and the book. Tullia was already there, with the tablets and the lamp, and a face that had been waiting to say something for three hours.

"How much?" Antonia said.

"Two hundred and forty for the dinner. Sixty for the wine from Daphnus, which was a gift and which he will want paid for in April. The aedile's clerk wants fifty, and he wants it in silver, and he wants it before the Ides."

"He will have it before the Ides."

"And the man from the petitions office wants three thousand."

Antonia sat down. "Three thousand."

"He says the rescript has to be copied, and the copy has to be carried, and the carrier has to be a man of consequence, and men of consequence are expensive."

"They are also common. Offer him two."

"He will refuse."

"Then offer him two and a half, and let him think he has beaten you."

Tullia wrote it down. She was forty-seven, a freedwoman of the household, and she had been keeping Antonia's accounts for nine years. She had a face like a closed door and a memory like a warehouse, and she was the only person in Rome who could tell Antonia the truth without being asked for it.

"There is something else," Tullia said.

"Of course there is."

"A man came on Tuesday. Not to the door. To the gate at the back, where the wine comes in. He asked Meroe about a child."

Antonia did not move.

"A girl," Tullia said. "Sold out of the house of Cominius Asiaticus eighteen years ago. He said he was buying, and that he paid well for information, and that he had been told this was a house that knew things."

"What did Meroe tell him?"

"That she had come to Rome from Capua in the year of the fire and knew nothing about any child."

"Good."

"She told me afterward that he had a mark on his hand. A burn, she said. Like a brand."

Antonia looked at the lamp. It was a good lamp, bronze, with a small bronze door on the handle. She had bought it in the year she stopped being poor.

"Find out who he is," she said. "Do not ask at the station. Do not ask Crescens. Use the boy who runs for the fullers."

"He will want money."

"Give him money."

Tullia made a note. Then she said, "The girl Dessa did not eat again."

"Which girl?"

"You know which girl."

"I know eleven girls."

"Dessa. The Dacian. She has not eaten since the Nones, and she has been sitting in the garden at night with her shoes off, which Meroe says is a sign."

"A sign of what?"

"Of a girl who is thinking about the road."

Antonia rubbed her eyes. "Have Eutychus look at her. If she is sick, she is sick. If she is thinking about the road, have Hilarus sleep across her door."

"And if she runs?"

"Then she runs," Antonia said. "And we find her before she gets to the gate, and we do not beat her in the street, and we do not make a spectacle of a girl with a good hand for figures. She costs me twelve thousand sesterces and she can add a column of numbers faster than you."

"She can add a column faster than me because I taught her."

"Then you should be proud, and not let her get to the gate."

Tullia gathered the tablets. At the door she stopped.

"You have not asked me about the other thing."

"What other thing?"

"The Cassian girl," Tullia said. "The one the boy talked about."

Antonia looked up. The lamp threw her shadow onto the wall, and the shadow was larger than she was.

"Go to bed, Tullia."

"Good night, domina."

When she had gone, Antonia opened the strongbox. Inside were the ledgers, wrapped in oiled cloth, and a smaller box of cedar, and in the cedar box a lead token the size of a thumbnail, struck with the image of a door. It had been broken across the middle. She had the half with the door.

She turned it over twice, the way she had turned it over a thousand times, and put it back, and locked the box, and sat for a while with her hand on the lid.

* * *

The knock came at the fourth hour, when the city was at its quietest and the only people awake were bakers and thieves and women who could not sleep.

Cerdon came to the door of the small room with his club in his hand and his cloak over his night-shirt. "Domina. The back gate."

"Who?"

"A woman. Well dressed. A slave with her, a girl. She says she will not go away."

"Does she have a name?"

"She says her name is Publia."

Antonia was quiet for a moment. Then she put on her mantle and went out through the garden, where the roses were still six weeks from flowering and the fountain made its small, patient noise, and stood at the back gate with the lamp in her hand.

The woman was veiled. The veil was good wool, the sort that costs more than it looks, and the cloak beneath it was the color of a dove's throat. Behind her, holding a second lamp, stood a girl of about eighteen in a plain tunic, with a slave's collar at her throat and a lead token on a cord beside it.

"Lift your veil," Antonia said.

The woman lifted it. She was sixteen, with a long face and her father's brows and a mouth that had been trained not to move. She looked at Antonia as though she had rehearsed the look.

"You are Publia Cassia," Antonia said.

"Yes."

"Your father is a consular. Your mother is dead. You are betrothed to Titus Caesennius Rufus, and the wedding is in September, and you have come to my back gate at the fourth hour with one slave and no escort, which means that either you are foolish, or you are desperate, or you are both."

"Both," Publia said.

"Then come in," Antonia said, "before the whole Subura sees you."

She led them through the garden and into the small room, and shut the door, and set the lamp on the table. The slave girl stood by the wall with her eyes down. She was a pretty thing, dark-haired, with a mark like a crescent on the inside of her wrist, and she held the second lamp as though it were a baby.

"Sit," Antonia said.

Publia sat. She did not look at the ledgers or the strongbox or the bronze lamp. She looked at Antonia, and her hands were steady, and her voice, when it came, was steady too.

"I want you to break my marriage," she said.

Antonia sat down opposite her. "Why?"

"Because I have seen him."

"Caesennius?"

"He came to dinner in January. He has a house on the Caelian and a wife in Baetica, or perhaps she is dead, no one is sure. He talked about his brickfields for an hour. He has a way of looking at a girl as though he were estimating her." Publia's mouth did not move. "He is fifty-four."

"And your father wants the match."

"My father wants the proconsulship of Asia. Caesennius has a cousin on the emperor's council, and a fortune, and no children of his own, and he has offered a settlement that would pay my father's debts and leave enough over for a campaign." She said it flatly, as though she were reading a list of prices. "I heard them discussing it. I was in the passage behind the curtain, and they did not know I was there, and my father said that a daughter is a door, and that a man must open it at the right time."

Antonia said nothing. The fountain made its noise in the garden. Somewhere in the house a door closed.

"And your stepmother?" Antonia said.

"Marcella says I should be grateful." Publia's voice did not change. "She says that when she married my father she was twenty-one and he was forty, and that she has never regretted it, and that gratitude is a better foundation for a marriage than love, because gratitude lasts."

"Marcella is a sensible woman."

"Marcella is a wall," Publia said. "You cannot get through her, and you cannot get round her, and if you stand close enough you can hear her thinking."

Antonia almost smiled. "What do you want me to do?"

"I don't know. You are said to know how to do things."

"Things."

"Men," Publia said. "You are said to know how to make men do what you want, and how to make them sorry if they don't."

"Who says that?"

"My father."

Antonia leaned back. "What does your father say?"

"He says you are the most dangerous woman in Rome."

"He is right."

"Then help me."

Antonia looked at her for a long moment. She was a child, and she was not a fool, and she had come to the one door in Rome that a girl of her name should never have approached. That took a kind of courage. It also took a kind of ignorance that could get them all killed.

"Listen to me," Antonia said. "If you are seen coming here, your father will not beat you. He will do something worse. He will be reasonable. He will take you to the country and find a widow to keep you, and he will move the wedding to June, and you will spend the rest of your life in a house in Baetica with a man who estimates you. And I will lose my house, and my girls will be sold, and the man who buys them will not be as kind as I am. Do you understand?"

"Yes."

"Do you?"

"Yes."

"Then go home," Antonia said, "and do not come here again."

Publia did not move. "There is a girl in my father's house," she said. "Her name is Myrtale. She is my maid. She has been with me since I was eight years old, and she is the only person in that house who has ever been kind to me without wanting something."

Antonia's hand was flat on the table. She did not move it.

"And my father will not sell her," Publia said. "He will not free her. He will not let her marry. He keeps her the way a man keeps a thing he does not want anyone else to have. I have asked him. He says she is provided for."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because I have nothing else to give you," Publia said. "I have a dowry, and a name, and a father who would sell me to a brickmaker for a consulship. If I offered you money you would laugh at me, and if I offered you my friendship you would not believe me. So I am offering you what I know."

"And what do you want in return?"

"To not be married to that man."

"That is not a thing I can give you. That is a thing you will have to do yourself." Antonia leaned forward. "If I help you, you will owe me. Not now. Later, when I ask. Do you understand what it means to owe a woman like me?"

Publia's steady hands were not quite steady. "I have heard."

"You have heard stories. You have not heard the half of it." Antonia sat back. "Go home. Think about what you are asking. If you still want it in a week, send me a message by the woman who sells flowers at the Porta Esquilina, and do not sign it, and do not come here again."

"And the girl? Myrtale?"

"What about her?"

"She will be sold when I marry. Or given away. Caesennius will not have another man's bastard in his house, and my father will not pay to keep her, and Marcella has been waiting eight years for an excuse." Publia's voice had gone thin. "She is the only person in that house who has ever been kind to me without wanting something. If you can do nothing else —"

"I have said I will think," Antonia said.

"Because you knew him," Publia said. "Before. I don't know how. But when he speaks of you, his voice changes, and my stepmother leaves the room. And because I think that if anyone in Rome can make him open a door, it is you."

Antonia looked past her, at the slave girl by the wall. The girl was standing very still, holding the lamp, and the light was on her face, and she was looking at Antonia with an attention that was not the attention of a servant.

"What is your name?" Antonia said.

The girl glanced at her mistress, then back. "Myrtale, domina."

"Who gave you that name?"

"The master's house. I was called it when I came."

"When you came from where?"

"I don't remember, domina. I was small."

Antonia looked at the token at her throat. It was lead, thumb-sized, on a leather cord, and it had been worn smooth by years against the girl's skin. There was a wreath on it. There was nothing else on it at all.

The room was very quiet. Antonia heard her own breathing, and the fountain, and the small dry sound of the lamp.

"Go home," she said, and her voice was steady, because her voice had been trained for thirty-nine years to be steady. "Both of you. Go out through the garden and turn left at the fuller's, and keep your veil down until you reach the Vicus Patricius."

Publia stood. At the door she turned. "Will you help me?"

"I will think about it," Antonia said.

When they had gone, she sat without moving for a long time. Then she opened the cedar box, and took out the half-token, and held it in her palm. The door on it had been struck by a die that had made ten thousand others. It was worth nothing. It was worth everything she had.

She turned it over. On the back, where the break was, there was the smallest trace of a leaf: the edge of a wreath, cut off when the token was snapped in two.

Antonia closed her hand around it and sat in the dark until the first carts began to move on the Clivus, and the city woke up, and the day came in through the shutters like a creditor.
