Morning in the house came in stages. First the water-carriers, whose wheels had a particular squeal on the cobbles of the Clivus; then the bakers, who opened their shutters and let out the smell of hot bread and burnt flour; then the fullers, whose tubs gave the whole lane a smell like a nailed-up dog. By the time the sun reached the top of the garden wall, the household had been awake for two hours and the day was already behind.

Antonia went out to the peristyle with her cup and stood looking at the roses. They were tight and green and would be nothing for weeks. In the corner the fountain ran into its basin with the sound of a small, patient argument. Dessa was sitting on the edge of the basin with her shoes off and her feet in the water.

"You will get a chill," Antonia said.

"It is warm, domina."

"It is March."

"In Dacia it is warm in March," Dessa said, and then looked as if she had said something she had been told not to say.

"In Dacia it is snow in March."

"Sometimes." Dessa drew her feet out of the water and put them under her, and her face closed the way a door closes when the person inside has decided not to answer.

She was twenty and looked seventeen, with the flat cheekbones of the north country and hair that went red in the sun. She had been taken at fourteen, from a village whose name Antonia had heard once and never again. She had come through three owners before this house. The first had taught her to carry water. The second had taught her to be quiet. The third, who was a money-lender with a taste for arithmetic, had discovered that she could count, and had used her for four years, and had sold her in the autumn for more than he paid.

"Tullia says you have not been eating," Antonia said.

"I eat."

"Tullia says you have been sitting in the garden at night."

"It is cooler."

"It is March." Antonia sat down on the edge of the basin, which put her at the girl's level, and Dessa looked away, at the roses, at the wall, at anything. "I am not going to ask you what you are thinking. You would lie, and then you would be ashamed of lying, and it would be worse for both of us. I am going to ask you one thing. If you were to run, where would you go?"

Dessa's jaw moved. "I don't know what you mean."

"You know exactly what I mean. Where would you go?"

"Home," Dessa said, and the word came out of her like something pulled.

"Your village is five hundred miles from here, on the other side of two provinces and a river, and there is no village there anymore. There is a fort, and a road, and a tax collector. The men who took you are dead or rich. The men who would take you now would take you to a different kind of house, and the house would not have a garden."

"I know what kind of house."

"Do you?"

"I know what I am worth," Dessa said. "Twelve thousand sesterces. Tullia told me, when I asked. She said a girl who can count is worth twelve thousand, and a girl who can sing is worth thirty, and a girl who can do both is worth a great deal more than her master thinks."

Antonia almost laughed. "Tullia should learn to be less helpful."

"She said you would not beat me for asking."

"She is right. I will not beat you for asking. I will beat you for running, because if you run, Cerdon has to chase you, and if Cerdon chases you, the watch sees it, and if the watch sees it, some clerk writes your name in a book, and the book says I keep slaves who run. Do you understand?"

"Yes."

"Then stay out of the water in March."

Antonia stood up and went inside. At the door of the tablinum she stopped. "Dessa."

"Domina?"

"Come to me after the midday meal. I have four hundred entries in the grain accounts and I have not the patience for them."

The girl's face did something complicated. "Yes, domina."

* * *

Tullia was in the tablinum with the tablets laid out in rows, and Meroe standing by the door with her hands folded, and the look of a woman who has been rehearsing her story and has decided to tell it badly.

"Tell me about the man," Antonia said.

"He came to the back gate on Tuesday, in the afternoon, when the wine comes. He was well dressed. Not fine — well. A good cloak, no fringe, boots that had been mended. He had the look of a man who works for someone else and is paid not to say so."

"Age?"

"Forty, perhaps. He had a burn on the back of his right hand, here, between the thumb and the finger, and it was old. He said he was looking for a girl child sold out of the house of Cominius Asiaticus in the year of the fire. He said he paid for information. He showed me a coin."

"A gold coin?"

"A silver one. He said there would be more."

"And you told him?"

"That I had come from Capua in the year of the fire and knew nothing of any child. And he said, 'A pity,' and he said, 'If you remember, ask for me at the shop of the sandal-maker in the Via Nova, and say the name Felix,' and he went away up the lane."

Meroe's family had come from Capua; the year of the fire was the year Vespasian's temple burned, though which fire a person meant depended on where they had been standing. Antonia had used Meroe's Capuan childhood as a screen for eleven years. It was a good screen because it was true.

"You did well," Antonia said. "If he comes again, tell him the same, and send the boy to me at once. Do not go to the station. Do not send to the aediles. If anyone else asks at this door about a child, they are to be told to come back in the evening, and then Cerdon is to shut the door in their face."

"How many people are going to ask?" Meroe said.

"That is what I mean to find out."

When Meroe had gone, Tullia put her hand flat on the tablets. "There is more."

"Of course there is."

"The flower-seller at the Esquiline sent a message at dawn. A girl came to her before light, veiled, and gave her two words. She said: the fourteenth."

"The fourteenth of what?"

"Just the fourteenth. The girl said the lady would understand."

Antonia turned the date over. The fourteenth was the eve of the Ides. On the Ides of March, in the year before the emperor was born, a man had been killed in the senate house for wanting to be a king, and Rome had been telling the story ever since, mostly to warn itself.

"She means the eve of the Ides," Antonia said. "At the fifth hour. Tell the flower-seller to send her to the fuller's yard behind the house, not to the door. And Tullia — the money. Three thousand for the petitions office was robbery. Send the man two hundred and tell him I have heard that the rescript is being held by the office of the ab epistulis, and that a man who charges three thousand for a copy is a man who has something to hide."

"That is a lie."

"It is a suspicion. It will do until I have a lie."

Tullia wrote it down.

"Now," Antonia said. "The Cassii."

Tullia sat back. This was her subject. She had been a slave in a great house before she was freed, and she had kept, from that life, a memory like a tax roll and the ability to be told a secret and remember the shape of it without remembering the words.

"The Cassii have been dining above their means for thirty years," she said. "Manius Cassius Verus's father had two consulships and a bad steward. When the old man died, the estate came with a debt of nearly two million. The son spent four years paying interest and none of the principal. Then he married Acilia Marcella, whose father was a banker at Puteoli, and she brought him a dowry of one and a half million and a way of looking at a bill that has saved the family twice over."

"And the second wife does not like her stepson."

"The second wife is pleasant to the stepson in public and has not been alone in a room with him in seven years. The stepson is the old wife's boy. The son has been in Dacia. The daughter —"

"The daughter is for sale."

"They are asking one million two hundred thousand for her, which is a great deal even for a girl with two consuls in the family, and Caesennius Rufus is offering eight hundred thousand, and there is a cousin on the emperor's council who wants a son-in-law with a future."

"And Verus's own debts?"

"Six hundred thousand to the fiscus, in three installments, and the first is due in June. Another three or four hundred thousand to men who do not write things down."

Antonia looked at the window. A fly was walking on the sill, from the light into the shade and back.

"So the marriage buys the proconsulship."

"The marriage buys the first installment, and the proconsulship buys the rest. A governor of Asia can make two million in a year without touching his own money. Everyone knows it. Nobody says it."

"And the girl in the house? The one the boy talked about at dinner?"

Tullia's mouth did something small. "There is a story. It came from the kitchen of the Cassian house by way of a woman who sells them fish. The child was bought as an infant, eighteen years ago, from a dealer. She was given to a nurse in the country until she was five or six, and then brought into the house, and she has been in the house ever since, and she is maid to the daughter."

"Bought from which dealer?"

"The fish-woman did not know. She knew the nurse. The nurse is dead."

"Everyone is dead," Antonia said. "That is what makes Rome so restful."

"There is another thing." Tullia turned a tablet over. "The wet-nurse's sister is still alive. She lives at Ficulea, on the road to Nomentum, and she is a midwife, and her name is Iusta. She is said to have attended a birth in the Subura in the year before the child was bought, a birth in a house of women, and she is said to remember it."

Antonia sat very still. "Who told you that?"

"The fish-woman. She did not know she was telling me anything. She was complaining about her sister, who is a midwife, and who will not lend her money, and who is always talking about a baby she delivered in the Subura that had a token tied to its wrist."

"A token."

"A lead token. The midwife thought it was a charm." Tullia looked up. "Domina, you have gone white."

"It is the lamp," Antonia said. "It is too close."

"The emperor knows it."

"The emperor has been in Dacia, and then in the forum, and then on the Danube, and now he is looking east. The emperor knows what his freedmen tell him. His freedmen are told by men like —" Tullia stopped.

"Men like Oppius Nepos," Antonia said.

"I was going to say men like me."

"Men like you tell me the truth. Men like Nepos tell the emperor a story." Antonia stood up. "I am going to the forum. Have Hilarus bring the litter, and have him bring the plain one, and tell him if he jogs on the Sacred Way I will have him sold to a teacher of rhetoric."

* * *

The Basilica Ulpia had been open four years and still smelled of new stone. It was the largest thing of its kind in the world, and it was not, strictly, needed: Rome had basilicas enough. That was the point. A man had wanted to build something that said what could be built, and the something said it, and the merchants had moved in under the arches with their scales and their tablets and their arguments, and now the most beautiful building in the city was a marketplace.

Daphnus had a corner between the second and third column, where the drafts were bad, because drafts were cheap. He sat on a folding stool with a board on his knees, and two clerks on either side of him wrote in a script so small that a reader needed good eyes and no conscience.

"You are early," he said.

"I am punctual. It is a different vice." Antonia sat on the bench opposite him. "The grain."

"The grain is good. The grain is very good. We have three ships of Egyptian wheat coming into Portus before the Kalends, and the price in Ostia is down two sesterces on the modius, and the annona is buying, which means the price will be up six by the summer."

"And our little venture?"

"Your little venture is twelve thousand modii in a warehouse that floods, and my cousin is watching it. If you want to be rich, buy the warehouse. If you want to be comfortable, lend to the men who own the ships."

"I want to be neither."

"Then you want to be interesting, and there is no money in it." Daphnus made a mark on his tablet. "You are not here about grain."

"I am here about a man. Not a name. A description. He wears a good cloak, he carries a silver coin in his pocket, and he has an old burn on the back of his hand, between the thumb and the first finger. He has been asking questions in the Subura about a child sold eighteen years ago. He told a woman to ask for him at the sandal-maker's in the Via Nova and to say the name Felix."

Daphnus stopped writing. "Felix is a common name."

"Yes."

"There is a Felix who works for the urban prefect's office. A freedman. He does the things the prefect does not want to sign."

"How do you know?"

"Because I have paid him," Daphnus said. "Twice. Once for a ship's manifest, once for a man's tax record. He is expensive and he is careful and he has never once asked me a question about why I wanted to know. If he is asking questions himself, someone above him has told him to."

"And who is above him?"

"Not the prefect," Daphnus said. "The prefect does not care about children. The prefect cares about order, and the grain, and the emperor's temper, and whether the vigiles can put out a fire in the fourteenth region before it reaches the Palatine. Children are not order." He looked at her. "Who is asking you about children?"

"Nobody, yet."

"Antonia."

"Nobody, yet," she said. "When somebody does, I will tell you."

She left him at his board and went out into the forum, where the light was so bright on the new marble that it hurt to look at the steps. There were men everywhere: senators in their litters going up the Clivus Argentarius, clerks with bundles of tablets, a group of Jews arguing near the arch, a boy selling water from a jar, two soldiers of the Guard standing at the corner, watching nothing with tremendous attention.

And there, at the foot of the steps, with four clients around him and a secretary at his elbow, was Manius Cassius Verus.

He was in a toga, which he wore the way other men wore a helmet: as a thing designed to be looked at. He was forty-five, tall, thin at the jaw and thick at the neck, with grey at the temples and the particular uprightness of a man who has been told all his life that he carries a family. He was listening to one of his clients, and his face had the patient attention of a man who is being told something he already knows and expects to be told again.

Antonia did not stop. She went down the steps on the far side, past the equestrian statue of the emperor on the horse, and she was almost at the bottom when the secretary looked up.

She saw the moment of recognition cross his face, and then she saw him lean and speak, and she saw Verus turn his head.

He looked at her the way a man looks at a door that has been left open in a house he thought was locked. It lasted less than a breath. Then one of his clients said something, and he turned back, and the secretary wrote, and the line of men closed around him like water.

She walked out through the arch of the forum and along the Argiletum to the Clivus, and her heart was going like a hammer, and she was furious that it was.

"Domina."

He had come out of the booksellers' row on the left, where the men who sold second-hand copies of the poets stood with their scrolls in baskets and their prices written on their fingers. He was in a pale cloak with a narrow border, and he had a roll of papyrus under his arm, and he was smiling.

"You are following me," Antonia said.

"I am walking in the same direction."

"To the Subura?"

"To the shop of a man who sells purple dye. I have a house to furnish." Nepos fell in beside her, and she did not stop, and he matched her pace as though they had agreed it. "You were at the basilica."

"I was."

"Daphnus is a good man. Careful. He has the largest private warehouse in Portus and he still buys his cloaks at the second-hand stalls." He glanced at her. "You were at the basilica, and before that you were at the forum steps, and Manius Cassius Verus was at the forum steps, and he looked at you as though you had been a bird in a room."

"You have good eyes."

"I have good clients. They tell me things." Nepos shifted the roll under his arm. "Have you thought about the petitions office?"

"I have."

"And?"

"I have thought that a man who wants three thousand sesterces for a copy of a rescript is either a fool or a thief, and I have thought that men who are either of those things usually turn out to be both. I have sent him two hundred, with a message about the office of the ab epistulis, and we shall see what he does with it."

Nepos laughed, and it was a real laugh, which was somehow worse than a false one. "You are a great deal cleverer than most of the men I work with."

"Most of the men you work with are senators."

"Yes," said Nepos. "That is what I said."

They walked a little way in silence. A cart went by, loaded with amphorae, and the driver shouted at them to move, and Nepos moved, and Antonia did not, and the driver went round her.

"One thing," Nepos said. "The Cassii."

"What of them?"

"They are an old family and an old family is a costly thing to keep. Verus has the name, and the name has the debts, and the debts have the marriage. The daughter is to be married in September, and the bridegroom is Caesennius Rufus, and the bridegroom's cousin sits on the council. That is the whole story of Rome this year: an old name, a new fortune, and a girl in the middle who will be told she is fortunate."

"You are very well informed about a wedding."

"I am invited," Nepos said. "Are you?"

Antonia stopped walking. "No."

"Then you will not see the bride's father give her away, with the tears in his eyes and the whole city watching, and the old name saved, and the money all in the right pockets." Nepos took a step and turned. "It is a great pity. They say Verus has been asking about a freedwoman in the Subura. Asking the prefect's office, through a clerk he knows. He is very discreet, and he is very frightened, and I have never known a frightened patrician to be discreet for long."

"Are you threatening me, Oppius Nepos?"

"I am telling you," he said pleasantly, "that a man may be a friend to a house without being invited to its wedding."

He went on down the Argiletum, unhurried, and the crowd took him, and Antonia stood in the middle of the street with the carts going round her and the dye-shops stinking of urine and murex, and her hands were cold again, and it was not the March wind.

* * *

The moonlight on the fourteenth came late and thin, and the fuller's yard behind the house smelled of stale urine and lime, and Antonia stood in the corner of it with her mantle up and waited, and told herself she was a fool.

Publia came alone this time, which was worse. She came through the gap in the wall by the fuller's shed, veiled, and she was breathing as though she had run, and when Antonia took her arm she felt the girl shaking.

"You are alone."

"Myrtale is with my aunt's household tonight. The old woman is sick and my father sent her to sit up." Publia pulled the veil down. "And my father has forbidden anyone in the house to speak your name."

"Then it will be spoken a great deal."

"He said it to the household." Publia's voice was flat. "He stood in the atrium, where the whole family could hear, and he said that the woman on the Clivus is not to be received, not to be greeted, not to be mentioned, and that any slave who speaks to her or to her people will be sold within the day. Then he went into his tablinum and shut the door and stayed there until dark."

Antonia looked at the girl's face in the thin light. "Why?"

"How should I know? He is my father." Publia wiped her eyes with the edge of her veil, and it was the gesture of a child, and it made her look about twelve. "My stepmother came to my room afterward. She said, 'Your father is not angry. He is frightened, and men who are frightened say things that cost other people very dear.' Then she said, 'Do not write to her, do not send to her, and do not go to her, and if you have already gone to her, do not tell me.'"

"And then what did she do?"

"She sat with me until I fell asleep." Publia's mouth twisted. "I do not understand her."

"Nobody understands Marcella. That is her great achievement."

They stood a moment in the smell of the fuller's yard. Somewhere a dog started up and stopped. Antonia could hear the city, all around, in its ten thousand ways of making noise.

"The girl," Antonia said. "Myrtale. You said she has a token."

"A lead token. On a cord, around her neck. She never takes it off. She says it was tied to her when she was small and she does not know who tied it."

"What is on it?"

"A wreath. Just a wreath. She showed me once." Publia hesitated. "Why does that matter?"

"It matters because I have seen such a token before." Antonia's voice was steady. "There was a dealer in Rome, eighteen years ago, a woman named Bassa, who bought children and sold them on. She had a token struck so that the women whose children she sold could be told, if they ever had money, which child was which. One half went with the child. One half stayed with the mother."

Publia's face changed, and went very still. "That is a horrible story."

"Yes."

"You are making it up."

"I am not."

"Then —" Publia stopped. Her hands had come together in front of her, and she was twisting her fingers, and Antonia saw her work through it, and saw the moment she arrived.

"Oh," she said.

"Yes."

"But she is a slave."

"She is a slave now," Antonia said. "She was not always."

Publia looked at her for a long time. "You know whose child she is."

"I suspect."

"Whose?"

"I will tell you when I am certain, and not before, because a thing like this said too early will get the girl sold into Africa and me into a cellar."

The girl's eyes had gone hard, and Antonia felt a flicker of something that was not quite respect and not quite fear. "You want something from me."

"I do."

"Name it."

"Two things. In your father's tablinum, among his papers, there will be a record of purchase for a slave girl, bought when she was an infant or a small child, about eighteen years ago. Find it. Read the date, the seller's name, the price, and the words. Do not take it — if it goes missing he will know the house is not safe and he will start burning things. Look at it, and remember it exactly, and bring me the copy."

"I have never been in my father's tablinum in my life."

"You are his daughter and he has been in this house for thirty years. There will be a day when he is at the baths. There is always such a day."

Fifteen years of hunger and an elephant, said Antonia, though not aloud. What she said was, "And the second thing?"

"You have to come to the wedding," Publia said. "In September. To my father's house. He cannot forbid a guest at a wedding without explaining why, and my stepmother will insist on a large wedding, because she is proud of the money she has spent on the house."

Antonia felt something cold move in her. "You want me to walk into that house."

"I want you to walk into that house," Publia said, "and stand in the room where they keep the shrine, and eat their dinner, and let them look at you. And I want to see my father's face when he cannot do a thing about it."

The dog started up again. The gate at the end of the yard moved a little in the wind and did not open.

"All right," Antonia said. "Both things. But you will do exactly as I say, and you will not tell the girl. Not a word. Not until I am ready."

"Why not?"

"Because she has been a slave for eighteen years and she has survived it by knowing exactly what her master wants to hear. If you tell her what I believe, she will do one of two things. She will believe it and be destroyed by it, or she will not believe it and you will have lost her. Either way she will still have to sleep in a room near his, and every servant in that house will see a change in her, and one of them will tell the master." Antonia put her hand on the girl's shoulder. "You will be kind to her, and you will not tell her, and you will find the paper. That is all. Do you understand?"

"Not telling her," Publia said, "feels like a lie."

"It is a lie. It is one of the three or four worth telling." Antonia looked past her at the gate. "Now go home, and go through the fuller's, and if you see a man with a burned hand, turn your face away and keep walking, and tell me about it in the morning."

Publia went. Antonia stood in the yard for a while after the girl had gone, with her hand on the cold brick, and thought about a house that had been shut against her for eighteen years, and about a door in a mosaic, and about a girl with a token on a cord who did not know she was her mother's.

Hilarus was waiting at the corner with the lamp shuttered. He was twenty-five and had the shoulders of a man who carried a chair up the Viminal four times a day, and he had once, in his first month, tried to run, and had been brought back by Cerdon and had expected to be sold. Antonia had made him carry her litter for a month with no supper and had then given him a knife and told him that if he ever wanted to go he should tell her first and she would give him a week's wages and a letter to a man in Ostia. He had cried, which had embarrassed them both, and had stayed three years since.

"Domina," he said. "There was a man at the corner."

"With a burned hand?"

"With a cloak. He watched the gate for an hour and then he went away up the hill."

"Did he see you?"

"I was a lamp," Hilarus said. "Nobody sees a lamp."

They went in through the garden and Antonia bolted the gate herself, and stood in the dark among the sleeping roses, and did the arithmetic she had been avoiding all evening. She had a fortune of perhaps three million sesterces, most of it in loans, warehouses and the goodwill of men who owed her. She had a house that could be closed by a single magistrate's order. She had no family name, no vote, no imperium, no father to speak for her in the senate house, and no legal standing to demand anything at all from a consular.

She had a book, and a token, and a promise made to a girl of sixteen in a fuller's yard.

It had begun, then. It would not stop until one of them was dead or free.
