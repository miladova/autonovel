The house of Oppius Nepos was on the Quirinal, in a street of houses that had been built in the reign of Claudius and had all been bought, in the last twenty years, by men who had made their money in the reign of Domitian. It had a portico of six columns, a garden with a fish pond, a library of four hundred scrolls that had been bought by the yard, and a doorkeeper who had been bought, Antonia suspected, in the same way.

"You are expected," the doorkeeper said.

"Am I."

"The master said to bring you to the garden and not to the tablinum, because the tablinum has a mosaic that cost him a great deal and he does not want it spoiled."

"By what?"

"By whatever it is you are going to say to him," the doorkeeper said, and stood aside.

Nepos was in the garden, feeding the fish. He was in a plain tunic with his sleeves rolled, and he had a bowl of bread in his hand, and he was dropping pieces into the water with the concentration of a man who has found something to do that requires no thought.

"Domina," he said. "Sit. Is the bench comfortable? I bought it in the year 106 from a man who was selling everything he owned to pay a debt, and I have always meant to ask him what the debt was, and now he is dead and I shall never know."

"You are in a good humor."

"I am in an excellent humor. I have had a letter from a friend in the east. The emperor has decided, or is about to decide, or has been persuaded by his architects, and there will be a war, and it will be in the spring, and every man in Rome who owns a ship or a warehouse or a cousin will make money, and every man who does not will pay for it." He threw the last of the bread into the pond. "Which of the two are you, domina?"

"That depends on the day."

"Today?"

"Today I am the second," Antonia said, "and I have come to find out what it will cost me."

Nepos sat down on the bench beside her, at a distance of a foot, and put the empty bowl on the ground. "You have my terms."

"You offered three hundred thousand for the book and a question about Verus. I have come to hear the terms in a room with a door, from a man who is not standing at a funeral with a crowd around him."

"This is a room with a door. It is a garden with a door. In Rome very little is a room." Nepos smiled. "But I take your point. Very well. The terms. I want the book, all of it, the original and every copy, and I want a statement, sworn before a magistrate, in your own words, describing the night of the Floralia as you saw it, and describing the household of Cassius Verus as you know it. In return I will pay you three hundred thousand sesterces, and I will see to it that the aedile's office closes its file, and that the prefect's office takes no further interest in your house, and that the register of the city forgets that a slave girl was ever concealed."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I will take the book in a different way, and it will cost me more and you will like it less." He looked at the fish. "I have been doing this for twenty years, domina. I know how a house like yours is held together. It is held together by secrets and by the fact that everyone in it agrees to pretend that the secrets are not there. I do not need to prove anything. I need only to make one of your people believe that another of your people has told me something, and then I need to sit back and wait."

"They will not believe it."

"One of them will. There is always one." Nepos turned. "The cook who is owed wages. The singer who wants her papers. The steward who has been keeping the accounts for nine years and has begun to think that the house would not run without her. I have a list of your household, domina. It is not a long list. I could read it to you."

Antonia looked at the fish pond.

"Show me the file," she said.

"What file?"

"The file. The one you have been keeping since 93. The one with the transcript in it. You did not bring me here to talk about a singer and a cook. You brought me here to show me that you have something that will put me in the dock if you wish it, and to watch my face while I read it."

Nepos looked at her for a long moment. Then he got up and went into the house, and came back with a bundle of tablets tied with cord.

"There are four copies of the transcript," he said. "One in the urban prefect's archive. One in the archive of the aediles. One in the house of Cassius Verus, in his own hand. And this one, which I had copied in the year 98 from the prefect's, when a clerk owed me a favor." He put it on the bench. "Do you want to read it alone?"

"No," Antonia said. "I want you to watch me read it. That is what you brought me here for."

She untied the cord.

* * *

The tablets were thin and the wax was old and the writing was small and sloping. The first page was a heading: a date in the year 93, the name of the investigating magistrate, the name of the household, the nature of the charge, which was treason, and the names of the two slaves to be questioned.

The second page was the transcript.

It was in the formal style. *Interrogatus, respondit* — asked, she answered. The clerk had written it down as it happened, and the clerk had not been interested in the pauses, or in the sound of a rope creaking against a beam, or in the smell of the room.

*Asked whether she knew the man Didius Probus, she answered that she knew him by sight.*

*Asked whether the said Probus had been received in the house of the elder Cassius Verus, she answered that he had come four times and had spoken with the master in the tablinum with the door closed.*

*Asked what was said, she answered that she did not know, that the door was closed, that she was a slave and did not listen at doors.*

*Asked again, with the question applied, she answered that she had listened at doors and had heard the said Probus speak of a letter to the governor of Germania and of a sum of money.*

*Asked whether she had told anyone, she answered that she had told the young master.*

*Asked what the young master did, she answered that the young master had told her to be silent and had gone to his father.*

*Asked —*

The letters on the page had blurred. Antonia looked up at the fish pond and the sky above the roof of the house of Oppius Nepos, and then she looked down again.

*Asked whether she had seen the said Probus receive money in the house, she answered that she had seen him receive a folded paper and no money.*

*Asked to name the persons present, she answered that she was alone with the said Probus on the day in question and that no one else was present.*

*Asked again, with the question applied twice, she answered that the young master had been present, that the young master had come into the passage behind the kitchen, that the young master had taken the folded paper from the said Probus, and that the young master had struck her and told her that if she spoke of it she would be sold.*

*Asked whether she had told the elder master of this, she answered that she had.*

*Asked what the elder master had said, she answered that the elder master had said nothing, and that the next day the said Probus was found dead in his bath, and that the young master came to her in the passage and told her that she was the only witness and that he was sorry.*

*Asked whether she understood what she had said, she answered that she understood.*

Antonia turned the tablet over. On the back, in the same small sloping hand, was a note:

*This is the evidence of the slave Prima, aged nineteen, of the household of the elder Cassius Verus, taken on the fourth day before the Kalends of October, in the consulship of —*

She stopped. She knew the date. She had known the date for eighteen years.

"You were there," Nepos said, from a great distance. "That is the part that interests me. Not what you said. That you were there. The file has one gap, domina, and it is not the transcript. It is the fact that the clerk who took the transcript was dismissed in the following year and his papers were burned, and that the whole file — the charge, the witnesses, the transcript — was closed within a fortnight, and that the reason given was that the accused man had died before trial."

"Didius Probus died before trial."

"Didius Probus died in his bath before anyone was charged," Nepos said. "The elder Cassius Verus was never accused. The son was never questioned. The only person who was ever asked a single question about the whole affair was a slave girl of nineteen, and she was questioned for eleven hours, and afterward she was manumitted by the mother of the house, which is a strange thing to do for a slave who has just accused your husband's son."

Antonia put the tablet down.

"What do you want?" she said.

"I want to know who closed the file," Nepos said. "I have wanted to know for twenty years. The file was closed by a man with influence, in a fortnight, in a year when files were not closed. And there is one more thing in this file that I have never been able to explain, and that is the note in the margin on the last page."

Antonia turned over the last tablet. There was a note in the margin, in a different hand, small and cramped.

*The woman was not sold. Let her be found a place.*

"It is not signed," Nepos said. "But the hand is the hand of the elder Cassius Verus."

The fish came up to the surface of the pond and made a small ring, and were gone.

"He did not sell me," Antonia said.

"He did not sell you," Nepos agreed. "That is the last thing I do not understand. A man who has just had a slave questioned for eleven hours writes in the margin that she is not to be sold, and lets her go. It is not mercy. I have known men all my life and that is not how mercy looks. It is —" he searched for the word — "it is an accounting."

"It is a bribe," Antonia said.

"To whom?"

"To himself. He could not say that he had done a terrible thing, so he did one small decent thing, and wrote it in the margin, and for eighteen years that margin has been his absolution." She set the tablet down. "You have read this file a hundred times and you have never once asked the only question that matters."

Before she left the garden, Nepos said one more thing, and he said it in the mild voice he used for the sentences that mattered.

"There is a girl in your house," he said. "The Dacian. The one the register says is in Africa."

Antonia's hand was on the door.

"I have no interest in her," Nepos said. "She is a slave and she saw something and she is not worth the trouble of taking her. But you should know that a man who is paid to look for something always finds something, and that the man I have paid to look for the book has been looking in your house for two months. He has looked in the warehouse, and the garden, and the cellar. He has not looked in the room next to yours, because the room next to yours is where you sleep, and a man who searches the room where a woman sleeps has to be certain of what he will find, and he is not certain."

"You have a man in my house."

"I have a man in every house," Nepos said. "It is not a boast. It is a business expense."

"Then tell him from me that if he comes into the room where I sleep, I will know it before he has crossed the threshold, because I have been in this city for thirty-nine years and I have never once slept without a knife under the pillow." Antonia opened the door. "And tell him that the room next to mine is not a room. It is a passage, and the passage has two doors, and the second door goes to the fuller's yard, and the fuller has four sons and a vat of piss, and none of them like men who come into houses at night."

She went out. Behind her, Nepos laughed — a real laugh, the second one she had heard from him, and the second one that was worse than a false one.

"Which is?"

"Who held the lamp," Antonia said.

Nepos looked at her. In the afternoon light, with a fish pond behind him and a bowl of bread at his feet, he was an ordinary man of forty-three, and there was nothing in his face to say that he had spent twenty years building a career out of other people's worst afternoons.

"The lamp," he said slowly.

"There were two men in that room. The old man asked the questions. The young man held the lamp." Antonia stood. "You have a copy of a file, Oppius, and you have carried it about for fourteen years like a boy carrying a stone in his pocket, waiting for the day he can throw it at a window. And it never occurred to you to ask who else was in the room, because a file is a thing written down, and what is not written down does not exist for a man like you."

She walked to the door of the garden.

"The book," Nepos said.

"On the Kalends of June," Antonia said, "you will have my answer."

"I have told you what will happen if it is no."

"I have told you that you have never waited for anything in your life," Antonia said. "That remains the most interesting thing about you."

* * *

She went first to the station of the seventh cohort, because she had promised, and because a promise to a man in debt was the only kind of promise she could afford to keep.

Crescens was in the yard with the pumps. He had a body on a board again — an old woman this time, from a fire on the Viminal, and she had not drowned, she had burned, and the smell of it was still in the yard.

"Not here," he said. "Come upstairs."

The room upstairs had a bed, a chest, a basin, a helmet on a nail, and a window the size of a hand. He shut the door and stood with his back against it.

"You asked to see a man's hands," he said. "When he has drowned fighting."

"I did."

"There was nothing to see. That was the point." Crescens crossed to the chest and took out a tablet. "I wrote it down, because a tribune who notices is a tribune who writes it down. No abrasions on the knuckles. No scrapes on the palms. A laceration of the forehead, transverse, above the brow, which is not consistent with a fall against a smooth marble rim. Water in the lungs, which the physician says means he was alive when he went in." He handed her the tablet. "I have not filed it. It is in this chest. If the prefect's office asks for my report, I will give them the one that says accident. This one is for you."

Antonia read it twice. It was a plain document, in a plain hand, and it said what she had known for a month and could not prove.

"Thank you," she said.

"Do not thank me. If it is ever read out in a court, I will be dismissed, and the prefect will say that I was bribed by you, and a man in Ostia will say that I owed money to three lenders, and both of those things will be true." Crescens took the tablet back and put it in the chest. "Where is the girl?"

"In my house, in the room next to mine."

"And the register says she is in Africa."

"The register says she is in Africa."

Crescens looked at her for a long moment. "I have been thinking about the register," he said. "It is a very good lie. It is such a good lie that it has made her into a thing that does not exist. And a thing that does not exist cannot be freed, domina. If you go to the praetor and say, this woman is free, the praetor will look at the register and say, this woman is in Africa. You have put her beyond the law to keep her safe, and now the law cannot give her anything."

Antonia sat down on the chest.

"Yes," she said.

"You had not thought of that."

"I had thought of it at the fourth hour of the morning after I did it, and I have been thinking of it ever since." She looked at the small window. "There is a way. It is a bad way. It requires the girl to be produced in court as a slave, claimed by a master, with an adsertor to assert her freedom. If the master produces her, the register is answered. If the adsertor wins, she is free and the register is corrected. If the adsertor loses, she is a slave, and the master takes her away, and the penalty is paid by the adsertor."

"Who is the master in this story?"

"Cassius Verus. Who else? He must be the one who produces her, or the whole thing is a fiction and the praetor will see it in a morning." Antonia looked at him. "He has agreed. He will claim her in court, and the adsertor will assert her freedom, and the case will be heard by the praetor with the decemvirs, and she will be freed by the rod or she will not."

"And who is the adsertor?"

"A free man of good standing who is willing to risk a penalty and his reputation on the outcome."

"Who?"

"I do not know yet," Antonia said. "It cannot be a freedman. It cannot be a client. It must be a man whose word is worth something in a court and who will not be bought." She looked at Crescens. "I had thought of Gallus."

"You cannot use Gallus. He is grieving and he is angry and he believes his son was murdered, and if you put him in a court as the adsertor for a slave girl in the house of the man he suspects, the whole thing will be a spectacle and the praetor will adjourn it before the second hour."

"Then I will find another."

"You will find another," Crescens said, "or you will find that the whole case turns on the one man in Rome who is willing to stand up in front of a praetor and say that a girl in a great house is free, which is a thing that no man in Rome has said in a hundred years without being paid for it."

He opened the door. In the yard below, the men were rolling the pumps back into the shed, and the old woman on the board had been covered with a sheet, and a boy was waving a branch at the flies.

"Domina," Crescens said. "When it comes to it, and you need a man to stand in a court and be ruined —" he stopped, and looked at his helmet on the nail, and did not look at her — "I am a tribune of the fire-watch, and I have no name, and I owe money to three lenders, and I am the worst adsertor in Rome. But I am better than nothing."

Antonia looked at him.

"Keep your helmet," she said. "You will need it, and I will need you where you are."

She went down the stairs and out into the street, and the sun was on the roofs, and she found that her eyes were stinging, and told herself it was the smoke.

* * *

She went from the Quirinal to the Vicus Tuscus, to a shop under the shadow of the temple of Castor where a man named Salvius sold old bronze and stone, and where a freedman called Epaphroditus had kept the records of the auctioneers' guild for thirty years.

Epaphroditus was seventy and had no teeth and a memory like a locked box, and he had known Antonia's — he had known, he said, the woman who used to come to the auctions with Gaius Antonius Felix, in the year when Felix was buying everything in Rome that had a good name attached to it.

"You want the guild's lists," he said.

"I want the list of the coactores for the year 71 and the year 72."

"Which coactores?"

"All of them."

"That is four hundred names."

"Then I want the name that is missing." Antonia put a purse on the counter. "There was a man in this guild, in the reign of Vespasian, who was a slave and worked as an auctioneer's clerk in the Porticus of Livia. He was manumitted in the year 71 or 72 and went into the grain trade at Puteoli, and he died rich, and he had a son who is now an equestrian in Rome and claims that his family has been in Puteoli for five generations."

Epaphroditus's mouth worked over his gums.

"Oppius," he said.

"Oppius."

"Gaius Oppius Philargyrus. He was in the guild. He was a slave of the Oppii, and he was a clever man with a crowd, and he ran the auctions for the wine lots in the Porticus. He was manumitted in the year 72, in the consulship of —" the old man stopped and closed his eyes — "the year of the two consuls who were brothers, whatever their names were. And he went to Puteoli, and he was in the grain trade, and he made a fortune, and he came back to Rome in the reign of Domitian and bought a house on the Quirinal, and he died in the year 96, in his bed, of a complaint of the liver, and his son sold the house and bought a better one."

"And the son?"

"The son was born in the year 69, in the house of the Oppii, and he was a slave for two years until his father was manumitted and bought him free." Epaphroditus opened his eyes. "It is all in the guild's book. The manumission is recorded, because the guild recorded every manumission of a member, and the fee was paid, and the name of the praetor who manumitted him is in the margin."

Antonia stood very still in the shop of old bronze and stone.

"You are certain," she said.

"I am seventy years old and I have no teeth," Epaphroditus said, "and the only thing I have left is that I am certain about the guild's book. The man was a slave. The son was a slave. There is no five generations in Puteoli. There is a tomb in Puteoli with the father's name on it, and the tomb says *libertus*, and the son has never gone back to see it, and the guild's book says why."

* * *

That night, in the small room, Antonia laid it all out on the table: the page from Bassa's ledger, the copy of the transcript that Nepos had allowed her to read and not to keep, the notes she had made from the guild's book, the half-token, and the ring with the cassia leaf.

Tullia stood at her shoulder. Dessa stood at the other end of the table with a lamp, and did not ask a single question, and watched everything.

"He is a freedman," Antonia said. "He has been passing as freeborn for twenty years. He is on the equestrian list, and a freedman cannot be on the equestrian list, and if it is known, he will not be prosecuted — nobody prosecutes a man for that after twenty years — but he will be struck off the list, and he will lose the right to sit in the first fourteen rows at the theater, and the right to wear the gold ring, and the right to be called anything but what he is. And he will lose the thing that matters to him more than any of it."

"Which is?" Tullia said.

"The prefecture," Antonia said. "He wants to be prefect of the grain, or prefect of the watch, or a procurator of a province. He has wanted it for twenty years. And a man who is discovered to be a freedman passing as an equestrian will never be given a prefecture as long as the emperor lives."

"You are going to tell him that."

"I am going to show him that I know," Antonia said, "and then I am going to give him what he wants, which is the book, and a statement about Verus — written and sealed, and false in every particular that matters. And he will take it, because it is what he has asked for, and because a man who is about to be exposed will believe any bargain that lets him keep his ring."

Tullia looked at the table. "And the book?"

"The book will be a copy," Antonia said. "A copy of the first twenty pages, which are all real, and the rest invented. And the statement will say that I saw nothing on the night of the Floralia, and that the house of Verus is respectable, and that the death was an accident, which is a thing that every man in Rome believes already."

"And what does that buy you?"

"Time," Antonia said. "Nepos will take the book and be satisfied, and he will go to the prefect with it, and the prefect will look at it and find nothing, and in the meantime the real book will be somewhere else, and the guild's book will be in my hand, and the man who thinks he has won will be learning arithmetic."

Dessa spoke for the first time. "And what does it buy me?"

Antonia looked at her.

"Nothing," Dessa said. "It buys you time. It buys me nothing."

"That is true," Antonia said.

"Then say it."

"It is true," Antonia said again. "It buys me time, and the time is for your mistress's daughter, and you are not in it. That is the arithmetic of this house, and I am not going to pretend it is anything else."

Dessa looked at her across the table with the lamp between them.

"You should have told me that in March," she said, "when you gave me a knife."

The lamp guttered. Antonia looked at the girl across the table and did not look away.

"Everything I have," she said, "I built by being useful to people I did not love and keeping my promises to them, and the promises were small ones, and I kept them because a woman in my position cannot afford to be unreliable. I did not promise you anything in March. I told you to stay out of the water and I gave you the accounts and then I gave you a knife, and you took it, and you have kept it, and you have not run, and you have not stolen, and you have not told anyone in this house what you saw in the lane. So I am going to tell you the truth now, and it is a worse truth than the one you had."

Dessa's chin came up. "Say it."

"When I walk into the praetor's court with the girl, you will be in that court, and the court will ask whether a slave saw anything, and I will say no. I will say that you were in the kitchen. I will perjure myself in front of a praetor of Rome, and if I am caught I will lose my house and my fortune and my name, and I will do it anyway, because the case is about the girl and not about the boy, and a witness who is a slave is not a witness, she is a piece of property to be questioned with the rod."

"They will find me."

"They will look for the girl in Africa and they will not find her, and they will look for you in my house and you will be in Ostia, in a warehouse, with a man who owes me and who will not ask questions. And you will stay there until the case is over, and then I will bring you back, and I will free you."

Dessa looked at her.

"You will not free me," she said. "You will say it now, and in the winter you will forget, because a woman who has lost her house does not have anything to give away."

"Then I will put it in writing," Antonia said, "and Tullia will witness it, and if I do not do it you may take the writing to the praetor, and he will look at it, and a written promise from a freedwoman to a slave is worth nothing in law. So it will be worth nothing, and I will give it to you anyway, and you may keep it, and when you are free you may keep it as a thing that was owed to you and paid."

Dessa looked at the paper that Antonia had not yet written, and then at the lamp, and then, briefly, at Antonia's face.

"Write it," she said.
