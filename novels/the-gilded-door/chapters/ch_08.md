The house of the Iunii stood on the Caelian with its back to the aqueduct and its face to a street that had been respectable for four hundred years and was now full of shops. It had a black threshold stone worn into a hollow by four centuries of feet, and a door with a bronze knocker in the shape of a bull's head, and a porter who had been in the family since he was a boy and who had not slept.

Antonia came at the second hour, in a plain mantle and no jewelry, with Tullia and Hilarus and a basket of white roses that had cost more than the man who carried them. She had been told not to come. She came anyway, because a woman who does not attend the funeral of a boy who died in her fountain has already made a public statement, and she preferred to make a different one in person.

The atrium of the Iunii was full of men. They stood in groups with their hands behind their backs, in the awkward silence of men who have come to be seen being sorry. The masks were out.

There were nineteen of them, in their cupboards along the wall, and the cabinet doors were open, and each mask had its own small bronze label with a name and a consulship. They were wax and wood and painted, and they had been painted so often that the faces had a shine to them, and the eyes were not carved but painted on, so that every dead Iunius looked out of the wall with the same fixed, courteous attention, as though he had just heard something mildly interesting and intended to mention it later.

And in the middle of the floor, on a bier, with lamps at the four corners, was the newest one, and it was not a mask. It was a boy's face, painted, with the mouth closed by a coin and the eyes closed by wax, and it looked nothing at all like Decimus and everything like every Iunius who had ever been born.

Antonia stood at the back with the basket of roses and looked at the nineteen masks and the one face, and thought about a family that keeps its dead in a cupboard and speaks to them through actors once a generation, and found that she understood it better than she wanted to.

Gallus came through the crowd. He was in the black of mourning, and his hair had gone white at one temple, which it had not been on the morning of the fountain. His wife was behind him, supported by two women, and she was making the sound women make at the front of a funeral, and it was a performance, and it was also true, and Antonia knew enough about both to know that the difference did not matter.

"You were told not to come," Gallus said.

"I was told not to come to the house. This is the atrium of the house, which is not the same as the house." Antonia held out the basket. "White roses. He would have thought they were a waste of money."

Something moved in Gallus's face. It might have been a smile.

"He would have said that the money would buy a good pair of boots," he said.

"Then he would have been right."

"He was almost always right about money and almost always wrong about everything else." Gallus took the basket and gave it to a slave without looking. "Come and see the masks. My wife does not want you here, and my wife's brother has told me that a woman who keeps a house of pleasure should not stand in a room with the death masks of the Iunii, and I have told him that the Iunii have had a great many dealings with a great many women, and that the masks have seen worse."

"That was unkind."

"It was true, which is worse." Gallus took her by the elbow and walked her along the wall. "This is my father. This is my grandfather. This is my great-uncle, who was consul twice and was caught taking money from the corn supply and paid a fine and was never spoken of again by anyone in the family until today. And this —" he stopped at a mask at the end of the row, a woman's face, small and narrow, with a mouth that had been painted and repainted until it was almost nothing — "this is my mother. The Iunii do not usually keep the women, but my father kept her, because she made him laugh."

"She looks kind."

"She was not kind. She was witty, and wit passes for kindness in a house where nobody listens." Gallus looked at the mask. "She used to say that a Roman family is a machine for turning dead men into furniture."

Antonia looked at the nineteen masks and thought of a cedar box with a lead token in it, and a girl in a garden with her head to one side.

"Lucius," she said. "I have to tell you something before the procession, and you will not like it, and you may put me out of the house after, and I will go."

Gallus did not turn. "Say it."

"Your son was in my house on the night of the Floralia, and he was drunk, and he struck a man in front of thirty guests, and he insulted me. None of that is new to you. But there is a thing that is new. He came back. The gate was open from the inside, and someone opened it, and he came back into my garden after he had been sent home, and he went to the fountain, and there is a cut on his forehead that is not the shape of the rim of that fountain, and his hands had no scrapes on them, which is not what a drowning man's hands look like." She said it quietly. "He was hit. Or he was pushed. Or he was held. I do not know yet which, and I am not going to pretend to you that I do."

Gallus turned and looked at her, and his face was the face of a man who has been holding up a wall and has just been told that there is a door in it.

"You told the aedile's man that he fell."

"I did."

"Why?"

"Because if I had told the aedile's man the truth on the morning after, he would have written down the word accident anyway, and taken my money, and told the Subura that the house on the Clivus had killed a consular's son and bought its way out. And because the man who did it would have known that I knew, and would have had a week before the funeral to do whatever he liked about it." Antonia held his eyes. "I gave you my word in the garden. I keep it now. There is one other person who knows, and she is a slave, and she will be sold to Africa in the register before anyone can question her, and she will not be questioned under the rod. That I will not permit. Not for your son, and not for mine."

"Your son?"

"I misspoke," Antonia said.

Gallus looked at her for a long moment. Then he turned back to the masks.

"The census is in three years," he said. "In the year 115 the emperor will hold a census, and the senate will be revised, and a man's rank can be taken from him at that revision if there is a scandal attached to his name. It has happened to better men than Verus. It has happened for less than a death in a brothel and a slave girl in a great house." He straightened the mask of his mother with one finger. "If you bring me the truth of what happened to my son, I will bring the Cassii down. Not in a court. In the senate. I have twenty years of friends there who owe me nothing and would enjoy a scandal in September. Verus will not go to Asia. His son will not hold even the decemvirate. The daughter's marriage will be broken, and the money will go elsewhere, and the house will be a name on a list of men who were once something."

"And the girl?"

"What girl?"

"The slave girl in the Cassian house."

Gallus looked at her. "You are asking me to save a slave."

"I am asking you to do what you are going to do anyway, and to do it in a way that does not crush a girl of eighteen who has done nothing."

"I do not care about a slave girl."

"You will," Antonia said. "Before the year is out, you will care about her a great deal, and it will cost you something to say so, and you will say it anyway, because you are a better man than you think and a worse one than your wife believes."

Gallus's mouth twisted. "That is the sort of thing my mother used to say."

"Then your mother was a sensible woman and you should have listened to her."

For a moment, in the middle of a funeral, in a room full of masks, Gallus laughed. It was a terrible laugh, with grief in it, and it stopped as suddenly as it had begun.

A slave came through the crowd with a folded tablet and put it in Antonia's hand, and went away before she could ask who had sent it. It was a woman's hand, small and careful, and it said: *I am not permitted to come. I have the tablet. It is under my mattress. He has moved her to the room next to the steward, and she is not allowed out of the house. Do not write to me again. The flower-seller is watched.*

Antonia put it in her sleeve.

"Go out to the street," Gallus said. "The procession starts at the third hour. Walk behind the family, with the women, and do not speak to anyone, and if my wife's brother says anything to you, tell him that I invited you and that he may take it up with me."

"I will tell him that you invited me," Antonia said, "and I will not mention you at all."

* * *

Before the procession there was the conclamatio.

It had been done the night before, when the body was brought home: the women of the household stood around the bier and called the boy's name three times, and then a fourth, and then they stopped, and the stopping was the point. Antonia had not been there. Gallus told her about it in the atrium, in a flat voice, in the middle of a sentence about the masks.

"My wife called his name," he said. "She called it four times. She has not spoken to me since."

They carried him out at the third hour. The bier was cedar, and there was a purple cloth over it that had been in the family for a hundred years and smelled of cedar and dust, and the designator walked in front with his staff calling out the route, and the flute players played the tune that is played at funerals and at nothing else, and the hired mourners walked with their hair unbound and their faces scratched, and they made the sound that hired mourners make, which is not a cry of grief but the shape of one, and it went down the Caelian like a wind through a chimney.

Behind them came the family, and behind the family the friends, and behind the friends the masks.

The procession went from the Caelian down the Vicus Tuscus into the Forum, and it took two hours, and it was the largest funeral in Rome that spring.

The crowd along the Vicus Tuscus was thick. A funeral was free entertainment, and a consular's funeral was the best kind, because there were masks and speeches and the possibility of a fight between the family's clients. There were women selling bread from baskets, and boys up on the shoulders of other boys, and a man with a monkey on a string who was doing a good trade until the flute players came past and the monkey bit him.

Antonia walked with the women of the household, at the back, with her veil down. The woman beside her was a cousin of Gallus's wife, and she talked for four streets about the price of the funeral and who had paid for it, and it took Antonia a while to understand that the woman was telling her, in the way that Roman women told each other things, that the Iunii were not as rich as they had been and that the funeral was being paid for by the wife's family under protest.

"Gallus paid for it," Antonia said.

"The house paid for it," the woman said. "There is a difference."

"There is always a difference. That is what makes a family interesting."

They came into the Forum at the fifth hour, and the procession stopped at the Rostra, and the crowd filled the square and the steps of the temples and the colonnades of the basilicas, and the masks were ranged behind the bier in their robes of office, and the oldest of them was a man who had been consul in the reign of Tiberius and whose face, painted and repainted, had become a kind of summary of every Roman who had ever held an office and been afraid of the next man.

Antonia looked at the masks and thought about the Cassian house, and the masks in the cupboard there, and the way a family could be a machine for turning people into furniture.

At the front of the crowd, near the Rostra, Verianus Cassius stood with his father. He was very pale, and he had his hand over his mouth, and twice Antonia saw him sway and twice saw his father's hand come out and take his elbow, without looking, without a word, the way a man steadies a table that has a short leg.

And once, in the middle of the eulogy, Verianus looked across the crowd and found Antonia's eyes, and held them, and his face was not the face of a murderer. It was the face of a boy who had done something in the dark and was waiting to be told what it was.



There were the flute players first, and the professional mourners, and the women of the household with their hair unbound, and behind them the bier with the boy's body, and behind the bier the family, and behind the family the masks.

The masks came on actors who had been hired for the day. Each actor wore the mask of a dead Iunius and the robe of the office that man had held, and each walked in order of the consulship, oldest first, and by the time they reached the Forum they were forty men deep, and they walked in silence, and the crowd along the Vicus Tuscus went quiet as they passed, because a procession of dead consuls is the one thing in Rome that can make a crowd go quiet.

Antonia walked with the women, at the back, with her veil down.

She did not watch the masks. She watched the living men who had come to be seen.

Verus was there, in the black of a family friend, with his son beside him and his wife behind. Verianus looked ill. He had not shaved, and there was a stain on his tunic that he had not noticed, and he kept his eyes on the ground in front of him with the concentration of a man who is afraid of what he will see if he looks up. Marcella stood behind her husband with her hands folded and her chin level, and she looked at Antonia once, across the heads of forty people, and her face was not hostile and was not kind. It was an accounting face. She was counting what this funeral would cost her house.

Publia was not there. A girl of sixteen did not walk in a funeral procession for a man she had never met; she stayed at home with her maid and her wedding clothes.

Nepos was there, of course. He was in the third rank of the family's friends, and he wore the correct expression, and when the bier passed he bowed his head with the air of a man who had known the deceased well, which he had not.

And at the Rostra, when the procession stopped, Gallus climbed the steps and spoke.

He spoke for a quarter of an hour. He spoke about his son's childhood, and his son's bad Latin, and his son's fondness for a particular kind of fig, and the way his son used to leave a lamp burning in his room because he was afraid of the dark. He said that the boy had been a fool and had been loved, and that the two things had not been in the right proportion, and that he was sorry for it. He said that he would have given his own consulship to have the boy back for an hour, and that he did not know what that said about him, except that he was a father before he was a senator, and that the discovery had come too late.

The crowd listened. Some of them wept. Most of them did not, and the ones who did not were the ones who would talk about it afterward.

Then they carried the body out through the Porta Ostiensis to the place where the pyres were, and the family said the words, and Gallus put the torch to it himself, and the smoke went up white and then black, and the nineteen actors who were wearing the masks of the dead Iunii stood in a line with their masks pushed back on their heads, and their own faces were young and sweating and bored, and that was the worst thing Antonia saw all day.

The fire took a long time. A body on a pyre does not burn the way a log burns; it goes slowly, and the cloth goes first, and then the hair, and then the shape of the thing changes, and there is a smell that Antonia had smelled twice before in her life and had never told anyone about.

Gallus stood at the front with his hands at his sides and watched the whole of it. He did not turn away. His wife had been led back to the litter before the torch was lit, and her women were around her, and the sound she made came out of the litter in small regular waves, like a woman in labor.

When the flames had taken the shape of the body, Gallus turned and walked back through the crowd toward the gate. People made way for him. Nobody spoke to him. He stopped once, by a stone, and put his hand on it, and stood for a moment with his head down, and then went on.

Antonia watched him go and thought about the nineteen masks, and the newest face in the cupboard, and the actor with the mask pushed back on his head, sweating, bored, and the fact that in twenty years a different actor would wear the boy's face at a different funeral and would not know whose face it was.

She stood at the back of the crowd with the smell of the pyre in her hair and thought: I have done this. Not the death; the other thing. I have stood in a garden and let a boy be humiliated in front of thirty people, and I could have stopped it earlier and I did not, because it was useful to let him show what he was. And then I sent him out into the night with a garland and a bleeding mouth and a bill for two thousand sesterces, and someone was waiting in the lane.

"Domina."

She turned. Nepos was at her elbow with his hands folded and his face arranged for a funeral.

"Oppius."

"A sad day. A very sad day. I came to pay my respects to Gallus, who is a good man, and to tell him that if there is anything I can do — anything at all — he has only to send to me."

"That is generous."

"It is what one does." Nepos looked toward the pyre. "I hear the aedile's office has closed the matter. An accident. A boy, a party, a fountain. I hear the house on the Clivus has closed for the month, and that the girls have gone to the country, and that the steward is selling the wine cellar." He turned. "And I hear that a slave girl was sold to a house in Africa on the Ides, and that the registration was made in the morning and stamped in the afternoon, and that the clerk who stamped it says the girl was never brought before him, which is a thing that happens, but not often, and not in the same week as a death."

Antonia looked at the pyre. The smoke was going up in a long black column, and the smell of it was in her mouth.

"You are very well informed for a man paying his respects."

"I am a friend of the house," Nepos said. "I am a friend of several houses. It is my only accomplishment, and I have worked hard at it, and I do not like to see a friend make a mistake." He turned to face her. "The book, domina. I will pay you three hundred thousand for it, and I will pay it today, in gold, in your hand, and I will ask you one question about the household of Cassius Verus, and you will answer it, and then this unhappy business will be over, and your house will open in June, and the wedding in September will be a wedding for other people."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I will be obliged to do what I am obliged to do," Nepos said. "I will tell the aedile's office that a slave girl who witnessed a death has been concealed by her mistress. I will tell the prefect's office that the house on the Clivus has been selling wine without a licence and harboring men of the senatorial order in a place of ill repute. Neither of those things is a crime that will hang you. Both of them together will close your door for a year, and a year is a long time in the Subura. Houses forget how to open."

Behind them the pyre cracked and settled. A woman began the ululation at the graveside, and the crowd took it up.

"I will think about it," Antonia said.

"That is what you said in March."

"In March I was lying. Now I am not." She turned to face him. "I will think about it until the Kalends of June. On the Kalends, I will send you my answer, and it will be a single word, and you will not need to read it twice."

Nepos inclined his head. "I have waited four months. I can wait a month."

"Then you will have my answer on the Kalends of June, and it will be the word no."

Nepos smiled. "You said you would think about it."

"I have thought about it," Antonia said. "While you were talking."

For the first time that day, Nepos's face did something unguarded. It was not anger and it was not fear. It was the look a man has when he has been counting on a thing and the thing turns out to be different from what he counted, and he discovers that he is no longer the cleverest person in the conversation.

"That is a mistake," he said.

"Probably." Antonia turned away. "But it is my mistake, and I have paid for it in advance, which is more than most men can say."

"You cannot," Antonia said. "You have never waited for anything in your life. That is why you will lose."

She walked away through the crowd, and her hands were shaking, and she did not let anyone see.

* * *

That evening, in the empty house, Tullia brought her the accounts and a cup of wine and stood by the table until Antonia looked up.

"Say it," Antonia said.

"You cannot beat him."

"I know."

"He has the prefect's office and the aedile's office and money and a name that is not his and a patron we cannot see. He has the register of the city and the ear of men who will believe him because he is well dressed." Tullia put the accounts down. "You have a book and a token and a girl in a cellar, and a consular who is mad with grief, and a tribune who is in debt to three money-lenders, and a house that is closed."

"I know all of that."

"Then what are you going to do?"

Antonia looked at the lamp. It had a bronze door on the handle, and she had bought it in the year she stopped being poor.

"I am going to do the thing he cannot imagine," she said. "I am going to tell the truth in a room full of men who have something to lose, and I am going to make them choose between the truth and the loss, one at a time, and I am going to start with the man who has the most to lose and the least time."

"Which man?"

"Manius Cassius Verus," Antonia said. "In his own house, in front of his own family, at his own daughter's wedding."

Tullia looked at her for a long moment.

"That is either the bravest thing I have ever heard," she said, "or the stupidest."

"Most days there is no difference." Antonia picked up the cup. "Send to the house of the Cassii in the morning. Not to Verus. To Acilia Marcella. Tell her that the woman from the Clivus asks for an hour of her time, in private, before the wedding, and that she will come to the back gate if she must."

"And the girl in the cellar?"

"The girl in the cellar is a problem I made this morning," Antonia said, "and I am going to have to live inside it."

She went down to the cellar with the cup in her hand. Dessa was awake, sitting on the stool with the cup of water she had not drunk, and she looked up when the door opened the way a dog looks up.

"You are not going to be sold," Antonia said.

The girl's face did not change. This was the worst thing about her, and about all of them: they had learned not to believe a sentence until the second half of it had arrived.

"You told the man from the aedile that I was sold."

"I did."

"Then in the register I am in Africa."

"You are in Africa," Antonia said, "in a house that does not exist, in a province where the man who owns it is a fiction I paid a clerk to invent this morning. Which means that for as long as the register says you are in Africa, no man with a warrant can take you out of this house and put you on a board and ask you questions with a rope. It means that you are, as far as the law of Rome is concerned, gone."

Dessa looked at her. "And if I walk in the street?"

"Then a man who knows you will see you and ask why a girl in Africa is buying bread in the Subura, and the whole thing will come apart." Antonia sat down on the other stool. "You will stay in this house until the wedding. Not in the cellar. In the room next to mine. You will keep the accounts with Tullia, and you will answer the door to nobody, and if the house is searched — and it may be searched — you will go through the passage to the fuller's, and the fuller will hide you in the vat room, and you will not come out until I send Hilarus with a red cloth."

"Why?"

"Because I need you alive," Antonia said. "Because you are the only person who saw the lane, and you are the only witness to what was done to a boy in my garden, and because I have already told one lie for you and I am not going to make it a waste."

Dessa looked at the floor. "The master in the house where I was before," she said, "he used to say that a slave who is useful is a slave who lives. And then he sold me anyway."

"Then he was a fool as well as a master." Antonia stood. "Eat something. Sleep. In the morning Tullia will bring you the grain accounts, and you will find three errors in them, because I have put three errors in them, and if you find all three I will give you a knife and teach you how to use it."

The girl's head came up. "A knife?"

"A small one. For the passage, and the vat room, and the dark." Antonia stopped at the door. "And Dessa. The mark on your wrist. The crescent. Was it there when you were taken?"

"It has always been there."

"Then it is yours," Antonia said, "and nobody can sell it."
