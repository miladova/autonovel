They came at the fourth hour of the morning, which was the hour the vigiles came and the hour the prefect's men came and the hour any man came who wanted to find a house asleep.

Antonia was awake. She had been awake most of the night with the speech in her head, and she was sitting at the table with the lamp and the book and a cup of cold water, and she heard the boots in the lane before she heard the knock.

There were eleven of them, and they had a magistrate's clerk, and the clerk had a warrant with the seal of the urban prefect, and the warrant said that the premises of the fuller's yard in the fourteenth region were to be searched for a book of accounts belonging to one Antonia Cassia, freedwoman, and for any person concealed therein contrary to the register of the city.

"Crescens," Antonia said.

"He is at the station," the clerk said. "The warrant is from the urban prefect's office and does not require the watch. Stand aside, domina."

She stood aside. There was nothing else to do; there were eleven of them and one of her, and Hilarus was at his sister's in the Aventine, and the fuller had gone to his mother's at Fidenae, and the yard had four women in it and a cat.

They found the book in three minutes. It was on the table, under her hand, in the lamplight, because she had been reading it.

The clerk took it and put it in a bag and wrote on the bag with a pen.

"That is a private account book," Antonia said.

"It is a book of accounts," the clerk said. "Whether it is private is a question for the prefect."

"It contains the names of four hundred men and women of this city, and the amounts they have paid, and the reasons, and if it is read by men in an office and copied, then every one of those people will know that I have been raided, and they will not blame the men who raided me. They will blame me."

"Yes," the clerk said. "That is generally how it works."

They took the women next. Rufina, and Cela, and the two youngest, and Meroe, and Beryllos, who had come back from his sister's in October and had been sleeping in the kitchen. They were taken out into the lane in their night clothes and put in a cart, and Cela was crying, and the youngest one, who was fifteen, was asking everyone whether she was going to be sold.

"Where are you taking them?" Antonia said.

"To the office, until the prefect has looked at the register."

"They are not registered to this yard, they are registered to my house on the Clivus, and the house on the Clivus is burnt and the register was corrected in October by a clerk in the fourteenth region whose name is in the record."

The clerk looked at her for a moment.

"You are very well informed," he said.

"I have been in this city for thirty-nine years and I have paid for every piece of information I have."

"Then you will understand," the clerk said, "that a warrant from the urban prefect's office does not have to be right. It has to be signed."

They took Tullia last. She came out of the shed with her cloak over her arm and her tablets in her hand, and she stopped in front of Antonia.

"The book," she said.

"Gone."

"Then they have won."

"They have the book," Antonia said. "They have not won. There is a difference, and it is the only thing I have ever been good at."

Tullia looked at her.

"Tomorrow," she said.

"Tomorrow," Antonia said.

"Domina, if they keep us at that office for a week, you will have no one to write anything down."

"Then I will have to remember it," Antonia said. "I have a good memory. It is the only part of me that has never been for sale."

They put Tullia in the cart, and the cart went down the lane, and the lane was full of people by then, standing in their doorways with their arms folded, watching.

The clerk was the last to go. He stopped at the door of the shed with the bag under his arm.

"There is a message," he said. "I was told to give it to you if you were civil."

"I have been extremely civil."

"You have." The clerk cleared his throat. "The message is: the woman may have the girl out of the cells on the morning of the senate, if she will come herself and sign a statement that the girl was never in the house on the night of the Floralia, and that the death of Decimus Iunius Gallus was an accident."

Antonia stood in the doorway of the shed.

"And if I do not?"

"Then the girl is questioned this afternoon," the clerk said, "and the man who questions her has been at it for thirty years, and he does not stop for the evening."

"Tell them no."

The clerk looked at her.

"Domina," he said, "I am a clerk. I have been a clerk for twenty-six years. I have carried a great many messages and I have seen a great many people say no, and I have noticed that the ones who say no in the doorway are always the ones who have not thought about the afternoon."

"I have thought about the afternoon," Antonia said. "I have thought about nothing else since July. Tell them no."

* * *

She went to the office at the ninth hour and they would not let her in.

She stood at the door with her hands at her sides while the porter told her that the office was closed to visitors, and that the girl was under an order, and that the order was being executed, and that the prefect's office was not in the habit of receiving women at the door.

"Then I will stand here," she said.

"You cannot stand here."

"I can stand anywhere in Rome. It is a public street."

She stood there for four hours. Men came and went past her, and some of them looked at her and some of them did not, and at the second hour a young man in a good cloak came out and said that the prefect's office would be grateful if she would go home, and at the third hour an older man came out and said that the prefect's office would be obliged if she would go home, and at the fourth hour a man in the uniform of the watch came out and stood beside her.

"You are relieved," he said. "The tribune has sent me. He says he cannot come himself, and he says you are to go to the house of the lady Marcella, and he says the lady Marcella has been expecting you since the fourth hour and has been making a great deal of noise about it."

Antonia turned her head.

"Tell him," she said, "that I will come when it is dark."

"Domina."

"Tell him that."

The watchman went away. Antonia stood in the street until the light went off the top of the wall. At the sixth hour the door of the office opened and two men came out carrying a stretcher with a cloth over it, and they put it in a cart, and the cart went down the hill.

She followed it.

* * *

The house of the prefect's physician was in the Via Nova, and it had a lamp over the door and a boy on the step, and the physician was a Greek of sixty called Sostratus who had been in Rome for thirty years and had never once refused a patient who was brought to him at night.

They would not let her in. She waited in the street, and after an hour Sostratus came out himself, with his hands washed, and stood in the doorway.

"You are the woman from the Clivus."

"I am the owner of the girl."

"The girl is not owned," Sostratus said. "The girl is a slave of the prefect's office, and I have treated her under an order, and I have been paid for it, and I have nothing to say to you."

"Her hand."

Sostratus was silent.

"Her right hand," Antonia said. "They put the cords on her wrists and her ankles, and they lift, and the shoulders come out, and the wrists swell, and if they leave the cords on too long in the same place, the hand does not come back. You have been in this city thirty years. You know the injury. You know it takes four hours to happen and four months to heal and that sometimes it does not heal at all."

The physician looked at her.

"It is her right hand," he said.

"And?"

"And it is not healing," Sostratus said. "The tendons are damaged at the wrist. She will have the use of the thumb and the first two fingers, and the little finger and the ring finger will not close, and she will not be able to grip anything heavier than a cup. In a year it will be a hand that looks like a hand and does not work like one." He wiped his own hands on his apron. "I have splinted it and I have given her something for the pain and I have told the office that she is not to be questioned again for a month. They will question her again in a month. They always do."

"Can I see her?"

"No."

"I am asking you to let me see her."

"You are asking me to break an order of the prefect's office for a woman I have never met, in a city where a physician who breaks an order of the prefect's office does not practice medicine again." Sostratus looked at her for a long moment. "Come back at the tenth hour. The boy goes home at the tenth hour and the man who sits with her is a fool who sleeps. Do not come to the front. There is a door in the alley at the back, and it is the door they use for the bodies, and if anyone asks, I will say that I know nothing and that you are a thief."

Antonia looked at him.

"Why?" she said.

"Because I am sixty years old and I have been in this city for thirty years," Sostratus said, "and every year I have said that I would not do this again, and every year there is one girl, and this year it is this one." He went inside and shut the door.

* * *

The room was small and had a bed and a stool and a lamp and a window with bars.

Dessa was lying on the bed with her right arm strapped to a board and her eyes open. She had lost weight; her cheekbones were sharp, and there was a bruise on her jaw that had gone yellow at the edges, and her hair was matted on one side.

"Domina," she said.

Antonia sat down on the stool.

"I am here," she said.

"You did not come."

"I did not know where you were until the end of August, and by then you were in the cells and no one would tell me which cells, and I have been to the prefect's office four times and to the station twice and to the house of a man in the Via Nova who would not open the door."

"The man with the burn."

"Felix. Yes."

Dessa looked at the ceiling.

"He told me you had sold me," she said. "In the cell in Ostia. He told me there was a bill of sale with a seal on it, and that you had sold me to a procurator for four thousand sesterces, and that I had been put on a cart to Ostia thinking I was being hidden."

Antonia sat very still.

"And did you believe him?"

"For three days," Dessa said. "And then I remembered that you had told me the truth about the arithmetic in the small room in May, and that a woman who tells the truth about the arithmetic does not sell a girl in the night. And I told him no." She turned her head. "I told him no four times, and the fourth time he laughed and said that I was a clever girl and that clever girls were the worst kind of witness, and then he went away and two men came and put me in a wagon and brought me to Rome."

Antonia's hands were shaking.

"What did they ask you?"

"About the garden. About the man in the lane. About the ring. About what you said to me afterward, and whether you told me to say that I had been sold to Africa, and whether you paid the aedile's clerk." Dessa's voice was flat. "I said that I was in the kitchen all night and saw nothing. I said it for two days. And then a man came with a rope and they put the cords on my wrists and my ankles and they lifted me off the floor by my wrists, and I said it again, and they lifted me higher, and after a while I said that I had seen the man in the lane and that he had a ring, and then they put me down and gave me water and asked me again, and I said it all again, and then they asked me about you."

"And what did you say?"

"I said that you were a hard woman and a liar and that you had sold me." Dessa looked at her. "I said it because it was what they wanted and because I was on the floor and because my arms had come out of the sockets, and I have thought about it every day since, and I am not sorry, and I am telling you so that you will know before you go into that senate house that they already have a statement from me, in my voice, signed with my mark, that says you are worse than they are."

The lamp burned.

"Good," Antonia said.

Dessa stared at her.

"Good?"

"Good. Because tomorrow I am going to stand up in the senate house and say that a slave of my household was taken from Ostia with a forged bill of sale and a genuine seal, and questioned for four days without a magistrate's order, and that she has a hand that will not close for the rest of her life. And the men who are going to want to know why I am saying it will produce your statement, and I will say: she said it on the floor, after four days, with her arms out of their sockets, and a statement taken that way is not a statement, it is a thing that was taken from her, the way the hand was taken." Antonia leaned forward. "You have given me the best thing in the case, and you did not know it, and you did it by telling me the truth about what you said. That is what I have been trying to tell you since May. The truth is not the thing that is convenient. It is the thing that is left when everything else has been taken and you are still alive and can say what happened."

Dessa looked at her for a long time.

"I want to say my name," she said.

"In the senate?"

"In the senate. Like the girl did in the basilica. I heard about it in the cell; the man who sits with me told me. He said a slave girl stood up in the basilica and gave her name and the whole hall went quiet." Dessa's chin came up. "I want to do that. I am a slave of your household. If I stand up in that room and say my name, they cannot say afterward that I was a thing in a cell. They will have to write it down."

Antonia looked at her.

"Then I will bring you," she said, "and I will bring your splint, and I will bring the physician, and I will stand beside you while you say it."

"They will not let a slave into the senate house."

"They will let a slave into the senate house if a consular of Rome stands up and says that the slave is a witness in a matter before the court and the court has asked for her, and if the clerk of the house writes it in the book, and if the empress has spoken to the consul." Antonia smiled. "I have been busy for a month. You have been in a cell. We have both been doing our work."

Crescens was waiting for her in the lane outside the fuller's yard when she got back, sitting on the step with his helmet beside him.

"I am suspended," he said.

"I know."

"You do not know. You know that a warrant came from the urban prefect's office and that I was not in it. What you do not know is that the prefect's office wrote to my prefect this morning and asked why a tribune of the seventh cohort had been seen four times at the house of a woman under inquiry, and my prefect — who is a decent man and a lazy one — has suspended me pending an explanation." Crescens picked up his helmet and looked at it. "I have until the Kalends to write the explanation. I have written four. They are all the same. They all say that I did what I did because it was the right thing to do, and none of them say anything that a prefect can act on."

"What will you do?"

"I am going to write the fifth one," Crescens said, "and in the fifth one I am going to say that I have kept a written record of the condition of a body found in a fountain in April, and that I did not file it, and that I am prepared to produce it before a magistrate. And I am going to send it to the prefect, and to the prefect's clerk, and to the man who signs for the aediles. And then I am going to sit in this lane and wait to be dismissed."

Antonia sat down on the step beside him.

"Give me the report," she said.

"It is a report of a tribune of the watch. It cannot be used in a senate house."

"Give me the report," Antonia said, "and I will read it out, and a tribune's report read out in the senate house is not evidence, it is a fact that three hundred men have heard, and a fact that three hundred men have heard is a fact that cannot be put back in a chest."

Crescens looked at her.

"If I give it to you," he said, "I am finished. There is no explanation I can write after that. The prefect will dismiss me and the cohort will be told that I was dismissed for a woman, and I will be thirty-four years old with a helmet and a debt and no post."

"Yes."

"And if I do not give it to you, you will go into that room with a letter from a dead man and a hand that will not close, and the whole of it will rest on the word of a freedwoman and a slave."

"Yes."

Crescens sat on the step with his helmet in his hands.

"I have been in the watch seven years," he said. "I have pulled forty-one people out of burning buildings. I have watched eleven of them die. I have been thanked twice. And I have spent the whole of this year discovering that the thing I am actually good at is not putting out fires. It is noticing." He held out the helmet. "There is a folded tablet in the lining. Take it."

Antonia took the helmet and turned it over, and there was a slit in the leather lining, and inside it a folded tablet wrapped in oilcloth.

"There is one thing," Crescens said. "When you have read it out, do not say my name. Say *a tribune of the seventh cohort*. There are six tribunes in the seventh cohort and the prefect will not be able to dismiss all of them without explaining why."

Antonia looked at him.

"You would let another man take the blame?"

"No," Crescens said. "I would let the prefect discover that the blame is a thing that cannot be assigned to a name, because the name is not in the record. It is the only trick I know that works in that office." He stood up and put on his helmet. "And domina. When it is over, and I am a man with a helmet and a debt and no post, I am going to come and sit in that shed again, and you are going to be there, and there will be a cat."

"I will be there," Antonia said.

* * *

She came out of the alley at the back of the physician's house at the eleventh hour and there was a man waiting for her at the corner of the Via Nova.

He was young and well dressed and he had been crying, and his hands were shaking, and he was holding a letter.

"Domina," Verianus said.

She stopped.

"My father is dead," he said. "He is dead in his bath, and there is a letter, and I have read it, and I have brought it to you, because there is no one else in Rome who will know what to do with it."

Antonia took the letter.

They stood in the Via Nova under the lamp with the shops shut and the whole city asleep, and she read it twice.

*To the magistrates of Rome. I, Manius Cassius Verus, consular, being of sound mind and about to do what my father should have done in the year 93, make this statement. In the April of this year, on the night of the Floralia, my son Manius Cassius Verus the Younger followed Decimus Iunius Gallus from the house on the Clivus Suburanus and struck him once in the garden of that house, and the boy fell and struck his head on the rim of the fountain and did not rise. My son came to me at the fifth hour and told me. I did not send for the watch. I did not tell the father of the dead boy. I paid a man in the aedile's office and I closed the file and I told my son that it was finished. It was not finished. It has never been finished.*

*The girl in my house, called Myrtale, is the daughter of Antonia Cassia, freedwoman, and of me, and was born free, and I have held her for eighteen years. The note in my hand in the tablinum says no more to be said. I say it now.*

*I have no other statement to make. I have held a lamp, and I have held my tongue, and I have held a girl in a house, and I have not held anything that was worth holding.*

Antonia folded the letter.

Verianus stood in the street with his hands at his sides.

"He has written that I killed him," he said. "He has written it down and he has signed it and he has left it on the table in the tablinum, and my mother found him and she read it before I did, and she is sitting on the floor of the atrium with the tablet in her lap, and she has not spoken."

"Verianus."

"He was going to Asia," Verianus said. "He was going to go to Asia and be a proconsul and be dull at dinner, and I was going to be a man who had killed someone and been forgiven. And now he has written it down and he has stopped, and I do not know whether he did it for the girl or for himself or to punish me, and I will never know, and that is the worst of it. That is the worst of it, domina. Not the death. The not knowing."

The house of Acilia Marcella was lit like a house with a death in it.

There were lamps in every room and the door was open and a slave was standing in the lane to tell people that the master was dead and that the household was in mourning. The atrium was full of women. Marcella was not among them. She was in the tablinum, sitting on the floor with her back against the desk and the letter in her lap, and she had been there for two hours.

"You have read it," Antonia said.

"Four times."

"Give it to me."

Marcella held it out without looking up.

"I have been sitting here," she said, "trying to decide whether he wrote it for the girl or for himself or to punish my stepson. And I have decided that it does not matter, and that it is the only thing he has ever written that was worth reading, and that I am going to have it copied twice before the light comes so that if it is lost there is another one."

Antonia sat down on the floor beside her. The marble was cold.

"Marcella."

"Do not be kind to me. I have been a wall for nine years and a wall does not require kindness." Marcella's voice was steady and her hands were not. "Do you know what I have been thinking, sitting here? I have been thinking that I have spent nine years managing the money and the household and the marriage and the slaves, and I have been proud of it, and I have told myself that I was the one who kept the house standing. And he has been dead for two hours and the house is standing, and the money is in the strongbox, and the slaves are in the kitchen making bread for the morning, and none of it needed me at all. The house was always going to stand. It was only the people in it that were falling down."

Antonia said nothing.

"He called me by my name," Marcella said. "Twice. Once when he came back from the prefect's office in October, and once on the night he wrote this. He said, *Acilia*, and then he said, *I have made a mess of it*, and I said, *yes*, and he laughed." She looked at the letter. "Nine years, and the only two conversations I ever had with my husband were in the last two months, and they were about the mess he had made. I do not know whether to be grateful or furious. I am both. I am going to be both for the rest of my life."

Verianus was standing in the door of the tablinum with his hands at his sides.

"Mother," he said.

Marcella looked up.

"Do not call me that in this room," she said. "You have not called me that in seven years and I have preferred it. Call me Acilia, and come and sit on the floor with us, and bring the lamp, and we will decide what to do with your father's letter before it is light."

He came and sat. Marcella took the lamp from him and set it on the floor between the three of them, and the light came up and made three faces out of the dark.

"There is one thing I want," Marcella said, "and then I will do whatever the two of you decide, and I will not say another word about it."

"Name it," Antonia said.

"When you read that letter tomorrow," Marcella said, "read the part about the girl first. Not the part about the death. Not the part about the aedile. The part where he says she was born free and he has held her for eighteen years and that he says it now. Read that first, in his words, before anybody has said anything else. Because that is the only part of it that was not about him."

Antonia looked at her.

"I will read it first," she said.

"Then take the letter," Marcella said, "and take my stepson, and go and get some sleep, because it is three hours before light and you look like a woman who has been standing in a street since the ninth hour."

Antonia got up.

"Marcella," she said. "The book is gone. The prefect's men took it from the shed this morning, with the women. They will read it, and they will copy it, and they will send word to every man in this city whose name is in it that the woman on the Clivus has been raided and that it was her fault."

Marcella looked up at her.

"Then they will have told four hundred people that the woman on the Clivus is dangerous and that her book was worth taking," she said. "I have been in this city for thirty-three years. There is no better advertisement."

* * *

Antonia looked at the young man in the lamplight.

"Come with me," she said.

"Where?"

"To the house of the lady Marcella, where there is a fire and a chair and a woman who has been waiting for me since the fourth hour, and where we are going to sit down, the three of us, and decide what to do with this letter before it is light."

"You are going to use it."

"Yes," Antonia said. "In the senate house, tomorrow, at the second hour. And you are going to stand beside me while I read it, and you are going to say, in front of three hundred men, that it is your father's hand and your father's seal, and that it is true."

Verianus looked at her.

"I will be finished," he said.

"Yes."

"I will be a man who killed somebody and was named in the senate house."

"Yes," Antonia said. "And you will be a man who told the truth about it in a room full of men, which is more than your father ever was, and more than most of the men in that room will ever be." She took his arm. "Come. It is cold, and we have four hours, and I have not slept in two days, and if I am going to destroy a family tomorrow I would like to do it sitting down."
