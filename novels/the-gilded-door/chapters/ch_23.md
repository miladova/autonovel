The senate house of Rome was a room four hundred years old, and it smelled of paper.

That was the thing nobody told you. People said the curia and they thought of marble and purple and the masks of the ancestors, and what it actually smelled of was paper: the dry, dusty, faintly sweet smell of a great many documents kept in a great many cupboards for a great many years, with the shutters closed.

Antonia came up the steps at the second hour with Gallus on her right and Tullia behind her, and at the door the clerk with the tablet looked at her the way a clerk looks at a woman who is not supposed to be there.

"Name."

"Antonia Cassia, freedwoman of the household of Cassia Severa, of the fourteenth region."

"Matter?"

"A statement of fact in a question of status, before the praetor urbanus, on the Nones of December. The house has been asked to hear it by the consul, at the request of Lucius Iunius Gallus, consular."

The clerk wrote it down.

"Wait in the portico," he said. "You will be called."

* * *

She waited for an hour in the portico, and it was the longest hour of her life.

The portico was full of men. They came past her in twos and threes, in their white woollen togas with the narrow or the broad stripe, and they looked at her and then away, and some of them looked twice. She heard her own name three times, and each time it was followed by something that was not quite a whisper.

At the third hour Gallus came out.

"The consul will call you after the reading of the letters," he said. "You will come in at the far door and you will stand there, and you will not move until he tells you. There are three hundred and eleven men in the room, and there are forty more in the gallery, and one of them is a freedman of the ab epistulis who writes everything down. Speak to the consul. Look at him. If you look at the room you will lose the thread and you will not get it back."

"Lucius."

"What?"

"I am frightened."

Gallus looked at her.

"So am I," he said. "I have been a senator for twenty-two years and I have never once spoken in that room without being frightened, and the men who are not frightened are the men who have never said anything worth saying." He took her arm. "You are not going in there as a petitioner. You are going in as a witness in a case of status, and the law of Rome has put you in the room, and every man in there knows it. They can shout. They cannot put you out."

Oppius Nepos found her in the portico at the third hour, ten paces from the door of the house, with her hands folded and her heart going.

He was in the broad stripe, which was not his to wear, and he had been in the portico all morning, and he came up to her the way a man comes up to a woman at a dinner: unhurried, with the confidence of a man who has arranged the seating.

"They will not let you speak," he said.

"They will."

"The consul has agreed to it because Gallus asked him and because the empress spoke to him. The consul has agreed to a statement of fact, of a quarter of an hour, in a matter of status. He has not agreed to an accusation of a man who cannot answer." Nepos looked out at the Forum. "I am not here to threaten you. I have been threatening you since March and it has not worked, and a man who threatens a woman for nine months and is not obeyed has lost the argument and should say so."

"Why are you here?"

"To make you a last offer, and to make it before you go in, because after you go in it will be too late for both of us." He turned. "The office will advance your petition. The rescript will be sealed before the emperor leaves. The house on the Clivus will be rebuilt with money from a fund that has no name on it. The girl will be freed in the praetor's court on the Nones with nobody appearing against her. And the girl Dessa will be out of the cells before the evening and the hand will be looked at by the best physician in Rome." He paused. "In return, you will say your statement and you will not say the word *seal*, and you will not say the name of any freedman in the prefect's office, and you will not say mine."

Antonia looked at him.

"That is a great deal of money and a great deal of convenience," she said, "for a word and three names."

"It is."

"Why are you offering it now, when you could have offered it in March?"

"Because in March you had a house and a book and a case and I did not think you would get as far as the portico," Nepos said. "And because I find, standing here at the door of a room I have been buying my way into for twenty years, that I do not want to be a man who has spent his life on the outside of it with a gold ring on his finger." He looked at her. "I want to be a prefect of the grain. It is not a great ambition. It is a modest one. My father was a slave and I have a house on the Quirinal and a library of four hundred scrolls and not one friend in this city, and what I want, domina, at the end of it, is for a man in a street to say *that is the prefect* and for it to be true."

Antonia stood in the portico of the senate house of Rome.

"No," she said.

Nepos's face did not change.

"Why not? You have been offered the thing you came here for."

"Because a woman who takes that offer will be a woman who sold a girl's hand for a rescript," Antonia said. "And because I have been in this city for thirty-nine years and I have learned that a bargain made at the door of a room is not a bargain, it is a leash. And because you are not the first man to offer me a house and a name, and the last one held a lamp."

Nepos looked at her for a long moment.

Then he did something she had never seen him do. He laughed, and it was not the good laugh, and it was not the false one either. It was the laugh of a man who has been beaten at a game he invented.

"You are the most expensive woman in Rome," he said, "and I have a great deal of money, and I have never once been able to buy you. It is the only thing in my life that I am proud of and it has cost me everything."

He went away down the portico, and the crowd took him, and Antonia stood with her hands folded and the door in front of her.

* * *

The doors opened at the fourth hour.

The room was longer than she had imagined and lower than she had imagined, and the light came from the doors at the far end and from the high windows, and the men were on their benches on both sides in their white and their purple, and the consuls were on the raised bench at the end with the curule chairs and the lictors behind them.

The consul was a thin man of fifty called Cornelius Priscus, and he had a face like a folded document.

"The house will hear," he said, "a statement of fact from the freedwoman Antonia Cassia, in the matter of a question of status to be heard before the praetor urbanus on the Nones of December. The statement is made at the request of a consular of this house, and the house will hear it in silence, and any man who interrupts will be asked to leave."

He looked at Gallus.

"The consular will vouch for the witness."

Gallus stood up.

"I vouch for her," he said. "She is a freedwoman of this city, of good standing in her own order, and she is the mother of the person whose status is in question, and she is the only person living who can tell this house what was done in a house on the Viminal in the year 93, because I cannot, and the man who did it cannot, because he is dead." He sat down.

A stir went through the benches.

"Dead?" someone said.

The consul's staff struck the bench once.

"The witness will speak," the consul said.

* * *

Antonia came out of the far door and stood in the middle of the floor of the senate house of Rome.

She had thought, on the way, that she would be unable to speak. She had prepared for it: the quarter of an hour, the ten seconds if it came, the not weeping, the voice. What she had not prepared for was the smell of the paper and the way the light came in and the fact that three hundred men in white wool look, from the middle of a floor, exactly like a wall.

"I am Antonia Cassia," she said, "and I was born a slave in the household of the Cassii, and I was manumitted in the consulship of Pompeius Collega and Priscinus, on the eleventh day before the Kalends of December, in the year 93."

Her voice was steady. She heard it going out into the room and she did not recognize it.

"In the September of the following year I bore a daughter in a room above a fuller's shop on the Aventine. She was born free, because I was free, and the law says that the child of a free mother is free, and there is no exception in the law and there never has been. At three months old she was taken from me by the man who had fathered her, who was a senator of this house, and she was sold into his own household under a false name, and she has been held there as a slave for eighteen years."

She took a breath.

"The house may say: this is a private matter. The house may say: it is a family matter, and a sad one, and the courts will decide it. I am not here to ask the house to decide it. The praetor will decide it on the Nones of December, and I have no doubt that he will decide it correctly, because the evidence is in the record and the man who holds her has admitted that he has no title."

She looked at the consul.

"I am here to tell the house what happened in the year 93, when I was nineteen years old and a slave in that household, and when a man of this house held a lamp while I was questioned for eleven hours about a paper that a friend of his father had left on a table."

The wall of white wool did not move.

"There was a man called Didius Probus," Antonia said, "and he was a friend of the household of Cassius Verus, and in September of the year 93 he came to the house at the eleventh hour with a folded paper. I was in the passage behind the kitchen. I was the ornatrix of the house, and I could read, and I spoke to him for a moment about the weather, and he said to me: *you are the young master's girl, and you should be careful, because the young master's father does not like to be surprised.*"

She paused.

"I told the young master what the man had said. Two days later the young master took me by the arm in the passage and asked me what I had heard, and I told him, and he told me to say that I had been in the kitchen and had seen nothing. I promised. I was nineteen and he was the son of the house and I had been in love with him for four months, and I promised."

A man on the benches said something, and another laughed, and the consul's staff struck the bench.

"The man Probus was found dead in his bath nine days later," Antonia said, "and the household said that he had drowned, and the city said what the city says. On the fourth day before the Kalends of October, at the fourth hour of the morning, I was taken from my room by four men and put on a board in a stone room at the back of the tablinum. The elder Cassius Verus asked the questions. A clerk wrote the answers. A man with a rope, who was not of the household, applied the question. And in the doorway, holding the lamp, stood his son, Manius Cassius Verus, who is a consular of this house and who was twenty-six years old."

Nobody laughed.

"It went on for eleven hours," Antonia said. "I answered the questions. Somewhere in the fifth hour I began to answer before they were asked. Somewhere in the eighth hour I named the young master, and the clerk wrote it down, and the old man said, *again*. And at the end of it he told me that I was alive because I had been clever, and that I would be freed because a freedwoman cannot be put to the question and a freedwoman's word in a court is worth nothing, and because a man who frees the witness has bought a silence that no one can accuse him of buying."

She lifted her left arm, slowly, to the height of her shoulder and no higher.

"This is what it did," she said. "It has been eighteen years. The arm does not go above the shoulder. I have not been able to raise my left arm above my shoulder since the fourth day before the Kalends of October in the year 93, and every day of my life, when I dress, and when I lift a cup, and when I lie down at night, I am told by my own body what this house did to me."

The room was entirely silent.

"I have not come to this house for sympathy," Antonia said. "I have come because of what was done afterward, and because it is still being done, and because the men who do it are sitting in this room."

* * *

She took the letter from her sleeve.

"Manius Cassius Verus is dead," she said. "He died in his bath on the third day before the Nones, and he left a statement, and his son has certified the hand and the seal, and I am going to read it to the house. The house will not wish to hear it. The house will wish very much not to hear it. I am going to read it anyway, because it is the only thing that man ever wrote that was worth reading."

She read it.

She read the part about the girl first, as Marcella had asked: *The girl in my house, called Myrtale, is the daughter of Antonia Cassia, freedwoman, and of me, and was born free, and I have held her for eighteen years. The note in my hand in the tablinum says no more to be said. I say it now.*

Then she read the part about the death: *In the April of this year, on the night of the Floralia, my son Manius Cassius Verus the Younger followed Decimus Iunius Gallus from the house on the Clivus Suburanus and struck him once in the garden of that house, and the boy fell and struck his head on the rim of the fountain and did not rise. My son came to me at the fifth hour and told me. I did not send for the watch. I did not tell the father of the dead boy. I paid a man in the aedile's office and I closed the file and I told my son that it was finished. It was not finished. It has never been finished.*

The benches were moving now. A man in the second row had put his hands over his face. Another was talking to the man beside him, urgently, and the man beside him was shaking his head.

And in the gallery, at the back, a young man in the broad stripe stood up.

"I am Manius Cassius Verus the Younger," he said, "and the letter is my father's hand and my father's seal, and I have signed it, and what it says is true."

The consul's staff struck the bench three times.

"There will be order," the consul said. "There will be order, or the house will be cleared."

Verianus sat down.

Gallus did not move. He was sitting very still on his bench with his hands on his knees and his face the color of the wall, and he did not look at Antonia and he did not look at Verianus, and Antonia thought: he knew, he has known since the summer, and now it has been said in a room with a clerk in it, and after this there is no version of the world in which his son is not a boy who was killed in a garden and lied about by two generations of a consular family.

She went on.

* * *

"There is one more thing," Antonia said, "and it is not about the Cassii."

The consul looked at her.

"The house has been patient," he said. "The house has been patient for a quarter of an hour. Say what you have come to say."

"In July of this year, a slave of my household named Dessa was taken from Ostia by four men with a bill of sale that was a forgery and a seal that was genuine. The seal was the seal of the office of the urban prefect. She was held in the cells of that office for five months, and four days ago she was questioned, and she signed a document with her left hand because her right hand no longer closes."

She turned and looked at the far door, and the physician brought Dessa in.

The girl came down the floor of the senate house of Rome with her arm strapped to a board and her chin up, and she stopped in the middle of the floor beside Antonia, and she turned and faced the benches.

Three hundred men looked at her.

"Dessa," Antonia said. "What is your name?"

"Dessa."

"Say what you have come to say."

Dessa looked at the consul.

"My name is Dessa and I am a slave of the household of Antonia Cassia," she said, in a voice that carried to the far end of the room. "I was in the garden on the night of the Floralia. I found the body and I turned it over by the shoulder and I screamed, and there was a man in the lane with a gold ring on his hand, and he went away down the hill. I was taken to Ostia in July and I was questioned for four days without an order of a magistrate, and I signed a document that I did not write and could not read, and my hand does not close." She paused. "That is what I have come to say."

The room did not applaud. It was not that kind of room. What it did was worse: it went quiet in a new way, a way that meant that every man in it was now thinking about his own household.

The consul waited until the silence had become uncomfortable, and then he said, "The witness may continue."

* * *

"The seal," Antonia said.

"The house knows what a seal is."

"The house does not know what this seal costs. There is a freedman in the prefect's office called Anicetus, who has been in that office eleven years, and who sells the use of the office's seal to men who will pay. There is a freedman called Felix, who carries messages and who has a burn on the back of his right hand, and who has a room in the Via Nova paid for by a landlord who is a client of a man called Oppius Nepos. And there is Oppius Nepos himself, who has been buying seals and men and documents for twenty years, and who would like the house to believe that his family has been in Puteoli for five generations, and whose father was a slave of the Oppii, and an auctioneer's clerk, and was manumitted in the year 72, and is buried in Puteoli with the word *libertus* on his tomb."

The benches erupted.

The consul was on his feet with his staff raised. "Order! The house will come to order!" He struck the bench four times, five, and the lictors moved forward, and the noise went down by degrees, and in the gallery the freedman of the ab epistulis went on writing.

"Those are accusations," the consul said, "of a grave nature, made in this house, by a woman, against men who are not here to answer. The house will hear the evidence."

"The evidence is in the guild's book," Antonia said, "and the guild's book is in a strongroom that three men must open, and one of the three is the keeper of the guild's records, a freedman called Epaphroditus, who is seventy years old, who has no teeth, and who is waiting in the portico of this house at this moment with a sealed copy in his hand and the name of the praetor who manumitted Gaius Oppius Philargyrus in the margin. And the second copy of the bill of sale that was used to take the girl Dessa from Ostia is in the hands of the urban prefect, who has had it since October, and who has been unable to act on it because the office that issued it is the office that would have to investigate it."

The consul looked at her.

"And the emperor's council?" he said.

Antonia looked at the benches. She found the man in the third row, on the right, in the purple stripe, sitting very still with his hands on his knees: a consular of sixty, with a good name and a bad memory, who had come to her house in the year 95 with a purse and a proposal.

"There is a man in this room," she said, "who came to me in the year 95, when I was two years out of a brothel and had nothing but a debt and a room, and who offered me four hundred thousand sesterces to leave Rome and never speak of the year 93 again. I took three hundred thousand of it, because I was twenty-two years old and hungry, and I have regretted it every day since, and I have his name in a book and I have his seal on a receipt and I have the copy of the payment in my strongbox."

She let the silence run.

"I am not going to say his name in this house," she said. "He is old, and he has a wife, and one of his sons is a friend of mine. But he is sitting in this room, and he knows it, and every man on those benches is now looking at his neighbour and wondering. That is the punishment I have chosen for him. It is a better one than the law would give me, and it will last longer."

The room was a wall of white wool with a hundred faces turning.

"I have said what I came to say," Antonia said. "The house may do with it what it likes. The praetor will hear the case on the Nones of December, and the girl will be freed or she will not, and the men who sold the seals will be prosecuted or they will not, and in ten years this will be a story that old men tell at dinner. But it has been said. It has been said in this room, in front of three hundred men and a clerk, and it has been written down, and no man in Rome can now say that he did not know."

The consul leaned forward.

"The witness has said that she was questioned in the year 93," he said. "The witness has asked this house to believe that she was questioned without cause. There was a charge, was there not? There was a matter of treason in that household, and a man died, and the elder Cassius Verus was never accused. Was the witness questioned in a matter of treason?"

"She was questioned in a matter of treason."

"And was a question of treason a matter for the prefect's court?"

"It was a matter for the emperor's court, and the emperor was Domitian."

"Then the house will observe that the questioning of a slave in a matter of treason was lawful."

Antonia looked at him.

"The questioning of a slave is lawful," she said. "The questioning of a slave is lawful and the torture of a slave is lawful and the sale of a freeborn child is a crime, and the law has never had any difficulty holding those three things at once." She paused. "I have spent eighteen years discovering what the law is. I know exactly what the law is. The law is a floor, and a man may stand on it, and a man may do a great many things standing on it, and the things he does are not the less wicked for being lawful."

The consul looked at her for a long moment.

"The house will observe," he said, "that the witness is a very unusual woman."

"I am a woman who kept accounts," Antonia said. "It is not a remarkable thing to be. It is remarkable only because the men who kept the accounts did not expect me to read them."

She turned to the consul.

"That is my statement," she said. "I am grateful to the house for hearing it."

* * *

The consul looked at her for a long moment.

"The house has heard it," he said. "The house will not debate it today. The matter of the status is before the praetor, and the matter of the seals is a matter for the urban prefect, and the matter of the guild's book is a matter for the guild, and the matter of a consular's death is a matter for his family and for the gods." He straightened his toga. "But the house will record that a statement was made in this room on this day, and the clerk will enter it in the book, and it will be read by the emperor's freedmen, and it will be preserved."

He looked at the benches.

"The house is adjourned until the Nones."

The room rose. Three hundred men began to talk at once, and the noise was like a wave coming up a beach, and Gallus was on his feet and moving toward the consul's bench, and the lictors were clearing a path, and in the gallery the clerk of the ab epistulis closed his tablets and looked down at Antonia with an expression she could not read.

Myrtale was standing in the gallery with Tullia.

She had stood up at some point during the speech, and she was still standing, and she was the only person in the gallery who was not moving.

She said something. Antonia could not hear it across the room. She read it off her daughter's mouth, and it took her a moment, and then she had it.

*Cassia.*

* * *

They came out of the senate house into the afternoon, and the steps were full of men, and the whole Forum was full of the news, which had gone out of the doors before they had.

Gallus was waiting at the bottom of the steps with his hat in his hand.

"Well," he said.

"Well."

"I have been in that room for twenty-two years and I have never heard anything like it, and I am not sure that I approve of it, and I am entirely certain that no one will ever forget it." He put his hat on. "The consul has asked me to tell you that the house will not move against the prefect's office while the emperor is in Rome, because the emperor is leaving and the office has to keep the grain moving, and that any prosecution will be a matter for the spring, and that he is sorry."

"I did not expect a prosecution."

"You did not?"

"I expected the record," Antonia said. "I have been in this city for thirty-nine years, and I have learned that a prosecution is a thing that a great man can survive, and a record is a thing that a great man's son has to live with."

Gallus looked at her.

"There is a thing I have to say to you," he said, "and I am going to say it badly, because I have a cold and because I have been holding it in since the middle of your statement. When you read that letter, and my son's name was in it, and the manner of his death —" He stopped. "I have wanted to know for eight months. I have wanted to know more than I have wanted anything in my life. And now I know, and it is exactly what I feared, and it has not helped at all. It has made it worse. Because now I know that my son was killed by a boy who was frightened, in a garden, over a joke about a girl, and that a family closed a file, and that my son is still dead, and that no amount of knowing will put him back."

"Lucius."

"I am not finished." Gallus took her arm. "I have been angry with you for eight months. I was angry with you in April for lying, and in August for telling me, and in October for making me choose. And today you stood in that room and read out the death of my son in front of three hundred men, and you did not once use it. You did not say *and this is why the house should listen to me*. You did not make it a part of your case. You put it in the middle and you let it stand there."

"It was not mine to use."

"No," Gallus said. "It was not. And that is the thing I have been trying to say." He let go of her arm. "I will stand in the praetor's court on the Nones and I will assert the freedom of your daughter, and I will do it as a consular of Rome and as the father of Decimus Iunius Gallus, and I will not say a word about my son, because there is a girl who needs to be free and a dead boy cannot be helped by it. And after the case is over, I am going to go home and I am going to be a man who has lost a son, and I am going to be that for the rest of my life, and you will come to dinner on the ninth day and we will not talk about it."

"All right," Antonia said.

"All right." Gallus blew his nose. "Now go home. You look like a woman who has been standing up in the senate house for a quarter of an hour, and there is a chair at the bottom of the steps with your name on it, and a physician, and a girl with a splint who is going to need a great deal of soup."

* * *

They went down the steps together, and the crowd at the bottom opened for them, and Antonia understood, as she came down into the light, that the whole Forum was looking at her.

Not the way a crowd looks at a woman of business. Not the way it looks at a famous courtesan or a rich freedwoman or a spectacle. It was looking at her the way it looked at a man who had come out of the senate house having done something, and she did not know what to do with her face.

"Domina," Myrtale said, at her elbow.

"Yes."

"Did it work?"

Antonia stopped at the bottom of the steps, in the middle of the Forum, with the whole city going past.

"I do not know," she said. "It is the Nones tomorrow, and there will be Caesennius Rufus and his instrument and a praetor who is a decent man and a lazy one, and a case is a case." She looked at her daughter. "But it is written down. Whatever happens in two days, it is written down, and it will be in the book of the senate of Rome when you are an old woman, and no one will be able to take it out."

Myrtale looked at the senate house.

"It is a very small building," she said.

"It is four hundred years old and it has a leaking roof and it smells of paper," Antonia said. "Most of the things that last are like that."
