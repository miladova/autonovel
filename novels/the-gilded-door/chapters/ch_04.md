The letter had said *come to me*, and she had not come. Three days later a second letter came, and it said nothing at all: it was a page of a poet, copied out in a careful hand, the one about the girl who waits at the door and cannot make herself knock. No signature. The cassia leaf in the wax.

Antonia read it twice and then sent Cerdon to the Viminal with a message that had taken her an hour to write and was four words long.

*The door is open.*

She spent the day making the house into a place where a consular could be received without either of them being able to pretend afterward that it had been an accident. The dining room was shut. The garden was swept. Beryllos was told to make nothing, and to be seen making nothing, which was a small insult delivered with great care. The girls were sent out: Kallisto to the house of a patron who had asked for her, Dessa to the market with Tullia and a list. Antonia put on a stola of dark blue, and a mantle the color of wine, and no jewelry at all, and looked at herself in the bronze for a long time.

At the ninth hour he came, on foot, with one slave and no litter, which was itself a statement: a consular who walks is a man making a point about the modesty of his fortune or the strength of his legs, and Verus had never in his life made a point about either.

While she waited, she did what she always did before a thing that frightened her: she walked the house.

The kitchen first, where Beryllos had put out a bowl of olives he had not been told to put out, in the hope of being noticed. "Take them away," Antonia said. "He is not to be fed." Then the passage, where the girls slept in three rooms with a lamp at the end and a bell on the door; then the bath, where the water had been changed and the bronze had been polished and the small crack in the mosaic by the step had been filled with white cement that did not match. Then the dining room, where the twelve couches stood empty in the last of the light and the painted Danaë held out her hands to a shower of coins.

In the year 93 this room had not existed. In the year 93 she had slept in a room above a fuller's shop with a straw mattress and a jug and a window the size of a hand, and she had woken every morning to the smell of the urine vats, and she had thought of the smell as the smell of being alive, because the alternative had been the smell of the other thing.

She stood in the doorway and made herself remember the Cassian house as it had been: the long fauces with the black threshold stone, the atrium with the masks in their cupboards, the garden with the old vine, the corridor behind the kitchen where the slaves slept, the lamp with the bronze door on its handle. She remembered the smell of the lamp oil, which was cheap and rancid, and the smell of the master's wool, which was not.

She remembered a rope. She did not let herself remember it for longer than it took to see it.

"Domina." Rufina was in the passage with the blue stola over her arm. "You are white."

"I am cold."

"It is the warmest day of the month."

"Then I am old." Antonia took the stola. "Rufina. If he raises his voice in this house, do not come. If he raises his hand, do not come. If he does anything at all that I do not answer, then come with the knife and stand between us and do not say a word."

Rufina's face did not change. She had been a slave in a house in Pompeii when the mountain went, and she had been sold four times since, and she had a scar on her forearm in the shape of a crescent, and she had never once in eleven years asked Antonia a question about her back.

"And if he does nothing?" she said.

"Then bring wine at the second hour, whether we want it or not, and say that the cook is asking about the fish."

* * *

At the ninth hour he came.

Cerdon brought him through the passage into the peristyle and left him there, as he had been told. Antonia came out under the colonnade and stopped at the edge of the light.

He had aged in a way that was almost an accusation. The jaw was still good, the shoulders still straight, but the skin under his eyes had gone thin, and there was grey in his close-cut hair, and he stood with one hand behind his back as though he were at a levy.

"Manius," she said.

He flinched. It was very small. She had not expected it to be so small, or to give her so much.

"Antonia."

"You may sit. The bench is clean. I had it cleaned."

He did not sit.

"Very well," she said. "Stand."

The fountain ran. Somewhere in the house a door closed. He looked around the garden, at the roses, at the painted wall, at the little bronze Priapus by the gate with his ridiculous load, and his face did the thing she remembered: it arranged itself. First the mouth, then the eyes, then the forehead, and by the time it had finished arranging there was a man standing there who had never been frightened of anything.

"You have done well for yourself," he said.

"I have."

"The house is —" he looked for the word, and found, as he always did, the word that cost the least — "comfortable."

"It is worth four hundred thousand. The garden is worth sixty. The girls are worth more than the garden and the house together, and the wine is worth more than the girls." She came out into the light. "You did not come here to value the property."

"No."

"Then say what you came to say."

He looked at her. Sixteen years, and something in the look had not changed, and she hated it for not changing.

"My daughter came to see you," he said.

"Yes."

"Twice."

"Yes."

"She is sixteen years old. She is betrothed to a man of equestrian family with a cousin on the council. She will be married in September, and the marriage will secure the future of my house, and her children will be Romans of two fortunes." He said it evenly. "And she came to you, at your back gate, twice, in the dark, with one slave, because you are the only person in Rome who would listen to her."

"I listened to her."

"You encouraged her."

"I told her to go home."

"You told her," Verus said, and his voice did not rise, "that her father was selling her."

"Her father is selling her."

"The word is marriage."

"The word is marriage when the woman has a choice and the man has a name. When the man is fifty-four and the woman is sixteen and the money goes to her father, the word is sale. You may call it a marriage in the senate house. In this garden we are among friends."

His jaw worked. "You have not changed."

"No. That is the tragedy of it." She sat on the bench and gestured at the other end of it. "Sit down, Manius. You are making my neck hurt."

He sat. He sat at the far end, with his hands on his knees, and for a moment they were two people on a bench in a garden in March, and the sixteen years were only weather.

"I did not come to quarrel," he said.

"You came to tell me to stay away from your house and your daughter and your wedding. Say it, and go, and we can both be finished before the wine."

"I came to ask you a question."

"Ask."

"What do you want?"

The fountain ran. Antonia looked at the roses.

"I want the rescript," she said. "I want a consular's endorsement. I want a man of your standing to carry my petition to the council and say that the woman named in it is known to him, and of good character, and deserving. That is what I want. It has been what I have wanted for eleven years, and you know it, because you know everything that happens in that office."

Verus's face did not move. "I will not do it."

"I know."

"Because you are not of good character."

"I know that also."

"Because a man who endorses the petition of a brothel-keeper —"

"There it is," Antonia said, and smiled, and the smile was a knife turned handle-out.

"— a woman who keeps a house of —" He stopped himself. He was a senator; he had been trained since boyhood to stop himself just before the word that would put him in the wrong. "A woman of your profession."

"Say it in Latin, Manius. You are a consular. You have a fine vocabulary. Say the word you have been using about me since the year 94."

He said nothing. The fountain ran.

"You cannot," she said. "That is the difference between us, and it has always been the difference. You know what I am, and you know it precisely, and you have known it in the dark. And you have spent sixteen years arranging your face so that no one could tell that you knew it." She stood. "I do not arrange my face. What I am is written on the door, and men come to the door, and they pay, and they are not the worse for it, and some of them are the better."

"You are not the worse for it," Verus said, and his voice had gone quiet. "That is what you tell yourself."

"It is what I know."

"You were not —" He stopped, and started again. "There was a girl in the house. In my mother's house. She had a room behind the kitchen, and she slept on a pallet, and she was clever, and she could read better than my sister. She used to sit with the steward's accounts and find the errors, and the steward hated her for it. She had a way of —" He looked at the fountain. "She had a way of putting her head to one side when she was thinking, like a bird. She was seventeen."

"I was nineteen."

"You were nineteen," he agreed. "And I was twenty-six, and I was going to be consul, and my father had told me since I was a child that the family was the only thing that mattered, and the family was not a thing you could see or touch. It was a name on a list. It was the order in which men stood at a sacrifice."

"It was a name on a list," Antonia said, "and I was a body on a pallet."

"Yes."

The word did something to the garden. Neither of them had expected him to say it.

"I have thought about that year," Verus said, "more than I have thought about any year of my life. I have thought about what I did and what I did not do, and I have made arguments to myself at night, and the arguments are good. They are the best arguments I have. And then I see a girl with her head to one side, and the arguments are worth nothing, and I am still the man who made them."

Antonia stood very still. She had imagined this speech for sixteen years, in perhaps forty versions, and in none of them had he said it in her garden, on a bench, with his hands on his knees, in the voice of a man reading a ledger.

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you asked me what I wanted."

"I asked you what you wanted. Not what you regret."

"What I want," Verus said, "is for the girl in my house to go on being in my house."

The garden went cold. Antonia heard her own blood.

"She is my daughter's maid," he said. "She has a room off the passage, and she sleeps there, and she is fed, and she is not beaten, and she has work that does not ruin her hands. She is twenty. In four years I will marry her to a freedman of my household, or I will set her free, and she will have a trade and a name and a place in the city. That is more than most women in Rome will ever have. That is more than her mother had."

Her mother.

Antonia said, "Say her name."

"I have not said the name in sixteen years."

"Say it."

"Prima," Verus said.

The word was not loud. It went through her like a nail.

"Do not," she said, "use that name in this house."

"It is your name."

"It was the name a man gave me when he bought my mother, and it means first, and I was not first, I was the fourth or fifth, and by the time I could read I knew what it meant." Her voice was steady, and she was proud of it. "I have been Antonia Cassia for eleven years. I bought the name with money. I earned it. It is in the records of the city, and if you say the other name again in my garden I will have Cerdon put you in the street, consular or not, and the whole Subura will see it."

Verus stood. For a moment he was close to her, close enough that she could smell the wool of his toga and the oil in his hair, and she did not step back, because stepping back was a thing the girl on the pallet had done.

"I know what you are doing," he said. "You are gathering a case. You have a girl who is frightened of her father, and a ledger of names, and a story about a token, and you think that one day you will stand in front of the praetor and say, that girl is free, and the praetor will look at the man who has been consul and say, oh, is she, and the whole city will fall down."

"I think no such thing."

"Then what do you think?"

"I think you have been very careful," Antonia said. "I think the purchase was made in the name of your steward, and the steward is dead. I think the girl was put with a nurse in the country for six years and brought into the house when the nurse died, and that the household believes she was bought from a dealer in the Subura, which is true. I think there is not one document in Rome that connects her to me, except the token around her neck, and a midwife at Ficulea who remembers a birth, and a woman in this house who kept the other half of the token, and a book I have kept for eighteen years." She looked at him. "And I think you know all of that, because you have been counting it, the way I have, every night, for eighteen years."

Verus's face had gone the color of tallow.

"If I wanted to destroy you," he said, "I could do it in a week. You are a freedwoman who keeps a house of pleasure. There are a hundred men in the senate who would sign a complaint against you and thank me for the pen."

"There are a hundred men in the senate who have been in this house, and their names are in a book, and the book is not in this house."

Something moved behind his eyes then, and it was not anger. Antonia saw it, and understood it, and was afraid of it.

"You have made a copy," he said.

"I have made nothing. I have kept a book. The book has been kept for eleven years, and it says who came, and when, and what they said they wanted, and what they paid, and what they were afraid of. It is not a weapon. It is a record. A weapon is a thing you use; a record is a thing that exists. Men have killed for less, and men have paid more to know that a record exists." She stepped back. "Go home, Manius. Tell your daughter to obey you. Tell your wife to be kind to the girl. And do not send any more men with burned hands to ask questions at my back gate, because the next one will be sent back to you in a cart."

Verus went to the door. At the edge of the colonnade he turned, and the lamplight was on his face, and for one moment he looked like the man in the garden of the house on the Viminal, twenty-six years old, with his whole life in front of him and a girl on a pallet behind the kitchen.

"What do you want?" he said again.

"Everything," Antonia said. "That is what I have always wanted. It is what you have always had."

He went out. Cerdon shut the door. Antonia stood in the garden for a long time, and then she went into the small room, and opened the cedar box, and took out the half-token, and looked at it, and put it down, and put her hands flat on the table.

* * *

The house of Lucius Iunius Gallus stood on the Caelian with a view of the aqueduct and a garden that had been laid out by his grandfather and had not been touched since. It had no peristyle, no bath, and one dining room, and the dining room had a leak in the corner that had been mended four times with red tiles, so that the ceiling looked like a page with corrections on it.

He was at dinner with two men and a boy of about ten, and he sent the men away when Antonia was announced, and the boy stayed because the boy was his grandson and had been allowed to stay up.

"You look like a woman who has been in an argument," Gallus said.

"I have been in a conversation."

"The difference being?"

"A conversation is what a woman calls an argument she has won." Antonia sat. "Who were the men?"

"A man from the corn office and a man who wants to be a man from the corn office. They came to ask me what I thought about the water supply, and I told them that the water supply is a matter for the aediles, and they told me that the aediles are a matter for the consul, and I told them that the consul is a matter for the emperor, and they went away satisfied, which shows how little they wanted."

"And what do you think about the water supply?"

"I think it is a great deal better than it was, and that the men who improved it are dead, and the men who will be praised for it are not born." Gallus poured her wine with his own hand, which was a thing he did for no one. "You did not come to talk about water."

"I came to ask a favor."

"Ah." He sat back. "Say it."

"I need a consular's endorsement for my petition."

Gallus looked at her for a long moment. The boy, at the end of the table, was eating a fig with great concentration, and pretending not to listen in the way children do, which is by listening with the whole body.

"Who has refused you?" Gallus said.

"Nobody has refused me. Nobody has been asked."

"That is not true. You have asked someone. You have the face."

"I have asked no one. I have been told that the office will not advance the petition without an endorsement, and that consulars are busy men, and that the ones who sign are the ones who are owed something or who owe something."

"And so you came to me."

"And so I came to you."

Gallus turned his cup. "You know what I am. I am a man who was consul for two months in the year 96, in a year when four men were consul and one of them was murdered, and I have not held a province, and I have not been on the council, and my name in the senate house is worth a good deal less than my father's was. I am a consular the way a man who has been to sea once is a sailor."

"You are a consular the way a man who has been to sea once is the only man in the room who knows which way the wind is blowing." Antonia set her cup down. "The endorsement does not need to open the door. It needs to be on the paper. The office will do the rest, or it will not, and if it does not I will have lost nothing but the fee."

"You will have lost something," Gallus said. "A petition endorsed by a consular and refused by the council is a petition that has been looked at and found wanting. The next time, the clerk will read the first page and not the second."

"I know."

"And if the reason it is refused is not the petition?"

"Then I will know that also."

Gallus was quiet for a while. The boy finished the fig and licked his fingers, and Gallus, without looking, put out his hand and the boy came and stood by his chair, and Gallus rested his hand on the boy's head.

"The proconsulship of Asia," Gallus said. "That is what the marriage is for, whatever the wedding songs say. Verus wants the province and the province costs money, and the bridegroom's cousin sits on the council, and the council draws up the list of the men who will govern. It is not a bribe. It is worse than a bribe. It is a favor done in the open, by men who will tell you afterward that they did nothing of the kind."

"Can he be stopped?"

"By whom? The emperor is in Dacia in his mind and in the east in his plans. The prefect is a lazy man. The senate will not stop a consular from going to a rich province, because every man in the senate hopes to go to a rich province himself." Gallus turned his cup. "There is a story that the emperor intends to send a man of his own to Asia, a legate with a special commission, to look at the cities' accounts. If that is true, the proconsulship of Asia will be worth a great deal less than Verus thinks, and the man who tells him so will not be thanked."

"Where did you hear it?"

"From a man who heard it from a clerk who heard it from a freedman of the empress." Gallus shrugged. "It may be nothing. It may be everything. In Rome, the difference between nothing and everything is how many men have heard it."

"Then I shall hear it again," Antonia said.

"Decimus has been in the company of young Verus," he said.

"I know. They were both at my table."

"The son is a fool and the father is a cold man, and I do not like the way the son looks at his father, or the way the father looks at nothing." Gallus's hand did not move on the boy's head. "There is a story in the city that the Cassii have a slave girl who is more than a slave. I have heard it twice, from two men who do not know each other. That is how a story becomes a fact in Rome: two men who do not know each other, saying it in the same week."

Antonia said nothing.

"If I sign your petition," Gallus said, "and it becomes known that I have signed it, there will be men who ask why. And the answer, whatever it is, will be attached to your name and to mine for as long as we are both alive."

"I know."

"And you are asking me anyway."

"Yes."

Gallus lifted his hand from the boy's head. "Go to bed, Gaius. Tell your mother I said so, and tell her I said it before the second cup, so that she cannot say I was drunk."

The boy went, and at the door he turned and looked at Antonia with an expression of enormous seriousness, and then he was gone.

"Bring me the petition," Gallus said. "I will read it. If it is honest and the witness is good, I will sign it and carry it myself. And if it is refused, I will want to know why, and I will ask, in the senate house, in front of men who will have to answer."

Antonia looked at him. She had come to ask a favor and had been given something else, and she was not sure yet whether it was a gift or a rope.

"Why?" she said.

"Because when I was thirty, a man I owed everything to asked me to do a small dishonesty, and I did it, and it cost another man his life, and I have been a decent man ever since to make up for it." Gallus refilled her cup. "It does not work, by the way. The decency. It does not make up for anything. But it passes the time, and it is better than the alternative."

* * *

Tullia found her there an hour later.

"The girls are back. Dessa bought the fish and got a better price than the list allowed, and she has put the difference in the house box without being asked, which is either the beginning of a very good steward or the beginning of a very careful thief."

"She is careful. She has been careful since she was fourteen. Careful is what a slave is instead of happy." Antonia looked up. "I want the Ostia accounts brought up. The grain venture, the warehouse, all of it. And I want a list of every ship's master we have paid in the last two years, and every manifest we have seen."

"You are going to Ostia."

"On Thursday."

"Thursday is the day of the wedding contract."

"The contract is in September."

"The contract is signed in April, in the house of the bridegroom, with the two families present. It is signed on Thursday."

Antonia looked at the wall for a moment. "Then I will be in Ostia on Thursday, and you will go to the signing in my place."

"Me."

"You. You are a freedwoman of this house and you are known to every steward in the city, and you will stand at the back with the slaves and the clients and you will watch Acilia Marcella's face when the dowry is named."

Tullia's mouth twitched. "And if the dowry is named at a sum that leaves the Cassii with nothing?"

"Then we will know what the wedding is worth," Antonia said, "and we will know what her father is willing to do to keep it."

Tullia went out with the tablets. Antonia sat alone in the small room with the lamp and the strongbox and the smell of the oil, and did the thing she did every night, which was to open the book and read one page of it at random and remember the man whose name was on it.

Tonight the page was from the fourth year. A praetor, two hundred thousand, for a favor about a will. A knight, thirty thousand, for an introduction. A man whose name she had written as a squiggle because he had paid in gold and asked her not to write him at all. A woman — there were eleven women in the book, and their names were written in a different hand, Tullia's hand, because Antonia had never trusted her own to write them — a woman who had come to the house at night and asked for a girl and had sat in the garden and wept for an hour and had paid nothing.

Antonia closed the book. In the passage, Cerdon was telling someone about the man with the burned hand, who had been at the corner again, and had stood in the rain for an hour, and had gone away when the watch came round.

She put the book back in the oiled cloth and locked the strongbox, and sat with her hand on the lid.

On Thursday she would go to Ostia, and she would find the man who had bought the child, or the man who had sold her, or the paper that said which. It had been eighteen years. It could wait four days.

It could not wait four days. She knew that, and she sat in the dark and knew it, and the lamp burned down, and the house breathed around her, and somewhere beyond the wall a woman was singing a song about a sailor who did not come home.

She had a superstition about that song. She had heard it first in the year 94, in a room above a fuller's shop, sung badly by a girl in the next building who had a baby and no husband and an opinion about everything, and she had heard it again in the brothel on the Aventine, from a woman who was dying of something the physician would not name, and she had heard it in this house on the night she took the lease.

"Go to bed," she said to the house, and the house did not answer, because it was a house and it had learned, in eleven years, to keep its opinions to itself.

She went to bed.
