The Floralia began with a goat and ended, most years, with a lawsuit. In between came six days in which the city did what it did at the end of April, which was to drink, to dance, to strip, to throw beans into the crowd, and to behave as though the gods had gone to the country and left the door unlocked.

Antonia did not like the Floralia. She liked the money it brought. In six days the house on the Clivus took more than in the whole of the preceding month, and the men who came to it came in a mood to be generous and to be seen being generous, and a generous man was a man who could be asked for things.

By the third day the lane outside was hung with garlands that had gone brown at the edges, and the doorkeeper had been offered three bribes and had taken two, and Beryllos had bought a whole tunny at the fish market and was in a state of high artistic fever, and Cela, the newest of the girls, had been sick on the mosaic and had blamed the wine.

"Take the beans out of the passage," Antonia said. "There are beans in the passage. Why are there beans in the passage?"

"Because the boys throw them," said Rufina.

"Then the boys can come in and sweep them up, and then they can be thrown out on their ears, and then they can throw their beans in somebody else's passage."

At the ninth hour a clerk came from the aedile, and Antonia received him in the tablinum with Tullia at her shoulder and a purse on the table.

"The games," the clerk said. "There will be a procession at the Circus on the last day, and the aedile has asked that the houses on the Clivus provide a canopy and a choir of six, and that the women of the houses stand in the front of the procession and throw flowers."

"How many flowers?"

"Two baskets each."

"Two baskets each is four hundred flowers, and the flowers will be thrown away in the street, and the women will be seen by every man in the Circus, and one of my girls will be recognized by her brother and there will be a knife fight." Antonia pushed the purse across the table. "There is sixty sesterces in that. The house will provide a canopy and four women and two baskets, and the four women will wear veils, and the aedile will say nothing about the veils, because the aedile's brother-in-law has been in this house three times since January."

The clerk took the purse. "The aedile's brother-in-law."

"His name is on a page. It is a good page. The aedile should be pleased that it is a page in a book and not a page in a court record."

The clerk looked at the purse, and then at her, and then put the purse in his cloak. "The aedile asked me to say that the games are for the people, and that the people love a great house."

"The people love a great house the way they love a thunderstorm," Antonia said. "They enjoy it and they hope it does not land on them."

When he had gone, Tullia came in with the day's book. "The canopy is forty sesterces. The flowers are sixty if we buy them at the Porta Esquilina before the second hour, and ninety if we buy them after, and the veils are twelve and I have already bought them because I know what you are like."

"What am I like?"

"You are like a woman who will argue about sixty sesterces for a quarter of an hour and then give a slave-girl a cloak that cost four hundred because it was raining."

"That girl is worth twelve thousand and she catches cold in the chest."

"The cloak was four hundred," Tullia said, and wrote it down, and Antonia let her have the last word because she had been letting her have the last word for nine years and it was one of the reasons the household worked.

In the garden the girls were rehearsing the procession. Cela, who was nineteen and from Mutina, had been given the front position because she had the best walk, and she was practicing it up and down the colonnade with a basket of dry beans on her hip, and the other three were in a line behind her, and they were all laughing, and Rufina was shouting at them about their shoulders.

Antonia stood at the door and watched them. This was the part of the day she would remember afterward, when everything else had been taken: four girls in cheap wreaths practicing a walk in a garden, in the last week of April, with the whole city drunk outside the wall.

"Domina," Cela called. "Is it the left foot first or the right?"

"The left. Always the left. Every procession in Rome starts with the left foot, and every priest who tells you otherwise is selling something."

"Then I have been doing it wrong."

"Then you will be doing it right by the sixth day, and nobody will know, and if they do know they will be too drunk to care."

Publia sent word on the morning of the last day, by the flower-seller, and the word was a place: the portico of Octavia, at the sixth hour, in the shade, where a great many women would be, so that one more would not be noticed.

Antonia went with Rufina and Hilarus and stood among the columns where the shade was deepest, and watched the crowd go by. The portico was full of the wives of Rome, and their daughters, and their slaves, and their parasols, and the noise was like a roof coming down.

Publia came at the half-hour, in a litter, with two slaves and her maid. She was in pale yellow with her hair up in the fashion of a woman who is betrothed, and she looked like a girl dressed as her own mother.

Myrtale walked behind the litter with her eyes down.

Antonia had prepared herself for it, and it did not help. The girl came along the colonnade in the ordinary way, carrying her mistress's fan and a basket, and her hair was dark and bound up, and she had a smudge of dust on one cheek, and she looked up once, briefly, at the painted ceiling, and her mouth made the smallest shape of pleasure, and she looked down again.

She had a way of putting her head to one side when she was thinking. She had her father's brows. She had Antonia's hands.

"Domina." Publia had come up to her, and was standing there, and her face was frightened. "You said to come to the portico. You did not say you would bring the whole street."

"I brought two people and a litter-bearer. The rest of the street came by itself."

Publia looked around, and then leaned close, and spoke quickly, the way people speak when they have rehearsed something and are afraid of forgetting it.

"I was in the tablinum on the Nones. My father was at the baths with Caesennius and the contract men. The steward was in the kitchen. I went in and I found the strongbox and I could not open it, and then I found the tablets in the cupboard, in the third pigeonhole, in a bundle tied with string and marked with the year."

"And?"

"And there is a page. It is not the purchase. There is no purchase. There is a note of a debt owed to my father by a man called Chryseros, who was his slave, and the debt is written as a sum of sesterces paid on his behalf, and beside the sum there is a note in my father's own hand, and the note says, *for the child, from Bassa, and no more to be said.*" Publia's voice was shaking. "I copied it. I copied it onto a wax tablet and I copied the tablet onto a second tablet and I burned the first one. I brought the second one today. It is under my mattress at home and I will bring it to you at the wedding, and if I am searched before then I will say I was practicing my letters."

Antonia looked at her. The girl had done it. She had gone into her father's cupboard and read his hand and copied it and burned the copy, and she was sixteen.

"What was the sum?" Antonia said.

"Ninety thousand sesterces."

The crowd went by. A child was crying somewhere. A woman laughed the long, bright laugh of a woman who wants to be heard.

"Ninety thousand," Antonia said, "for a child of three months, sold to a man's own house, with the price written as a debt in the man's own book and the words *no more to be said* beside it."

"I do not understand it."

"You are not meant to. It is not a purchase. It is a bribe to himself." Antonia looked at the girl. "He could not write that he had bought a child from a dealer, so he wrote that his slave had bought her on his behalf, and he paid the slave's debt, and he wrote a note to himself so that he would remember why. That is what men do when they cannot bear the thing they have done and cannot stop doing it."

Publia's face had gone white. "There is more."

"Say it."

"My father has found a buyer for Myrtale. He is a man in Formiae who has a farm and a wife who is ill, and he will pay twelve thousand, and she is to go after the wedding, when the house is quiet." Publia's voice cracked. "I heard him tell my stepmother. He said the girl has been in the house too long and has become a thing the house talks about, and that the wife in Formiae will not last the winter, and that the man will be kind to her."

"And what did Marcella say?"

"Marcella said, 'Then you had better sell her before the wedding, and not after, or the girl will be in the house when the bride comes and the bride will see her, and the bride will have a mother, and mothers talk.'"

Antonia stood very still. Somewhere beyond the colonnade a trumpet sounded, and the crowd turned toward it like a field turning in the wind.

"When does he mean to send her?"

"I do not know. Before September. Perhaps in the summer, when the city empties." Publia caught at her sleeve. "You said you would help me. You have done nothing. The contract is signed and the money is promised and there is a man in Formiae and I am to be married in September and she will be gone before the wedding and I will not even be able to say goodbye, because they will send her while I am at my aunt's."

"I have not done nothing."

"What have you done?"

"I have bought a page from a dead woman's ledger that says the child was sold to your father's house. I have a midwife at Ficulea who attended the birth. I have a woman in my house who held the other half of a token. And I have a consular of Rome who has read my petition and will carry it in the senate house, and in two weeks I will stand in front of the praetor with all of it, and your father will be summoned to answer a charge under the lex Fabia." Antonia took her by the shoulders. "I have not been idle. I have been building a wall, and a wall is made of stones that are carried one at a time, and if I had told you in March you would have run into your father's tablinum and taken the page, and he would have found it gone, and the girl would have been on the road to Formiae before the week was out."

Publia stared at her. "You are going to accuse my father in court."

"I am going to accuse him of holding a free woman as a slave."

"She is not a free woman. She has a collar."

"She has no collar. She has a cord with a token. And she was born free, because her mother was manumitted before the birth, and the manumission is in the records of the city, and the midwife remembers the token and the birth, and the dealer's book says the child came from Iusta the midwife and was sold to the house of Caesar —" Antonia stopped herself. "To the house of Cassius Verus."

"Her mother," Publia said slowly. "Who is her mother?"

"Not here."

"Who is her mother?"

Antonia looked past her, at the girl standing in the shade with the fan, and the words were in her mouth, and she had been carrying them for eighteen years, and they would not come out.

"Not here," she said. "Not today. When it is done, and when it is safe, I will tell you. And I will tell her."

"Domina." It was Myrtale. She had come up behind her mistress with the fan, and she was looking at Antonia with a small, careful frown. "Forgive me. The lady's fan is broken. The pin has come out of the hinge."

Antonia looked at the fan. It was ivory and gilt and the pin had indeed come out, and the girl was holding it together with her fingers.

"Give it to me," Antonia said.

She took the fan and turned it over, and took a pin from her own hair, and bent the point of it with her thumbnail, and worked it through the hinge, and gave it back. The whole thing took less than a minute. The girl watched her hands the entire time.

"You have done that before," Myrtale said.

"I have mended a great many things."

"You have the hands of a woman who mends."

"What hands does a woman who mends have?"

"Quick ones," Myrtale said. "And a mark here." She touched her own wrist, on the inside, where a crescent-shaped mark would be. "Like mine."

Antonia's own wrist was bare. She had a mark on the back of her left hand, from a splash of hot oil in her first year in the house above the fuller's, and it was not a crescent.

"Show me," Antonia said.

The girl hesitated, and glanced at her mistress, and then turned her wrist up. The mark was small and pale and shaped like a paring of moon. Antonia had seen it before, in the small room at the fourth hour, when the girl had held the lamp.

"How did you come by it?"

"I was born with it. The nurse used to say it was the moon's thumbprint, and that a child with the moon's thumbprint would travel far." Myrtale smiled, and it was a good smile, and it was ruined by the fact that she was being kind to a stranger. "I have not traveled far. But the nurse was old."

"Was she kind to you?"

"She was old," Myrtale said again, as though that were an answer.

Publia's voice was sharp. "Myrtale."

"Yes, domina." The girl stepped back, and put the fan in her mistress's hand, and went to stand with the slaves and the litter, and did not look at Antonia again.

Antonia stood in the middle of the portico and heard the crowd and the water and her own blood. Somewhere in her chest was a sound she had not made in eighteen years, and she held it there, and it stayed.

"You had better tell her before the court," Publia said.

"You had better tell her before the court," Publia said, and her voice was not the voice of a child anymore. "Because a court is a room full of men, and the men will say the girl is a slave, and the girl will hear them, and she will believe them if no one has told her otherwise."

* * *

That night the house on the Clivus had thirty guests, which was ten more than the dining room held, and the overflow was on the terrace under an awning strung between the walls, with lamps in the branches of the fig tree and cushions on the ground. The night was warm. The Floralia was ending. Somewhere up the hill a party of young men were singing a song about a Vestal, and somewhere down the hill a woman was screaming at a man, and between them the whole Subura was alive and loud and full of the smell of grilled fish.

Decimus Iunius Gallus arrived in a garland of roses that had been made for him by a girl in the Circus, and by the second hour he was very drunk, and by the third hour he had decided that the evening's entertainment was to be his wit.

Verianus came late, alone, in a dark tunic with his hair wet from the baths. He had been drinking since noon; Antonia could see it in the way he held his cup, which was with both hands, carefully, as though it might escape.

Nepos did not come. He sent a gift instead: a pair of silver cups with a horse on each, and a note wishing the house a good season, which was the sort of gift a man sent when he wanted it remembered that he had not come.

Kallisto sang, and the house was quiet, and then it was not quiet, and the wine went round. Dessa carried the jug with her eyes down and her mouth set, and Antonia watched her because she watched everything, and saw that the girl had not eaten and that she had been to the gate twice.

Daphnus came late and found her by the fig tree, and he had brought a jar of Falernian and the look of a man who has news.

"The ship from Caesarea," he said. "The one with the passenger."

"Menander."

"Menander has been to the harbor office three times, and the third time he came with a man from the prefect's staff, and they asked to see the manifests of every ship that came in from Egypt in the last month." Daphnus put the jar down. "They are not looking for grain."

"No."

"They are looking for grain that is not there. It is the oldest way. You find a discrepancy in a man's manifests, and then you have a reason to open his warehouse, and then you have a reason to look at his books, and then you have a reason to look at his name." He looked at her. "What is in your books, Antonia?"

"Names."

"Whose?"

"Yours," she said. "And a great many others."

Daphnus was quiet for a moment. Then he laughed, shortly, and without much joy. "I have been in business thirty years and I have never once been frightened of a ledger until tonight."

"Then you have been lucky. Ledgers are the most frightening thing in Rome. That is why the men who keep them are careful, and why the men who do not keep them are loud." Antonia took the jar from him. "Go home, Gaius. Do not come to the house for a month. If anyone asks, you have not spoken to me since March, and you do not know what I am."

"And if they ask me under oath?"

"Then tell them the truth. It will be the first time in your life and they will not believe you."

At the fourth hour the terrace was crowded and the lamps had burned down and the party had that loose, happy, dangerous feel of a good night going wrong. Antonia was at the far end with Gallus, who was talking about the water supply with the loving detail of a man who has no other subject, and she saw Decimus get up from his couch and cross the terrace to where Verianus sat alone.

She watched them the way a woman watches a pot come to the boil. Decimus said something, and laughed, and put his hand on Verianus's shoulder. Verianus said something that was not a laugh. Decimus stepped back, and said something else, louder, and there was a small circle of silence around them, and the people near them turned to look.

"— the old man's eyes," Decimus said, and his voice carried, because drunk men's voices carry. "That is what I said in March, and I will say it again in September. Your father keeps a girl with his own eyes, and when the bride comes into that house, the bride will have to look at her every morning and know what the old man —"

Verianus hit him.

It was not a drunkard's swing. It was fast and low and it caught Decimus under the jaw, and he went back over the couch and knocked a lamp off the table, and the oil caught and there was a small blue flame running across the mosaic, and two women screamed and a man laughed, and Hilarus was there with a cloth before it could reach the cushions.

Verianus stood over him. His hands were shaking.

"Say it again," he said. "Say it again in my father's house, and I will do worse."

"It is not your father's house," Decimus said from the floor, holding his jaw. "It is a brothel. Your father is too proud to come to it, so he sends his son."

The terrace had gone quiet. Thirty people were looking at Antonia.

She walked across the terrace. She did not hurry. She had learned a long time ago that a woman who hurries in her own house has lost it.

"Decimus," she said, "you have broken a lamp, and there is oil on a mosaic that cost me two thousand sesterces, and you have insulted my guest in my house, and you have done it while wearing a garland of flowers that my doorkeeper paid for. I am going to give you a choice. You may go home now, in a litter, with your garland, and in the morning you may send me the two thousand sesterces with a note of apology, and we will say that the wine was strong."

He looked up at her, and his mouth was bleeding, and his eyes were the eyes of a boy who has just discovered that the world will not let him be funny forever.

"And the other choice?"

"You may stay, and be carried out, and I will still have the two thousand sesterces, and the story will be a great deal better."

Decimus got up. For a moment Antonia thought he would be sensible. Then he looked at the thirty people, and at the girls, and at Verianus, and he did the thing that young men do when they have been humiliated in front of an audience, which is to make the audience bigger.

"You are all very fine," he said. "You sit in this house and you drink her wine and you tell yourselves she is a businesswoman. She is a freedwoman who sells women. My father says the emperor's grandmother sold salt and her son sold grain and now the emperor sells war, and the whole empire is a market, and the only difference between the people at the top and the people at the bottom is the price. She is a stall. You are buying at a stall."

He threw the garland on the floor. Nobody stopped him. He walked out through the garden and the gate, and Cerdon shut the door behind him, and the party breathed again, and somebody laughed, and the music started, and Kallisto sang the song about the woman at the door, and it was the fourth time.

Verianus sat down. His hands were still shaking. Antonia stood by the fig tree with the lamps in it, and looked at the dark beyond the awning, and thought about the way a night could turn.

At the sixth hour she went to her own room. The house was quieting; the guests were going; the last litter went out of the lane. Rufina brought her a cup of water and took her shoes, and she lay down with her eyes open and listened to the house settle.

Then a woman screamed.

It was not a party scream. It was the other kind. It came from the garden, and it stopped in the middle, the way screams stop when the screamer has put both hands over her own mouth.

Antonia was up and through the passage before she had decided to move. The garden was dark and the fountain was running, and the lamps had been put out, and the moon was behind the wall. She came around the corner of the colonnade and saw the body in the fountain.

He was face down in eighteen inches of water, with his arms out and his palms up, and his good tunic was dark with wet, and the water around him was moving and then it was still.

Hilarus got there first. He took the shoulders and turned the body over, and the face came up into the moonlight, and it was Decimus Iunius Gallus, nineteen years old, with his mouth open and his eyes open and a small cut on his forehead where the lip of the basin had caught him.

"He is dead," Hilarus said.

"Get away from him." Antonia was kneeling. "Get away from him and do not touch anything else. Where is the girl?"

"Which girl?"

"The one who screamed."

"In the passage. It was Dessa. She came running and she was —"

"Is she hurt?"

"She is not hurt," Hilarus said. "Domina, she is not hurt, but her hands are wet."

Antonia looked at the fountain. She looked at the water, and the body, and the cut on the forehead, and the palms turned up. Then she looked at the terrace, at the little table where Verianus had been sitting at the fourth hour, and at the dark of the garden beyond the wall.

"The gate," she said. "Is the gate open?"

Cerdon came running. He was in his night-shirt with his club. "The gate is open. The bolt is drawn from the inside. Domina, the boy Verianus is not in the house."

Antonia stood up in the wet grass. Her hands were cold. In the dark, beyond the wall, the Subura was still making its noise, and the city was going on being the city, and a nineteen-year-old boy was lying in her fountain with his eyes open, and the last thing that had happened to him had happened in her house.

"Nobody leaves," she said. "Nobody comes in. Cerdon, go to the station and get Crescens. Not the aediles. Not the prefect. Crescens." She looked down at the body. "And find the girl. Find Dessa, and bring her to me, and do not let anyone else touch her before I have."

She stood in the garden in the dark and listened to the water running, and thought about Gallus, who would be told before noon, and about the two thousand sesterces, and about the garland of roses lying on the terrace where a boy had thrown it.

She thought: I am going to lie about this.

She did not know yet why. She only knew that the thought had come, whole and cold and certain, the way the truth usually came.

Crescens came within the half-hour, with two of his men and a lantern, and he came through the garden without a word to anyone, and knelt by the body, and looked at it for a long time before he touched it.

"Who found him?"

"The girl. Dessa."

"Where is she?"

"In the passage. Rufina has her."

"Keep her there. Do not let her wash her hands." He looked up. "You have guessed why."

"I have guessed that a boy came to my house, was hit in the face by another boy, and then was found in my fountain with a cut on his head. A man could make a story out of that. A man could make four."

"A man could make one," Crescens said. "The one that says he was pushed, and fell, and hit his head on the rim, and drowned in eighteen inches of water, which is a thing that happens more often than you would think, because a man who is stunned and face down in a fountain does not get up."

"Or a man could make the one that says he was held under."

"With what?" Crescens said. "There are no marks on his arms. If a man held him under, he would have fought, and there would be marks. Unless the man was very strong, or the boy was already dead, or the boy was so drunk he did not know he was drowning."

"He was drunk."

"He was very drunk. Everybody on that terrace was very drunk." Crescens stood up and wiped his hands. "The aediles will call it an accident. The aediles call everything an accident. A young man, a party, a fountain, a great house, a family that does not want a scandal three months before a wedding. It is an accident. It is the sort of accident that happens every week in this city, and the aediles will write it down and take their fee and go home."

"And you?"

"I am a tribune of the fire-watch. I put out fires." He looked at her. "But I am also the man who notices, and I have noticed that the boy Verianus left this house at the fourth hour and did not say goodbye to his hostess, and that the girl who found the body has wet hands and has not spoken a word since, and that you, domina, have already decided what you are going to say."

"I have not decided."

"You have decided. I can see it in your face." He came closer. "Whatever it is, say it to me now, before the aediles come, so that when they ask me what I saw I can be a liar in the same language."

Antonia looked at the body in the fountain, and at the cut on the forehead, and at the palms turned up, and she thought of a boy of nineteen throwing a garland on a mosaic and telling thirty people what he thought of her house. She thought of his father at dinner with a boy on his knee. She thought of a girl in the passage with wet hands, who had seen what she had seen, and who was a slave, and whose word in a Roman court was worth nothing at all unless it was given under torture.

"I am going to say," she said, "that I found him like this, and that I do not know how he came to be here, and that he was a guest in my house and I will pay for his funeral."

Crescens's face did not change. "And the girl?"

"The girl is not going to be questioned."

"That is not what I asked."

"The girl," Antonia said, "is not going to be questioned. If a man comes with a warrant, he will find that she was sold last month to a house in Africa and that the sale is registered, and he will find the registration, because I will have made it before he arrives."

Crescens looked at the fountain.

"You are protecting a witness," he said. "And in protecting her, you are making yourself the only person who knows what she knows. And that is a very dangerous place to stand."

"I know."

"Then I hope to God she is worth it."

"She is worth twelve thousand sesterces," Antonia said, "and she can add a column of figures faster than Tullia, and she has not eaten in a week because she has been thinking about the road. She is worth a great deal more than my safety, and I have only just discovered it."
