The court sat in the Basilica Julia, in the great hall on the south side, where the light came in from the square and the noise of the Forum came in with it, and where the praetor urbanus heard cases of status on the Ides and the Kalends and, if the weather was bad and the calendar was full, on any day he chose.

By the second hour the hall was full.

Antonia had expected a crowd. She had not expected this. There were the parties, and the advocates, and the clerks, and the eight men of the jury bench if the praetor called one, and then there were the others: the clients and the hangers-on and the men who came to the basilica every morning because the basilica was where the news was; and behind them, at the back, in the shadows of the great columns, the women.

The women were not supposed to be there. A woman could appear in a status case as a party, or as a witness if the praetor allowed it, but the rest of it — the audience, the watching, the being seen to watch — was not a woman's business, and the women who had come had come early and had taken the dark corners behind the columns, and they were there in their dozens.

Antonia came in at the second hour with Tullia and Hilarus and Myrtale.

Myrtale had come from Ficulea the night before, in a hired carriage, with Iusta the midwife and a bundle and a face that had gone very still. She was in a plain white dress and a grey mantle, and her hair was up, and she had the token on its cord around her neck, outside her dress, in the sight of everyone.

"Are you frightened?" Antonia said, in the carriage.

"Yes."

"Good. So am I." She had taken the girl's hand. "There is a thing you must understand about this room before you go into it. The men in there are going to talk about you as though you were a piece of furniture. They will say *the person claimed*, and *the value*, and *the title*, and they will not look at you. Do not be angry. Be still. And when the praetor asks you your name, say it in a voice that the whole hall can hear, and say the name I gave you."

"Cassia," Myrtale said.

"Cassia," Antonia said. "Say it again."

"Cassia."

"Again."

"Cassia."

* * *

Before the court sat, Verianus came out of the shadows of the colonnade.

He was sober. That was the first thing Antonia noticed, and it was more frightening than the drinking had been. He was in the broad stripe and his hair was cut and he had the look of a man who has been up all night with a cold cloth on his forehead.

"Domina," he said.

"Decemvir."

"I am to sit on the bench today."

"I know."

"I have applied to be excused and the application has been refused, and my father has told me that if I sit on that bench and do my duty by the law, he will not speak to me again, and if I do not sit on it, the praetor will have me removed and the whole city will know why." He stood in front of her with his hands at his sides. "I am twenty years old and I have been drunk for six months and I have not slept for a week, and I am asking you to tell me what to do."

Antonia looked at him.

"I am the last person in Rome you should ask," she said.

"I know. That is why I am asking." Verianus's voice was very quiet. "You are the only person in this who has never once lied to me about what I am."

Antonia looked at the colonnade and the morning crowd and the light on the marble, and she thought about the boy in the fountain with his palms turned up, and the boy's father in a garden with a boy on his knee, and the nineteen masks in a cupboard and the actor with the mask pushed back on his head.

"Go and sit on your bench," she said, "and do your duty by the law, and do not look at your father, and do not look at me. And afterward, when it is over and the girl is free or not free, you will come and find me, and you will tell me what happened in that lane in April, and I will listen to all of it, and then you will have to decide what to do about it, and I will not help you with that."

Verianus's face did something.

"You know," he said.

"I know there was a ring in the lane and a man who went away down the hill. I do not know what happened in the garden. I have never known, and I have never asked, and there is a part of me that does not want to know." Antonia looked at him. "But I have a girl in a cell in the prefect's office who saw a man in the lane, and she is going to be questioned in December about what she saw, and if she is questioned, she will not come out of it with a whole hand. So you may sit on your bench today and do your duty by the law, and then you may come and find me and tell me the truth, and if the truth is what I think it is, we will have a great deal to say to each other."

Verianus looked at her for a long moment.

"I will sit on the bench," he said, "and I will do my duty by the law."

He went in.

* * *

Gallus was at the second hour with a cold, a roll of papers, and three witnesses.

He had a fever, and his nose was red, and his voice was a wreck, and he had been up half the night with the papers, and he was the most magnificent thing Antonia had ever seen in a public place. He came across the hall to her with the roll under his arm and stood in front of her and blew his nose into a cloth.

"You are late," he said.

"I am punctual."

"You are late, and you are pale, and you are wearing the blue stola, which is a good choice, because it makes you look like a respectable widow instead of the most dangerous woman in Rome." He looked over her shoulder at Myrtale and his face did something complicated. "This is she."

"This is she."

Gallus looked at the girl for a long moment. Then he said, "Domina, I am Lucius Iunius Gallus, and I am going to stand up in that court and say that you are free, and I want you to know before I do it that I have lost a son this year, and that I am not doing this for you, and that I intend to do it properly anyway."

Myrtale looked at him.

"I am sorry for your son," she said.

Gallus's face twisted. He turned away and blew his nose again, and when he turned back he was a consular.

"Right," he said. "Now. The praetor is Marcus Acilius Strabo, and he is a decent man and a lazy one, and he does not like a spectacle, and he does not like to be hurried. The decemvirs will sit with him, and one of them is Verus's son, and I have applied to have him excused on the ground of interest, and the application will be refused, because a decemvir is not excused for interest, he is excused for a defect of status, and Verianus has no defect of status, he has a father." He blew his nose. "So we will have him on the bench, and he will sit there for six hours, and he will either be a man or he will not."

* * *

The praetor came in at the third hour and the court rose.

Marcus Acilius Strabo was sixty and had the look of a man who had been sitting in this hall for thirty years and had heard every story that could be told in it. He took his seat, and the decemvirs took theirs on the bench to his right, and Verianus Cassius was the third from the end, in a white tunic with the broad stripe, and he was sober, and he was grey, and he did not look at his father, who was sitting on the bench to the left with his advocate.

The clerk read the matter.

*A question of status, in the matter of a person claimed, a woman of about eighteen years, held in the household of Manius Cassius Verus, consular. The freedom of the said person is asserted by Lucius Iunius Gallus, consular, as adsertor libertatis. The said person is claimed as a slave by Manius Cassius Verus, consular, who holds her in his household and who has produced her before this court.*

The praetor looked up.

"The person is produced?"

"She is produced, domine," said the clerk.

"Let her stand before the bench."

Myrtale came forward through the crowd. She walked the way a girl walks who has been taught to walk in a great house, and she came to the middle of the floor and stood in front of the praetor's bench with her hands at her sides and her chin up and the token on its cord around her neck, and the whole hall looked at her.

Antonia stood in the fourth rank of the crowd with her hands folded and her heart going like a hammer and her eyes on her daughter's back.

"The adsertor," the praetor said. "You may state your claim."

Gallus stood up.

He had a cold, and his voice was a ruin, and he began badly: he began with the law, and the law is a thing that makes men's eyes glaze. He spoke for a quarter of an hour about the principle that a child born to a free mother is free, and about the burden that lies on the man who holds a person to show his title, and about the lex Fabia.

The hall shifted. A man near the back said something to his neighbour. The praetor looked at the water-clock.

And then Gallus stopped in the middle of a sentence about the *causa liberalis*, and blew his nose, and said, "Domine, I am making a botch of this, and the reason is that I have a fever and I have not slept, and I am going to do the thing that every advocate has told me not to do, which is to tell the court what actually happened."

The hall went quiet.

"There was a slave in the household of the elder Cassius Verus," Gallus said, "in the year 93, and her name was Prima. She was nineteen. She was the ornatrix of the house, and she could read, and she was manumitted by Cassia Severa on the eleventh day before the Kalends of December of that year, and the manumission is in the record and the record is in the archive of the fourteenth region and the clerk has brought a copy. She was free from that day. In the following September she bore a daughter in a room above a fuller's shop on the Aventine, and the midwife who attended the birth is in this court today, and will tell you what she saw. She saw a lead token tied to the baby's wrist, and she saw the child taken three months later by a litter, and she was paid by a slave called Chryseros to forget it and she did not forget it."

The praetor leaned forward.

"In the same year," Gallus said, "a dealer in the Subura named Bassa sold a child of three months to the household of Cassius Verus, and her book says so, and her book is in this court as an exhibit. The price was ninety thousand sesterces, which is ten times the price of a healthy adult, and it was not paid in money: it was written as a debt owed by the slave Chryseros to his master, and beside it, in the hand of Manius Cassius Verus, there is a note which reads, *for the child, from Bassa, and no more to be said.*" He paused. "That child is standing in front of you."

The hall made a sound.

"The defendant," Gallus said, "holds her and produces her and has produced no title, because there is no title. He cannot produce a bill of sale, because the purchase was written as a debt. He cannot produce the dealer, because she is dead. He cannot produce the slave, because he is dead. What he can produce is a household in which a freeborn woman has been held for eighteen years as a maid, and a note in his own hand, and a woman who has waited nineteen years to stand in this hall and say her daughter's name."

He sat down.

* * *

The praetor looked at the papers in front of him for a long moment.

"The defendant," he said. "You may answer."

Verus stood up.

He was in a plain toga, and he had come alone, without clients, without his wife, without his son, and he stood in the middle of the hall of the Basilica Julia with the whole of Rome looking at him.

"I have no answer," he said.

The silence in the hall was a physical thing.

"I claim the person," Verus said. "I have held her. I have no title to produce. The note in my hand is mine, and the debt is mine, and the purchase was mine, and the household is mine, and the girl has been in it for eighteen years and I have not freed her and I have not told her what she is." His voice was steady. "There is no answer. The adsertor has stated the matter correctly."

The praetor sat back.

"The court will hear the witnesses," he said.

* * *

Iusta the midwife was sixty-eight and had delivered four hundred children and had never in her life spoken in a public place. She came forward with her hands folded and her chin trembling and she told it in the flat, detailed way that a midwife tells a birth: the room, the hour, the nine hours of it, the girl's name, the mark on the wrist, the token. She told it twice, because the praetor asked her to tell it again, and the second time she told it better, because she had found the rhythm of it.

Then the exhibits. Bassa's page, sealed and copied and read out. The manumission record, read out. The garden, the vine, the room behind the kitchen, the fifty-one times — no, the fifty-one times were not in court, because the fifty-one times were not law, and Gallus had been right not to put them in.

Then the mother.

"The court will hear the witness Antonia Cassia," the praetor said. "The witness is a freedwoman of the household of Cassia Severa, manumitted in the consulship of Pompeius Collega and Priscinus, and she appears as a witness in a question of status, and the court has been informed that she is the person who asserts herself to be the mother of the person claimed." He looked up. "Domina, you will speak to the court, and you will remember that everything you say will be written down."

Antonia came out of the fourth rank and stood in the middle of the floor.

She had stood in this hall before. Not in the middle of it, and not in the blue stola: she had stood at the back of it eleven times in eleven years, with a tablet, in a corner, waiting for a clerk, in the way that a woman with money and no standing waits for a clerk. She had never once been in the middle of it.

"I was born in the house of the Cassii," she said. "I was a slave of that household until the eleventh day before the Kalends of December in the year 93, when I was manumitted before the praetor by Cassia Severa, and the record is in the archive and the clerk has read it to this court. In the September of the following year I bore a daughter in a room above a fuller's shop on the Aventine, and the midwife Iusta attended the birth, and she has told this court what she saw: a girl, with a mark like a crescent on her left wrist, and a lead token tied to the wrist, and the token struck with a wreath on one side and a door on the other and snapped across the middle."

She held up her hand. In it was the other half.

"The door half was mine," she said. "The wreath half went with the child. The girl in front of this bench is wearing it."

The praetor leaned forward. Antonia put the half-token on the table in front of him, and the clerk brought the girl forward, and the girl lifted the cord over her head and put the token down beside it, and the two halves lay on the table with the break between them.

The praetor put them together himself.

There was no sound in the hall at all.

"It is one token," he said.

"It is one token," Antonia said. "I had it struck at a shop in the Subura for four sesterces, because I had nothing else to give her, and because I knew that a man who took her would take her name, and I wanted her to have one thing that had not been given to her by the man who kept her."

"Who took the child?"

"The man in this court who has just told the court that he has no answer." Antonia's voice was steady. "He came for her three days after I had agreed to give her up, in a litter, with the nurse, at the sixth hour, when the lane was empty. He put her in the litter and he carried her down the stairs of a house above a fuller's shop, and he promised me that he would free her at sixteen and give her a name, and he swore it by the household gods of the Cassii, and he did not do it."

Verus sat on the bench with his hands on his knees and did not look up.

"Why did you give her up?" the praetor said.

Antonia looked at him.

"Because I had eleven sesterces a month for the room," she said, "and there was no work for a woman with a child, and the man who kept the house was a gambler who had begun to talk about the price of a woman in the Subura, and because I was twenty years old and I believed him." She paused. "And because I have told myself for eighteen years that I had no choice, and that is not true. I had a choice. It was a bad one, and I chose it, and I have been paying for it ever since, and I would like the court to write that down as well as the rest, because I am not here to be a good woman. I am here to be a true one."

The praetor looked at her for a long moment.

"The court will write it down," he said.

Then Myrtale.

"The person claimed," the praetor said. "What is your name?"

Myrtale stood in front of the bench.

"Cassia," she said, in a voice that the whole hall could hear.

"Cassia," the praetor said. "Who gave you that name?"

"My mother," Myrtale said.

The hall made a sound again, a different one this time, and Antonia stood in the fourth rank of the crowd and did not move.

"Do you know your mother?"

"I met her in September," Myrtale said. "In the house where I was a slave. She came at night with four men and a boat, and she told me who I was, and I did not believe her at first, and then I did."

"And what did she tell you?"

"That I was born free, and that a man had held me for eighteen years, and that there was a case, and that I would have to stand in a court and say my name in front of a room full of men." Myrtale's voice did not shake. "And that if I did not, no one in Rome would ever be able to say that I had been free."

The praetor looked at her for a long moment.

"Domina," he said, "I am going to ask you a question and you will answer it truthfully, and it does not matter what the answer is, and nobody in this hall will be permitted to make a noise about it. Do you wish to be free?"

The hall was so quiet that Antonia could hear the water-clock.

"I am free," Myrtale said. "The question is whether Rome will say so."

* * *

That should have been the end of it.

The praetor had the papers, the admissions, the record, the midwife, the girl's own voice. He had a defendant who had declined to answer and an adsertor who had stated the case. Antonia, standing in the fourth rank with her hands folded, had begun — for the first time in eight months — to let herself think about the afternoon.

And then the man stood up at the back of the hall.

"Domine," he said. "I appear."

The praetor looked up. "In what matter?"

"In the matter of the person claimed. I am Caesennius Rufus, equestrian, of the house of Caesennius, and I have an interest in this person, and I have a document which the court has not seen." He came forward through the crowd. He was fifty-four and grey and solid, and he was holding a scroll, and he was not a man who had come to make a scene: he was a man who had come to do a piece of business. "The person claimed was conveyed to my household on the fourth day before the Kalends of September as part of the dowry of my wife, Publia Cassia, and the conveyance is written in the marriage instrument, which was signed by her father and witnessed and sealed, and which I produce."

The hall exploded.

The praetor struck the arm of his chair three times with his staff.

"Silence," he said. "Silence, or I will clear the hall and hear the case in an empty room." He took the scroll from Caesennius and looked at it for a long time. Then he looked up. "The instrument is regular. It is signed by Manius Cassius Verus and witnessed by four men, and it conveys to the household of Caesennius Rufus, among other property, a female slave named Myrtale, valued at twelve thousand sesterces."

The clerk wrote. The decemvirs looked at each other.

"The court is obliged to observe," the praetor said, slowly, "that the defendant in this matter has produced the person claimed, and has no title to her, and has now admitted as much. The court is also obliged to observe that the marriage instrument produced by Caesennius Rufus is a conveyance executed before the fact of this hearing, that it purports to convey the person claimed to a third party, and that the third party is now before the court claiming an interest." He set the scroll down. "The question the court must determine is whether the person claimed was free at the time of the conveyance. If she was not free, the instrument conveys her. If she was free, the instrument conveys nothing at all, because a man cannot convey a free person, and the instrument is evidence of a crime."

He looked at Gallus.

"The adsertor has made his case. The claimant has produced an instrument. The court will hear argument on the instrument on the Nones of December, in this hall, at the second hour, and in the meantime the person claimed will remain in the custody of the court and not in the household of either party."

The hall broke. The clerks began to gather the tablets. Caesennius Rufus was standing with his scroll in his hand, looking at nothing, and at the back of the hall a woman in a drawn litter had opened the curtains.

Gallus got up.

"Domine," he said, "the person claimed is a girl of eighteen who has spent her life in a household where —"

"On the Nones of December," the praetor said, and rose, and the court rose, and the hearing was over.

* * *

They came out of the basilica into the Forum, and the sun was on the marble and the whole square was going about its business as though a girl had not just said her name in front of the whole of Rome.

Antonia stood on the steps with her hand on the stone.

"December," Gallus said. "Three weeks. Three weeks, and he produces a marriage instrument, and the question becomes whether the conveyance conveyed a free person." He blew his nose. "It is a bad three weeks and a good case. The instrument is the best thing that could have happened."

"How can it be the best thing?"

"Because it puts the crime in writing," Gallus said, "and because it is signed by four witnesses, and because it makes Caesennius Rufus a party, and a party with a cousin on the council does not want to be a party to a crime under the lex Fabia. He will settle. His advocate will come to me within the week and offer to give her up and pay the costs and say nothing, and we will refuse, and he will offer more, and we will refuse again, and he will begin to be frightened."

"And the senate?"

"The senate is the day before the case," Gallus said. "The day before the Nones. I have asked the consuls for the house to hear you, and the consul has said that the house will hear a statement of fact from a woman of the city if a consular vouches for her and if the statement concerns a matter before a court. He is a careful man and he has covered himself in three directions." He looked at her. "You have three weeks to find a reason for the senate to do more than listen."

"What reason?"

"Plotina," Gallus said. "The empress is in Rome until the Kalends. She is the only person in this city who can make an office move in a week, and she has a garden, and you have been standing at the gate of it since August." He put on his hat. "Go and see her. And domina — when you go, do not go as a petitioner. Go as a woman who knows something she does not."

Publia was waiting at the foot of the steps.

She was in a dark mantle with her hair covered and her veil down, and she had come alone, on foot, from the house on the Caelian, and she was out of breath and her eyes were enormous.

"I heard," she said. "The whole street heard. A girl stood up in the Basilica Julia and gave her name, and they say the praetor put the two halves of a token together on the table and the hall went silent." She caught Antonia's hands. "Is she free?"

"Not yet. December. There is a document and a third party and three weeks." Antonia looked at her. "Your husband is the third party."

Publia's face changed.

"He came to the court," Antonia said. "He produced the marriage instrument, and he read it out, and the instrument conveys Myrtale to his household as part of your dowry, and he is now a party to the case."

"He said nothing to me." Publia's voice was flat. "He said nothing to me at all. He went out at the second hour and he came back for dinner and he talked about the brickfields, and I sat at the table and listened to a man talk about brickfields for an hour, and all the time he had been in a court claiming my maid as property."

"Would you have stopped him?"

"Yes."

"Then he was right not to tell you." Antonia took her by the arm and walked her out of the crowd. "Listen to me. In three weeks there is a second hearing, and the question is whether he can hold her. His advocate will come to Gallus and offer to give her up, and Gallus will refuse, and he will offer more, and Gallus will refuse again, and then he will begin to be frightened. And when he is frightened, Publia, he will come to you, because you are his wife and you are the daughter of the man who signed the instrument, and he will ask you what to do."

"What should I say?"

"Say nothing. Not one word. Let him talk, and look at him, and when he has finished, say: *I do not know, Titus. I am only a woman.*" Antonia's eyes were hard. "A man who is used to being clever will talk himself into a corner with a woman who will not help him. It is the oldest trick in Rome, and it works because men cannot bear a silence."

Publia looked at her.

"I hate this," she said. "I hate all of it. I hate the brickfields and the dinner and the calling me by another name and the fact that my father is going to be destroyed and my husband is a third party and I am sixteen years old and I am standing on the steps of a basilica being told to be a wall."

"Then hate it," Antonia said. "Hate it and do it. There is a whole class of women in this city who have done nothing but hate it and do it, and they are the reason anything is ever done at all."

* * *

Myrtale was in the carriage with the curtain drawn, and Antonia got in beside her, and they sat for a moment in the noise of the Forum.

"Cassia," Myrtale said.

"Cassia," Antonia said.

"I said it in front of all of them."

"You did."

"They looked at me as though I were a chair that had spoken." Myrtale's hands were shaking, and she looked at them with interest. "I have never been looked at like that. In the house, nobody looked at me at all, and that was worse, and now the whole hall has looked at me, and I find that I do not mind it. I mind it less than being a chair."

Antonia took her hand.

"Three weeks," she said. "And then December, and Caesennius Rufus, and a scroll with a seal on it. And before that, a senate house, and an empress, and a man in a shed in the Subura who is going to have to find a respectable dress."

"Whose dress?"

"Mine," Antonia said. "I have been in a burnt house for two months and I have nothing to wear to the senate house, and I am going to have to buy a dress and a pair of shoes and a chair to be carried in, and I have exactly one friend in Rome who will pay for all of it and complain about the price."

"Marcella," Myrtale said.

"Marcella," Antonia said, and the carriage went out of the Forum and up the Clivus, and the whole of the afternoon was in front of them.
