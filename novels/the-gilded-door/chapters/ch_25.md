The dedication was in May, which the priests had chosen and the engineers had not, and the whole city came out to see a hundred and ten feet of marble with a bronze man on top of it.

They had been building the forum for eleven years, with money that had come out of Dacia in ox-carts, and Antonia had walked past the site four hundred times and had never once been inside, because it was not a place for a woman with business. Now she was inside.

The crowd filled the colonnade and the steps of the basilica and the whole of the open square, and the square was paved in white marble from end to end, and the light came off it in a way that made her feel she was standing in a bath. There were two libraries, Greek and Latin, and between them the Column rose, and the Column was the thing everyone had come to see.

"It is smaller than I thought," said Sabina, who was fifteen and had been free for nine months and had developed opinions.

"It is a hundred feet high," Hilarus said.

"I know how high a hundred feet is. I have carried things up three flights of stairs in the Subura." Sabina put her chin up. "I thought it would be bigger."

Myrtale laughed.

Antonia stood between them in the good blue stola with the grey mantle and looked up the shaft of the Column and did not say anything at all.

* * *

The frieze went up in a spiral, twenty-three turns, and it had been cut by men who had never been to Dacia and who had worked from drawings and from the accounts of soldiers and, Antonia suspected, from their own invention, and the whole war was on it.

The army crossing the river on the bridge of boats. The baggage train. The siege. The Dacian horsemen with their curved swords and their caps. The village burning. The women in the village. The soldiers presenting heads to the emperor. The emperor on a platform, four times, at four different points of the spiral, receiving the submission, receiving the treasure, receiving the embassy, receiving the news.

Dessa walked around it.

She had come in her plain dress and her shoes on, and she had said almost nothing since they had come through the gate, and she walked around the base of the Column once, slowly, and then again, and then a third time, and then she stopped in front of a panel about a third of the way up and stood there with her hands at her sides.

Antonia went and stood beside her.

"That is a village," Dessa said.

"Yes."

"It is not my village."

"No."

"It is not anyone's village." Dessa's voice was flat and not loud. "There is a river and a bridge and a village on a hill, and the village has eleven houses and a wall with a gate with two towers, and the soldiers are coming up from the south. My village had one tower and the gate was on the east and there were fourteen houses and a stone by the gate that had been there since before the Romans came, and they came up from the north because the north side was the low side."

She looked at the marble.

"It is a good village," she said. "It is better than mine. Some man in a workshop in Rome made a village out of the word *Dacia*, and now the emperor will stand on the top of it on a bronze horse, and the whole of Rome will look up and say, there is Dacia."

"Dessa."

"I am not going to cry," Dessa said. "I have not cried since the wagon, and I will not begin in the forum of the emperor with three thousand people in it."

"I would mind," she added. "I wanted to find it. That is what I came for, domina. One panel out of twenty-three turns with a thing in it that I knew — a gate, a stone, the shape of a hill — and I have walked around it for a quarter of an hour, and there is nothing. It is not that they got it wrong. They got it right in the way that a man gets a face right when he has never seen the face."

The crowd moved past them, and a child was lifted onto a man's shoulders somewhere behind, and the sound was the sound of a great many people being pleased.

"Then you will have to be the panel," Antonia said.

Dessa looked at her.

"You are the only Dacia that is going to survive," Antonia said. "In a hundred years the marble will be a ruin with grass on it, and men will read the panels and say, that is how it was, and the ones who could tell them otherwise were carried off at fourteen and set to washing cups, and they are the ones who will be listened to least. So you will have to write it down."

"I can barely write."

"You can count," Antonia said, "and you can remember, and I have a book and a strongbox and a boy in the Subura who copies documents for two sesterces a page." She looked at the column. "I am going to start a second book. The villages, from the people who were in them. And when it is done I will give a copy to the Latin library, so that a man who comes in here in fifty years to read a poem will walk past the shelf with your village in it, and if he is the sort of man who looks, he will find that the village on the column has the well in the wrong place."

Dessa looked at the marble for a long moment.

"It had fourteen houses," she said. "The gate was on the east."

"Write it down," Antonia said.

* * *

Gallus found them at the base of the Column, where he had been carried in a chair and set down under the colonnade, and where he was in the middle of an argument with a man from the praetor's office about whether the dedication counted as a holiday.

"You came," he said. "Well. What do you think of it?"

"I think it is a book," Antonia said, "and I think it has one author, and I think it has been very carefully written."

Gallus was silent for a moment.

"That is exactly what I think," he said, "and I have not been able to say it to anybody, because everybody wants to talk about the engineering. The war is in stone and the names of the dead are not, and every man in this square this morning believes that he is looking at history. He is looking at a receipt."

"A beautiful receipt."

"The most beautiful ever made," Gallus said. "That is not nothing. If I had made it, I would be pleased with myself." He blew his nose. "And the door?"

"The door is new. Real gilt, not paint. The old one had four hundred men's bootprints in the bottom of it, and I have had the oak made into a bench for the garden."

Gallus laughed, and it turned into a cough that went on for a while.

"You will be the death of me," he said, when he had got his breath, "and I will go into the ground saying that the last thing I did as a consular of Rome was to stand up for a freedwoman's daughter against the whole order, and that the wine at her house is better than the wine at the palace."

"That is not saying much. The wine at the palace is not meant to be drunk. It is meant to be seen."

* * *

The rescript came in July.

It came in the afternoon heat, when the whole Subura smells of dust and the cook's fires, and it came in the hands of Callirhoe, who was the empress's woman and who came in a plain chair with two men and a sealed box and would not give the box to anyone but Antonia.

She set it on the table in the room with the strongbox and put her hand on it and said, "Before you open it, I am to tell you three things, and I am to tell them in order, and you are not to interrupt me, because if I am interrupted I will lose my place and she will ask me what you said and I will be ashamed."

"Say them."

"The first is that she is sorry it took until July. The office sat on it since March and she had to go to the emperor twice." Callirhoe folded her hands. "The second is that the rescript is what she said it would be, in every particular, and that you should read the second clause aloud in a room with the door shut, because she wrote it herself with a jurist behind her, and she wants to be told whether you cried."

"I do not cry."

"She said you would say that." Callirhoe's mouth twitched. "The third is that the office has attached a condition, and that she did not know until the box was sealed, and that she has been in a temper about it for four days, and that she is not to tell me what it is, and that I am not to wait for an answer."

"Then what am I to do?"

"You are to read it and decide and send me a line with one word in it. Yes or no. And whichever word it is, she will not think less of you, and she has never said that about anybody."

She went out.

Antonia sat with the box for a long time before she opened it.

* * *

The rescript was two leaves of good papyrus, sealed and counter-sealed, with the subscription in the emperor's own hand at the bottom and the date.

*Imperator Caesar Nerva Trajanus Augustus, Germanicus Dacicus, pontifex maximus, to Antonia Cassia, freedwoman of the household of the Cassii: greeting.*

*Whereas it has been shown to us that the said Antonia Cassia was manumitted lawfully and has lived nineteen years in this city without reproach before the law, we have resolved, of our own motion, to restore to her the standing of one born of free parents, and to order that she be held and reputed freeborn in all courts and in all places, as though she had been born free and had never borne the stain of servitude.*

*And we order that the register of the fourteenth region be corrected accordingly, and that the manumission by which she was freed be cancelled of record, as being made in error of her true condition.*

Antonia read it twice, and then a third time, and then she sat with it in her lap and looked at the wall.

She had wanted it for eleven years. She had wanted it in the way that a woman wants water in the eighth hour of a summer day, and she had built a house and a book and a name in order to be in a position to want it, and she had been refused, and she had said to a great many people that the refusal did not matter, and she had not believed a word of it. She had imagined this moment perhaps four hundred times, and in every one of the imaginings she had been a good deal more dignified than she was now.

The clause was exact. *Freeborn in all courts and in all places, as though she had been born free and had never borne the stain of servitude.*

She would be, in law, a woman of free parents, born in a house she had never seen, of a father whose name she did not know. There had never been a room behind a tablinum. There had never been eleven hours. There had never been a lamp held by a young man of twenty-six, or an arm that did not go above the shoulder.

And there had never been a manumission on the eleventh day before the Kalends of December in the year 93.

The whole case for her daughter's freedom had rested on that manumission. It was the first thing Gallus had said in the basilica; it had been read out to the court. If the register was corrected and the manumission cancelled as made in error, then the record that a free woman had borne a free daughter in September of 94 would be a record of something that had never happened. The girl's decree would stand, because a decree is a decree. But it would stand on a fiction, and any man who wanted to be unpleasant about it in twenty years could say that the whole case had been a confusion.

She was still standing at the window when Tullia came in.

"The box," Tullia said. "The condition."

"Yes."

"It is on the other leaf."

"I know."

"Are you going to tell me what it is, or am I going to stand here until the lamp is lit?"

Antonia turned.

"The condition," she said, "is that I deposit the book of the house with the imperial treasury. All of it. The four hundred and six names, the amounts, the reasons. It is to be sealed and kept in the archive of the ab epistulis and not opened, the office says, except by the order of the emperor or his successor in a matter of public necessity." She held up the leaf. "The phrase is *in a matter of public necessity*. There is no definition. There has never been a definition of that phrase in the history of the office, and there is not going to be one."

Tullia sat down slowly on the chest.

"Then it is not an archive," she said. "It is a drawer. And the drawer belongs to them."

"Yes."

"And every man in the book —"

"Every man in the book has paid me for something he wanted forgotten," Antonia said, "and most of them have behaved well since, and a dozen of them have done things that would put them in a cell, and one of them is a consular who offered me four hundred thousand sesterces in the year 95 to leave Rome, and one of them is a woman who has since paid for a temple. If the drawer is opened, the men who bought and sold seals will find their names in it, and the men who bought a quiet evening and a good dinner will find their names in it too, and there will be no difference made between them, and I will have handed the machine its list."

"And if you say no?"

"Then the rescript is not sealed," Antonia said. "The condition is on the rescript. It is not a bargain that can be separated; it is one piece of papyrus."

Tullia looked at the floor.

"Say no," she said.

"I have wanted it since the day I was manumitted. I have wanted it more than the house and more than the money, and there was a night in October when I sat in a shed behind a fuller's yard and thought: if I lose the case and the house and the girl, at least let me have the name."

"And now you have it, and it costs the book."

Antonia stood at the window with the rescript in her hand.

"No," she said. "It does not cost the book. It costs a copy of the book."

Tullia looked up.

"The condition says that I deposit the book of the house with the imperial treasury," Antonia said. "It does not say that I deposit the original. I have a boy in the Subura who copies documents for two sesterces a page. I will have the whole of it copied, all three hundred and eleven pages, in a fair hand, with the pages that are not accounts left out — the numbers, the six words about the stone room, the name that is crossed out, all of that stays with me. And the copy goes into the treasury, sealed, with the four hundred and six names of the house's clients in it, and if the office opens the drawer in a matter of public necessity, then what the office gets is the names of a great many men who paid a woman for a good evening, and I will have written in the front of it, in my own hand, what the book is, so that no man who opens it can pretend that he has found a list of criminals."

"You will give four hundred people to the office."

"I will give the office a copy of what four hundred people already knew that I knew," Antonia said. "It is not the same as giving them to the office, and it is not nothing either. They have been paying me for years to keep their secrets in a strongbox, and the strongbox is going to have a second key, and the second key will be the emperor's. Half of them will stop coming to the house, and the other half will come more often, because a secret kept in a drawer in the palace is a secret that a man can do nothing about, and a secret kept by a woman with a book is a secret that a man can buy."

Tullia looked at her.

"That is a very cold piece of arithmetic," she said.

"It is. I am good at arithmetic. It is the only thing I have ever been good at." Antonia put the rescript down. "In the morning I am going to the Palatine to tell Callirhoe the word, and then I am going to find the boy, and we are going to spend six weeks copying three hundred and eleven pages. And then I will be a woman of free parents, and my mother, who died when I was six, will become a person who was never a slave, and I will have no idea what to do with that."

* * *

Plotina received her in the garden, in the third week of August, on a day so hot that the box hedges had gone yellow at the tips.

She was sitting on the bench with the mended arm, in a plain dress, with her shoes off, which Antonia had not expected and never afterward forgot.

"You said yes."

"I said yes. With the copy."

"It is better than I hoped," Plotina said. "I thought you would refuse the whole thing and make a speech at me. I had prepared three answers to it and one of them was quite good." She waved at the bench. "Sit down. Do not stand there being respectful. I have been respected all morning."

Antonia sat down.

"The office will read the copy," she said. "You know that."

"Of course they will read it. They will read it in the first week and make a list and lose the list, and in two years someone will find it in a cupboard and not know what it is." Plotina turned her head. "I am going east with him in October. Did you know? Every empress since Livia has stayed in Rome, and I am going. He will have a war to fight and a hundred cities to be flattered in, and there will be no one in his tent at night who is not afraid of him."

"You are afraid of him."

"I have been married to him for thirty-five years and have never once been afraid of him, and that is the whole of what I know about marriage." Plotina drew her feet up under her. "I am telling you because in September you will be a woman of free parents, and in a year you will find that it has made very little difference to anything, and that the difference it has made is in a room you did not expect. That is what happens to people who get what they want. They find out what it was for."

"And what was it for? For you."

"When I was thirty-one I walked into this palace in the wrong shoes and the wife of the consul looked at them, and I have spent thirty years in rooms where the only thing that mattered was that a woman should be looked at and not listened to. And then a woman came to my gate with a book of accounts and no patience at all, and I found that I could do a thing that no one had asked me to do, in a way that no one could stop, and that there was no one in the world to thank me for it." She looked at Antonia. "That is what it was for. It was for the doing. There is no other reward and there never was, and every woman in this city who has ever got anything done knows it, and none of them say it, because it sounds like complaining."

Antonia sat in the empress's garden with her shoes on and her hands in her lap.

"I will not see you again," Plotina said. "I will be in Antioch and then in Ctesiphon if the gods are kind, and by the time we come back I will be an old woman with opinions about roads. You will be fifty and you will have a third book and a house with a new door, and there will be a girl who calls you something you have not heard yet."

"You know about that."

"I know about a great many things that people think I do not know about. It is my only accomplishment." Plotina stood up. "Do not come to the gate. Callirhoe will be unreasonable about it for a week."

"Domina. Thank you. For the rescript. And for the shoes."

Plotina looked at her.

"Get out of my garden," she said, "before I say something that will embarrass us both."

* * *

The emperor left for the east in the last week of October, and the whole city went down to the gates to see it.

Antonia did not go. She had seen a triumph and two funerals and a dedication, and she had learned that a great man going away looks very much like a great man arriving, and that the crowd is the same crowd.

She spent the morning going through the autumn accounts. The house had taken eleven thousand in September, down from the year before; four of the regular clients had stopped coming and six had started, and one of the six had asked at the door whether it was true that the book was in the treasury, and had been told that it was, and had come in.

At noon Dessa came in with the second book.

It was a cheap codex of six quires, sewn badly, with a wooden cover, and it had eleven villages in it so far, and the fourth quire was Dessa's own, written down over three months with the gate on the east and the fourteen houses and the stone that had been there before the Romans came. There were five other Dacians in the city whose villages were in it, and the men had cried when asked and then had told it in more detail than anyone wanted.

"Twelve," Dessa said. "The man from the Via Lata came this morning. His village had a mill."

"Write it down."

"I have written it down." Dessa set the codex on the table. "Domina. I want to say a thing badly, because if I try to say it well I will not say it. I have been your slave for seven years, and you have fed me and taught me to read and got me out of a cell, and I am not grateful." She looked up. "Gratitude is what a slave feels when he is not beaten, and it is a small thing that men like because it costs them nothing. What I feel is bigger than gratitude and it has no name in Latin that I know."

Antonia looked at her.

"Then I will have to give you a name," she said, "and it will not be gratitude, and it will not be a name with my house in it, because you have a country and a language and a mill in your village and you do not need mine."

She opened the drawer and took out the manumission, written in September with the witnesses and the seals, and kept in the drawer for six weeks, waiting for a day when it would not look like a payment.

"Take it," she said. "You are free from the day it is entered in the register, and I have entered it and paid the fee, and you do not owe me anything. You do not have to leave and you do not have to stay, and if you stay you will be paid a wage like a free woman, and it will be a good wage, and you will be able to tell me what you think of it."

Dessa took the tablet with both hands and looked at it for a long moment.

Her right hand did not close. She held it in her left and turned it over and read the words, slowly, the way she read everything, with her lips moving.

"The gate was on the east," she said.

"What?"

"The clerk has written *freedwoman of the household of Antonia Cassia* and he has not written my country, because there is no line for it." Dessa put the tablet down. "In the spring I am going north. There is a man in the Piscina Publica going to Aquincum with a wagon of glass, and he will take a free woman who can keep accounts and pay her passage. I want to see whether the stone is still by the gate."

"The village was burned in the year of the war."

"I know," Dessa said. "I want to see whether the stone is still by the gate."

Antonia looked at the codex of villages on the table.

"Then take a copy of that book with you," she said, "and write down what is in the ground, and what the people who came after have built on it, and whether the well is where it was. And when you come back — if you come back — I will put it in the third book, and there will be a page in the Latin library with your village on it that has the gate where the gate was."

Dessa looked at her.

"I will come back," she said. "There is nothing in Dacia but a stone and a hillside. I have a book and a wage and a room with a window in it. I will not live in a burned village to prove a point."

* * *

The door was the last thing.

It had been hung in March, and it was iron and oak with real gilt and a bronze knocker in the shape of a lion's head, and it was, everyone agreed, the finest door in the Subura. Antonia did not like it. The old door had been patched at the bottom, with studs of iron painted to look like bronze, and it had fooled people, which was the point. The new door did not fool anyone.

In the last week of October, on the day the emperor's column went out of the Porta Capena and the whole city was at the gates, someone knocked on the new door at the ninth hour.

Vitalis came into the garden.

"Domina. There is a woman at the door."

"There are a great many women at the door."

"She says her name is Publia Cassia, and that she is —" Vitalis hesitated. "She says that you will know her, and that she has left her husband's house, and that she has nowhere to go, and that she is not asking for money."

Antonia stood in the peristyle with the watering can in her hand.

She thought about a door in April, and a girl in a blue dress with her veil down asking, *will you help me*, and a woman saying, *I will think about it*, and then thinking about it for seven months.

"Show her in," she said.

Publia came into the garden in a plain travelling cloak with the hood back and no jewelry at all, eight months gone with a child, and she stood in the middle of the peristyle with her chin up and looked at Antonia the way her mother looked at people.

"I have left him," she said. "He has never beaten me. He has been perfectly correct for ten months and he will be kind to the child, and I cannot stay in that house another day. It is a house where nothing is ever said, and I have begun to hear the nothing at night."

"And your mother?"

"Gone to her sister's at Tibur. She told me I had made my bed, and then she sat in the dark for two hours and came up and gave me her purse. It is not enough, and I have nowhere to go."

Antonia put the watering can down.

"Do you know what you are asking me? You are asking me to take in the daughter of the man who held my daughter for eighteen years, with a child in you that will be born here in a month and will have a claim on the name I spent a year in the courts making worth something."

"Yes."

"I have thought about nothing else for three weeks," Publia said, "and I will not pretend that my father was a good man. I am asking for a room. I will pay for it out of my mother's purse, and if you want me gone in the morning I will go in the morning."

Antonia looked at her for a long moment.

"Vitalis," she said, "take the lady's things up to the room at the top of the stairs, the one with the window on the garden. And tell Rufina that there will be one more for dinner and that she is to make the fish, not the pork, because the lady is with child and pork lies heavy."

Vitalis went.

Publia did not move.

"Why?" she said.

"Because I have been the woman at the door," Antonia said, "and no one who has been it should ever do it to anybody else. It is not noble. It is arithmetic."

* * *

At the eleventh hour Myrtale came down from Ficulea, where she had been for a year, and where she had a room and a wage and a man who kept bees and who was, everyone agreed, very slow.

She came into the garden where Antonia was sitting with the lamp and the second book and a cup of wine that she had not drunk, and she sat down on the bench at the end of the peristyle without being asked, and she looked at the new door and the lamp on its bracket and the shadows of the vine on the wall.

"There is a woman in the room at the top of the stairs," she said. "Vitalis told me. He tells me everything. It is his only fault."

"Publia."

"Publia." Myrtale was quiet for a moment. "She was the only one in that house who was kind to me, and she was kind in the way a person is kind who does not know what she is being kind to. She gave me her old shoes. Once she cried in front of me because a bird had flown into the window, and I held her hand. She was the daughter of the house and I was her maid, and neither of us knew that we were sisters."

"You are not sisters."

"We are not sisters," Myrtale said. "That is what I have come to say. I have been trying to say it for a year, in the room at Ficulea, at night, to the wall."

Antonia waited.

"I have been angry with you," Myrtale said, "and I have been right to be angry, and I am not going to give it up because it is the only thing I have that is entirely mine. But I have thought about the room behind the court, and the clerk with the tablet, and the three names. You did not give me my name. The law gave me my name. You gave me a choice, and you told me the truth about both of the names, and then you let me choose." She looked at Antonia. "Nobody in that house ever let me choose anything. Not what I wore, not what I ate, not what I was called. And you gave me three names and the truth and a pen."

"Myrtale."

"I am not finished. I have been practising this." Myrtale took a breath. "I am not going to call you mother. I have tried it and it does not come out, and it may never come out, and I will not say a word to you that is not true. But there is a word I have found, and it is the word I chose in the room behind the court, and it is mine now, and I am going to use it, and you will have to put up with it."

She turned on the bench.

"Cassia," she said.

Antonia sat in the garden of her house with the lamp and the book and the cup of wine she had not drunk, and her daughter said her name, and it was not the name her manumittor had given her, or the name a dead man in marble had paid for, or the name the city had made for her out of a brothel and a debt.

It was the name she had made herself, and it was in the register of the fourteenth region, and it was on the page of a book in a room with a strongbox, and it had been said in the senate house of Rome, and there was a girl of nineteen sitting at the end of the bench who had chosen it and would carry it when she was dead.

"All right," Antonia said. "Cassia will do."

* * *

They sat in the garden until the second hour of the night.

Rufina brought out the fish and the bread and the good wine, and Hilarus came up from the yard with the news from the gates, which was that the emperor had got as far as the ninth milestone and the carts had blocked the road and a donkey had kicked a praetorian, and that the whole of Rome had laughed. Publia came down for an hour and ate a little and went up again. Tullia came out with the household accounts and read them aloud, because she had been keeping them for twenty years and was not going to stop on a night like this one.

At the third hour the lamp was low, and Myrtale had fallen asleep on the bench with her head against the wall, and Antonia sat with the second book open on her knee and the pen in her hand.

She turned to the first page, which was blank, and she wrote:

*The house on the Clivus Suburanus, in the fourteenth region of the city of Rome, in the nineteenth year of the reign of Trajan. A copy of the first book of this house is in the treasury of the emperor, and the first book is in the strongbox, and the second is here and is not finished, and the third is not yet written. The door is new and the gilt on it is real, and the door within the door is in the threshold where it has always been, and everything that is written in this house is written because a woman in the Subura learned to read and was not content.*

She blotted it, and closed the book, and sat for a while listening to the city.

Beyond the door the Subura went on with its noise: the carts and the dogs and the men coming up from the Forum with their boots on the stone, the woman two doors down who sang badly and knew it. The gilt on the door caught the lamp and gave it back, and from the Clivus, at the right angle and not too close, the house of Antonia Cassia looked like the entrance to a magistrate's house.

She got up and put out the lamp, and woke her daughter, and they went in.
