The house of the Cassii had a garden with an old vine in it, and in the evening, when the sun had gone off the wall and the bees had stopped, the vine made a green shade that smelled of dust and sweetness, and it was the best place in Rome to sit if you were a slave with an hour to yourself.

Prima sat there most evenings. She was nineteen, and she was the ornatrix of the household, which meant that she dressed the hair of the mistress and the daughters and, on the days when the old lady was well enough, the old lady too. She had a good eye and a light hand and a memory for what a woman wanted to look like, and she had, in nine years, made herself necessary.

That was the whole of her position, and she knew it. She had been born in the room behind the kitchen to a woman whose name she was never told, and she had been put out to a nurse in the country at three days old, and brought back at six, and put in the kitchen, and told to keep out of the way. At eight she had been found reading a shopping list by the cook, who had said, *the girl can read, do not tell the master*, and had taught her sums. At twelve she had been taken into the household as a maid to the old lady's attendant, and had learned hair. At fifteen she had been given a room of her own behind the kitchen, which was more than any other slave in the house had, and a lamp, and a stool.

She had been proud of the room. She had been proud of the lamp.

The old lady was Cassia Severa, the mother of the household, and she was sixty-one and had a disease of the joints that had turned her hands into knots. She had been a beauty in the reign of Nero and she still dressed like one, and she had decided, for reasons of her own, that Prima was worth teaching. She taught her to read Latin properly, and to write a good hand, and to keep quiet when the men were talking, and to know the difference between a thing that was said to be heard and a thing that was said to be repeated.

"Women in this house," the old lady said once, "are either furniture or weather. Be weather."

Prima did not know what that meant, and thought about it for years afterward, and understood it at last when she was thirty and rich and had learned to keep every door in a house locked.

* * *

The young master came home in the spring of that year from the army.

He had been away three years, on the Danube, as a tribune of a legion, and he came back brown and quiet with his hair cut short and a way of standing that made the room he was in seem smaller. He was twenty-six. His father met him in the atrium and embraced him and said, in front of the household, "You have come back a Roman," and the young master said, "I have come back," and the two of them looked at each other for a moment in a way that Prima, standing at the back with the other slaves, did not understand until much later.

She served him wine that night at dinner. He looked at her once, and she looked away, and that was all.

For a month it was all.

He walked in the garden in the evenings. She sat under the vine in the evenings. For a month they sat at opposite ends of the same garden and did not speak, and then one evening he said, "You are the one who reads."

"Yes, dominus."

"My mother says you read better than my sister."

"The lady is kind."

"The lady is not kind. The lady is accurate." He sat down on the bench, at the other end, with a distance between them that was almost a courtesy. "Do you read the poets?"

"I read what is in the house."

"Then you read the tax rolls and the household accounts."

"And the poets. When the steward throws them out."

The young master laughed. It was a good laugh, and it was surprised out of him, and it was the first time she had heard it.

That was the beginning. It went on for four months, and it was, for most of it, a thing of the garden and the lamp: he would come out in the evening and sit at the other end of the bench, and they would talk about the poets and the army and the family, and he would tell her things he had never told anyone, and she would listen the way she had been trained to listen, with her hands folded and her face attentive, and it was not entirely a lie.

By the fourth month it was not a bench with a distance on it. It was the two of them in the passage outside her room with the lamp on the wall, and his hands on her waist, and her hand flat on his chest, and the words he said into her hair, at night, in a voice that had nothing of the senate house in it.

* * *

She was not a fool. She had been in the household nine years and she knew what happened to a slave who was taken by the young master: she knew the three cases in the house's own history, and she knew where two of the three women had gone. She knew that a man's affection was a room with a door and that the door was on his side.

And she was nineteen, and she was lonely in a way she had no words for, and she had been read to only twice in her life, and he read to her.

It lasted four months.

In the month of September, in the fourth month, a man came to the house at the eleventh hour with a folded paper and asked for the master, and was told that the master was at his villa at Tibur and would not be back till the morning, and said that he would wait, and waited in the tablinum with a cup of wine, and left before dawn, and on his way out through the passage behind the kitchen he met Prima.

He was a fat man with a good cloak and a ring with a horse on it, and he spoke to her pleasantly for a moment about the weather, and then he said, "You are the young master's girl."

"I am the lady's ornatrix."

"You are the young master's girl," he said, "and you should be careful, because the young master's father is a man who does not like to be surprised." He smiled. "I am a friend of the house. Tell the young master that Didius Probus left the paper on the table."

She told him. She told the young master in the garden the next evening, and he went white, and he went into the house, and he came back an hour later and said that it was nothing, that it was a business matter, that she was not to worry, and that she was not to speak of the man to anyone.

Two days later the young master came to her in the passage and took her by the arm, hard, and said, "What did you hear?"

"Nothing, dominus. I heard nothing."

"You were in the passage. You were there. What did you hear?"

"I heard a man say that your father does not like to be surprised, and I heard him laugh, and I heard him go out."

"That is all?"

"That is all."

He let go of her arm. His hands were shaking. He said, "If anyone asks you — anyone at all, my father, the steward, anyone — you say that you were in the kitchen, and you did not see the man, and you did not speak to him."

"Dominus."

"Say it."

"I was in the kitchen," she said. "I did not see the man. I did not speak to him."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he said, "I am sorry," and went away, and she stood in the passage with her hand on her arm where he had held her, and did not understand what she had just promised.

* * *

The man Didius Probus was found dead in his bath nine days later, and the whole city said that he had drowned, and the whole city said it in the way Rome said things, which is to say that nobody believed it and everybody repeated it.

The old master came back from Tibur on the day the body was found. He came into the house at the fourth hour and shut himself in the tablinum with the young master and the steward, and they were in there for two hours, and when they came out the young master's face was grey.

Prima was in the passage. She was always in the passage; it was where the work was. And when the old master came out he stopped and looked at her, and she had been in the house nine years and she had never once been looked at by him in that way — not with desire, which she had learned to expect, but with the appraising look of a man who is deciding what a thing is worth.

"You," he said. "The ornatrix. Come here."

She came.

"You were in the passage," he said, "on the night the man Probus came to the house."

"Yes, dominus."

"Did you speak to him?"

"No, dominus."

"You did not speak to him."

"No, dominus."

The old man looked at her for a long moment. Then he said, "We shall see," and went into the atrium, and Prima stood in the passage and felt the floor go soft under her.

* * *

They came for her at the fourth hour of the morning, nine days later, when the house was asleep and the lamps had burned down, and they took her from her room behind the kitchen and along the passage to the small room at the back of the tablinum, where the steward kept the wine and where the walls were stone.

There were four of them. The old master stood by the window. The young master stood by the door with a lamp in his hand. There was a man with a rope, who was not a slave of the household and who did not give his name, and there was a clerk with tablets in the corner, who was old and had ink on his fingers and would not look at her.

"Prima," the old master said. "You are of this household, and you will answer what you are asked."

"Yes, dominus."

"Did you see the man Didius Probus in this house?"

"Yes, dominus."

"Did you speak to him?"

"Yes, dominus."

"What did he say to you?"

"He said that he was a friend of the house, and that the young master's father did not like to be surprised, and that the young master's girl should be careful."

The clerk wrote. The old master did not move his eyes from her face.

"And what did you say to the young master?"

"I told him what the man had said."

"And what did the young master say to you?"

"He said that it was a business matter and that I was not to speak of it."

"And what else did he say?"

"He said that if anyone asked me, I was to say that I was in the kitchen and had not seen the man."

The room was quiet. The young master's lamp shook, and the light moved on the wall.

The old master said, "Again."

"Dominus?"

"From the beginning," he said. "And this time, we will do it properly."

* * *

It went on for eleven hours.

They did not do it all at once. That was the thing she had not known, and would spend years trying to explain to people who had not been in the room: that it was not one long act but a series of them, with intervals, and in the intervals they gave her water and the clerk sharpened his pen and the old master sat down and read what had been written and said, "Again."

The man with the rope was not cruel. That was the other thing. He was a professional, and he worked carefully, and twice he stopped and said to the old master, "She will not last if we go on at that rate," and the old master said, "Then go slower."

And the young master stood by the door with the lamp in his hand.

He did not speak. Not once, in eleven hours. She looked at him the first time, and he looked away, and after that she did not look at him again, because there was a thing in his face that was worse than the rope, and she was nineteen years old and she understood, in the first hour, exactly what it meant: that he would not stop this, and that he would remember not stopping it, and that he would not be able to look at her again without remembering, and that this would be his reason, afterward, for everything he did.

Somewhere in the fifth hour she began to answer before they asked, which is a thing the body does, and the clerk wrote it down, and the old master said, "Good."

Somewhere in the eighth hour she heard herself say the young master's name, and heard the clerk write it, and heard the old master say, "Again," and she understood that he had known all of it before he began.

And in the eleventh hour, the old master said, "Enough," and the man with the rope untied her, and she fell off the board onto the floor, and lay there on the stone with her cheek against the cold, and the old master came and stood over her.

"You are a clever girl," he said. "You have been clever all night. That is why you are alive."

She looked up at him.

"If you had lied," he said, "I would have had you killed. Do you understand? A slave who lies in this house is a slave who will be sold to a man who will make her tell the truth, and then she will tell it in a market, and then it will be a story. But a slave who tells the truth is a slave who can be trusted with a silence." He knelt down beside her, and his voice was almost gentle. "You are going to be freed. Not because you have earned it. Because a freedwoman cannot be put to the question, and a freedwoman's word in a court is worth nothing, and because a man who frees the witness has bought a silence that no one can accuse him of buying."

He stood up.

"Take her to the room behind the kitchen," he said, "and have the physician attend her, and tell my wife that the girl is to be manumitted before the Kalends of December, and that the reason is her long and faithful service."

The young master took a step forward, and the old master turned on him.

"You will not go to her," he said. "You will not speak to her. You will not look at her. You have done enough for this family in one month to last a lifetime, and if I hear that you have been in the passage behind the kitchen, I will send you to your uncle's estate in Africa and you will not come back."

The young master put the lamp down on the table. His hand was shaking so badly that the flame guttered.

Then he came and knelt on the stone floor beside her, and put his arms around her, and lifted her, and carried her along the passage to the room behind the kitchen, and set her down on the pallet, and sat with his back against the door until morning.

She remembered his hands. She remembered them for eighteen years.

* * *

The physician came at noon and put a salve on her back and told her that she would heal, and that the arm would not lift above the shoulder, and that she should not be surprised if the skin never sweated in that place again.

The old lady came in the afternoon, with her knots of hands folded, and stood in the door of the room and looked at her for a long time.

"You are going to be freed," she said.

"Yes, domina."

"My husband is a coward," the old lady said, "and my son is a coward, and I have lived in this house for forty years and I have been a coward too, and this is the one thing I can do." She looked at the room, at the pallet, at the jug, at the nail in the wall. "When you are free, you will have a name and a trade and nothing else. You will find that freedom is a room you have to pay for. Do not come back to this house."

"I have nowhere to go."

"Then find somewhere." The old lady put a small purse on the stool. "That is my own money. It is not from him. There are two hundred sesterces in it and it will keep you for a month if you are careful and a week if you are not. And Prima —" she stopped.

"Yes, domina?"

"Whatever he gives you, afterward, take it," the old lady said. "Take it and do not thank him. A man like my son will give you things for the rest of his life, and every one of them will be a payment, and if you refuse one he will think you are asking for something better."

She went out.

The manumission was done on the eleventh day before the Kalends of December, before the praetor, with the rod, in the company of the steward and two witnesses. The old lady paid the tax. The record was made, and Prima's name was written in it, and the name was the only thing that was given to her that day that she kept.

* * *

She found a room above a fuller's shop on the Aventine, for eleven sesterces a month, and she found work: hair, in three houses, for women who paid her by the visit and never asked her to stay for dinner. She was good at it. She had always been good at it.

The young master came in January.

He came at night, with a cloak and no slave, and stood in the lane below her window and would not go away, and she went down because a man standing in a lane looking at a window is a scandal and a scandal is a thing that closes doors.

"Antonia," he said.

"My name is Prima."

"Prima." He stood in the mud of the lane with his cloak pulled round him. "I have brought you money."

"I do not want your money."

"Take it."

"No."

"Then I will leave it on the step."

"And I will give it to the fuller's wife, and she will tell the whole street that the young master of the Cassii leaves money on the steps of houses at night."

He was silent for a moment. Then he laughed, and it was the laugh she remembered from the garden, and it was a terrible thing to hear in that lane.

"What do you want?" he said.

"I want to be left alone."

"That is a lie."

"It is not a lie."

"You sat in the garden with me for four months," he said. "You talked to me. You let me —"

"I was a slave," Prima said. "You were the young master. If I had told you to go away, what would you have done?"

He did not answer.

"Now I am free," she said, "and I am telling you to go away, and you are still standing in the lane."

He looked at her.

"I love you," he said.

"Do not," she said, and shut the door.

* * *

He came back in March. He came back in April and in May, and in June he came with a fever and she took him in because a man with a fever in a lane at night is a dead man, and because she had been nineteen in a garden and had not forgotten it, and because the room was small and the lamp was hers and the door was on her side of the room.

In July she knew.

She knew in the way that women know, and she sat on the bed in the room above the fuller's shop with her hand flat on her belly and did the arithmetic, and the arithmetic was a house on the Viminal and a man with a name and a girl with no name at all.

She did not tell him for a month. When she told him, in August, in the garden of a tavern on the Aventine where nobody knew either of them, he put down his cup and looked at her and said, "Are you certain?"

"Yes."

He was quiet for a long time.

"I will provide for it," he said.

"It."

"The child. I will find a house, and a nurse, and —"

"You will find a house," Prima said. "And a nurse. And the child will be a thing you provide for, and I will be a thing you provided for, and in ten years there will be a girl in a house somewhere who does not know her mother."

"That is not —"

"That is exactly what will happen. I have watched it happen in your father's house three times."

He looked at her. He was twenty-seven years old and he was going to be consul, and he had been raised in a room that smelled of ink, and he had held a lamp for eleven hours, and his hands were shaking on the table.

"Then tell me what to do," he said.

She looked at him for a long moment.

"Marry me," she said.

He did not answer. He did not laugh, or look away, or make a speech. He sat in the garden of a tavern on the Aventine with his hands on the table and looked at her, and the silence went on, and in the silence she saw the whole of the rest of her life.

"A senator cannot marry a freedwoman," he said at last.

"No."

"Not even —"

"Not even," Prima said. "Not even if she is rich. Not even if she is respectable. Not even if she has borne him a child. It is not a rule, it is a law, and the law is not a wall, it is a floor. You can stand on it. You cannot go under it."

"Prima."

"Do not say my name like that."

"Then what?" he said. "What am I to do?"

She looked at him across the table.

"Nothing," she said. "You are to do nothing. You are to go to the senate house and be a Roman, and I am to go home and have this child, and you are to think about her every day for the rest of your life, and you are to be a man who thinks about her and does nothing, and that is what you are, and I have known it since the fourth month and I have been pretending otherwise because it was pleasant in the garden."

She got up and went out, and he did not follow her, and it was September, and the light on the Aventine was the color of old honey, and she walked home alone.

* * *

The birth was in the middle of the month, in the room above the fuller's, with a midwife from Ficulea called Iusta who had been recommended by the fuller's wife, and it took nine hours, and at the end of it there was a girl.

The midwife put her in Prima's arms and went to wash her hands, and Prima lay on the bed with the child against her chest and looked at her for a long time. She had a great deal of dark hair and a nose that was not anybody's yet, and a small crescent mark on the inside of her wrist, and her hand closed on Prima's finger and would not let go.

"You are not a thing," Prima said to her. "You are not a thing that a man provides for. You are a person, and your name is —"

She stopped. She had no name to give. A slave's child takes the mother's condition, and a freedwoman's child is free, and free people have three names and a family and a shrine and a record, and she had none of it; she had a room above a fuller's shop and a purse with two hundred sesterces and a name that had been written in a record in December.

"Your name is Cassia," she said, "because I have nothing else to give you, and a name is a thing that nobody can take away if you write it down."

She wrote it down. She wrote it on the back of a tablet, and she drew a door on the front of the tablet, and a wreath, and she took it to a man in the Subura who struck lead tokens for the dealers, and she paid him four sesterces to strike two halves of one token and break them across the middle.

* * *

The father came in November, and stood in the room with his hands at his sides and looked at the child in the cradle, and did not touch her.

"She is small," he said.

"She is three months old."

"She looks like my mother."

"She looks like a baby."

He sat down on the stool by the bed. He had brought a purse, and he put it on the table, and she did not look at it.

"I have found a house," he said.

"For what?"

"For the child. A nurse in the country, near Ficulea. A good woman. She will be well fed, and she will be taught, and when she is six she will be brought into the household."

Prima sat up.

"No."

"Prima —"

"She is not going into that house."

"She will be with me. She will be in the household, where I can see her, where I can —"

"Where you can see her," Prima said. "Where you can look at her across the garden and not say a word to her, and where she will grow up as a maid in her father's house and never know it, and where your wife will look at her every day and know what she is and never say it, and where she will be sold the moment you are dead or bored or need money for a consulship." She was shaking. "I have been in that house since I was six years old. I know exactly what happens to the girl in the passage. I was the girl in the passage."

"It will not be like that."

"It will be exactly like that."

He sat on the stool with his hands on his knees.

"Then what?" he said. "You cannot keep her. You have eleven sesterces a month for the room and no work while you are nursing and a child who will be four before you can leave her to go to a house and do a woman's hair. You will be on the street by the spring, and then you will be in a house worse than mine, and she will be sold to whoever will take her, and I will not be able to stop it. You know that I am right."

She looked at him.

She knew that he was right. She had known it for three months, in the way that a woman knows the ice is thin, and she had been walking on it with the child in her arms and telling herself that the water was warm.

"What is the arrangement?" she said.

Her voice was flat. She had heard that voice in other women, in the house, and had never thought she would use it.

"The nurse in the country until she is six," he said. "Then the household. She will be a maid to my sister's daughter, when my sister has a daughter, and she will be brought up with her, and she will be taught to read and to keep accounts, and she will have a room and food and a trade. And when she is sixteen I will free her, and give her a dowry, and marry her to a freedman of the household, and she will be a free woman in Rome with a name and a trade, and nobody will ever know."

"And me?"

"You will have money," he said. "Every month. For the rest of your life. And you will be able to see her —"

"No."

"Prima —"

"No. If I see her, I will want her, and if I want her I will do something, and if I do something you will take her away where I cannot find her." She looked at the child in the cradle. "I would rather know where she is and not go. That is the arrangement. Those are my terms. I will not come to the house, I will not write, I will not stand at the corner, and you will bring me word once a year that she is alive and well, and you will give me your word on the thing you hold most sacred that you will free her at sixteen and give her a name."

Verus looked at her for a long time.

"I swear it," he said, "by the household gods of the Cassii."

"Then take her," Prima said.

* * *

He came for the child three days later, with the nurse, in a litter, at the sixth hour, when the lane was empty because the whole street was at dinner.

Prima wrapped her in the good blanket and put the token round her neck, and the door half in the cedar box, and she held her for a long time, and the child slept through all of it, and that was the worst thing: that the child slept.

"Her name is Cassia," she said. "Write it down. Not the name of the house. Her name."

"I will write it down."

"Say it."

"Cassia," he said.

He carried her down the stairs. Prima stood at the window and watched the litter go up the lane, and the fuller's wife came out into the yard and looked up at the window and then went back in, and the lane was empty, and it was November, and the light was going.

She stood at the window until it was dark.

Then she sat down on the floor with her back against the bed and her hand flat on the place where the child had been, and she did not get up for four days.

* * *

On the fifth day the fuller's wife came up and put a bowl of soup on the floor.

"You owe me for the week," she said. "Eleven for the room and four for the wood. If you cannot work you cannot stay, and if you cannot stay you will be on the street, and a woman on the street in this city lasts about a year."

Prima looked at the bowl of soup.

"I can work," she said.

"Then eat, and come down, and wash."

She ate. She went down. She washed clothes for eleven hours a day for a year and a half, and her hands went red and then white and then hard, and she learned to keep accounts in her head while her arms were in the tub, and every month a slave from the house on the Viminal came with a purse and a single sentence: *she is well.*

Then in the year of the fire the fuller's wife died, and her son took the shop, and the son was a gambler, and by the spring Prima owed him eleven months' rent, and he sold the debt — and her with it — to a man in the Subura called Cominius Asiaticus, who kept a house on the Aventine and did not care that she was free, because a freedwoman with a debt and no patron was, in practice, a thing that could be sold.

She was in that house for two years.

She was twenty-two when Gaius Antonius Felix came into it, and twenty-three when he paid her debt, and twenty-six when he died and left her everything, and thirty-nine when she stood in the Hall of the Decemvirs and told a praetor of Rome what had been done to her in the passage behind the kitchen of the house of the Cassii.

But that is another chapter, and this one ends where it began: with a girl of nineteen sitting under a vine in a garden in the evening, with a lamp in her hand and a book in her lap and her whole life in front of her, thinking that she was necessary, and that necessity was the same thing as safety, and that a man who reads to you in a garden is a man who will not destroy you.

She was wrong about all of it, and it took her eighteen years and a fortune to be wrong in comfort.
