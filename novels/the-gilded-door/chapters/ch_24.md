The Nones of December came up grey and cold, with a wind off the river that got into the Subura and stayed there.

Antonia was up before the light. She washed in cold water because the furnace had gone out in the night and there was no one to light it, and she put on the blue stola and the grey mantle and the plain gold pins, and she looked at herself in the bronze for a while and found a woman of thirty-nine with grey in her hair and a mouth that had set in a way she did not entirely like.

Four of the seven women had come back from the prefect's office on the afternoon of the senate, with their names written in the register and their clothes still smelling of the cells. Meroe was still held, because Meroe had been bought in Puteoli and the bill of sale was in Puteoli, and a clerk had decided that this required a letter. Rufina had come back and gone straight to the kitchen and started cooking, and Cela had come back and cried for two hours and then slept for fourteen, and the youngest, who was fifteen and whose name was Sabina, had come back and asked Antonia whether she was going to be sold, and had been told no, and had not believed it, and had asked again in the morning.

And the book was gone.

The book had gone out of the house on the morning of the raid in a bag with a clerk's writing on it, and it was in the prefect's office, and the prefect's office had made no answer to three letters from Gallus and one from the consul. Tullia had a man in the office who had been feeding her information for six years, and the man said that the book was in a cupboard in the second room and that nobody had been authorized to read it, and that the prefect had said, in front of four clerks, that he would rather be eaten by dogs than be the man who opened that cupboard.

"But he will open it," Tullia said, in the doorway of the bedroom, while Antonia put her pins in. "He will open it because the emperor's freedmen will make him, and then four hundred names will be in the hands of an office, and half of them are men who pay us and the other half are men who would like to see us ruined, and after today nobody will be able to tell which is which."

"I know."

"Then what do we do?"

"We go to court," Antonia said, "and we get the girl free, and then we spend the rest of the winter getting the book back."

"And if we do not get it back?"

"Then we have lost a book," Antonia said, "and I will have to start another one, and I am thirty-nine years old and I have a good memory and I have kept the names in my head for twenty years. A book is a thing that can be lost. That is what makes it a book and not a wall." She turned from the bronze. "Where is the chair?"

"In the lane. Hilarus has been standing beside it since the sixth hour, and he is cold, and he has been telling the bearers about his sister."

* * *

The Basilica Julia at the second hour of the Nones was fuller than it had been on the Ides.

The crowd was three deep at the doors and there were men on the steps and men on the roof of the colonnade opposite, and the whole south side of the hall was a press of bodies, and the clerks had given up trying to keep the aisles and had put a rope across the back instead.

"They have come to see the verdict," Gallus said.

"They have come to see whether a slave can be freed by a woman who keeps a brothel."

"They have come to see whether the law works," Gallus said. "It is the same thing, in this city, and it is the only entertainment that is free."

Caesennius Rufus was already at the front with his advocate and two of his four witnesses. He looked older than he had in November. His advocate came over while the court was settling, a thin man in a good toga with the careful manner of a man who has been sent to do something unpleasant, and he asked to speak with Gallus in the corner by the door of the clerks' room, and Gallus went, and Antonia went with him because she was not going to stand in a hall and be told afterward.

"My client will withdraw the instrument," the advocate said.

Gallus looked at him.

"Withdraw it how?"

"He will declare in open court that the conveyance was made without his knowledge of the facts, and that he has no wish to hold a free woman, and that he releases any claim he may have, and he will pay the costs of the action and a sum to the person claimed for her trouble." The advocate's face did not move. "Ten thousand sesterces."

"And the instrument?"

"He will surrender it to the court to be cancelled."

"No."

The advocate blinked.

"Domine —"

"No," Gallus said. "I will not have it cancelled. The instrument is the crime. It is signed by Manius Cassius Verus and sealed and witnessed, and it purports to convey a freeborn woman as part of a dowry, and if it is cancelled then there is no evidence that any such document ever existed, and in six months there will be a story in this city that Caesennius Rufus was dragged into a court over a mistake in a marriage contract by a freedwoman with a grudge. I have spent eight months on this case. I do not intend to spend the rest of my life explaining it to men at dinner."

"Then what do you want?"

"I want the instrument read into the record, and I want the court to rule on it, and I want the ruling to say what the law says, which is that a free person cannot be conveyed and that the instrument is evidence of a crime under the lex Fabia." Gallus put his hands behind his back. "And then your client may go home."

The advocate looked at Gallus for a long moment.

"Domine," he said, "my client is fifty-four years old and has a wife who is pregnant and a cousin on the emperor's council, and he did not buy the girl. He married a woman whose father put a line in a contract. If you do this, he is a man who has been publicly attached to a crime of which he is innocent, and he will spend the rest of his life denying it."

"Yes," Gallus said. "He will. And the girl will spend the rest of her life being a woman who was conveyed in a marriage contract, and she will not have the consolation of being innocent, because she was there."

The advocate looked at Antonia.

"Domina," he said. "You are the injured party, in a manner of speaking. Will you not be merciful?"

Antonia looked at the front of the hall, where Caesennius Rufus was standing with his scroll in his hand and his face set in the expression of a man who has been told that the morning will be expensive.

"I have been merciful for eight months," she said. "I have been merciful to the aedile's clerk and to the prefect's office and to a man who held a lamp, and every time I was merciful a girl was taken further away from me. I have run out of mercy. It is not a moral position. It is an inventory."

The advocate looked at her.

"You are a hard woman," he said.

"I am a woman who has been asked to be soft by every man who has ever wanted something from me," Antonia said. "Tell your client that the court convenes in a quarter of an hour, and that if he wishes to be merciful he may be merciful by telling the truth in a loud voice in front of three hundred people, which is the only mercy that has ever done anybody any good."

* * *

The praetor came in at the second hour and the clerks called the hall to order and it took a long time, because the hall was full and the crowd at the back kept pressing forward to see.

"The matter of the person claimed," the praetor said. "The court is in receipt of an instrument produced on the Ides and of the argument of the adsertor. The claimant will speak to the instrument."

Caesennius Rufus came forward with his scroll.

He was not a fool, and he had been advised by a man who was not a fool either, and he had obviously been up most of the night. He said what he had come to say in a flat, clear voice: that when he signed the marriage instrument in August he had been told that the woman Myrtale was a slave of the Cassian household and part of his wife's dowry at a valuation of twelve thousand sesterces; that he had never seen the woman and had no interest in her; that when the case came before the court in November and he heard what was said in the hall, he had gone home and asked his wife's father to explain the transaction; and that he had received no explanation.

"I have been a fool," he said, "and I have been made a fool of, and the instrument is mine because my hand is on it, and I do not ask the court to believe that I am innocent of a crime. I ask the court to believe that I am guilty of nothing but signing a paper that a consular put in front of me at a wedding, and to allow me to withdraw it, and to release the woman, and to accept from me the sum of ten thousand sesterces for her maintenance, and whatever the court thinks fit to add to it."

The hall stirred.

The praetor looked at him for a moment.

"The court accepts the sum and the release," he said, "and the court does not accept the withdrawal."

Rufus's face went the color of tallow.

"Domine —"

"The instrument is before the court," the praetor said, "and the question the court must determine is whether the person claimed was free at the time of the conveyance. The claimant does not get to determine that question by withdrawing. Sit down."

Rufus sat down.

"Adsertor."

Gallus stood up.

"I will be brief," he said, "because the facts are in the record and the court has heard them twice, and because there is a girl in this hall who has been waiting since the Ides of November to be told what she is." He took a breath. "The instrument conveys *a female slave named Myrtale, valued at twelve thousand sesterces*. She was not a slave. She was born free in September of the year 94, in a room above a fuller's shop on the Aventine, to a woman who had been free for less than a year, manumitted before the praetor of this city, whose manumission is in the archive of the fourteenth region. A free woman bore a free daughter, and a free daughter cannot be conveyed, and a document that purports to convey her is not a contract. It is a false statement made with the intent to hold a free person in slavery, and that is a crime under the lex Fabia de plagio. The court knows this. The court knew it in November. The court has been waiting three weeks for the claimant to produce a defence, and the claimant has now told the court, in his own words, that he has none."

He looked at the praetor.

"The court should rule," he said. "And the court should rule in a way that a clerk can write down in a sentence that a man in a street can repeat, because for eighteen years the whole of this city has been repeating a sentence that was written in a tablinum by a man who is now dead, and it is time the city had a better one."

The praetor looked at the papers in front of him for a long moment.

Then he looked up.

"The court finds," he said, "that the person claimed, called Myrtale, was born free, being the daughter of a freedwoman manumitted before her birth, and that she has been held in slavery contrary to law for eighteen years, and that the instrument produced by Caesennius Rufus is void, and being void conveys nothing, and being an instrument that purports to convey a free person it is to be retained by the court and a copy sent to the curator of the lex Fabia." He set the paper down. "The person claimed is free. She is free from this hour, and the court directs that the fact be entered in the register of the fourteenth region, and that a copy of this decree be given to her."

The hall broke open.

It was not applause. It was something older and worse and better: three hundred people making the noise that people make when a thing they have been waiting for arrives, and half of them were pleased and half of them were angry and all of them were loud, and the clerks were shouting, and somewhere at the back a woman screamed, and Rufus's advocate put his hand over his eyes.

Antonia did not hear any of it.

She was looking at Myrtale, who was standing in the middle of the floor in the white dress with the token on its cord, and who had not moved, and whose face was doing something that Antonia had never seen on it before and could not name.

* * *

"The court will complete the form," the praetor said, when the hall had been brought back to order. "The lictor will attend."

This was the oldest part of it, and the part that nobody in the hall had ever seen performed for a woman of eighteen who had been held in a great house for all of her life. It was the form by which a person in slavery was made free before the praetor: the rod laid on the head, the words, the turning, the hand.

The lictor came forward with the vindicta, which was a thin wand of beech, and he stood beside Myrtale, and the praetor said the words of the old formula, and the lictor laid the rod on her head and said, in a voice that carried to the doors, *hunc ego hominem liberum esse aio, et hunc vindicta libero* — I say that this person is free, and I free her by the rod.

And then Myrtale turned.

Antonia had not known that she would turn. Nobody had told her what the form was, and the praetor had not explained it, and she turned because the rod was on her head and there was a hand on her shoulder turning her, and it was the lictor's hand, and she turned the way a door turns on a hinge.

She turned and she was facing the crowd, and Antonia was in the fourth rank on the left, and for a moment their eyes met and neither of them moved.

Then the praetor said, "The person is free. The court is adjourned."

* * *

There was a scene at the door.

There is always a scene at the door. The crowd wanted to see her come out, and the clerks wanted her name for the register, and a man from the office of the ab epistulis wanted to know whether the adsertor would be making a statement, and there was a boy selling hot wine, and two of Caesennius Rufus's witnesses were trying to get out of a side door without being seen.

The clerks took Myrtale into the room behind the court to enter her in the register.

Antonia waited outside with her hands folded and her heart going.

The clerk who came out was a thin, tired man with ink on his fingers, and he came out with his tablet and said, "Domina, there is a difficulty."

"What difficulty?"

"The register requires three names."

"It does."

"She has one. *Myrtale.* She says she has one." The clerk looked at his tablet. "A person freed by the rod takes the name of the man who asserts her freedom, or of her household, or of her father, if she has one. She has no father in law. The adsertor is a consular and has not offered his name, and the court will not assign one, and she has declined the name of the household in which she was held."

"She has declined it?"

"She said, and I wrote it down, that she would not be entered as a freedwoman of that house for any man's convenience." The clerk turned the tablet around. "The clerk's instruction is that we cannot enter a person with one name. It is a matter of the form. The form is four hundred years old and there is no one in this building who can tell me what to do with a woman who has one name and a great deal of self-respect."

Antonia looked at the tablet.

"May I speak to her?"

"You may."

Myrtale was standing in the little room behind the court with the decree in her hand and her chin up and her face still doing the thing Antonia could not name, and she looked at Antonia in the doorway and said, "I am not going to be a Cassia."

"I know."

"They will want to write it. They will want to write Cassia because it is the house and it is respectable and the clerk needs a line to fill. And I will be a Cassia in the register of the fourteenth region for the rest of my life, and every man who reads it will think I was freed by a consular family out of kindness, and no one will ever know what was done."

"Then do not be a Cassia," Antonia said.

Myrtale looked at her.

"What else is there?"

"There is mine," Antonia said. "It is not a family name. I took it from a man who was kind to me and who is dead, and I took the other half of it from the house that owned me, and I have carried it for eighteen years in this city and it has been a door and a shield and a stone around my neck by turns. It is not a great name. No consul has ever had it. It is the name of a freedwoman who kept accounts."

Myrtale's jaw worked.

"And if I take it," she said, "then the register says that I am your daughter."

"Yes."

"Everyone will know."

"Everyone already knows," Antonia said. "There were three hundred men in the senate house yesterday and half of Rome was on the steps this morning. The only question left is whether the register says it, and the register is the thing that lasts."

Myrtale stood in the little room with the decree in her hand and did not answer for a long time.

Then she said, "Antonia."

The clerk wrote it down. *Antonia Myrtale, freed by the rod, daughter of Antonia Cassia, freedwoman, of the fourteenth region.* And then, because the form required a third name and there was no father and no patron, the clerk looked up and said, "The third name?"

"Cassia," Myrtale said.

The clerk's pen stopped.

"You said —"

"I said I would not be called Cassia for that house." Myrtale's voice was steady and her eyes were on Antonia and not on the clerk. "Cassia is my mother's manumittor's name and it is the name of the house that held me, and it is also the name of the woman who died in that house and who freed my mother and who did not know what her son was. I will take it, and I will take it for her, and I will not take her son's."

The clerk wrote it down.

*Antonia Cassia Myrtale.*

* * *

They came out of the basilica into the Forum at the fourth hour, and the whole square was full of the news, which had gone out of the doors before them and was already at the rostra and the steps of the temple and the booths in the Via Julia.

Myrtale walked out between Antonia and Gallus with her decree rolled in her hand, and a man in the front called out, "What is your name?" and Myrtale stopped and said, "Antonia Cassia Myrtale," in a clear voice, and the man said, "That is too many names," and she said, "I have the paper for all of them," and the crowd laughed, and it was the first time Antonia had heard her daughter make a joke in public.

At the bottom of the steps Iusta the midwife was waiting with her hands folded and tears running down her face, and she took Myrtale's face in both her hands and said something in Greek, and Myrtale bent her head and let her say it, and the two of them stood there for a moment in the middle of the Forum with the crowd going past.

Gallus blew his nose.

"Well," he said. "That is the case. Sixteen years of practice and eight months of the worst year of my life, and it took a quarter of an hour, and I am going to go home and sleep for two days."

"Lucius."

"What?"

"Thank you."

Gallus looked at the crowd.

"I did it for the law," he said. "I did it because a man in this city put a line in a contract and I have spent my life believing that a line in a contract is a thing that can be argued with. I did not do it for you, and I did not do it for the girl, and I certainly did not do it for my son." He put his hat on. "But I am glad it is done, and I am going to come to dinner on the ninth day, and you are going to give me the good wine, and we are not going to talk about any of it."

* * *

There was a letter waiting at the house.

It had come at the third hour in the hands of a freedman of the ab epistulis with a seal that had been opened and closed again, and Tullia had put it on the table in the room with the strongbox and stood beside it, because she had read it, having been given leave to read anything that came into the house for twenty years, and because she wanted to see Antonia's face when she read it.

It was from the office of the ab epistulis, and it concerned Manius Cassius Verus the Younger.

*Whereas the said Manius Cassius Verus the Younger has confessed in the senate house of Rome, on the day before the Nones of December, to having caused the death of Decimus Iunius Gallus in the month of April of this year; and whereas the father of the said Manius Cassius Verus has by his own hand confessed to the concealment of the said death and to the corruption of a magistrate's clerk; and whereas the family of the said Manius Cassius Verus has petitioned that the matter be heard by the emperor's council and not by the court of the lex Cornelia; the council has determined that the said Manius Cassius Verus shall be relegated to the island of Gyaros for a term of five years, and that his father's name shall be struck from the fasti of the consulship of the year 103, and that the imagines of the house of the Cassii shall be veiled for a year and a day.*

*The council has further determined that the freedman Anicetus, of the office of the urban prefect, shall be put to the question and, if he confesses, sold beyond the hundredth milestone; and that the freedman called Felix shall be sought; and that the matter of the seals shall be laid before the urban prefect, who will report to the emperor before his departure.*

*The council has not determined the matter of the house of the Cassii, which is a matter of private law and is before the praetor.*

Antonia read it twice.

"Five years," she said.

"Five years," Tullia said, "and the island of Gyaros, which is a rock with a spring on it. And his father's consulship struck from the fasti, which has not been done to a consular in forty years. And the imagines veiled." She folded her hands. "It is more than I expected. It is not what I wanted."

"What did you want?"

"I wanted him tried in the court of the lex Cornelia, in the Forum, with the father's letter read out and the mother in the front rank." Tullia's mouth was a thin line. "And instead the emperor's council has taken it, because a consular family asked them to, and they have given him an island, which is where great men go when they are tired, and in five years he will come back with a beard and a Greek library and a great deal of self-pity, and there will be men in this city who say that he was badly used."

"Yes," Antonia said. "There will."

"And you are not angry."

"I am extremely angry," Antonia said. "I am angry in a way that I intend to be for a long time, and I will not spend it on a letter from an office. I will spend it on the book."

* * *

The book came back four days later.

It came back because Petronius Celer, the urban prefect, sent it back, with a covering note in his own hand that said, in its entirety: *Domina, this is not my office's business and I have no wish to make it mine. The cupboard was opened in the presence of three clerks and nothing was copied. I have had the bag burned. — P. Celer.*

The bag had been burned. Antonia, who had been in this city for thirty-nine years, did not believe for a moment that nothing had been copied, and she took the book and put it on the table and opened it at the last page.

There were four hundred and six names, with the amounts and the reasons and the dates, in her own hand and in the hands of two clerks before her, and in the back the pages that were not accounts: the six words about the stone room, the numbers, the name of a consular written once and crossed out.

The book was exactly as it had been.

Antonia sat down at the table with it.

"Tullia," she said. "Bring me the pen and the ink, and a new page, and the girl."

Myrtale came in with her decree in her hand, which she had not put down in four days.

"Sit down," Antonia said. "You are going to write in the book."

Myrtale looked at the book on the table.

"That is the book."

"That is the book. It has four hundred and six names in it and it is the reason the house was raided and the reason I have been eight months in a bad temper and the reason I have a strongbox with a false bottom. It is also the only thing in this house that will still exist in a hundred years, because paper lasts longer than marble if it is kept dry, and because a marble arch says what an emperor did and a book says what people are." She pushed the pen across the table. "Write your name at the top of the page. The full name. The one the clerk wrote."

Myrtale took the pen.

She wrote slowly, with her tongue between her teeth, the way people write who have been taught to read and not to write: *Antonia Cassia Myrtale, freed by the rod before the praetor urbanus on the Nones of December in the consulship of Trajan, consul for the sixth time, and of Titus Sextius Africanus.*

"Now write the sentence underneath," Antonia said. "In your own words. Not mine. The sentence you would want a man in a street to be able to repeat."

Myrtale sat with the pen over the page for a long time.

Then she wrote: *I was born free and I was held for eighteen years and I am not going to be quiet about it.*

Antonia read it.

"That will do," she said. "That will do very well."

* * *

Myrtale went out at noon to see about a room.

She had decided, in the way she decided things, quickly and without consulting anyone, that she was not going to live in the house on the Clivus, or in a room that Antonia had paid for, and that she would live with Iusta at Ficulea until the spring and then find work.

"There is work," she said, standing in the doorway with her bundle. "There is work in the gardens and work in the markets and there is a woman in the Via Lata who wants someone to keep her accounts and cannot read. I can read. I have been able to read since I was seven, and I have never been paid a copper for it."

"You can keep accounts here," Antonia said.

"I know."

"I am not asking you to forgive me. There is nothing to forgive. I have spent eighteen years looking for you and eight months putting my house and my name and my body in front of a court to get you out, and I have not done one thing in all of it that I am ashamed of."

"I know," Myrtale said again, and her voice was not hard, which was the worst of it. It was patient. "And I am not punishing you. I am not even angry with you. I am angry with the whole of it, and I have nowhere to put it, and if I stay in this house I will put it on you, and you do not deserve it. So I am going to Ficulea, and I am going to be a free woman in a small room for a while, and I will come to dinner on the ninth day."

"All right."

"And domina —"

"Do not call me domina."

Myrtale stood in the doorway.

"I do not have another word yet," she said. "I have been trying them out. I tried *mother* in the room behind the court and it did not come out of my mouth, and I will not say a word to you that does not come out. When I find one, I will say it."

She went out.

Antonia stood in the doorway of her own house and watched her daughter go down the Clivus with a bundle on her shoulder, and she did not cry until the corner, and then she cried for about the length of time it takes to boil water, and then she went in and washed her face and had the chair brought round, because there was a man in the Via Larga who owed her money.

* * *

In the evening, Marcella came.

She came in a closed chair with two of her husband's men and no jewelry, stood in front of the table with her hands folded and said, "I have come to be told what happened, because my husband will not tell me and my daughter will not speak to me, and the only person in this city who will tell me the truth is a woman I have been taught to despise."

Antonia told her.

She told her about the court, and the rod, and the clerk, and the three names, and the sentence in the book. She told her about the letter from the ab epistulis, and the island, and the fasti, and the imagines.

Marcella listened with her hands folded.

"So he is not dead," she said, when Antonia had finished.

"Verianus? No. He is on a ship to Gyaros."

"My husband is dead and my son is on a rock in the Aegean and my daughter is a woman whose father's consulship has been struck from the fasti, and the girl is free and has a name and has gone to live with a midwife." Marcella looked at the wall. "I have been in that house for twenty-six years. I have kept its accounts and its keys and its silences. I found the purchase record in 104 and I said nothing for eight years, and the reason I said nothing was not fear and it was not loyalty. It was that I had done the arithmetic and decided that the price of speaking was higher than the price of silence, and I was right, and I have been right every day for eight years, and it has made me the kind of woman who is right."

"Marcella."

"I am not asking for comfort. I am telling you what I have been." She opened her purse and took out a small, flat object wrapped in cloth and set it on the table. "This is the seal ring. His father's. The one in the strongbox, the one with the ship on it, the one the household has used for forty years to seal everything it ever said. My son gave it to me before he was taken and told me to give it to the girl, and I have decided that I will not, because it is a thing that belongs to a house that is finished, and because a seal ring in the hand of a freedwoman is a temptation and not a gift."

She unwrapped it. It was gold and heavy, and the ship was worn almost smooth.

"I am giving it to you instead," she said. "You are the only person I know who would use it and not be corrupted by it, and if I am wrong about that, then I have been wrong about one more thing, and I am too tired to mind."

Antonia looked at the ring.

"I do not want it," she said.

"I did not want to be the woman I am," Marcella said. "We do not always get the thing we want. Take the ring and put it in your strongbox with your book and your receipts, and when a man comes to you in twenty years and says that the Cassii were a great house, you will have the ring and the book and the letter, and you will be able to tell him what a great house is."

She went out without waiting for an answer.

Antonia sat at the table with the ring in front of her and the lamp burning and the whole house quiet.

Then she opened the book at the page where her daughter had written, and she took the pen, and underneath the sentence she wrote, in her own hand, the last entry in the book of the house on the Clivus:

*On the Nones of December, in the consulship of Trajan for the sixth time, the praetor urbanus of Rome declared Antonia Cassia Myrtale free, and her mother paid nothing for it, and the law did the work, and it took eighteen years.*

She blotted it, and closed the book, and put it in the strongbox with the false bottom, and blew out the lamp.
