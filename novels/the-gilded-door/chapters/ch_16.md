She dressed in the dark, before the household was up, because she had learned a long time ago that the days that frighten you are best met before anyone else is awake to see your face.

The dark blue stola. The mantle the color of wine. The gold earrings. The shoes with the small pearl on the strap, which she had bought in the year 104 and had worn four times. She put up her own hair, and it took her longer than it used to, and she did not call Rufina to help, because Rufina would have seen her hands.

At the fifth hour Tullia came with bread and olives and stood in the doorway and looked at her.

"You are not eating."

"I will eat at the wedding."

"You will not eat at the wedding. You will stand in a corner with a cup in your hand and watch the room, and at the end of it you will be hungry and tired and you will have drunk nothing." Tullia put the plate on the table. "Eat the bread. For me. I have been up since the third hour making sure the girls are dressed and the litter is clean and the gift is wrapped, and I am not going to be told at the ninth hour that you have fainted in a great house."

Antonia ate the bread.

The gift was in a box on the table: a mirror of polished silver, on a stand of ivory, with a handle in the shape of a girl's arm. It had cost four thousand sesterces and it was the sort of thing that a woman gave a bride, and it had no history in it, and no message, and nobody would be able to say anything about it at all.

"Is it too much?" Tullia said.

"It is exactly too much. If it were less they would say I was stingy and if it were more they would say I was insolent. Four thousand is the exact amount of money that says *I am richer than you and I am not going to apologize for it, but I am also not going to make a scene.*"

"You have a mind like an account book."

"Thank you."

"It was not entirely a compliment." Tullia came and straightened the mantle at the shoulder, and her hands were kind and quick, and Antonia stood still for it as she had stood still for it for nine years. "The flower-seller came at dawn. She says the girl is well. She says the mistress of the house has been in her room every evening this week, and that the master has not spoken to the girl since the Kalends, and that the steward has been told to have a litter ready on the fifth day before the Kalends of October."

"Where for?"

"She does not know. The litter is to be ready and the destination is not written down. That is how a man sends a girl away quietly: he tells the steward the day and the hour and not the place, and the bearers are hired from a man who does not ask."

Antonia put on her mantle.

"Then we have a month," she said.

"We have a month," Tullia said, "and a wedding today, and a case in November, and a book that a man in the prefect's office is still looking for, and a girl in a cellar in Ostia who is going to be brought up to Rome to be questioned in front of a magistrate, and a mistress who has not slept properly since March."

"You forgot the petition."

"I did not forget the petition. I left it out on purpose." Tullia handed her the box. "You cannot carry all of it at once. Nobody can. Today you carry the wedding."

* * *

They came to the house on the Viminal at the fourth hour, and the street was full.

There were litters along the whole lane, and the bearers squatting in the shade of the wall, and a crowd of the poor at the corner being kept back by two of the household slaves with sticks, and the torches already stacked by the door, and the flute players warming up in the porch with the particular insolence of men who know they will be paid whether or not they are good.

The side gate was open. Antonia went in through the garden with the women, and the garden was full of them: the aunts, the cousins, the wives of the bridegroom's friends, the wife of the praetor, the widow of the twice-consul, and Marcella in dark red with her hair in the old fashion and a face like a city wall.

"You came," Marcella said.

"I said I would."

"You came in through the side gate."

"You told me to."

"I did not think you would." Marcella took her arm and walked her into the house. "Come. I will put you where the whole room can see you, and where my husband cannot reach you without walking past eleven women, and where the bride can see you when she comes down."

The atrium had been transformed the way a great house is transformed for a wedding: the masks were still in their cupboard, but the cupboard doors were shut, which Antonia had never seen, and there were garlands on the columns and a couch for the bride and cushions on the floor and a table with the marriage contract on it and the two families' seals.

And the household was there, all of it, in its best clothes: the steward, the cook, the porters, the kitchen boys, the maids, standing at the back along the wall in a line, with their hands folded.

Myrtale was among them.

She was in a pale dress, and her hair was up, and she had the bride's veil over her arm because she was the bride's maid and would put it on her at the door. She stood with her eyes down and her hands steady and the token at her throat, and she did not look at Antonia at all.

Antonia stood where Marcella put her, in the second rank of women, under the garland, in full view of the room.

She had not been in this atrium since May.

The cupboard of masks was shut, and the doors were polished, and the bronze labels had been cleaned, and there was a bowl of roses on the table where the marriage contract lay. And behind her, to her left, the passage went to the kitchen. She could feel it behind her the way a person feels a door in a dark room: not by seeing it, but by knowing exactly where it is.

She had walked along that passage ten thousand times. She had carried a jug along it, and a lamp, and a basin, and a tray, and her own body. At the end of it, on the right, there was a door, and behind the door a room with a pallet and a jug and a nail in the wall, and she had slept in that room for fourteen years, and had been taken out of it on a September morning and put on a board in a stone room at the back of the tablinum.

She stood in the atrium in the dark blue stola with the gold earrings and let the memories come, one at a time, and did not turn around, and did not look at the passage, and answered when she was spoken to.

At the far end of the room, by the door to the garden, the steward Callistus was standing with his tablet. He was fifty and grey and he had the face of a man who has been given a little power and has decided to become it, and he had been a young under-steward in the year 93, and he had been in the passage on the morning after, and he had been the one who was told to carry her to the room behind the kitchen when the young master could not do it.

He looked at her across the atrium. She looked back.

He inclined his head, very slightly, and then looked down at his tablet, and Antonia understood — with a clarity that was almost physical — that in nineteen years this was the first time the man had ever acknowledged that he remembered her, and that the acknowledgment was not an apology and was not meant to be one. It was an accounting. He was telling her that he knew what she had come for, and that he had been in the house long enough to know how these things ended, and that he intended to be on the side that was still standing when it was over.

* * *

Before the ceremony, in the garden, one of the aunts came and stood beside her.

She was a small woman of seventy with a face like a dried apple, and she was dressed in a great deal of purple, and she held a fan and used it against the heat with the air of a woman who had been using a fan against the heat since the reign of Nero.

"You are the woman from the Clivus," she said.

"I am."

"My daughter's husband has been to your house."

"Many men have been to my house."

"His name is Pomponius. He is a praetor this year. He told my daughter that he went to your house for a dinner and that the dinner was better than anything he had had in a year, and my daughter has not spoken to him since." The old woman fanned herself. "I told her that a man who is going to be a praetor must be seen in the right places and that a house of entertainment is a place where a man may be seen, and that a wife who makes a scene about a dinner is a wife who will spend her life making scenes. I did not tell her what I actually think."

"What do you actually think?"

"I think that a woman who sells nothing but dinner is a woman who has understood something about this city that the rest of us have not, and that the rest of us are too proud to ask her what it is." The old woman looked up at her. "Is it true that you keep a book of every man who has been in your house?"

Antonia looked at her.

"I keep accounts," she said.

"Ah," said the old woman, and smiled, and it was a smile with thirty years of a bad marriage in it. "Then you are cleverer than my daughter, and I shall tell her so."

* * *

The bride came down at the fifth hour.

She was in white, with the tunica recta that she had woven herself, and her hair was dressed in the six locks with the woollen band, and she carried the spindle and distaff that her mother had carried, and she came down the stairs with her aunt on one side and her stepmother on the other, and she was white as the dress and she did not stumble.

The room made the sound a room makes at a wedding.

Publia came through the atrium and stood by the couch, and Myrtale came forward and put the veil over her, and the two of them were close together for a moment, and Antonia saw Myrtale say something, and saw Publia's mouth move, and saw the veil go down.

The bridegroom was waiting with the priest and the ten witnesses. Titus Caesennius Rufus was fifty-four, and he was not fat and he was not ugly, and that was somehow worse: he was a solid, grey, well-kept man with the hands of a man who had never done a day's work that did not involve a ledger. He looked at his bride the way a man looks at a contract he has been assured is sound.

The words were said. The *ubi tu Gaius, ego Gaia* was said by the bride in a clear, flat voice, and the room sighed with pleasure, and the contract was read, and the dowry was named.

One million two hundred thousand sesterces.

There was a small movement in the room. The dowry was a great deal of money, and everyone in the room knew exactly what it meant, and the bridegroom's cousin from the council smiled at the bride's father and the bride's father smiled back, and for a moment the two of them looked like what they were: two men shaking hands over a field.

Then the joining of hands. The bride and the bridegroom took each other's right hand, and the priest said the words, and the bride's hand was small and white in the man's big one, and Antonia watched it and thought about a girl in a byre and a girl in a passage and a girl on the floor of a stone room, and she did not look away.

* * *

The dinner was in the garden, at three tables, and it went on for four hours.

Antonia ate very little and drank less and stood where she could see the door. She was, she discovered, a spectacle: the women came past her in a stream, and some of them spoke, and some of them did not, and one of them, the wife of the praetor, went so far as to say, "You are the woman with the house," and then to say, "I have heard it is very fine," and then to go away in the certainty that she had been gracious.

At the second table, a man was talking about the east.

He was a senator of about fifty, and he had the loud, cheerful voice of a man who has decided that the world is a place where a man of good will can always make himself understood, and he was telling the woman beside him that the emperor would go to Parthia in the spring, and that the whole thing would be over in a year, and that there would be a new province and a great deal of money to be made in the carrying of it.

"And who will go with him?" the woman said.

"Every man who can get a commission," the senator said. "There are already four hundred applications for the staff, and the emperor has not yet announced the route. There is a man from the prefecture who is said to have made a list of the men who are to be taken and the men who are to be left, and a man on that list can name his price for the next three years."

"And who is the man from the prefecture?"

"Attianus," the senator said. "Or one of his freedmen. They say the list is kept in a book."

Antonia stood with her cup in her hand and thought: in a book; everything in this city is kept in a book; and understood, with a coldness that went down her arms, exactly what it was that Oppius Nepos wanted, and why he wanted it in the autumn, and what he intended to do with it when he had it.

Verus did not come near her.

He was at the head of the first table with his wife and the bridegroom and the bride, and he made a speech at the end of the second hour that was exactly the right length, and in the speech he said that a daughter is a door, and that the whole art of a father's life is to know when to open it.

The room applauded. Antonia watched his face while he said it, and his face was the face of a man who has said a thing that everybody will remember and nobody will understand.

Verianus was not at the table. He came in at the third hour and stood by the wall with a cup, and he was very drunk, and his eyes kept going to the corner where the household stood with their hands folded.

At the fourth hour, the bride and groom were taken through the ceremony of the nuts. The boys threw walnuts on the floor and the guests scrambled for them, and the bridegroom went through the room scattering coins, and the flute players followed him, and the whole wedding went out into the street.

* * *

The torch procession went from the Viminal to the Caelian, and it took an hour and a half, and it was the largest one Antonia had ever seen.

There were the flute players, and the boys with the nuts, and the guests with torches, and the bride with her two attendants, and the bridegroom with his friends, and the whole thing wound down the Clivus and along the Vicus Tuscus singing the wedding songs, the rude ones first and then the tender ones, and the crowd came out of the houses to see and the crowd threw flowers that had been bought for the purpose.

Antonia went with the second rank of women, at the back, with a torch in her hand.

At the corner of the Caelian the procession stopped, and the bridegroom took the bride by the hand and pulled her, gently, so that she stumbled, because a bride who does not stumble is a bride who has not been properly carried, and the room laughed. Then he took her over the threshold of his house and the guests followed and the door was shut in the faces of the crowd, and the songs went on in the street for a while and then stopped, and people went home.

Antonia stood in the street with the torch in her hand and the smell of it in her hair.

"Domina." Hilarus was at her elbow. "The litter."

"Not yet."

"It is the fourth hour of the morning."

"I know what hour it is." She watched the closed door. "I am waiting for something and I do not know what it is, and if I get into the litter I shall go home and lie down and it will be morning and I will not have waited."

They waited an hour. The crowd thinned. The last of the flute players went away down the hill with their instruments under their arms, and the torches burned down in the holders, and the street was empty, and the door of the house of Caesennius Rufus was shut.

"I was married once," Antonia said.

Hilarus did not move.

"Not truly. There was a man in the year 98, a freedman — no, a freedman's son; a citizen, but poor. He wanted the name and the respectability and the money, and I wanted to be a woman with a husband, and it lasted a year. He was not cruel. He was simply not there. He was a man who wanted to be a gentleman and could not afford it, and he drank, and when he drank he told me that I was a woman with a shop and that a shop was not a marriage." She looked at the closed door. "I have not thought about him in eleven years."

"Domina."

"What?"

"There is a woman at the corner."

Antonia turned.

Publia was standing at the corner of the street, in her wedding clothes, alone. Her veil was off and her hair was down and there was a mark on her wrist as though somebody had held it too hard, and she was carrying her shoes in her hand.

* * *

The house on the Clivus had never seemed as quiet as it did at the fifth hour of that morning.

Tullia had sent everyone to bed. There was a lamp on the table in the small room, and Antonia sat in the chair, and Publia sat on the stool with her wedding dress in folds around her and her hair loose and her face the color of a candle.

"I walked out of the house," she said.

"I know."

"I walked out of the house, and nobody stopped me, because they were all drunk, and because the porter thought I was one of the guests' daughters, and because a bride is not supposed to walk out of the door on the first night." Her hands were in her lap and she was turning her wedding ring around and around. "He put me in the bedroom and he put his hand here, on my arm, and he said that he could see I was frightened and that I should not be, and then he —"

She stopped.

"You do not have to tell me," Antonia said.

"I do have to tell you. If I do not tell someone, then nothing happened, and that is the thing they all want, that is the thing my stepmother wants, and my father, and the priest, and the whole room at the wedding. They want it to be nothing that happened." Publia's voice was very quiet. "He could not. He tried for a long time, and he was very — he was embarrassed, and he apologized, and then he said that it was because he had drunk too much and that I should not say anything to anyone, and then he put his face in his hands and he cried, and then he called me by a name. And the name was not my name."

Antonia sat very still.

"Whose name?"

"His first wife's. He called me Sabina, and he called me it twice, and then he got up and went out of the room and I heard him in the atrium, and he was talking to someone and I could not hear what he said." Publia looked up. "And do you know what I felt? I felt relief. I felt relief that he had left the room, and then I felt ashamed of the relief, and then I thought: I am sixteen years old, I have just been married in front of four hundred people, and I am sitting on a bed in a strange house, and the thing I am feeling is relief."

Antonia got up and came and sat on the floor beside the stool, and took the girl's hands.

"You are not going to cry," she said.

"I am not going to cry."

"Good. Because it is four hours before dawn and we have a great deal to do before the household wakes up, and I am going to tell you what is going to happen now, and you are going to listen." Antonia held her hands. "You are going back to that house before the sixth hour. You are going in through the garden, and you are going up to your room, and you are going to be in bed when your maid comes in, and when your husband asks you in the morning how you slept, you are going to say that you slept well."

"Why?"

"Because you are a married woman and the whole of Rome is watching a house where a man has been called by his first wife's name, and if you leave him on the first night, in the first year of the marriage, your father will be ruined and your stepmother will be able to do nothing and the money will be a scandal, and there will be a suit, and the case in November will be drowned in it. And because you will be able to leave him in a year, with your own money, in a way you choose, and you will be able to take his money with you." She looked at the girl. "And because a man who calls his second wife by his first wife's name on the first night is a man with a grief in him, and I have known men like that, and I have found that they can be reasoned with, in time."

"He cried."

"He cried," Antonia said. "Remember that. He cried, and he apologized, and he did not put his hand on you a second time. He is not a monster, and a man who is not a monster can be managed."

Publia looked at her hands.

"Did your husband cry?" she said.

"I was never married in a house with a shrine," Antonia said. "I was never given a dowry and a priest and a room full of women. I have been a wife in my own way, and I have had a husband in my own way, and none of it was ever said in front of a priest." She let go of the girl's hands. "Now. There is a thing I have to tell you, and I was going to wait until November, and I cannot wait any longer, because you walked out of that house and came here and you did it without telling anyone, and there is not going to be a better evening for it."

Publia looked up.

"Myrtale," Antonia said. "The girl you call Myrtale. She is my daughter."

The room was very quiet.

"I know," Publia said.

"You know."

"I have been sitting in that house for six months, and I have read the tablet, and I have spent every evening with her, and I have looked at her hands." Publia's voice was steady, which was, in its way, the worst thing Antonia had ever heard. "You both do the same thing with a cuff. You turn it twice and then you pull it straight. I noticed it at the portico in April. I said nothing. Do you know why?"

"Tell me."

"Because I wanted to see how long it would take you to tell me," Publia said, "and because I wanted to be trusted with it, and because I was so angry with you in that portico that I could not speak." She looked at Antonia. "She knows I have been copying a page. She knows my father looks at her. She does not know what I think she is. And I have not told her, and every day I have not told her has been a stone in my chest."

Antonia sat on the floor of her own small room, with her daughter's mother — with the slave girl she had been, and the woman she had made, and the girl of sixteen she had been when she first learned what a great house was — and she found that she was shaking.

"There is a place for her," she said. "In the spring. A house on the Aventine, with a woman who will be respectable because she is happy, and a trade, and a name. If we win in November, she will not go back to that house. Not for a single night."

"And if you lose?"

"Then I will lose with her standing next to me," Antonia said, "and she will have heard her mother's name said out loud in a court, in front of the praetor, in front of the whole city, and there is not a man in Rome who can take that back from her even if the law says she is a slave."

Publia looked at her for a long moment. Then she got up, and went to the table, and poured a cup of water, and drank it, and put it down.

"I have a confession," she said. "It is not a great one. It is the sort of thing that a woman remembers when she is married to a man who calls her by another name."

"Tell me."

"In the tablinum, on the Nones, I found the bundle and the page, and I read it, and I copied it." She turned. "And then I went to the kitchen and I told Myrtale everything I had read. I told her in the kitchen, in front of the cook. I could not help it. I was so frightened and so angry that I could not hold it in, and the woman on the Clivus had said not to tell her, and I told her, in the kitchen, with the door open."

The lamp guttered. Antonia looked at her.

"Who else was in the kitchen?"

"The cook. The cook's girl. The porter came in at the end of it to fetch his supper." Publia's voice was shaking now. "And Callistus was in the passage. I did not see him, but I heard the door of the storeroom, and when I came out there was nobody there."

She stopped. The house was very quiet.

"Then your father knows that she knows," Antonia said.

"Yes."

Antonia stood up. Her legs were steady. Her hands were cold, and she had got used to that, and she did not permit it to mean anything.

"Then the litter on the fifth day before the Kalends of October is not taking her to Formiae," she said. "It is taking her somewhere that a woman with a case in November cannot follow her."

She went to the door and opened it and called for Tullia.

"Get Hilarus," she said. "Get him up, and get Cerdon, and tell them to put on dark clothes and take the small lamp. And the fuller's boy — send to the fuller's yard and tell him that I want the man with the boat at the Transtiberim and I want him at the top of the Clivus within the hour."

Publia stood in her wedding dress with her hands at her sides.

"What are you going to do?" she said.

"I am going to move a girl out of a house," Antonia said, "and I am going to do it badly and in the dark, because we have come to the end of doing things well."
