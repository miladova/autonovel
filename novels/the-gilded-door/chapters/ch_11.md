Myrtale woke before the light, as she had done for as long as she could remember, and lay for a moment in the dark listening to the house.

A great house at night has a voice. The Cassian house talked in its sleep: the beams over the atrium creaked when the wind came off the Viminal; the water in the impluvium dripped into the cistern below with a sound like a small, patient counting; the dog in the porter's lodge dreamed and whined; and somewhere at the back of the house, in the kitchen, the cook's boy turned over on his pallet and said something to nobody.

She knew every sound. She had lived in this house since she was six years old. She could tell the hour by the sounds: the first cart on the Viminal meant the fourth hour, the baker's shutter meant the fifth, and the porter's cough meant that the porter had been drinking again and would be useless until noon.

She got up. The room was not hers; it was the room off the passage that the maids shared, and there were three of them in it, and the other two were still asleep on their pallets with their mouths open. Myrtale folded her blanket and put it on the shelf and washed her face in the basin and put on the grey tunic that she had washed the night before and hung by the window to dry.

Then, before she went out, she took the cord from the nail and put it over her head and tucked the token inside her tunic, against her skin, where it always was.

She had worn it since she was small. She did not remember being given it. She remembered the nurse — a big woman with a heavy arm and a smell of onions, who had kept her in a cottage in the country until she was six, and who had called her *little moon* because of the mark on her wrist. The nurse had died in the winter of the year she came to Rome, and Myrtale had been brought to the house on the Viminal by the steward, and the steward had said, "This is the girl," and the master had looked at her for a long time and had said, "Yes."

She had been six years old. She had thought that she was being brought to a house to work. She had not been wrong.

* * *

The morning of a great house was a machine made of small parts.

Myrtale went down the back stairs to the kitchen, where the cook had the fire going and the boy was grinding pepper and the steward was standing in the doorway with his tablet, saying what had to be bought and what had to be cooked and what had to be sent to the house of the lady's sister before noon.

The steward's name was Callistus. He was fifty, and he had been in the house for thirty years, and he had the face of a man who has been given a little power and has decided to become it. He had never beaten her. He had, twice, made her stand in the passage for an hour with her hands on her head, and had watched her while she did it, and had not said anything at all.

"Girl," he said. "The lady's bath at the eighth hour. The lady's hair at the ninth. The lady's dress is to be brought up from the press and aired, and if there is a crease in the veil you will be the one who is blamed for it."

"Yes, Callistus."

"And the master's letters. He wants them copied before the second hour, and he wants the copy in a better hand than the original, and he has said that if the copy is worse than the original he will not pay for the ink." The steward's mouth moved. "He has said that to me three times."

"Then I will make it better than the original."

"You will make it legible. Better than the original is a thing that annoys him." Callistus turned away. "And the girl — the daughter — is not to leave the house today. Her father has said so. She is to stay in her rooms and she is not to send messages, and if I find a tablet in her room it will be my fault and your fault and everyone's fault, and I will begin by blaming you."

"Yes, Callistus."

She went up with the tray.

The house was waking. The porter was coughing in the lodge. The ladies' maid was in the passage with a basin. The garden door was open, and the light in the garden was the color of the inside of a fig, and the old vine was in flower, and the bees were already at it.

* * *

Publia was awake, and she was in a mood.

"I will not wear it," she said.

"Domina."

"I will not wear the green. He chose the green. He stood in the room with the goldsmith and he chose the green for the betrothal and he did not ask me, and I will not wear it."

"It is the betrothal," Myrtale said, "and the green is the color of the house, and if you do not wear it he will be angry, and if he is angry he will be angry with Callistus, and Callistus will be angry with me."

Publia looked at her. She was sitting on the edge of her bed with her hair down and her face still soft with sleep, and she was sixteen, and she looked about twelve.

"That is the most horrible thing I have ever heard," she said, "and it is true, and I hate it, and I will wear the green."

"Yes, domina."

"You are the only person in this house who tells me the truth."

"That is not true."

"It is true. The others tell me what I want to hear because I am the daughter, and my father tells me what he wants me to be, and my stepmother tells me what she has decided I am, and you tell me the truth because you have to live here."

Myrtale put the tray down. She had been Publia's maid since the winter she came, and they had grown up in the same house, eight years apart, and there was a thing between them that had no name in any language she knew. It was not friendship. It was closer than friendship and it was wrong in ways that Myrtale could not have explained to anyone, and it had kept her alive.

"I am not brave," she said. "I am careful."

"That is what bravery is, when you are a slave." Publia pulled the comb through her hair. "Myrtale. If you were free, what would you do?"

"Domina?"

"If you were free. Not freed — free from the day you were born. What would you do?"

Myrtale thought about it. It was a game they played sometimes, and it had become harder to play as the years went on, because the answers she had given at twelve were not the answers she had at nineteen, and the new answers frightened her.

"I would have a shop," she said. "A small one. Not in the Subura. On the Viminal, near the gate, where the women come to the market. I would sell ribbons and thread and pins, and I would keep the accounts, and I would have a dog, and I would not get up before light."

"You would still get up before light. You would be a shopkeeper."

"Then I would get up before light for myself," Myrtale said, "and it would be a different thing."

Publia looked at her in the bronze mirror. Her eyes were bright and unhappy.

"Would you take me with you?" she said.

Myrtale stopped combing.

"I would," she said. "But I would make you get up before light."

"You are not allowed to make me do anything. I am the daughter of the house."

"Then I would ask you very politely to get up before light, domina, and you would complain about it, and then you would get up."

"That," Publia said, "sounds like the best of all the lives I have imagined." Then she turned on the stool. "Myrtale. There is a thing I have never told you, and I am going to tell you now, and then I am going to ask you never to speak of it, because if it is spoken of in this house I will be sold before the week is out."

Myrtale put down the comb. "Domina?"

"There is a reason my father has forbidden the lady on the Clivus to come to this house," Publia said. "And there is a reason he has moved you to the room next to the steward's. And I am not supposed to know either of them, and I do know them, and I am going to tell you, because you are going to be sold in September and I would rather be hanged for telling you than be silent for the rest of my life."

Myrtale said, "There is a thing I have never told you, too."

"Then we shall trade," Publia said, and her hands were shaking, and her voice was steady.

And they traded. Myrtale told her about the tablinum, and the fifty-one times, and the cord. And Publia told her about the tablinum on the Nones, and the strongbox, and the page in her father's own hand that said *for the child, from Bassa, and no more to be said*, and the lady on the Clivus, and the word that had been used, twice, in the portico of Octavia: *her mother*.

"Then the lady on the Clivus is —" Myrtale stopped.

"She can be anything," Publia said. "She mends fans."

Myrtale sat down on the edge of the bed. The room was very quiet, and outside the bees were in the vine, and somewhere below in the kitchen Doris was singing an African song that she had sung in this house for twenty years.

"Domina," Myrtale said, "there was a page you were not supposed to see."

"Yes."

"Did you copy it?"

Publia's eyes came up.

"Yes," she said.

"Then you had better show it to me," Myrtale said, "because if I am going to be sold in September, I would like to know what I am worth."

Publia got up and went to the chest at the foot of the bed, and took out a wax tablet from under a folded cloak, and put it in Myrtale's hands.

"Read it," she said.

Myrtale read it. It was eleven words and a note in the margin, and it was in the hand of the master of the house, and she read it twice, and then she sat down on the edge of the bed with the tablet in her lap.

"She is the most dangerous woman in Rome," Publia said. "That is what my father says. He says it the way a man says a thing he is afraid of."

"Yes, domina."

"And my father has forbidden her the house."

"Yes, domina."

Publia looked at the painted birds on the wall for a long moment.

"Then we shall have to be very careful," she said, "and very quiet, and we shall have to do it all before September."

She turned back to the mirror, and Myrtale took up the comb again, and for a while the room was quiet except for the small noise of the comb in the hair.

"Domina," Myrtale said at last. "You are going to marry a rich man and live in a house with a garden."

"Yes," Publia said. "Would you take me with you?"

Myrtale combed.

"Yes," she said. "But I would make you get up before light."

Publia laughed. It was a small, cracked, unhappy laugh, and it was the first time she had laughed in a month.

"Then it is settled," she said. "We will run away to the Viminal and sell ribbons, and my father will come after us with the whole senate, and you will have to hide us both in a vat."

"I would hide you in a vat," Myrtale said. "But you would ruin your dress."

"I would ruin my dress," Publia agreed, "and it would be the best day of my life."

* * *

The news of the death came down to the kitchen before it came to the atrium.

It came with the boy who fetched the water, who had it from the porter of the house opposite, who had it from a client of the Iunii, and by the seventh hour the whole household knew that a young man had drowned in a fountain in a house of women on the Clivus, and that the young man was the son of a consular, and that the house was the house of Antonia Cassia.

Myrtale was in the kitchen when the cook heard it. The cook's name was Doris, and she was from Africa and had been in the house for twenty years, and she had a way of taking news the way a cat takes a fish: she held it down with one paw and looked at it before she ate it.

"In a fountain," she said. "How does a man drown in a fountain?"

"He was drunk," said the boy.

"Drunk men drown in rivers. A fountain is eighteen inches deep and has a rim around it." Doris turned the spit. "There is more to it."

"There is not more to it," said Callistus from the doorway, and everyone stopped talking. "There is a death, and the family will be in mourning, and there will be no music in this house for a month, and the master will be in a temper for a week, and if any one of you repeats a word of what the street says, I will have the whole kitchen on bread and water until the Kalends." He looked around the room. "The lady of the house is to be told nothing until the master comes home. The daughter is to be told nothing at all."

He went out. The kitchen breathed again.

"Eighteen inches," Doris said, and turned the spit.

Myrtale carried the bread up. In the passage she met the porter, who was coughing, and who said, "Girl. The master wants you in the tablinum."

"Now?"

"Now. And he has been sitting in there since the fourth hour with the door shut, and the steward has been standing outside it like a man waiting for a dog to bite him." The porter wiped his mouth. "Say as little as you can."

She went to the tablinum. The master was at the table with a cup and a lamp, and there was a letter open in front of him, and he was not reading it.

"Shut the door," he said.

She shut it.

"Come here."

She came. He looked at her for a long time, and she stood with her hands folded and looked at the floor, and the silence went on so long that she began to count her own heartbeats.

"You are to move to the room next to the steward's," he said. "Tonight."

"Master."

"You are not to leave the house. You are not to speak to the porter, or the boy, or the cook's girl. You are to serve your mistress and you are to come to this room when you are sent for. Do you understand?"

"Yes, master."

He picked up the letter and looked at it, and then put it down again, and his hand was shaking.

"There was a boy," he said, "who died in a house on the Clivus. Did you hear of it?"

"Yes, master. In the kitchen."

"And what did the kitchen say?"

Myrtale thought, very fast, about the truth and about the price of the truth.

"The kitchen said that he was drunk, master, and that he drowned."

"Yes," the master said. "That is what happened." He looked at the letter. "That is what happened. Now go."

She went. In the passage, outside the door, she stood for a moment with her back against the wall, and the porter's words went through her: *say as little as you can*, and she had said as little as she could, and the master had said *that is what happened* in the voice of a man who is telling himself something.

She went up to her mistress.



She took them to the tablinum, where he was sitting at the table with the tablets piled in front of him and the window open onto the garden. He did not look up when she came in. He never looked up when she came in. He looked up when she had finished.

"Put them there."

She put them on the corner of the table, and stood with her hands folded, and waited.

"Read me the second one."

She read it. It was a letter to a man in Antioch about a debt, and it was full of numbers, and she read the numbers correctly, because she had been taught to read numbers by a woman in a cottage in the country, and had been taught to read them better by a cook who had found her reading the shopping list at eight years old, and who had said, "The girl can read. Do not tell the master."

"They are correct," he said. "Sit down."

She sat. She had been told to sit in this room perhaps a dozen times in thirteen years, and each time it had lasted about a quarter of an hour, and each time she had left and had gone to wash her hands.

"The wedding is in September," he said. "You will go with your mistress to the house of Caesennius Rufus. You will be her maid there. You will have a room in the household, and you will be under the steward of that house, who is an old man and a good one."

"Yes, master."

"Do you want to go?"

She looked at her hands. There was a right answer. There was always a right answer, and it was never the true one, and she had learned to find it the way a cat learns to find the warm place in a room.

"I will do what is best for my mistress."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he got up and went to the window and stood with his back to her, looking out at the garden.

"You have a look of your mother," he said.

Myrtale's hands went cold.

"Master?"

"She was in this house when I was a young man. She was clever, and she could read, and she used to sit in the garden in the evening and sing to herself. You sing. You have her voice." He was silent for a moment. "She died. You were very small."

"I do not remember her."

"No," he said. "You would not."

He turned around. And then he did what he did, the thing that happened perhaps four times a year, and that she had never once spoken of to anyone, because there was no one to speak of it to and because she did not have words for it that did not make it worse.

He looked at her. He stood in his own tablinum with the light behind him and looked at her for a long time, and it was not a look that a man gives a slave, and it was not a look that a father gives a daughter, and Myrtale sat with her hands folded and looked at the floor and waited for it to end.

It always ended. He would turn back to the window, and say something about the letters or the garden or the weather, and she would stand up and go out. It had happened four times a year for thirteen years. She had counted. Fifty-one times.

This time he said, "Take off the cord."

She did not move.

"The cord. Around your neck. Take it off."

"My mother —"

"I said take it off."

She lifted the cord over her head and put it on the table, and the token lay on the wood between them, and the wreath on it caught the light.

He looked at it for a long time. Then he pushed it back across the table.

"Put it on," he said, "and go."

She put it on. She went out. She got to the passage, and her legs would not hold her, and she sat down on the step by the store-room door with her back against the wall, and put her hands over her mouth, and stayed there for a long time.

* * *

The lady in the blue mantle came on the eighteenth day of May, and Myrtale was told to bring wine.

She had heard the household talking about her for a week before she came. The lady on the Clivus. The woman who kept a house of pleasure, and was richer than the master, and had once been — the talk stopped when she came near, and started again when she went away, and what it said was not clear, and what it did not say was very clear indeed.

She came into the garden with the mistress, and she sat down on the bench, and Myrtale brought the tray.

The lady was not what she had expected. She had expected paint and jewels and a voice like a woman selling something. The lady in the blue mantle had grey at her temples and lines at the corners of her eyes and a plain gold earring, and she sat with her back straight like a soldier, and when she looked up at Myrtale her eyes were the eyes of somebody who is doing arithmetic.

"Thank you," she said.

"Domina."

Myrtale went back into the house. In the passage she stopped, because the lady had said thank you, and nobody in the house of the Cassii had said thank you to her in a year.

Later, in the portico of Octavia, the lady mended her mistress's fan.

She did it fast, with a pin from her own hair, and her hands were quick and certain, and Myrtale watched them because she had spent her life watching people's hands. And she said, "You have the hands of a woman who mends," and the lady looked at her with an expression that Myrtale did not understand and could not forget.

And then, at the end, the lady turned her own wrist up, and said, "Show me your mark."

And Myrtale, who had never in her life shown her mark to a stranger, turned her wrist up and showed it.

* * *

After the portico, the house changed.

She did not know how she knew. Nobody told her anything. But the house changed: the steward stopped meeting her eyes, and the cook gave her an extra piece of bread at supper and did not say why, and the porter, who had never spoken to her in thirteen years, said "Good evening, girl," in a voice that was almost kind.

And on the second day of June, the mistress sent for her.

Acilia Marcella was in her own room with the shutters half closed and a box of papers on the table, and she was not dressed, which Myrtale had never seen, and her hair was down, which she had never seen either, and it made her look like a different woman: a woman of thirty-three, with a tired face.

"Sit down," Marcella said.

Myrtale sat.

"You are to move," Marcella said. "Tonight. Your things are to be taken to the room next to the steward's, and you are to sleep there until the wedding."

"Domina."

"Do not ask me why. I am not going to tell you why, and if you ask me I will have to lie to you, and I have been lying to too many people this month and I have run out of lies that are any good."

Myrtale looked at her hands. The room was quiet. Outside, the bees were in the vine.

"Domina," she said. "Am I to be sold?"

Marcella's face did not move. She looked at the box of papers on the table for a long time.

"There is a man in Formiae," she said, "with a farm and a wife who is ill. He has offered twelve thousand sesterces for a maid. Your master has accepted."

The room went very far away. Myrtale heard the bees, and the drip in the cistern, and her own breathing, and all of them were very loud and very slow.

"When?"

"After the wedding. When the house is quiet." Marcella's voice was flat. "He is not a cruel man, the man in Formiae. He is dull, and he is old, and his wife will not last the winter, and after she dies he will want a woman to run his kitchen. You will have a room and food and work, and you will not be beaten, and in four or five years he will free you, because men like that always free the woman who runs the kitchen."

"And my mistress?"

"Your mistress will be married and gone, and she will not be able to do anything, and if she tries, her husband will tell her that a bride does not interfere in her father's household, and she will learn the first lesson of a married woman, which is that you cannot save everyone."

Myrtale sat with her hands in her lap. She was not crying. She noticed that she was not crying, and she noticed that she had known, somewhere, for weeks, and that the knowing had been sitting in her chest like a stone.

"Is there anything I can do?" she said.

"Yes," Marcella said.

"What?"

"Nothing that I can tell you," Marcella said, and her voice was strange, and she looked at the box of papers, and then she pushed one of them across the table. It was a page of a letter, in a hand that Myrtale did not know, and it said: *the woman from the Clivus asks for an hour of your time, in private, before the wedding.*

"The lady who mended the fan," Myrtale said.

"The lady who mended the fan."

"Why are you showing me this?"

"Because I am not a good woman," Marcella said, "and I am not a bad one either, and I have been sitting in this room for an hour trying to decide whether to burn that page or to give you a reason to hope, and I have decided to give you a reason to hope, and I do not know whether it is the right decision, and I will never tell anyone I made it."

She took the page back and folded it and put it in the box.

"Go and pack," she said. "And Myrtale — if the lady on the Clivus sends for you, go. If she tells you something, listen to all of it before you say anything. And do not tell your master. Not one word. Do you understand?"

"Yes, domina."

"Then go."

Myrtale went out into the passage. The house was quiet, and the light was the color of the inside of a fig, and the bees were in the vine, and she stood in the passage with her hand on the wall and did not cry, and did not cry, and then went up to pack.

* * *

That night she lay in the room next to the steward's, which was small and clean and had a window that looked at a wall, and she took the cord from her neck and held the token in her hand.

She had never looked at it closely. It was a piece of lead the size of her thumbnail, worn smooth by thirteen years against her skin, with a wreath on it and a break across the middle where something had been snapped off. She turned it over. On the back, at the edge of the break, there was a mark that might have been the edge of a leaf or the edge of a door.

The nurse had told her, when she was small, that the token was a charm and that she must never take it off. The nurse had told her that her mother had died. The nurse had told her a great many things, and the nurse had been paid to tell them, and Myrtale had known for years that the things the nurse told her had been paid for.

She held the token in her hand and thought about the lady in the blue mantle, and the way she had mended the fan, and the way she had said *show me your mark*, and the way she had looked at her in the garden with the eyes of somebody doing arithmetic.

Then she thought about the master, and the fifty-one times, and the way he had said *take off the cord*.

She sat up in the dark and put the token back around her neck and tucked it inside her tunic. Then she got up and felt under the mattress for the knife she had stolen from the kitchen in March, which was small and dull and had a wooden handle, and she put it under the pillow.

It was not much. It was a knife, and a token, and a name she did not know, and a lady who mended fans.

But it was hers, and nobody in that house had given her anything that was hers, and she lay in the dark holding all of it, and waited for morning.
