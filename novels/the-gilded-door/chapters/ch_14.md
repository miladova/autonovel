The cart left the Porta Ostiensis at the fourth hour with a load of wool and two passengers, and Dessa sat at the back with her knees drawn up and watched the city go away from her one wall at a time.

She had not been outside the walls in six years. She had forgotten that the country made a sound. In the city there was always a noise, but it was a noise made of people, and the country made a noise out of things: wind in poplars, a dog barking three farms away, water going over stones, the creak of the cart, the wool shifting.

Tullia had put her in the cart with a purse and a letter and a cloak, and had said, "You are to be at the warehouse of Gaius Calpurnius Daphnus before dark, and you are to give him the letter, and you are not to speak to anyone on the road."

"And if the cart is stopped?"

"Then you are the niece of the fuller's wife, going to Ostia to nurse a sick aunt, and you have never heard of any house on the Clivus." Tullia had looked at her for a moment. "And Dessa. Do not run."

"I am not going to run."

"I know you are not. I am telling you so that you will remember that I told you." Tullia had adjusted the cloak, and her hands had been gentle, and her face had not. "The mistress has done a great many hard things in her life and she has done them in a particular order, and the order has always been the house first, and the women second, and herself last. It is not the same as kindness. But it is not nothing."

"I know what it is," Dessa had said.

"Then you know more than most of the people who work for her."

* * *

She had come from a village in the mountains, and she could not remember its name.

She had tried, in the first year. She had lain on a pallet in a stranger's house and said the name over and over to herself, and it had been there, and then it had been a shape, and then it had been a sound, and then one morning she had reached for it and it was gone, the way a road is gone when the snow comes.

She remembered the road to the village. She remembered that it went up, and that there was a stream on the left, and that in the spring the stream was full of snowmelt and the noise of it was the first thing you heard in the morning. She remembered the house: one room and a byre, with the animals in the byre in the winter and the door open in the summer. She remembered her mother's hands, which were large, and her brother, who was older, and who was called something that began with a D, or a V, or a B.

She remembered the soldiers.

They had come in the last year of the war, in the autumn, in the morning, and there had been eleven of them on horses and forty on foot, and the village had not fought, because the village had no men in it; the men had gone to the fortress in the spring and had not come back.

She had been fourteen. She had been in the byre with the goat, and she had heard the noise, and she had stayed in the byre because she was fourteen and frightened of men on horses. And then a man had come into the byre and taken her by the arm, and she had bitten him, and he had hit her, and the rest of it she remembered in pieces: the rope on her wrists, the line of people, the smell of the horses, a woman screaming in a language she knew, the fort, the fort, the fort, and then the sea.

She had been sold three times since. The first man had taught her to carry water, the second to be quiet, the third to count. She had learned that last thing well, and it had made her worth twelve thousand sesterces, and it had brought her to a house in the Subura where a woman with grey at her temples had given her a cloak and a knife.

* * *

The warehouse of Gaius Calpurnius Daphnus smelled of wheat and rats.

Daphnus read the letter twice, and then he looked at her for a long time, and then he said, "You are the one who can count."

"Yes."

"Show me." He pushed a tablet across the table. "That is the last four days of the Egyptian wheat. There are eighty-one entries. Tell me whether the third column totals."

She did it in her head while he watched, and it took her a little less than the time it takes to boil an egg.

"It totals," she said, "but the fourth entry on the second day is wrong. It says forty modii and the price is for sixty."

Daphnus picked up the tablet and looked at it and put it down.

"The clerk will be sorry to hear it," he said. "He has been with me nine years and he has been stealing from me for two, and I have known it, and I have not been able to prove it, because I cannot add a column faster than he can." He looked at her. "What is your name?"

"Dessa."

"Dessa. You are going to stay in the room over the office, and you are going to keep the books for me until the autumn, and in return I will teach you the shipping trade, which is the only trade in the world that is honest, because a ship either arrives or it does not." He stood up. "There are rules. You do not go out of the yard. You do not go down to the harbor. If a man comes to the gate asking after a woman from Rome, I am the only person you speak to. Do you understand?"

"Yes."

"Say it back to me."

"I do not go out of the yard, I do not go to the harbor, and if a man comes asking, I say nothing and I fetch you."

"Good." Daphnus went to the door and stopped. "There is one more thing. You are not the first person who has been hidden in this warehouse by that woman, and I have never lost one yet, and I intend to keep the record."

* * *

The room over the office had a window with a shutter and a view of a wall and a bed with a straw mattress, and it was, by a considerable distance, the best room Dessa had ever slept in alone.

She kept the books. She was good at it. She found the clerk's two years of theft in four days, and Daphnus dismissed him with a written statement and a threat and no prosecution, and the man went away with his box and his cloak and a face like a closed door.

She ate with the porters. She learned the names of the ships and the names of the shippers, and the difference between a manifest and a bill of lading, and why a ship that was listed as carrying wine and had a shallow draught was a ship that was carrying something else. She learned the tides. She learned that the grain fleet came in twice a year and that the whole town lived from it and that in the months between, half of Ostia was in debt and the other half was in the courts.

And every evening, when the work was done, she went up to the room and stood at the window and looked at the wall, and beyond the wall there was a lane, and beyond the lane the harbor, and beyond the harbor the sea, and beyond the sea, three weeks' walk and two provinces away, there was a road that went up, with a stream on the left, and a village whose name she could not remember.

On the eleventh day she went out of the yard.

She told herself that she was going to the baker, and she did go to the baker, and she bought bread, and she came back, and nobody said anything. On the twelfth day she went to the baker and then to the corner of the lane, and looked at the harbor. On the thirteenth day she walked to the corner and stood for a moment where she could see the water.

That was where the man saw her.

He was a small man with a stick and a dog, and the dog was a big one, brindled, with a scarred muzzle, and it sat at the end of its rope and looked at her with the flat attention of a thing that has been trained to look at people that way. The man looked at her too, and then at the yard, and then at the water, and then he came over.

"You are new," he said.

"I am nobody's."

"Everybody is somebody's." He smiled. He had four teeth. "What house?"

"I am a free woman."

"A free woman with the mark of a collar on her neck." He nodded at her throat, where the lead token hung on its cord, and where the skin under it had a band of pale, unweathered flesh. "That is a collar mark, that is. Six years, I should say. Maybe more."

Her heart was going. She had been trained, in three houses, in what to say to such a man, and the training had gone out of her head entirely.

"I am a freedwoman," she said, "of the house of Gaius Calpurnius Daphnus, and he is at the warehouse, and if you come to the gate he will show you the papers."

"Papers," the man said. "Papers are a wonderful thing." He looked at the yard, at the gate, at the porters unloading a cart. "And what is your name, lady?"

"Dessa."

"Dessa," he said, and wrote something on the inside of his hand with his thumbnail, which is a thing men do who cannot write. "Dessa. And where were you before the house of Gaius Calpurnius Daphnus?"

She did not answer.

"That is what I thought," he said. "You have a good evening, lady."

He went away up the lane with the dog, and she stood at the corner with the bread in her arms and her hands shaking so badly that she dropped it.

* * *

She did not tell Daphnus. That was the first mistake, and she knew it was a mistake while she was making it, and she made it anyway, because Daphnus would have written to Rome and the woman on the Clivus would have come, or sent, or ordered her moved again, and Dessa had been moved six times in six years and she had begun to understand that being moved was a thing that happened to property.

She told herself that the man with the dog was nothing. Men with dogs stood at corners in every port in the world. She told herself that she was a freedwoman of the house of Daphnus, and that Daphnus would speak for her, and that a man who came to the gate would be sent away.

On the third night after that, she woke at the second hour because the yard was quiet in a way it should not have been, and she got up and went to the window, and there were three men in the yard and the gate was open.

She had the knife. She had had it since March, and she had kept it under the mattress, and she took it now and put it in the waistband of her tunic, and she went down the back stairs into the yard, and the yard had a wall on three sides and a gate on the fourth, and the gate had three men in it.

"There she is," said the small man with the dog.

The big one came across the yard. He was not fast and he was not slow; he came the way a man comes when he has done this a great many times and has never yet been stopped by anything a woman could do. She got the knife out and held it, and he stopped, and laughed, and then he took a stick from his belt.

"Put it down," he said, "and you will not be marked."

She did not put it down. She had been fourteen in a byre and she had bitten a man's hand, and she had been fourteen for a long time since.

He hit her across the wrist with the stick, and the knife went out of her hand and skittered across the yard, and then he had her by the hair and her feet were off the ground, and the dog was barking, and the small man was saying, "Careful, careful, she is worth twelve if she is worth a sesterce."

And then there was a light in the doorway, and Daphnus was standing there with a lamp in one hand and an iron bar in the other, and behind him the two porters with their hooks.

"Put her down," Daphnus said.

The big man put her down. She fell on the cobbles and rolled and got to her feet, and her wrist was already swelling, and the porters were on either side of Daphnus with their hooks, and it was four men against three and the three were not paid enough to die in a yard in Ostia.

"This is a fugitive," said the small man. "There is a reward."

"Whose slave?"

"That is my business."

"Then go and do your business at the office of the duumvir in the morning," Daphnus said, "with papers, and a claim, and the name of the owner, and I will bring the woman and the ledger and my own freedman's papers, and we will see what the duumvir says about three men who broke a gate in the night."

The small man looked at him for a long moment.

"There is a man in Rome who wants this girl," he said.

"Then the man in Rome may come himself," Daphnus said, "and he may bring a warrant, and he may explain to the duumvir of Ostia why a freedwoman of my household is worth twelve thousand sesterces to a man who is not her owner." He raised the bar. "Get out of my yard."

They got out. The big man went last, and he looked at Dessa as he went, and it was not a look of anger. It was a look of calculation, and it was worse.

Daphnus shut the gate and barred it, and put the bar down, and stood in the yard in his night-shirt with the lamp, and he was shaking.

"Inside," he said. "Now."

* * *

He was not angry. That was the worst of it. He sat her down at the table in the office and put the lamp between them and looked at her, and he said, "Tell me."

She told him. She told him about the man with the dog at the corner, and about the three nights, and about how she had not told him because she had been moved six times and had learned not to be a thing that caused trouble.

Daphnus listened. When she had finished, he got up and went to the strongbox and took out a purse, and put it on the table.

"There is a ship," he said. "The Isis, out of Alexandria. She sails for Massilia in three days with a cargo of grain and hides. The master is a man called Serapion, and he owes me eleven thousand sesterces, and he will take a passenger to Massilia and from there to Narbo and from there, if you are clever and you keep your mouth shut, up the Rhone and across to the Danube road. It is eight hundred miles. It will take you four months, and it will cost everything in that purse and everything I can put in it besides, and you will be in a country where nobody speaks Latin and the men are not Romans and the roads are worse."

Dessa looked at the purse.

"Why?" she said.

"Because you are worth twelve thousand sesterces to someone," Daphnus said, "and I have been in this town thirty years and I know what happens to a thing that is worth twelve thousand sesterces to a man who has decided he wants it. The law is a slow horse in Ostia, and a fast one in Rome. So you will go, and I will write to the woman, and she will be angry with me, and I will tell her that I made a judgment, and she will tell me that I had no right to make a judgment, and we will have an argument, and it will be a fine argument and I shall enjoy it."

"He will come back."

"He will come back, and he will find an empty room and a man who is very sorry and a duumvir who has been paid to be sorry in the right direction." Daphnus pushed the purse across the table. "Take it."

She took it. She was not crying. She had not cried in six years, and she did not intend to start in an office in Ostia with a man in a night-shirt looking at her.

"There is one thing," she said. "The knife. It is in the yard."

"I will find it in the morning."

"No." She stood up. "I am going to find it now."

She went out into the yard. The moon was behind the wall and the cobbles were wet, and she got down on her hands and knees and felt along the ground by the gate for a long time, and at last her fingers found the wooden handle, and she picked it up and put it in her waistband and stood in the yard with her hand on it.

Then she went up to the room and packed the cloak and the bread and the letter, and lay down on the bed with the knife under the pillow, and did not sleep.

* * *

The ship sailed on the third day at the second hour, and Dessa was not on it.

She had gone down to the harbor at dawn with the purse in her belt and the cloak over her arm, and she had walked along the quay to the berth where the Isis lay, and she had stood at the foot of the gangplank and looked at the ship.

It was a merchantman, broad and dirty, with a cargo of grain in sacks and hides in bales and a crew of nine men who were, at that hour, sitting on the deck eating and spitting into the harbor. The master was a fat Greek with a beard, and he looked at her the way a man looks at a passenger he has been paid to carry and has not been paid enough for.

"Massilia," he said. "Twenty days if the wind holds, forty if it does not. You will sleep on the deck and eat what the crew eats and you will not complain, and if a pirate comes you will go below with the others and you will not scream."

"I will not scream."

"Good." He looked at her for a moment. "You are the girl from the warehouse."

"Yes."

"He said you could count."

"Yes."

"Can you count the sacks in a hold?"

"Yes."

"Then you can count them on the deck," the Greek said, "and I will pay you four sesterces a day and you will earn your passage like everybody else."

She stood at the foot of the gangplank for a long time.

Then she said, "I have to go back."

The Greek shrugged. It was not his business. He had a cargo and a passenger and a wind, and one of them had changed her mind, and there would be another one.

* * *

She went back along the quay and up the lane to the warehouse, and Daphnus was in the yard with the porters and he stopped when he saw her.

"The ship," he said.

"I am not going."

"Why?"

She stood in the yard with the purse in her belt and the cloak over her arm, and the sun was coming up over the roofs of Ostia, and she was twenty years old, and she had been a slave for six years, and she had spent three days deciding, and she had decided in the middle of the night, in the dark, with the knife under the pillow.

"Because if I get on that ship," she said, "I am a thing that is being moved in the night. And I have been moved in the night six times. And the woman in Rome is a hard woman and she has done hard things, and she has lied for me and she has lost her house for a girl who is not her daughter, and she gave me a knife and a room and told me the truth about the arithmetic. And if I run, they will say that a slave ran, and they will say that everything she said about the girl was a lie told by a woman who could not keep her own household, and the case will fail, and the girl will be sold, and it will be my fault."

Daphnus looked at her for a long moment.

"You would be alive," he said.

"I would be alive," Dessa said, "and I would be the girl who ran."

* * *

The men came in the afternoon, with the duumvir's clerk and four of the town watch, and they had papers.

The papers said that a slave woman named Dessa, of the household of Cominius Asiaticus, deceased, had been sold to a buyer in Rome and had absconded from that buyer's service, and that a reward of five hundred sesterces was offered for her recovery by the procurator of the estate. The duumvir's clerk read them out in the yard with the porters standing in a line and Daphnus beside him, and the papers had a seal on them, and the seal was the seal of the office of the urban prefect.

"This is a forgery," Daphnus said.

"The seal is genuine," the clerk said.

"The seal is genuine and the paper is a forgery. There is no estate of Cominius Asiaticus. He has been dead eighteen years."

"Then it is a matter for the courts," the clerk said, "and the woman will be held at the town's expense until the courts have decided, and you may bring your own papers, and your freedman's papers, and your witnesses, and it will be heard at the next assizes, which is in October."

"October."

"October," the clerk said. "Unless the buyer's procurator withdraws the claim."

Daphnus looked at the papers and then at Dessa, and his face was the face of a man who has been in business thirty years and has just discovered that he has been playing a game whose rules he did not know.

"I will write to Rome today," he said.

"By all means," the clerk said, and took Dessa by the arm.

* * *

The cell was under the town's forum, in a row of three, and the other two were empty, and it had a stone bench and a drain and a window at the level of the street, so that she could hear the feet of the people going past.

She was there for four days. The clerk's man brought bread and water twice a day. Nobody asked her anything. On the second day she heard Daphnus in the passage outside, arguing with the clerk, and then he was gone.

On the fourth day the door opened and a man came in with a lamp and set it on the bench, and sat down on the other end of the bench, and said, "You are Dessa."

He was a well-dressed man of about forty with an old burn on the back of his right hand, between the thumb and the first finger.

"Yes."

"You were in the house on the Clivus on the night of the Floralia."

She did not answer.

"You were in the garden," the man said, "and you found the body, and you put your hands in the water. And then the woman told the aedile's office that you had been sold to Africa in the Ides of March." He smiled. "I have been looking for you for three months."

"Who are you?"

"My name is Felix," he said. "I work for the prefect's office. Not for the prefect — for the office. There is a difference, and it is the difference between a man who is paid to keep order and a man who is paid to know things."

"What do you want?"

"Nothing at all," Felix said. "I am here to tell you something, and then I am going to leave you alone, and you will think about it for a night, and in the morning you will tell me which you prefer."

He leaned forward.

"The woman on the Clivus has sold you," he said. "Not to Africa — that was a fiction, and a good one. To a man in Rome, for four thousand sesterces, the day before she sent you to Ostia, because a witness is a liability and a liability is a thing that can be turned into money. I have the bill of sale. It is in the papers I showed the duumvir: a slave named Dessa, sold by the house on the Clivus to a procurator, five hundred reward. You were sold twice, girl. Once to Africa in the register, and once in fact, in a bill of sale with a seal on it, and you walked onto the cart that took you to Ostia thinking you were being hidden."

Dessa sat on the stone bench with her hands in her lap. She had known it in the yard. She had known it when Daphnus's face had changed. She had known it, and she had gone back anyway, and she had thought that she was choosing to be brave, and she had been choosing to do exactly what the woman on the Clivus had told her to do.

"The bill of sale is a forgery," she said.

"Of course it is." Felix smiled. "That is not the question. The question is what a court will believe, and a court will believe a sealed paper from the prefect's office before it believes a freedwoman on the Clivus and a shipper from Ostia with a grudge and a slave who cannot testify without the rod." He stood up. "There are two ways out of this cell. In one of them, you are a runaway slave with a forged bill of sale against you and a woman in Rome who has already sold you once, and you will be questioned under the rod about what you saw in that garden, and the questioning will last as long as the magistrate wants it to last, and when it is over you will be sold to whoever will take a girl with a ruined hand."

"And the other way?"

"The other way," Felix said, "is that you tell me what you saw, in your own words, in front of a clerk, and I take you out of this cell myself, tonight, and put you on a ship, and give you papers that say you are a freedwoman of the household of Gaius Oppius Nepos, and four thousand sesterces, and a letter to a man in Massilia who will find you work." He picked up the lamp. "The woman on the Clivus will be closed, and her book will be taken, and there will be a scandal, and the scandal will be about her and not about you. That is what my master wants. He does not want you. He has never wanted you. He wants the book, and he wants her closed, and you are the key to the door."

Dessa looked at the lamp in his hand.

"Think about it," Felix said. "I will come in the morning."

He went out. The door shut. The lamplight went out of the crack under the door, and the cell was dark, and somewhere above her the feet of Ostia went past on the pavement, and beyond the wall the sea made its noise.

She sat on the stone bench with her hands in her lap and thought about a woman with grey at her temples who had said, *I am going to have to live inside it*, and about a knife with a wooden handle under a pillow in a room over an office, and about the two ways out of the cell.

Then she lay down on the stone bench and put her arm over her eyes and did the arithmetic, because she was good at arithmetic, and arithmetic was the only thing that had ever been hers.

In one way she was free and the woman was closed and the girl in the great house was sold in September and the case never happened.

In the other way she was a runaway slave in a cell in Ostia, and the woman on the Clivus was looking for her, and there was a knife with a wooden handle in a room over an office, and a knife is a small thing, but it is not nothing.

She made her choice before the light came, and it was the same choice she had made in the yard, and she was going to have to make it again and again, every day, for the rest of her life, and she did not know that yet.
