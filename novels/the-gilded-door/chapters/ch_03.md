The Baths of Trajan opened at the sixth hour and by the half-hour there were three hundred people in the outer hall, and the noise was like a market inside a drum. Antonia went in with two of her women and a slave carrying the oil and the strigils, and paid the quadrans at the door, and handed her cloak to a boy who would lose it.

Rome went to the baths to be clean. It went more to be seen, and to see, and to find out what the price of a thing was before the forum opened the next morning. A man could learn more in the tepidarium than in a week of the morning call, because a man in the tepidarium has nothing to defend himself with but a towel.

Antonia did not undress. She had a room off the women's side, which cost her four hundred sesterces a year and was worth every coin of it, and in that room she took off her mantle and her shoes and her earrings and put on a wool tunic that reached her knees and belted it, and went out into the steam with her hair bound up.

This was unusual, and noticed, and had been noticed for fifteen years. She had heard every explanation the city had invented: that she had been branded, that she had been burned in a fire, that she had a growth on her shoulder that would make a strong man faint, that she was a Jew, that she was a Christian, that she had once been a man. She let them all stand. A rumor was a wall, and a wall with four sides was better than a wall with one.

The truth was on her back and her left shoulder, and it was not a brand. It was the hand of a man with a rope in it, applied over an afternoon and a night and part of a morning in the year 93, and it had healed into a map of something that had happened to somebody, and if she raised her left arm above her shoulder the skin pulled and the arm would not go higher than her ear.

Rufina, who had been with her eleven years and was the only person alive who had seen her without the tunic, took the oil and began at her shoulders.

"Not the left."

"I know, domina."

"Not even gently."

"I know, domina."

She closed her eyes. Around her the steam billowed and the women talked: about a marriage, about a miscarriage, about the price of pepper, about the praetor's wife who had worn the same dress twice in a week. There was a girl of about nineteen sitting on the edge of the plunge with her feet in the water, and she was crying, and two older women were talking past her as though she were furniture.

"Rufina. The girl."

"One of the house of Pomponius. The father is selling her to a man in Africa."

"How do you know?"

"Because the woman with the yellow hair told the woman with the mole, and the woman with the mole told everyone else in the pool, and I have been sitting here for a quarter of an hour listening to them enjoy it."

Antonia opened her eyes. The steam was thick and the light in it was like light in milk. She watched the girl cry, and did nothing, because she had learned at twenty-five that a woman who interferes in another woman's marriage in a public bath makes an enemy of the husband, the father, the aunt and the girl, and rescues nobody.

After a while she said, "When we leave, give her the address of the fuller on the Clivus. Not mine. The fuller's. And tell her that if she must run, run in the evening and go to Ostia, not to the north road, because the north road is watched."

"That is a great deal of advice for a stranger."

"She is not a stranger. She is what I was." Antonia stood, and the steam rolled off her, and she went out through the tepidarium into the cold room, and the shock of it made her eyes water, and she stood under the cold spout until her heart slowed, and then she went to do business.

The man she had come to see was waiting in the corner of the cold room with his hands under his arms, shivering, bald, sixty, and richer than half the senate. His name was Gaius Iulius Hermes, and he had begun life as a slave in the household of a man who had owned brickfields, and he now owned four of those brickfields and had a contract to supply tiles to the emperor's new forum, and he had a son who was a knight and a daughter who had married into a family with a consul in it.

He kissed her hand, which was more intimate than he intended and less than she allowed.

"You are late," he said.

"I am never late. You are early, because you are cold, and because you are afraid the man from the fiscus will find you here."

"The man from the fiscus will not find me here. He is at the baths of Titus, where the water is warmer and the company is worse." Hermes shuffled in his towel. "The contract."

"The contract is good. You will deliver forty thousand tiles to the site by the Kalends of June, and you will be paid in two parts, and the second part will be late."

"It is always late."

"It is always late because the fiscus is always short, and the fiscus is always short because the emperor is building a column one hundred feet high with a spiral of two thousand five hundred figures on it, and somebody has to pay for the figures." Antonia took the tablet he had brought, which was wrapped in oilcloth and was a copy of the contract. "Who signed for the fiscus?"

"Haterius."

"Haterius is a clerk."

"Haterius is a clerk who takes a fee, and the fee is one and a half per cent, and I have paid it, and I have written it down as a gift to a temple, and if the temple asks I will say it was for a new roof." Hermes looked at her. "You are not listening."

"I am listening. I am also counting." She turned the tablet. "The contract is for forty thousand tiles. The site needs sixty thousand. There will be a second contract in the summer, and it will go to whoever gives Haterius the larger gift, and Haterius will let the contract to a man whose brother-in-law is in the office of the a rationibus. That is how it works."

"Then I must give Haterius a larger gift."

"Then you will be paid less than the gift, and you will make it up on the thickness of the tiles, and one day a tile will be too thin and a roof will fall in and kill a man, and the man's widow will go to the prefect, and the prefect will look for a name, and it will not be Haterius's name."

Hermes was silent. The cold room dripped. A man came out of the hot room, red as a lobster, and threw himself into the plunge with a shriek.

"What do you advise?" Hermes said.

"Give Haterius his fee and take the second contract, and make your tiles the thickness the site requires, and keep the contract in a strongbox with a copy of the receipt. In three years, when the emperor is in the east and the accounts are being audited by men who were not there, a receipt is the difference between a fine and a cart to the docks."

"You are a hard woman."

"I am a careful one. Hard is what people call careful when it is a woman."

She gave him back the tablet, and he put it under his arm and held out his hand, and she let him kiss it again, and he went away shivering into the steam.

* * *

The women's side let out into a garden of clipped box with a portico around it, and the portico was where the real business of the city was done, the business of reputations. Antonia came out with her hair still damp and her mantle over her arm, and saw the group at the far end, and knew that she had been seen, and knew that it had been arranged.

Acilia Marcella sat on the stone bench with two other women and a slave holding a parasol, though the sun was behind a cloud. She was thirty-three and dressed like a woman of forty-five, in the old fashion, in the heavy wool and the deep colors, with her hair braided close to her head. It made her look like a portrait of somebody's grandmother, and it was a lie, because she had the sharpest eyes in any room she entered.

"Antonia Cassia," she said, and smiled. "Come and sit. We were talking about marriage."

The two other women made room. One of them was the wife of a praetor; the other was the widow of a man who had been consul twice and had died of a stroke in his own bath.

"I have nothing to say about marriage," Antonia said.

"That is what makes you so useful." Marcella gestured, and the slave with the parasol moved it, and Antonia had no choice but to sit in the small shade of it. "This is the month of weddings. My stepdaughter is to marry in September. I have spent the morning with the goldsmith."

"In that case, you have my sympathy."

"The goldsmith is a poet. He showed me a bracelet and said that it was a line from Sappho, and I said that I did not read Sappho, and he said that he could tell, and I paid him twice what the bracelet was worth because he had made me laugh." Marcella's smile was thin and real. "Tell me. If a girl must marry a man she does not know, what should her stepmother give her?"

"Time," Antonia said. "Everything else can be bought later."

"Time is what her father has least of."

"Then her father is poor in the only thing that matters."

The widow laughed, and then stopped laughing when Marcella did not. Marcella turned her cup in her hand, and the sun came out from behind the cloud, and the shadows of the box hedge lay on the paving like a grid.

"My stepdaughter has been unwell," Marcella said. "She has been going out in the evenings. She comes back with her hands cold and her eyes bright, and she will not tell me where she has been, and I have not asked her, because a girl of sixteen with a secret is a girl with a friend, and a friend is better than a fever."

Antonia said nothing.

"She is a good girl," Marcella said. "She is not clever, but she is honest, and honesty in a woman is a kind of cleverness that takes longer to show. She will make a good wife to Caesennius, who is not a bad man, though he is dull, and dullness in a husband is a mercy."

"You are convincing yourself," Antonia said. "Do not do it in front of me. I have no interest in your comfort."

The two other women became extremely interested in the view. Marcella set her cup down.

"You have a reputation," she said, "for saying the unsayable in a pleasant voice. I had heard it. I had assumed it was an exaggeration."

"It is not."

"No." Marcella stood. "Walk with me a little. The portico is long and my knees are bad."

They walked. The two women did not follow, and the slave with the parasol followed at a distance, and the long portico with its painted ceiling was empty except for a pair of old men arguing about a charioteer.

"You know a great deal about my household," Marcella said.

"I know what the city knows."

"You know more than the city knows." She stopped by a column, and put her hand on it, and looked out at the garden. "I have been married to a Cassius for nine years. In nine years I have learned that a great family is not a family. It is a company. It has assets and it has debts, and the debts are people, and the assets are people, and the difference between the two is a matter of the account books. My husband loves his name more than he loves his children, and he loves his children more than he loves me, and he loves his own idea of himself more than any of it."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you are going to do something," Marcella said, "and I would like to know what it is before it is done."

"I am going to open a jar of wine," Antonia said. "This evening. Would you like to come?"

Marcella looked at her for a long moment. Then she laughed, and it was a good laugh, and it made her look thirty-three and tired.

"I have a girl in my house," she said, "who is twenty and pretty and has no more idea of what she is than a cat. And I have a husband who does not look at her, and a stepson who does not look at anything but the bottom of a cup, and a stepdaughter who is being sold to a brickmaker for a consulship." She turned. "If you are going to break my husband's house, do it in the spring, before the wedding, and do it in a way that leaves the rest of us standing."

"I have not said I was going to break anything."

"You have not said anything at all. That is how I know." Marcella smiled, and it was almost kind. "You should not have worn the tunic. Everyone can see you are hiding something, and everyone can see you are not frightened of being seen. It makes them think about you for a quarter of an hour longer than they otherwise would."

She walked away, and the parasol went with her, and Antonia stood by the column with her wet hair on her neck.

* * *

At the door of the women's side, the girl from the plunge was waiting with her shoes in her hand.

"Rufina said you had spoken to her about me," she said. Her eyes were red and her voice was steady, which was worse than crying. "She said you had told her to tell me about Ostia."

"Rufina talks too much."

"Is it true that the north road is watched?"

"It is watched in the spring, when the shepherds come down. It is not watched in the winter, because nobody goes north in the winter." Antonia looked at her. "You are not going to run."

The girl's chin came up. "How do you know?"

"Because you are standing here asking a strange woman for directions instead of walking out of the gate. A girl who is going to run does not ask. She goes." Antonia took her by the wrist, gently, above the bruise. "You have a father who is selling you and a man in Africa who has bought you and no money of your own. That is a bad hand. But it is not the worst hand in Rome, and I have seen the worst hand played well. Do you know what the worst hand needs?"

"What?"

"A witness," Antonia said. "Not a friend, not a lover, not a god. A witness. Someone who will say, in front of other people, that they saw what was done to you. Find one before you go anywhere. A neighbor, a nurse, a priest of Isis with a good memory. Then, when you are gone, there will be a woman in Rome who says your name out loud, and a man who has beaten you will have to explain why."

The girl looked at her. "Did someone witness for you?"

"Yes," Antonia said. "And it took her eleven years, and she was too late, and I have been paying for it ever since. Go home. Do not go to Africa in October; the crossing is bad and the men who arrange it are worse. If you are still in Rome in the summer, send to the fuller's on the Clivus and ask for Tullia."

She walked away without looking back, because looking back would have made it a kindness, and kindnesses given in doorways were remembered by the wrong people.

* * *

The office of petitions was in a wing of the imperial forum, up a stair, behind a door with a bronze plate that said nothing at all. It had been designed, Antonia thought, by a man who had been told that citizens would come here only in the last extremity, and who had decided to make the last extremity as comfortable as possible so that their patience would last.

It did not work. The room held forty people and had chairs for six. There were petitioners from the provinces in good cloaks that had been worn for a week. There was a Jew from Antioch with a bundle of letters and a Greek from Corinth with a claim about a well. There was a woman in black with a boy of about eleven, and the boy had a letter in his hand, and was holding it the way a person holds a bird.

Antonia did not sit. She stood in the corner with Tullia beside her, and she waited the way a woman waits for news, which is to say without moving, and for a great deal longer than anyone around her expected.

At the third hour of waiting, a clerk came out with a tablet and called six names, and three of them went in. At the fourth, he came out again and looked at Antonia, and came over.

"You are Antonia Cassia."

"Yes."

"The secretary will see you."

The secretary was not the man Nepos had named. He was a freedman called Haterius, about sixty, with the hands of a copyist, and he had been in this office for twenty years, and he had watched three emperors and seen the same petitions arrive year after year in the same handwriting.

"The petition," he said, "is for a grant of freeborn standing to a freedwoman of the household of Cassia Severa, manumitted in the consulship of Pompeius Collega and Priscinus, on the eleventh day before the Kalends of December."

"That is correct."

"The fee has been paid. The witness has been called and has confirmed the manumission. The office has read the petition." He put it down. "The office has no objection to the petition."

Antonia did not smile. Men in offices said the first part of a sentence in order to watch a person's face before they said the second.

"The office has no objection," Haterius said, "but the office cannot advance the petition to the council without the endorsement of a consular, and a consular is not a clerk who signs whatever is on his desk."

"I know what a consular is."

"Then you know that they are busy, and that a consular is asked for three hundred endorsements a year, and that the ones he remembers are the ones asked for by a man he owes something to, or a man who owes something to him, or a woman who has done one of them a service and is owed in her own right." He looked at her. "Do you have such a consular?"

"No."

Haterius nodded, and made a mark on the tablet. "Then the petition will wait until you find one. It need not be withdrawn. It will not be forgotten. It will wait."

"How long?"

"Some petitions have waited eleven years." He said it without unkindness. "The emperor is leaving for the east in a year, or two, or five. The office will go with him, in part. A petition that needs his hand will go to the east and come back with a seal or it will not come back at all."

Antonia said, "There is a man in the Via Nova with a burned hand, who says his name is Felix, and who says he works for the prefect's office."

Haterius looked at her. She watched him decide not to have heard it, and watched him decide that not hearing it would be worse.

"There is a Felix who works for the prefect's office," he said. "He is a freedman of the household of the praetorians. He carries messages."

"Between whom?"

"Between men who do not want their names in a letter."

"And the petition about a child?"

The old man's face did not move. "I am a clerk," he said. "I write things down and I read what is put in front of me. If there were a petition about a child, it would be in a different room of the empire, and I would not be told about it."

"Then tell me which room."

"Domina," Haterius said, and for the first time there was something in his voice that was not a clerk's voice. "Go home. Find a consular. Do not ask a clerk which room, because a clerk who tells you which room will be in the room next to you, and you will not like his company."

Tullia took her arm and drew her out. In the corridor the woman in black was still waiting, and the boy had fallen asleep with his head on her lap, and the letter was still in his hand, folded, sealed, and addressed in a fine hand to a man who was dead.

Antonia stopped. "What is your petition?"

The woman looked up. She had a broad provincial face and a bruise on her wrist the color of a plum, and she had been sitting on that bench since before dawn.

"My husband was a centurion in the second legion," she said. "He died at Sarmizegetusa, in the last year of the war. The legion wrote that his pay was owed and would be paid to his heir, and his heir is my son, and I have been to the office of the legion three times and the office has moved, and I have been to the treasury twice and the treasury says the second legion was paid, and I have been to the governor's office and the governor's office says the second legion was paid to the legion, not to the men. And my son has been to the temple of Mars and back and he is eleven and he can read and he wrote this letter and the man it is addressed to died in the year of the fire."

"Give it to me," Antonia said.

The woman hesitated. "You are not an official."

"I am not. I am worse." Antonia took the letter and opened it and read it while the woman watched her. It was a good letter. It was in a clear hand and it said what the boy had been told to say and it ended with the boy's name and the name of his father and a request for the pay of six years, and a request for the money the legion had promised for the funeral, and a request, at the end, in a smaller hand, that the emperor might remember the men of the second.

"How much is owed?"

"Nine hundred and sixty sesterces. And the funeral money, which is three hundred."

Antonia folded the letter. "Come to the house on the Clivus Suburanus on the day after tomorrow, at the ninth hour, and ask for Tullia. Bring the letter and the marriage record and your son's birth record if you have it, and if the legion's man asked for a bribe, bring his name and the amount."

The woman's mouth worked. "I have nothing to give you."

"You have the letter," Antonia said. "That will do."

They went out into the forum. It was late afternoon and the light had gone long and yellow, and the great equestrian statue threw a shadow the length of the square. Tullia said, after a while, "You cannot get a legion's pay out of the treasury in a month, and certainly not before the wedding."

"No."

"Then why did you tell her to come?"

"Because in nineteen years nobody has told her to come to anywhere," Antonia said, "and because I am going to walk into a house on the Viminal in September and ask for a girl, and I will find it useful to have been the sort of woman who once asked for a soldier's pay."

* * *

The night was the only part of the day that was hers.

Crescens came at the eighth hour, off a watch, with the smell of smoke in his cloak. He had taken off the uniform and put on a plain tunic, which was his way of saying that he came as a man and not as a tribune, and by now she knew what it meant when he did that, and she knew what it would cost her to care.

"You have eaten," she said.

"I have eaten. The prefect's wife sent bread and cheese down to the station, which she does once a year, and we all stood around eating it and pretending we did not notice that she had sent it because a man died in a fire on the Caelian and the prefect did not want a complaint."

"Which man?"

"A shopkeeper. His name was the same as the shop's, and the shop was his whole family, and the family slept above it, and none of them woke up." He put his helmet on the table, and looked at it, and did not sit. "The shop was a bakery. The men ran out of the bakery and the family did not."

Antonia came and stood behind him and put her hands on his shoulders. He was thirty-four and his shoulders were hard with carrying, and he leaned back against her a little and let out his breath.

They went to bed, and it was good, and it was quiet, and afterward he lay on his back with one arm over his eyes and she lay beside him with the sheet up to her shoulder and looked at the ceiling where the lamplight moved.

"You are thinking," he said.

"I am always thinking."

"You are thinking in a particular way. You are thinking about a thing you will not tell me." He moved his arm. "I am not asking. I am telling you that I can tell. It is my one accomplishment."

"Your one accomplishment is that you can pull a man out of a burning building."

"That is other people's accomplishment. Mine is that I notice."

She turned her head. In the dark he was a shape with a glint of grey at the temple. He was eleven years younger than she was, and this had mattered to her at the beginning, and did not matter now, and would not be spoken of.

"There is a man," she said. "A freedman with a burned hand, who works for the prefect's office. His name is Felix. He has been asking about a child sold eighteen years ago from the house of Cominius Asiaticus."

Crescens was quiet for a moment. "Cominius died in the year of the fire."

"Cominius died in his bed, with a slave beside him, and the slave was sold with the house."

"How do you know that?"

"Because I bought her," Antonia said. "She is sixty now. She sits by the door at night and she does not remember the man's name but she remembers his hands."

"You bought a witness."

"I bought a woman who could not be sold to anyone else." Antonia turned on her back. "Who is Felix's master?"

"His master is the prefect. But the prefect's office is not the prefect's. The prefect is Petronius Celer, who is a good man and a lazy one, and the office runs on three freedmen, and one of them is a man called Anicetus, and Anicetus does not do anything unless he is told by a man with a name. I do not know which name."

"Find out."

"I am a tribune of the seventh cohort. I put out fires. I am not a spy."

"You are the fire-watch," Antonia said, "which means you are the only official in Rome who can knock on any door in the city at any hour and have a reason. You are not a spy. You are a man who notices."

Crescens was quiet for a while. Then he said, "If I ask, and someone above me hears that I asked, I will be transferred to the docks, and there will be a fire in the docks, and I will be blamed for it, and I will be dismissed, and I will owe three money-lenders money I cannot pay, and the only house in Rome that would take me in would be this one."

"I know."

"You know, and you are asking anyway."

"Yes," Antonia said.

He turned his head. "Why?"

"Because you are the only man I know who could ask and survive the asking. Because you owe me eleven thousand sesterces and you have never once pretended you did not. Because I am nearly forty and I have spent my life making men want me, and it turns out that what I wanted all along was to be able to tell one of them the truth."

The lamp guttered. Crescens put his hand on her arm, and his hand was warm, and she did not tell him not to touch the left.

"What is the truth?" he said.

"The truth," Antonia said, "is in a house on the Viminal, and I have been invited to a wedding."

She did not say more. She lay awake after he had fallen asleep, and listened to the fountain, and to the noise the city made at night, which was never silence and never quite noise. Somewhere a door shut. Somewhere a woman laughed, or cried, or both. The house settled, and creaked, and held.

In the morning, before dawn, Cerdon came with a folded tablet sealed with a cassia leaf.

Antonia broke the seal under the lamp and read it twice. It was six words and no signature.

*Come to me, and come alone.*

She held the tablet over the flame until it caught, and dropped it in the bowl, and watched the cassia leaf blacken and curl.
