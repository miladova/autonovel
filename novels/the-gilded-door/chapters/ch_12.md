There is a kind of man who is not afraid of anything except the opinion of a particular room.

Manius Cassius Verus had been afraid of one room since he was seven years old. It was not the senate house, though he had been afraid of that too, at first. It was the tablinum of the house on the Viminal, and in it his father had sat at a table with a wax tablet and a pen, and the room had smelled of ink and of the oil in his father's hair, and his father had said, without looking up, *the family is the only thing that lasts.*

He was forty-five now and his father had been dead for nineteen years and the room was his, and he still could not sit in it without putting his shoulders back.

* * *

The morning after the woman from the Clivus had stood in his garden, his wife came into the tablinum and shut the door.

Acilia Marcella was thirty-three, and she had been in his house for nine years, and in nine years she had never once shut the door of the tablinum. She had been a good wife. She had been better than a good wife: she had brought money when there was none, and she had taken the household in hand when his mother died and the slaves had begun to steal, and she had never, not once, said a word about the room behind the kitchen.

"Manius," she said. "Sit down."

"I am sitting."

"You are standing behind the chair and holding the back of it as though it were a shield. Sit down."

He sat.

"Did you tell her?" he said.

"Did I tell her what?"

"Anything."

"I told her that I had read a page in your tablinum in the year 104," Marcella said. "And that I had asked you about it and that you had told me it was a family matter and that I was not to speak of it again. And I told her that I had not spoken of it again."

"Why?"

"Because she already knew," Marcella said. "That is the thing you have never understood about this, Manius. She has known everything for eighteen years. She has known the child was in this house since the day the steward brought her back from the country. She has been building a case against you since before I met you, and she has been doing it in a house in the Subura with a book and a set of accounts, and you have been sitting in this room telling yourself that a thing not written down does not exist."

"There is nothing to build. The purchase was in Chryseros's name. Chryseros is dead."

"Chryseros is dead, and Bassa is dead, and the nurse is dead, and half the household that knew is dead, and you are alive, and you have a daughter in your house with a lead token on her neck." Marcella came and stood in front of the table. "She has the token, Manius. The mother has the other half. That is what she came to this house to see, and she saw it, and now she has it."

He looked at the table.

"I want you to tell me why," Marcella said.

"Why what?"

"Why you did it. Not why you bought her — I have worked out the buying. I want to know why you kept her. You could have sold her at six, at ten, at thirteen. You could have given her to a house in the country and never seen her again. You could have freed her at sixteen and married her to a freedman of the household and never spoken to her again, and there would have been no scandal and no case and no woman on the Clivus." Her voice was steady. "Why did you keep her?"

He did not answer.

"I have a theory," Marcella said. "I have had it for eight years and I have never said it out loud. My theory is that you cannot bear to be a man who did what you did, and that as long as the girl is in this house, being fed and not beaten and taught to read, what you did is not what it was. It is something else. It is a thing with a room and a name and a coat. And the day she leaves this house, it becomes what it is."

He looked up.

"That is not —"

"Then tell me the true reason and I will believe you," Marcella said. "Tell me in one sentence. Not a speech. One sentence, and I will believe you, and I will never ask again."

Verus sat behind his father's table and looked at his father's wife — no, at his own wife, at the woman who had saved his house with her dowry and had kept her mouth shut for eight years — and he opened it, and nothing came out.

"There," Marcella said. "That is what I thought."

"It is not that simple."

"It is exactly that simple. You are a man who did a shameful thing in his father's house, and instead of confessing it or repenting it or even enjoying it, you built a room around it and put a girl in the room and called the room a household, and now the room is falling down and you are standing in it with your hands behind your back." She came around the table. "I am going to tell you what is going to happen. She will go to the praetor. She will not go to the praetor with a case she cannot prove; she is too careful, and she has been too careful for eighteen years. She will go to the praetor with a case she can prove, and she will have the midwife, and the page, and the token, and a man of standing to assert the girl's freedom, and she will do it in the autumn, before the wedding, because after the wedding you will be in Asia with the dowry in your hands and there will be nothing left of the girl to free."

"Then she will fail."

"Will she?"

"She cannot prove a birth. There is no record of a birth. Births of slaves are not registered."

"There is the midwife," Marcella said. "There is the dealer's page. There is the token. There is the fact that a child of three months was bought for ninety thousand sesterces, which is the price of ten healthy adults, and there is the note in your hand that says *no more to be said*. And there is a woman in the Subura who has kept a book of names for eleven years and has used it to make herself the richest freedwoman in Rome, and who has not yet spent a single page of it on this." Marcella's voice was very quiet. "You are going to be asked, in a public court, in front of a praetor, why you bought a child for ninety thousand sesterces, and you are going to have to say something, and every word you say will be written down."

Verus put his hands flat on the table.

"What do you want me to do?" he said.

"I want you to do the thing you should have done eighteen years ago," Marcella said. "I want you to free her, in public, with the words said properly, and take whatever comes after."

"And the wedding?"

"The wedding will be in September," Marcella said. "If you are quick, and if she is quick, the case will be heard in November, and the wedding will be a month past and Caesennius's cousin will have his money, and the proconsulship will be decided in the spring, and there will be a scandal, and it will be a scandal about a girl who was freed, and not about a girl who was sold. Do you understand the difference?"

"I understand the difference."

"Then say it."

"I will free her," Verus said.

Marcella looked at him for a long moment.

"Say it without the sigh," she said.

"I will free her."

"There," Marcella said. "That is the first true thing you have said in this room in nine years. I am going to send for the jeweler about the wedding bracelets. You will receive her — the mother — at the end of the month, in this house, with me present, and you will not raise your voice to her, and you will not call her anything but her name."

"She will not come to this house again."

"She is coming to the wedding," Marcella said. "That was my decision, and it stands, and if you tell me to uninvite her I will tell my father's steward to stop paying your interest on the loan in June, and you will find out what a freedwoman on the Clivus is not the only woman in Rome with a long memory."

She went out and shut the door.

* * *

Before he left the house, his son came to him.

Verianus was twenty, and he had not been sober in the daytime since the Floralia, and he stood in the doorway of the tablinum with his hair uncombed and a cup in his hand and the look of a man who has been waiting a month for a conversation and has decided to have it badly.

"You are going to the palace," he said.

"I am."

"To be looked at for Asia."

"Sit down."

"I do not want to sit down." Verianus came into the room and put the cup on the table, hard enough that the wine came over the rim. "I want to know what you are going to say about the night of the Floralia."

Verus looked at his son.

"I am going to say nothing," he said, "because nothing has been asked."

"It will be asked."

"It has been asked and answered. The aedile's office has written it as an accident."

"The aedile's office is a man you have paid." Verianus's voice was shaking. "The aedile's office takes money. You have been buying men since I was a child, and you have been buying them in my name, and now a boy is dead and I am the one who hit him and you are the one who paid for it to go away, and I did not ask you to."

"You did not have to ask."

"I did not want it."

"Then you should not have hit him," Verus said.

Verianus stared at him. Then he laughed, and it was a terrible sound in a room with a door.

"That is what you always do," he said. "You take the thing that happened and you turn it into an accounting. I hit him, and the aedile was paid, and the account balances, and now everybody can go to the palace and be looked at." He picked up the cup and put it down again without drinking. "I have not slept in a month. Do you know that? I have not slept, and I have not told anyone, and I have been walking the streets at night, and last night I walked to the Clivus and stood at the corner and looked at that door, and I could not go in, and I did not know why, and then I understood."

"Verianus."

"I understood that I was going to be a man who had killed somebody and had been forgiven by his father, and that I would be forgiven every year of my life, and that the forgiveness would be worse than the death." He looked at his father. "I would rather you had beaten me. In front of the family. In the atrium. I would rather have been beaten every day for a year."

Verus stood up.

"Nothing has been forgiven," he said. "Do you understand me? Nothing has been forgiven. I have paid a man and I have closed a file, and those are not the same as forgiveness. Forgiveness is a thing that happens between two people who are still alive, and the boy is dead, and the only way you will ever be forgiven is to say it out loud in a public place and take what comes."

The room was very quiet.

"Say it out loud," Verianus said.

"You heard me."

"Say what you did."

Verus looked at his son. He was twenty, and he had his mother's eyes, and his mother had been dead for eleven years, and he had been raised by a woman who had married his father for money and had been kind to him in public and had not been alone in a room with him in seven years.

"I held the lamp," Verus said.

"What?"

"That is what I did," Verus said. "In the year 93, in this house, in the passage behind the kitchen. Your grandfather questioned a slave about a letter, and there was a rope, and I stood in the passage and held the lamp. And when it was over I untied her." His voice was flat. "She was nineteen years old, and she could read, and she sang, and six months later your sister's maid was born."

Verianus's face had gone the color of wax.

"Verianus."

"Which slave?"

"Her name was Prima."

"And where is she?"

"In a house on the Clivus Suburanus," Verus said. "She is the most dangerous woman in Rome."

* * *

The council met at the palace on the Ides, in the small hall off the portico where the emperor worked when he was in Rome, and the emperor was not in Rome.

He was at Antium, or at Centumcellae, or on the road; the letter had said Antium. In his absence the council met anyway, because the machinery of the empire did not stop for the emperor's holidays, and because a hundred questions a week came up from the provinces that required the opinion of men of standing.

There were eleven of them. There was the urban prefect, Petronius Celer, who was tired. There was the prefect of the guard, Acilius Attianus, who was said to know everything and who sat with his hands folded and said nothing. There was Neratius Priscus, the jurist, who was seventy and had the best mind in Rome and no ambition of any kind left. There was a young man from the emperor's own staff, a Greek freedman called Haterius, who took the notes.

And there was a fourth chair along the table, which was the chair of a consular who was being considered for a province, and Verus had spent a week choosing the clothes he would wear to sit in it.

The first three hours were the provinces. A complaint from Bithynia about a road. A question from Africa about a boundary. A letter from Egypt about the Nile, which had risen generously, which meant bread and taxes and no trouble, and the eleven men wrote a sentence of thanks to a river and moved on.

At the fourth hour, Attianus said, "There is the question of the Asian proconsulship."

The room changed. It always changed when the word *Asia* was said. Verus kept his hands still.

"The emperor has asked for three names," Attianus said. "The senate will send five, as usual, and the emperor will choose from the five, and he has asked that the five include no man who is currently under any —" he looked at his tablet — "*unresolved obligation to the fiscus.*"

There was a silence of the kind that follows a sentence that has been aimed.

"Meaning what?" said Celer.

"Meaning that the emperor is tired of appointing governors who arrive in a province with debts and leave it with a fortune," Attianus said. "He has said, in his own words, that a man who cannot govern his own household cannot govern a province. He said it in the presence of the empress and three of his staff, and Haterius has it written down."

Verus looked at the table. Six hundred thousand to the fiscus in three installments. The first had been paid in June, with money that had come from Caesennius Rufus as an advance on the dowry.

"Are there other names?" Celer said.

"There is Gallus," said Attianus.

"Gallus is in mourning."

"Gallus is in mourning and Gallus is a consular and Gallus has no debts and no province and no scandals, and a man in mourning is a man who can be presented to the senate as a man of gravity." Attianus turned a page. "There is also Verus."

Verus looked up. Every man at the table looked at him, with the particular courtesy of men who are deciding how much to spend on a funeral.

"Verus has been consul," Attianus said, "and has a name, and has a wife with money, and a son in the decemvirate. He also has a debt to the fiscus of four hundred thousand, and a daughter whose marriage contract is not yet signed, and —" he looked up — "a household that has been mentioned in a recent inquiry by the aediles."

The room did not move. The young Greek went on writing.

"There was a death," Attianus said, "of a young man of the Iunii, in a house in the Subura, on the last night of the Floralia. The aedile's office has written it as an accident. The prefect's office has taken an interest, because the house belongs to a woman of some note, and because a man called Oppius Nepos has made a representation to the prefect about the conduct of the woman's establishment."

"He has made a representation," Verus said. His voice was steady, which he was proud of. "About the woman."

"About the woman, and about the register of the city, and about certain irregularities in the manumission records of the fourteenth region, which nobody has ever looked at and which he seems to know a great deal about." Attianus looked at him. "You know him?"

"I know him as a man in the street knows a man at a window."

"Then you know him better than I do," Attianus said, and closed his tablet. "The list of five goes to the emperor in September. We will meet again before then. I would advise every man at this table to have no unresolved obligations, of any kind, by the Kalends."

He got up. The meeting broke. Men gathered their tablets and their cloaks and began the delicate work of leaving a room without appearing to be the first to leave it.

Verus was the last. He stood at the table with his tablet in his hand, and Attianus came back from the door.

"Cassius," he said.

"Prefect."

"The emperor likes you. He has said so, twice, in my hearing, and he is not a man who says such things often." Attianus's face was old and calm and entirely without comfort. "He likes you because you are a man who keeps his accounts and does not complain. That is the whole of what he asks of a senator. He does not ask that you be a good man; he has been emperor too long for that. He asks that you be a tidy one."

"I am tidy."

"Yes," Attianus said. "That is what I am afraid of."

He went out.

Verus stood alone in the small hall with the portraits of the emperors on the wall, and the light coming through the window, and the noise of the palace going on beyond the door.

* * *

He did not go home. He walked.

He walked down from the Palatine through the Forum, past the new basilica with its shops full of merchants, past the temple of Castor, past the place where the Rostra stood and where a boy had been spoken of for a quarter of an hour in May. He walked through the Subura because he did not admit to himself that he was walking there, and he came up the Clivus Suburanus in the late afternoon with the shopkeepers putting up their shutters and the smell of hot bread from the bakeries and the tanners beginning at the bottom of the hill.

The house was at the corner of a lane. The gilt was fresh, and there were no garlands on the door, and the doorkeeper was a big man with a club and a good cloak who looked at him without recognition, which was an insult he had not been prepared for.

"I am here to see the lady."

"Which lady?"

"Antonia Cassia."

"And you are?"

He opened his mouth to say his name, and found that he could not. Not in that lane, with a baker's boy watching and a litter going by and a woman with a basket of leeks stopping to look at a consular in a toga standing at that door.

"Tell her," he said, "that a man has come about a girl."

The doorkeeper looked at him for a moment. Then he turned and went in, and the door closed, and Verus stood in the street for a long time, and the light went off the top of the wall, and the lanterna were lit at the corner.

The door opened.

"He will see you," the doorkeeper said.

* * *

She received him in the garden, and she was not alone. There was an old woman with a face like a closed door sitting in the corner with a tablet, and there was a young woman standing by the fountain with her hands folded, who looked at him for a moment and then went out without being told.

"The girl," he said.

"Not that one," Antonia said. "That is Dessa. She was in the garden on the night of the Floralia, and she saw a man in the lane with a ring on his hand, and she is under my protection, and if you have come here to ask about her you may leave now."

"I have not come about her."

"Then sit down."

He sat. The old woman with the tablet did not move. The fountain ran. Antonia sat opposite him in a chair with a footstool, and she had her hair down, and she looked tired, and he found that he was looking at her hands.

"There will be a case," she said. "The praetor's court. In November, before the wedding season ends and after the harvest. I have the midwife, and I have the page, and I have the token, and I have found a man to assert her freedom. What I do not have is the thing without which the whole of it is a story."

"And what is that?"

"You," Antonia said. "In the court. Producing her as the slave you claim, and admitting that you claim her, and giving the court the thing it needs to hear from your own mouth."

He looked at her.

"You want me to stand in a public court and say that I own her."

"I want you to stand in a public court and say that you bought her, and that you have held her, and that you cannot produce the title under which you hold her, because there is no title, and there never was. And then I want you to stand down, and say nothing, and let the adsertor speak."

"And if I do not?"

"Then the case fails, and she stays in your house, and in September you sell her to a man in Formiae for twelve thousand sesterces, and you go to Asia with the dowry and the scandal is a scandal about a freedwoman's claim that she could not prove." Antonia looked at him. "And you will have won. And you will sit in the proconsul's chair in Ephesus, and every night for the rest of your life you will know exactly what you are."

The fountain ran.

"I have held her for eighteen years," Verus said. "I have fed her and clothed her and kept her from the men who would have bought her, and there have been four. I have never raised a hand to her. I have never said a word to her that —"

"You have looked at her," Antonia said.

He stopped.

"You have looked at her," she said again, "in a room with the door shut, and you have said nothing, and she has stood there with her hands folded and waited for it to end. She has told me how many times. She has counted. Fifty-one times in thirteen years." Antonia's voice was very low. "Do you know what a girl learns, standing in a room while a man looks at her and says nothing? She learns that she is a thing. She learns that the only question is what kind of thing, and how much, and when. And she learns it from the man who is her father, and she does not know he is her father, so she thinks it is the world that does it, and she thinks it is normal, and she thinks that every girl in Rome stands in a room while a man looks at her."

"I never touched her."

"Fifty-one times," Antonia said.

"I never touched her."

"You held the lamp," she said.

The word went into him like a knife going in slowly.

* * *

He had been twenty-six.

He had been in the passage that night because his father had sent for him, and because he had come from a dinner with the woman who was going to be his wife, and because he had wanted to tell his father about the dinner. The house had been quiet in the way a house is quiet when everybody in it is awake.

There had been a lamp in his hand. There had been a rope on the beam. There had been a chair, and his father's voice, and the questions, and the clerk in the corner with the tablets, and the woman on the board who was not screaming, which was the thing he could not bear, even now, eighteen years later: that she had not screamed.

She had answered the questions. She had answered them for eleven hours, and her voice had gone strange, and in the middle of it his father had said, *again*, and the man had done it again, and she had answered, and the answers had been what his father wanted, which was that his son had taken a folded paper from a man who was dead.

And at some point in the night, Verus had understood that his father had known, before he began, that the boy had taken the paper. That the whole of it was not a search for the truth; it was a demonstration. His father was showing him what the family cost, and what it was worth paying.

He had stood in the passage with the lamp in his hand and had not said a word.

And in the morning, when it was over, he had untied her himself.

* * *

"I have thought about it every day," he said.

"I know."

"How do you know?"

"Because I have thought about it every day," Antonia said, "and men are not so different from women, whatever they tell themselves."

He looked at her. She was sitting with her hands in her lap and her hair down, and she had a scar on the back of her hand from a splash of hot oil, and she was the richest freedwoman in Rome, and she was the mother of the girl in his house.

"I will do it," he said.

She did not move.

"Say it properly," she said.

"I will produce her in the praetor's court. I will claim her as my slave. I will give the court no title. I will stand down." He swallowed. "And when it is over, I will sign the admission, and she will be freeborn from the day of her birth, and it will be recorded."

"Before the wedding."

"Before the wedding," he said, "and the wedding will happen, and the marriage contract will be honored, and Caesennius will have his bride, and my daughter will go to a house on the Caelian and be a rich woman, and no one will take her." He looked up. "That is the price. That is my price. The case, the girl's freedom, the admission, the scandal — all of it, and the wedding goes ahead."

Antonia looked at him for a long moment.

"You are selling one daughter to save the other," she said.

"I am saving both."

"No," she said. "You are selling one to save the other, and you are calling it both, because you cannot say the thing you are doing." She stood. "I accept. The wedding will happen. I will be in your house in September, and I will stand in the room with the shrine, and I will eat your dinner, and I will watch your daughter be given to a man of fifty-four for a million sesterces, and I will not raise my voice, and I will not spoil it. And then, in November, I will take you into a court and pull your house down around you."

"You said you would not bring the rest of it."

"I said I would not bring the rest of it to a court if you freed her. I did not say I would not bring it to the senate, or to your wife, or to your son, or to the man who is going to be your son-in-law's cousin." Antonia's voice was steady. "I have spent eighteen years learning that the law is a slow and expensive way of doing what a well-informed woman can do in a week. The court is for the girl. The rest is for me."

Verus stood up. His hands were shaking.

"Then why should I do any of it?" he said. "Why should I stand in a court and ruin myself, if you are going to ruin me anyway?"

"Because the girl is your daughter," Antonia said, "and because there is one thing in you that is not the family and not the name and not the room, and I have seen it, twice, in eighteen years: once in the passage when you untied me, and once in your garden when you said you had loved her in the only way you were permitted." She stepped close to him. "You are a coward, Manius Cassius Verus, and a hypocrite, and a man who held the lamp. And you are the only person in that house who can give that girl her name back. So you will do it, and you will do it properly, and then you will go to Asia, or you will go nowhere, and either way you will have done one thing in your life that was not for the family."

He looked at her.

"And you?" he said. "What will you have?"

"My daughter," Antonia said. "For a start."

* * *

He walked home. The lamps were lit along the Viminal and the shops were shut, and a girl was singing somewhere in a courtyard. He went in through the front door and the porter said something to him and he did not hear it.

The passage behind the kitchen was dark. There was a lamp on the wall, and he took it down, and he stood at the bottom of the back stairs with it in his hand.

The room next to the steward's had a door, and the door had a crack of light under it. He stood outside it for a long time with the lamp and did not knock.

He could hear her moving: a stool, a box being opened, the small noise of a person putting things away in the dark.

He thought: I will knock, and she will open it, and I will say, *I am your father.*

He thought: and she will look at me, and she will not say anything, because she has been trained not to say anything, and then she will shut the door.

He stood there for a long time.

Then he turned and went up the stairs with the lamp, and the light under the door went out behind him.

In the morning he sent for the family's advocate, and told him that a question of status was to be prepared for the praetor's court in November, and the advocate asked who the person was, and Verus said: a girl of my household, and no more.
