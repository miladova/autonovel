The house on the Caelian had a covered walk that ran along the garden wall, and Gallus had taken to walking it in the evenings, up and down, forty paces one way and forty back, for an hour at a time. The household had got used to it. The slaves kept out of the walk, and the steward put a lamp at each end, and the grandson had been told that his grandfather was thinking and was not to be disturbed.

Antonia came at the eighth hour and found him at the far end of the walk with a letter in his hand.

"You had better sit down," he said.

"I have been standing in burnt houses for a month. I will stand."

"Sit down," Gallus said, "because I am going to read you something, and I would rather you were sitting when I read it."

She sat.

He read it. It was a letter, and it was in a good hand, and it was written by a man who had been paid to write it, and it said:

*To Lucius Iunius Gallus, from a friend. You have been deceived by the woman who calls herself Antonia Cassia. On the night your son died, she knew that Manius Cassius Verus the Younger had left her house at the fourth hour and had not returned, and she knew, by her own slave's account, that a tall man with a gold ring had been standing in the lane outside her garden. She did not tell you. She told the aedile's office that the death was an accident and paid two hundred thousand sesterces to have the word written down. She concealed the slave who found the body, and registered her as sold to Africa, and the slave is now in the custody of the prefect's office and will be produced before the magistrate in December. The woman has done all of this to protect a case she means to bring in November, and she has used your grief to find herself an adsertor of consular rank. Ask her. She will not deny it.*

Gallus folded the letter.

"Well?" he said.

Antonia looked at him. The lamps at the ends of the walk made two small yellow points, and beyond them the garden was dark, and the aqueduct went over the wall.

"It is true," she said.

"All of it?"

"Almost all of it. I did not pay two hundred thousand. The aedile's office asked for a hundred thousand, and I offered two, because the price of a thing in Rome is usually twice what a man first asks, and I wanted him to remember it." She folded her hands. "And the slave is not in the custody of the prefect's office with my consent. She was taken from Ostia in July by men with a forged bill of sale and a genuine seal, and I have been looking for her for three months."

"You told me in August that there was a man in the lane."

"I told you there was a man in the lane with a ring on his hand, and that Verianus had the ring, and that his father had told me so."

"You did not tell me that you had known it in April."

"No."

"You let me stand in a court and swear to be your adsertor," Gallus said, "and you let me do it in the belief that I was doing it for a girl and against a man, and all the time you knew that the man's son was in the lane outside your house on the night my son died, and you did not tell me."

"I told you in August."

"Four months," Gallus said. "Four months, and my son was in the ground in May, and you sat in my garden and drank my wine and let me talk about the masks."

Antonia did not answer.

"Why?" he said. "That is the only question. Why did you keep it?"

"Because on the morning after your son died I did not know what had happened, and because by the time I knew, I had already lied to the aedile's office, and a woman who has lied once about a death cannot go to the father of the dead boy and say, I lied, but here is a part of the truth." She looked at him. "And because I wanted the case, and I knew that if I told you in April you would have gone to the prefect and there would have been a scandal and an inquiry and the girl would have been sold to Formiae before the summer was out. I made a choice between your grief and my daughter, and I chose my daughter, and I have been paying for it every day since."

Gallus walked to the end of the walk and came back.

"You are the most honest woman I have ever known," he said, "and I find that I would rather you had lied to me now."

"I know."

"You are going to tell me the rest of it," Gallus said. "Not the part that is convenient. The whole of it. And then I am going to decide whether I stand up in the praetor's court in November and assert the freedom of a girl on behalf of a woman who has used me."

Antonia told him. She told him about the fountain and the palms and the cut on the forehead. She told him about the girl in the passage with wet hands, and about the register and the lie of Africa, and about Dessa in the cell in Ostia. She told him about Verianus's face at the funeral, and about Verus in the garden in May with his hands over his face. She told him about the fifty-one times in the tablinum, and about the note that said *no more to be said*, and about the eleven hours in the stone room.

When she had finished, Gallus stood at the end of the walk with his back to her for a long time.

"There is a thing I want," he said at last.

"Name it."

"I want you to say it in the senate house."

Antonia sat very still.

"Not in the praetor's court," Gallus said. "In the senate. In front of the whole house, on the day of the case, before the praetor sits. I want you to stand in the curia and say what was done to you in that house in the year 93, and what was done to the girl, and what you did in April when my son died. I want it said by you, in your own voice, in the room where the men who are going to decide the province of Asia are sitting." He turned. "And I will stand beside you and I will say that I have read the papers and that the girl is free, and that the man who held the lamp is not fit to govern a province or to sit in that house."

"They will not let a woman speak in the senate house."

"They will let a woman speak in the senate house when a consular of Rome stands up and asks the house to hear her," Gallus said. "It has been done. Not often. Four times in my lifetime. Once for a woman of the family of the Gracchi, once for a British queen, once for a woman who had been wronged by a governor, and once for a woman I have never been able to find out anything about, in the reign of Vespasian, who spoke for a quarter of an hour and was not written down." He came and stood in front of her. "I will ask. And they will let you speak because they will be curious, and because the whole house knows about Verus and the province and the wedding, and because a scandal is the only thing that makes a senator forget his dinner."

"And then?"

"And then you will say what you have told me tonight, all of it, and they will do nothing — the senate does nothing, the senate is a room full of men who agree to be careful — but they will have heard it, and a thing that has been heard in that room is a thing that cannot be unanswered. Verus will not go to Asia. His son will not sit in the decemvirate. The girl will be freed by the praetor in the afternoon, because no praetor in Rome will rule against a woman who has just told that story in front of the consuls."

"And I will be finished," Antonia said.

Gallus looked at her.

"Yes," he said. "You will be finished. You will be a freedwoman who was put to the question and kept a house of pleasure and lied to the aediles, and it will all be said in one afternoon in the hearing of the whole city, and there is not a door in Rome that will open for you afterward."

"And Dessa?"

"Dessa will be in a cell in December, and the prefect's office will question her, and if I am standing in the senate house on the day of the case I will not be able to stop it."

Antonia looked at the lamps at the end of the walk.

"That is the price," she said.

"That is the price."

* * *

Verus came to the burned house in October, on a grey afternoon, with one slave and a litter and a face like a man who has not slept since the wedding.

The house was a shell. The roof was gone from the dining room and the upper floor was a fall of black beams, and the mosaic in the atrium was under a layer of ash and broken tile, and the painted Danaë had lost her face to the smoke. The workmen had propped the front wall and were taking the rubble out in baskets, and the lane was full of dust, and Antonia was standing in the middle of it with a tablet, arguing with a builder about the price of a beam.

She saw Verus at the corner and came out to him with the tablet in her hand.

"You look like a woman who has been in a fire," he said.

"I have been in a fire." She wiped her face with the back of her wrist and left a smear of ash. "Say what you have come to say. I have thirty men and a beam to argue about."

"Send them away."

"No."

"Then walk with me."

They walked to the end of the lane and stood by the wall of the fuller's yard, which was black at the top where the fire had come over.

"I have been to the prefect's office," Verus said. "I have seen the papers on the girl Dessa. They are forgeries — the bill of sale is a forgery, the seal is genuine, and the procurator who is named on the bill does not exist. I have had my advocate look at it. It would not survive an hour in front of a praetor."

"Then why have you come to tell me?"

"Because the prefect's office does not care whether it would survive an hour in front of a praetor. It cares whether it survives until December. And it will." Verus looked at the burned wall. "I can get her out."

Antonia turned her head.

"How?"

"Petronius Celer is the urban prefect. He is a lazy man and an honest one, and he does not know what his office is doing, and if a consular comes to him with a forged bill of sale and a witness who will swear to the forgery, he will be extremely angry, because a prefect who is made to look a fool by his own freedmen is a prefect who will not be made a fool of twice." Verus's voice was flat. "I can be that consular. I can go to him tomorrow with my advocate and the papers and the man who sold the girl to the dealer in Rome, and the girl can be out of the cell in a week."

"Why would you do that?"

Verus was silent for a moment.

"Because I have been sitting in my tablinum since the night you took her out of my house," he said, "and I have discovered something about myself that I would rather not have discovered. I have spent my whole life being a man who does not do a thing. And now I find that I cannot stop doing things. It is a very tiring condition, and I do not recommend it."

"That is not an answer."

"No." He looked at her. "I want the case to be in November. I want it heard, and the girl freed, and the record made, and the whole thing done properly, and then I want to go to Asia and not come back. And I want the other thing not to happen. That is the answer."

"What other thing?"

"The senate," Verus said. "Gallus has been to see me. He came four days ago and he stood in my atrium and he told me what he intends to do, in front of my wife and my steward and my son, and he did it in the voice of a man who has decided to be reasonable, which is the most frightening voice there is." He looked at Antonia. "He will ask the house to hear you. And you will tell them about the stone room, and about the lamp, and about the fifty-one times, and about my son in the lane, and about the aedile's two hundred thousand, and I will be finished, and I will deserve it, and my wife will be finished too, and my daughter will be a woman whose father was destroyed in the senate house a month after her wedding, and she will spend the rest of her life being that."

"And what do you offer?"

"I offer the other bargain," Verus said. "You drop the senate. You keep the case, and the girl is freed in November by the praetor, and the record is made, and I sign the admission and pay whatever penalty the court names and then go to Asia. And I get the girl Dessa out of the cell, and I find out who forged the bill of sale and I put him in front of the prefect, and I do not stop until the prefect's office has been turned inside out."

"And Gallus?"

"Gallus will be very angry," Verus said. "He will be angry with you, and he will be angry with me, and he will go on being angry for the rest of his life, and his anger will be the only thing that is left of his son, and I do not know how to give him anything better."

Antonia stood by the burned wall of the fuller's yard.

"You are asking me to choose," she said, "between a girl in a cell in December and a man's name in the senate house."

"I am asking you to choose between two bad things," Verus said. "I have been doing it all my life. You get used to it. That is the worst thing I can tell you about it."

* * *

Crescens came at the tenth hour, off a watch, and found her in the shed behind the fuller's yard, which had a table made of a door and a lamp and a bed and a chest and a cat that had adopted the place and would not be evicted.

He had brought bread and a jar of wine and a face that she had learned to read in the way that a woman learns to read a man's face when she has decided not to ask him anything.

"The bill of sale," he said. "I have found the clerk who stamped it."

Antonia put down the tablet.

"In the prefect's office?"

"In the prefect's office. His name is Anicetus, he is a freedman of the household, and he has been in that office for eleven years. He did not stamp it himself. He told a clerk to stamp it and then took the clerk's tablet away and had a clean one made." Crescens put the bread on the table. "The clerk is frightened. He is nineteen and he has a wife and a baby, and he came to me because he knew that a tribune of the watch is not the prefect's office and because he has been carrying it about for four months and cannot eat."

"Will he swear to it?"

"He will swear to it in front of the prefect, in front of a praetor, in front of anybody, so long as his name is not written down." Crescens sat down on the chest. "And there is more. Anicetus answers to a man called Felix, and Felix answers to nobody in the office, and Felix has been paying the rent on a house in the Via Nova that is not in his name and never has been. The landlord is a client of Oppius Nepos. That is three steps, domina. I have two of them and the third one is a landlord who is frightened of his patron and will talk if he is pressed."

Antonia looked at him.

"You have been busy," she said.

"I have been a tribune of the watch for seven years," Crescens said, "and in seven years I have learned exactly two things: how to get a pump up a flight of stairs, and how to ask a question in a way that a frightened man can answer without losing his courage. It turns out the second one is useful in other trades."

"Give it to Gallus. He is the adsertor, and he has the standing to take it to the prefect."

"I am not giving it to Gallus."

"Why not?"

"Because Gallus is a grieving consular and he will take it to the prefect in a rage and it will become a public quarrel between two great men, and the clerk will be found dead in a ditch before the Kalends." Crescens looked at her. "Give it to Verus."

Antonia sat back.

"I never thought I would hear you say that."

"I never thought I would say it. But Verus has something no one else in this has: he wants to be clean. He has spent thirty years being a man who does not do a thing, and now he is doing things, and a man who is doing things for the first time in his life is the most useful instrument in the world, because he will not stop and he does not know how to be careful."

"You are asking me to trust him."

"I am asking you to use him," Crescens said. "It is not the same thing, and you know it, and you have been using men all your life."

Antonia looked at the lamp.

"Yes," she said.

Crescens was quiet for a moment. Then he said, "I have a thing to ask you, and it is not about the case."

"Ask."

"I have eleven thousand sesterces of debt to three lenders. I have a tribune's pay and a room at the station and a helmet on a nail. I am thirty-four years old and I have never owned a house or a field or a horse that was mine." He was looking at the lamp and not at her. "And I have been offered a post in the eleventh cohort at Ravenna, which is a promotion, and it is four days' journey from Rome, and it is a place where a man can save money and be somebody, and I have been sitting with the offer in my chest for a month and I have not answered it."

"Why not?"

"Because I do not want to go to Ravenna," Crescens said. "I want to stay in Rome and put out fires and be shouted at by people whose houses are burning, and I want to come to this shed at the tenth hour and find a woman with a tablet and a cat and a ruined house, and I want to be the man who is here." He looked up. "I am not asking you to marry me. I know what you would say to that, and I know that a woman in your position cannot afford a husband any more than a senator can marry a freedwoman. I am telling you what I want, because you are the only person in Rome I have ever told the truth to, and because if you are going to be finished in the senate house in November, I want it said before then."

Antonia sat in the shed with the cat on the chest and the lamp between them.

"You are a fool," she said.

"I know."

"You are thirty-four years old and you are in debt and you have a helmet on a nail, and you have just refused a promotion in Ravenna to sit in a shed in the Subura with a woman who is going to be destroyed in a senate house in a month."

"Yes."

"Say it again."

"I want to be the man who is here," Crescens said.

Antonia got up and came around the table, and took his face in her hands, and kissed him, and the cat got off the chest and went out through the door with the dignity of a thing that had been inconvenienced.

"Then be here," she said. "In November, and in December, and in the spring when there is no house and no money and no book, and I have nothing to give you but a ruin and a great deal of trouble."

"I like trouble," Crescens said. "I am a fireman."

* * *

Marcella came the next morning, in a litter, with a slave and a basket and a face like a city wall that had been rained on.

"I have come to be unpleasant," she said.

"Come in. There is a chair. It is the only one."

Marcella sat in the chair and put the basket on the floor and looked around the shed at the door on trestles and the lamp and the bed and the cat.

"You are living in a shed."

"I am living in a shed."

"You are the richest freedwoman in Rome."

"I am the richest freedwoman in Rome and my house has no roof and my strongbox is in the fuller's cellar and my book is in a jar under a floor in Ostia, and I am living in a shed because a shed is what I have." Antonia sat on the chest. "Say what you have come to say."

"My husband has told me everything," Marcella said. "The senate. Gallus. The girl in the cell. The bargain he offered you in the lane and the bargain he did not offer, which is the one where he goes to the prefect and makes a fool of himself and does a decent thing for a slave he has never met." She looked at Antonia. "He has not slept since the wedding. He sits in the tablinum at night with a lamp and he does not read. He has begun to talk to me — actually talk to me, in sentences, about his father and the year 93 and the stone room — and I have discovered in the last month that I have been married for nine years to a man I did not know at all, and that the man I did not know is worse than the one I married, and that I prefer him."

"That is a strange thing to say."

"It is a strange thing to discover." Marcella's hands were folded in her lap. "I came to ask you not to do it."

"The senate."

"The senate. Take the bargain. Let the girl be freed by the praetor in the afternoon with the doors closed and the record made and no speeches. Let him go to Asia with his name and his debts and his bad nights. Let my stepdaughter be a woman whose father went to a province and came back and was dull at dinner." Marcella looked at her. "You have a daughter. You have spent eighteen years wanting one thing. You are about to get it, and you are about to spend it on a speech."

Antonia was silent for a moment.

"Yes," she said.

"Then do not."

"I am going to do it," Antonia said, "and I am going to tell you why, and you are not going to like it, and you are going to go home and be angry with me for a year." She leaned forward. "If I take the bargain, the girl is freed and the record is made and your husband goes to Asia, and in ten years there is not a man in Rome who remembers that anything happened. My daughter will be a freedwoman who was freed by a praetor, quietly, for reasons that nobody wrote down. And every time she meets a man who knows, she will see him decide not to mention it. That is what a quiet freeing is. It is a thing that is done and not said, and a thing that is not said belongs to the man who did it, not to the woman it was done to."

Marcella looked at her.

"And if it is said?"

"Then it is said in the room where the men who govern this city sit, in front of the men who are going to decide whether your husband governs Asia, and it will be written down by a clerk, and it will be in the record of the senate of Rome for as long as there is a senate of Rome, and no man will be able to decide not to mention it." Antonia's voice was steady. "She will be the girl whose mother stood up in the senate house. That is not a small thing. In this city, that is the only kind of thing that lasts."

Marcella sat in the chair with her hands in her lap.

"You are going to destroy my husband," she said.

"Yes."

"And my stepdaughter."

"Probably."

"And yourself."

"Yes."

Marcella looked at the basket on the floor and then at the cat, which had come back in and was sitting on the chest, and then at Antonia.

"I brought you food," she said. "It is in the basket. It is a chicken and some bread and a jar of the wine my father sends from Puteoli, and I brought it because I have been told that you are living on bread and olives in a shed, and because I am unable to have a conversation with a woman I admire without feeding her." She stood up. "Do not thank me. And do not expect me to come to the senate house."

"I do not expect it."

"I will be in the portico outside, in a litter, with the curtains drawn, and I will hear all of it, and I will not come in, and afterward I will go home and I will not speak to my husband for a week." Marcella went to the door. "That is the best I can do, and it is more than you deserve, and it is less than I would like."

She went out to the litter. At the door of the shed she stopped.

"The girl in the cell," she said. "The Dacian."

"Yes."

"My husband will get her out. He has already begun. He went to the prefect on the fourth day before the Ides and he made a fool of himself in the prefect's own tablinum, and he came home and told me about it in sentences, and I have never seen him so pleased with himself in nine years." She looked back. "He is a poor creature, my husband. But he is doing a decent thing, and I find that I am glad of it, and I am telling you because there is no one else I can tell."

She got into the litter, and the bearers took her away down the Clivus, and Antonia stood in the doorway of the shed with the basket of chicken and wine in her hands and the cat winding around her ankles, and the whole Subura going about its business in the October sun.

* * *

She went back to the house and worked until dark, and then she walked up the Clivus to the corner where the lanes met and stood looking down at the ruin of her own front door.

The gilt was gone. The studs were black. The door itself was standing, because it was oak and iron and it had been made by a man who knew his trade, but it had a hole in it the size of a man's head where the vigiles had put an axe through it, and through the hole she could see the passage and the black stairs.

Tullia came up the lane with a lamp and a basket.

"You have not eaten," she said. "Again. The builder says the beam will be eleven hundred and he will not come down, and the mosaic man says he can save the atrium floor if he starts in the spring, and the fuller's wife says she will keep the girls in the yard until the roof is on, and the fuller's wife is a good woman and she is charging you four hundred a month for a shed."

"Pay her six."

"Domina."

"Pay her six hundred a month and tell her it is for the shed," Antonia said, "and that I know it is not for the shed."

Tullia put the basket down.

"What has happened?" she said.

"Verus came."

"I saw him."

"He offered me the other bargain." Antonia told her, standing in the ash of the lane with the lamp between them. "The case, the girl freed, the record, and Dessa out of the cell, and the man who forged the bill in front of the prefect. And nothing in the senate. And Gallus goes on being angry for the rest of his life."

"And if you take it?"

"Then in ten years Verus will be a proconsul who was once a man who bought a child, and the girl will be a freedwoman in a house on the Aventine, and nobody in Rome will remember that it cost anything, and it will have cost everything, and there will be no record of it except in my book, and the book will be a book of names." Antonia looked at the hole in the door. "And Dessa will be out of the cell, and that is not nothing. That is a great deal. She is twenty years old and she has been in a cell for three months and she is going to be questioned in December about what she saw in the garden, and if she is questioned she will not come out of it with a whole hand."

Tullia was silent for a while.

"And if you do not take it?" she said.

"Then I go into the senate house in November and I tell the whole of it, and the whole city hears it, and the girl is freed in the afternoon and Dessa goes on being in the cell, and I do not know what happens to her." Antonia's voice was steady. "And Verus is finished and Verianus is finished and Marcella is a woman whose husband was destroyed in the senate house, and Publia will be a woman whose father was destroyed a month after her wedding, and I will have done to her exactly what he did to me."

Tullia looked at her.

"You are not asking me which is right," she said.

"No."

"You are asking me which one you can live with."

"Yes."

Tullia bent and picked up the basket.

"I have been with you nine years," she said. "In nine years I have watched you do a great many hard things in a particular order, and the order has always been the house first, and the women second, and yourself last. And I have never once seen you choose the thing that was good for you, and I have sometimes thought that it was because you did not think you deserved it, and I have sometimes thought that it was the only thing about you that was not for sale."

"And now?"

"Now I think that you are standing in a lane with a hole in your door and a dead man in the ground and a girl in a cell, and you are asking a freedwoman with a basket which of two bad things she can live with, and the truth is that you will not be able to live with either of them." Tullia put the lamp in her hand. "So you will have to choose the one that will do the most good for the people who are left, and I am telling you, as the woman who keeps your accounts, that the people who are left are not the ones in the senate house."

* * *

She wrote to Gallus that night, and she wrote it three times, and the third one said:

*I will do it. I will stand in the senate house and say all of it. But not on the day of the case. On the day before, so that the praetor hears the case knowing what was said, and the whole city has a night to think about it before the girl is produced.*

*And I will not say a word about your son. Not one word. You will say it, or no one will. I have done enough to you.*

*If you want to withdraw as adsertor, withdraw now. I will not think less of you. I would think less of a man who stayed.*

The answer came back within the hour, on a scrap of tablet, in the old man's hand.

*I will not withdraw. I have been a consular for sixteen years and have never once done a thing that mattered. I do not intend to begin now.*

*One correction. You will say one word about my son, and the word is his name.*

*Bring the girl to the court on the Ides of November. I will be there at the second hour with the papers and the witnesses and a very bad cold, which I intend to use to make the house listen to me.*

*L. Iunius Gallus.*

Antonia sat in the back room of the fuller's shed with the letter in her hand, and outside the shed the Subura was being the Subura, and she laughed until she had to put her hand over her mouth.
