The station house of the seventh cohort stood at the foot of the Viminal, in a narrow street between a bakery and a yard where a man kept pigs, and it had been built in the reign of Augustus and had been repaired, badly, by every emperor since. It had a courtyard with a well, a shed for the pumps, a stable with four horses that were too old to pull anything, and a room upstairs where the tribune slept when he slept.

By the time Antonia was brought there, at the second hour, the courtyard was full. There were twelve firemen in leather aprons, three of them still black to the elbow from a chimney the night before. There was a clerk at a folding table with a wax tablet and a pen. There was a man from the aedile's office in a good cloak, standing apart, with his hands behind his back and the expression of a man who has been woken up and intends to make somebody pay for it. And there was a body on a board under a sheet, in the corner, out of the way, with a boy of about fifteen sitting beside it and waving a branch at the flies.

Antonia came in a plain mantle with Tullia and Hilarus, and she came on foot, because a litter at a station house in the morning was a statement, and she had not yet decided what statement to make.

"Antonia Cassia," the clerk said. "Of the house on the Clivus Suburanus."

"Yes."

"The aedile's office has been informed of the death of Decimus Iunius Gallus, of the house of the Iunii, son of Lucius Iunius Gallus, consular. The body was found in the fountain of the house of Antonia Cassia at the sixth hour of the morning, by a slave. Is that correct?"

"It is correct."

"You will tell the office what you know."

"I will tell the office what I saw. I did not see him fall."

The man from the aedile's office turned. He was about fifty, and he had the voice of a man who had spent thirty years saying the same sentences and had never once been contradicted by anyone with more money than the man who sent him.

"You will tell the office what you know," he said again.

"I will tell you what I saw," Antonia said. "And then you will tell me what you want."

The man's name was Vibius. He was a freedman of the aedile's household, and he had a chain of offices behind him and a chain of favors in front of him, and he had been sent out at dawn because a consular's son had drowned in a house of ill fame, and because the aedile did not want to know what had happened, he wanted to know what it would cost to make it have not happened.

"Tell me about the evening," Vibius said.

She told him. She told it well. She had spent twenty years learning how to tell a thing so that it was true in every particular and false in its whole. There had been a party. There had been thirty guests. There had been wine, and a quarrel between two young men, and one of them had struck the other, and the other had gone home. She had retired at the sixth hour. She had been woken by a scream. She had come out and found the body in the fountain and had sent for the watch at once.

"Who was the young man who struck him?"

"Manius Cassius Verus the Younger."

"And where is he now?"

"I do not know. He left the house at the fourth hour, before the body was found, and he did not speak to me."

"Did he strike him hard?"

"He struck him once, in the face, and the boy went over a couch."

"And then?"

"And then I stopped it. I gave Decimus the choice of going home or being carried, and he chose to go home, and he went home." Antonia looked at him. "That is what I saw. If he came back, I did not see it."

Vibius wrote. The clerk wrote. The firemen watched from the far side of the yard, and one of them, a big man with a broken nose, was watching her the way men watch a thing they have been told not to touch.

"The aedile will want to know," Vibius said, "whether the house was in the habit of serving wine to men of the senatorial order without limit, and whether the girls of the house were in the garden at the time, and whether any of them were —"

"Say the word," Antonia said. "You have been circling it for a quarter of an hour. Say the word and I will answer it."

Vibius looked up. Something in his face closed like a shutter.

"The aedile will want to know," he said, "whether the house is a place where such a death could be called an accident without further inquiry."

"Ah," said Antonia. "That is a different sentence. Now we understand each other." She sat down on the bench by the well, and arranged her mantle, and looked up at him. "How much?"

The clerk stopped writing. The firemen went very still. Vibius's face did not move, but his hands did, and Antonia had been reading hands for twenty years.

"That is not —"

"It is exactly that. You have come here at dawn with a clerk and a tablet and a body, and you have not sent for the coroner, and you have not sealed the house, and you have not questioned the slaves one by one, and you have not asked a single question about the fountain. You have asked me about the wine and the girls and the word accident. So: how much?"

Vibius looked at her for a long moment. Then he said, "The aedile is a busy man. A young man drowned at a party. It happens. The family will want it quiet, the house will want it quiet, and the city will want it quiet in September, because there is a wedding."

"There is a wedding."

"There is a wedding," Vibius said, "and the aedile has been invited."

Antonia stood up. "Then the aedile shall have a gift. A hundred thousand sesterces to the games fund, in the name of my house, and my house will close for the month of May, and the girls will go to the country, and there will be no parties and no noise and no stories in the Subura. That is what the aedile's office may tell him."

Vibius's mouth moved. "A hundred thousand."

"Two hundred," Antonia said, "if the office writes the word accident before the sun goes down."

The clerk wrote it. Vibius took the tablet and read it, and nodded, and said that the aedile would be grateful, and that the body would be released to the family at the second hour, and that the office would make a note of the cooperation of the house.

"And the slave," Vibius said. "The one who found him."

Antonia's face did not change. "What of her?"

"The office will want to question her. A slave's evidence is taken under —"

"The slave was sold last month," Antonia said. "To a house in Africa. She went out on the ship from Puteoli on the Ides. There is a registration."

Vibius looked at her. "You told me she found the body."

"I told you a slave found the body. I did not say which slave. There are eleven in my house. The one who found him has been with me for six years and her name is Rufina, and she will tell you the same story I have told you, because it is the true one."

The man from the aedile's office looked at her for a long, long moment.

"You are a very cool woman," he said.

"I am a very expensive one," Antonia said. "Cheap women are the ones who lose their nerve."

When Vibius had gone, with the tablet under his arm and the clerk behind him, the courtyard of the seventh cohort went back to being a courtyard. The firemen rolled the pumps out to dry them. The boy with the branch went on waving it at the flies. And Crescens came down the outside stair in his uniform, with his helmet under his arm, and stood beside her at the well without saying anything for a moment.

"You lied to him," he said.

"I did."

"You lied to him in my station house, in front of my men, and now my men think that the tribune of the seventh cohort takes money from a brothel to write the word accident on a boy's death."

"Did you take money?"

"No."

"Then your men think you did, which is the same as having taken it, and you will have to live with that, or you will have to tell them the truth." Antonia looked at him. "Which will it be?"

Crescens put his helmet down on the well-head. He looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with the night.

"I have been in this cohort six years," he said. "I have pulled forty-one people out of burning buildings and I have watched eleven of them die, and I have been thanked twice. I have been in the Subura at midnight and at noon, and I have seen what a landlord does when a wall falls down and kills a tenant, and I have seen what a father does when his daughter is taken by a man with a name. I know what the aedile's office is. I have known for six years. I did not need you to tell me."

"Then why are you angry?"

"Because I thought I was the one man in Rome who could stand in a room and say what happened, and now I am standing in a room with you and saying what you tell me to say." He picked up his helmet. "There was something on the boy's hands. Or rather, there was nothing on them, and there should have been. Did you notice?"

"I noticed."

"Then you know more than Vibius, and Vibius knows more than the aedile, and the aedile knows more than anybody will ever say out loud." Crescens looked at the door. "I am going to write in my report that the body was found face down, that the water was shallow, that there was a cut on the forehead consistent with a fall against the rim of the basin, and that there were no marks of a struggle. All of that is true. I am not going to write that the cut was on the front of the head and that a man falling forward into a fountain cuts his chin or his cheekbone and not the middle of his forehead, because if I write that, the report goes to the prefect, and the prefect's office has a man called Felix with a burned hand who has been asking questions about your house, and I do not want Felix reading my report."

Antonia looked at him. "You have been asking."

"I have been asking. Quietly. At the station, and at the baths, and once at a wine shop where the prefect's clerks drink." He put his helmet on. "Felix is not the prefect's man. He is paid by the prefect's office and he answers to a man called Anicetus, who answers to nobody in the office at all. Anicetus has a patron. I do not know the name. The name is not written anywhere, which means the man who owns him is either very great or very careful."

"Or both."

"Or both," Crescens said. "Go home, domina. And when the funeral is over, come to me at the station at the second hour, and I will show you what a man's hands look like when he has drowned fighting, and then you will know how much of a lie you have told."



* * *

She got back to the house at the fourth hour, and Gallus was already in the atrium.

He had come alone, which was worse than coming with a crowd. He was in the plain tunic of a man who has been woken in the night and has not thought about his clothes, and his face was the face of a man who has been told that his son is dead and has not yet been told anything else.

"Where is he?" he said.

"At the station house. They will bring him to your house at the second hour. I have sent word to your steward."

"He was in your fountain."

"Yes."

"You sent for the watch."

"Yes."

"You did not send for me."

Antonia stood in the middle of her own atrium and did not look away. "I sent for the watch, and then I sent for you, and you were asleep, and your steward was told, and he said he would tell you when you woke."

"He did not tell me. A boy from the station told me. A boy in a helmet, with a note in his hand, at the door, in front of my wife." Gallus's voice was very quiet. "Do you know what my wife said? She said, 'Which son?'"

Antonia did not answer.

"She has three sons," Gallus said. "Two by me and one by her first husband. She said, 'Which son?' and the boy said, 'Decimus,' and she sat down on the step. And I stood in my own doorway with the whole street looking at me, and I thought, the woman on the Clivus knew before I did."

He walked past her into the garden, and stood looking at the fountain.

The water had been drained. The basin was dry, with a dark stain on the bottom and a scattering of leaves, and someone had scrubbed the rim, and the scrub had not taken out the mark where the boy's head had struck the marble.

"Tell me," Gallus said.

She told him. She told him the same story she had told Vibius, in the same words, and she watched him listen to it, and she watched him take it in, and she watched the moment he did not believe her.

"Decimus was drunk," he said.

"Very."

"He insulted your house."

"He did."

"And Verus's son struck him."

"Once. In the face."

"And then he went home." Gallus turned. His eyes were dry, and that was the worst of it. "My son went home, and then he came back to your house, and he climbed over your wall or he came through your gate, and he went to your fountain and he fell in and he drowned. In the dark. Alone."

"That is what I know."

"You know more."

"I know what I saw."

Gallus took two steps and took her by the arm, and his grip was not violent, it was worse: it was the grip of a man holding on to something in a storm.

"He was nineteen," he said. "He was a fool, and he was vain, and he spent my money, and he made jokes about people who could not answer him, and he was my son. And I have spent the morning in my own house listening to my wife say that it was a house of women, and that he should never have gone, and that she had told me so, and she had, and she was right. And now you are standing in your garden with your hands folded and telling me that a boy fell into a fountain."

Antonia put her hand over his.

"Lucius," she said. "Let go of my arm."

He looked down. He let go.

"I will find out," she said. "Whatever there is to find, I will find it. Not for the aedile, and not for the city, and not for your wife. For you. And when I have found it, I will bring it to you and no one else, and you will decide what to do with it. That I promise you. On the house, and on the water, and on whatever I have that is worth swearing by."

Gallus looked at the fountain.

"There is a thing I have to ask you," Gallus said. "It is not about the fountain."

"Ask."

"The masks. The family masks. There are fourteen of them, and my father had them repainted in the year he was consul, and they are in the cupboard in the atrium, and on the third day they will bring them out and the actors will put them on, and the whole family will be a procession of dead men with my son at the front." Gallus's voice was steady and terrible. "And I find that I cannot bear the thought of my son's face being made into a mask and put in a cupboard and worn by an actor at somebody else's funeral in twenty years. I find that I would rather there were no masks at all, and no procession, and no speeches, and no city looking at us."

"Then have no masks."

"My wife wants the masks. My wife's family wants the masks. The Iunii have been consuls for two hundred years and the masks are what a family has instead of money." Gallus looked at the dry basin. "I have spent my life believing that a family is a thing that continues. And now I find that what continues is the masks, and the name, and the debts, and that the boy is not continued at all. The boy is simply gone, and the masks go on without him."

"Lucius."

"I am not asking you to agree with me. I am telling you what I have discovered in the last six hours, because you are the only person I can tell it to who will not repeat it." He turned. "He was afraid of the dark," he said. "When he was small. He would not sleep without a lamp, and his mother used to say that a Roman should not be afraid of anything, and I used to leave the lamp. Every night. For eleven years." He turned and walked to the door. "Find out, then."

At the door he stopped. "The funeral is on the third day. There will be masks. There will be a procession with the whole family's death masks, and the actors will wear them, and my son will be the newest face in the cupboard, and the whole city will come to look at us."

"Lucius."

"Do not come," he said. "Not to the house, and not to the funeral. Not until you bring me what you promised."

He went out. Cerdon shut the door.

* * *

Dessa was in the cellar.

It was not a prison, though it had been built as one, in the year when Antonia had bought the house and had found, under the kitchen, a room with a barred window and a ring in the wall. She had the ring taken out and the window enlarged, and she used the room for wine, and the smell of the wine had gone into the walls, so that the place smelled like a good year even when the year was bad.

The girl was sitting on a stool with a cup of water in her hands. She had been there since before dawn. Her hands had been washed — Rufina had washed them, without being told, which was either a kindness or a disaster — and her face was the color of the wall.

"Leave us," Antonia said.

Rufina went. Antonia sat down on the other stool and waited. She had learned, from years of dealing with frightened people, that the first thing a frightened person says is never the thing that matters, and that the second thing is usually the truth, and that the way to get to the second thing is to wait until the silence has become more uncomfortable than the lie.

The silence lasted a long time. The wine in the racks breathed. Somewhere above them, Beryllos dropped something.

"I did not push him," Dessa said.

The girl had been in the cellar for a night, and she had not slept, and she had the particular stillness of a slave who has decided that the safest thing in the world is to be small. Antonia had seen that stillness in her own hands once, in a room above a fuller's shop, and she had spent eighteen years learning to see it in other people and to hate it.

"Before that," Antonia said. "Everything before that. Start at the beginning of the day, and do not leave anything out because you think I will be angry. I have been angry at better men than you and it has never cost me a night's sleep, so you may tell me anything."

Dessa looked at the cup. "There was a man in the lane in the afternoon. He came to the gate with a message for Cerdon and Cerdon told him to come back at dark. And then at dark there was a man in the lane again, and I thought it was the same one, and I watched him from the kitchen window, and he did not come to the gate. He stood at the corner by the fuller's and looked at the house."

"For how long?"

"Until the lamps were lit. And then he went away up the hill, and came back." Dessa's hands tightened on the cup. "He came back after the party began. He stood in the lane and he watched the terrace, and I could see him through the gap in the wall, and I thought, that man is counting the guests."

"Could you see his face?"

"The lamp was behind him."

"His cloak?"

"It was dark. It had a hood."

"His height?"

"He was tall. Taller than Cerdon." Dessa stopped. "He was standing the way the master used to stand."

"Which master?"

"The one who sold me. When he was waiting for me to be brought to him. He stood with his hands behind his back and his chin up, and he did not move, and he was not in a hurry, and that was the worst of it." She looked up. "This man stood like that."

Antonia sat very still. She knew that stance. She had seen it in a senate-house corridor and in a garden on the Viminal and in a doorway at the fourth hour, and it belonged to a particular kind of Roman who has been waited on since he was four years old.

"Go on," she said.


"I know."

"I did not touch him. I went out to the garden because I could not sleep and I heard a noise in the fountain, and I went and there was a man in the water and I put my hands in to turn him over, that is all, that is all I did, and then I screamed and then Hilarus came."

"You put your hands in the water."

"To turn him over."

"Which way?"

"What?"

"Which way did you turn him over? Toward you, or away?"

Dessa looked at her. "I — toward me. I took him by the shoulder and pulled, and he came up, and his face was —" She stopped.

"He was face down."

"Yes."

"And you turned him toward yourself, which means his head went back, and his face came up, and you saw it."

"Yes."

"And his arms were out, with the palms up."

"Yes." Dessa's voice had gone very small. "I remember the hands."

Antonia sat back. It was not proof. It was not even much of a fact. But a man who is drowning fights; a man who is drowning puts his hands down and pushes and scrapes and takes the skin off his knuckles on the marble, and there had been no scrapes on the boy's knuckles. A man who is stunned and goes into the water face down floats with his hands out and his palms up, and there had been a cut on his forehead, and a cut on a forehead needs something to cut it.

The rim of the fountain was smooth. She had felt it herself, in the dark, with her fingers, before the watch came. It was smooth as a dish.

"When you came into the garden," Antonia said, "was there anyone else there?"

Dessa did not answer at once. She looked at the cup in her hands.

"The gate was open," she said.

"Yes. You told Cerdon."

"I told Cerdon the gate was open. I did not tell him that I saw a man in the lane."

Antonia's heart moved. "What man?"

"I do not know. He was at the end of the lane by the fuller's, in the dark, and he was not walking, he was standing. And when I screamed, he went."

"Which way?"

"Down the hill."

"Was he tall?"

"I think so."

"Did he have a cloak?"

"I could not see. It was dark."

"Did he have a lamp?"

"No."

"Then he knew the lane," Antonia said. "A man in the Subura at night without a lamp either knows the lane or is drunk or is up to something. Did you see his hands?"

Dessa looked up. "His hands?"

"Did you see his hands?"

"No, domina." The girl's face changed, and something came into it that Antonia could not read. "I saw his ring."

"His ring."

"It was bright. It caught the light from the passage, when I came out with the lamp. It was on his hand, and it was bright."

"A gold ring?"

"Yes."

"With what on it?"

Dessa thought. "A leaf," she said. "It was a little leaf."

Antonia sat very still on the stool in the wine cellar, and the smell of the good year came up out of the walls, and she heard, quite distinctly, the sound of her own voice saying, sixteen years ago, in a house on the Viminal, *give me back my ring.*

* * *

That afternoon the house closed.

It was done in an hour, the way a house closes when it has closed before. The shutters went up on the street side. The garlands came down and were burned in the yard with the dry beans. Beryllos packed his knives in a cloth and sat on a chest in the kitchen and wept, briefly and without embarrassment, and then went to his sister's in the Aventine with a ham under his arm that Antonia pretended not to see.

Kallisto went to a patron's house at the fourth hour in a litter with two boxes, and at the gate she turned and said, "When it opens again, I want the papers. Not a share. The papers."

"When it opens again," Antonia said, "we will talk about the papers."

"That is what you said in October."

"In October I was lying. Now I am not." Antonia looked at her. "If the house does not open again, go to Daphnus at Ostia and tell him I sent you. He will find you a place on a ship, and a house that will take you in Puteoli, and he will not ask you for anything, which will confuse you."

Kallisto's mouth worked. Then she got into the litter, and the bearers took her away down the Clivus, and the lane was empty, and the gilt on the door looked cheap in the afternoon light.

Cela and the two youngest went to the country house of a patron's wife at Ficulea, with Rufina to keep them, and were glad to go, and did not understand what had happened. Hilarus stayed, and Cerdon stayed, and Meroe stayed, and Tullia stayed, and Dessa stayed in the cellar because there was nowhere else for her to be that was safe.

At the sixth hour Antonia walked through the empty house and listened to it. A house with thirty people in it makes a hundred small noises; a house with seven makes a different hundred, and each of them is a door being closed by someone who is being careful.

In the dining room the painted Danaë held out her hands to a shower of coins. Antonia looked at her for a while.

"You were not a fool," she said. "You were a woman with a father. It is not the same thing."

That night she wrote three letters.

The first was to the house of Lucius Iunius Gallus, and it said that the body would be brought at the second hour, and that the house on the Clivus would pay for the funeral, and that the writer would not attend unless sent for. She sealed it and gave it to Cerdon.

The second was to Daphnus at Ostia, and it said: *The men from Caesarea will look at the manifests. They will find nothing, because there is nothing, and then they will look for something else. Close the warehouse at the end of May and move the Egyptian wheat to the smaller one. Do not write to me until June.*

The third she wrote twice and burned the first one.

The second said: *You were in the lane. The girl saw the ring. Come to me before the funeral or I will come to you, and I will bring the watch with me.*

She sealed it with wax and pressed the seal with the ring that had a leaf on it, the ring she had taken from his hand in the year 93 and had kept for eighteen years and had never once worn.

Then she sat in the small room with the lamp and waited for morning, and the house was silent, and the city was silent, or as silent as it ever was, and somewhere in the dark a man was turning over in his bed with a leaf on his finger and thinking about a fountain.
