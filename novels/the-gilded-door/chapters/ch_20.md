The garden of the house of Pompeia Plotina was not a large garden and it was not a rich one. There was a gravel walk between two beds of box, a fig tree, a bench with a broken arm that had been mended with wire, and at the end of the walk, where the wall turned, a small fountain with a lion's head and a basin full of green water.

Antonia had expected something else. She had expected marble and peacocks; she had expected a garden that said what a garden says when an emperor's wife owns it. What she found was a garden that said nothing at all, and she understood, standing at the gate with Callirhoe's hand on her arm, that this was the garden of a woman who had spent thirty years being looked at and had decided to have one place in the world where nothing was arranged.

While she waited at the gate, a girl came out of the house with a basket of figs and a jug and set them down on the step and said, "The empress says you are to have something while you wait, and that the figs are from the tree at the back and are not very good, and that you are not to say they are good."

"Tell her they are bad," Antonia said, "and that I have eaten four."

The girl went away delighted. Antonia sat on the step of the empress's house with a basket of figs in her lap and the whole Palatine going on above her, and found that her hands had stopped shaking for the first time in three days.

"Walk with me," the empress said.

She was fifty-four and small, and she wore a plain grey dress with no ornament at all but a pair of pearl earrings, and she walked slowly, and she had a way of looking at the ground in front of her feet that was not shyness.

"You are the woman who sent the petition in March," she said.

"I am."

"And in August you stood at my gate for an hour and would not go away."

"Yes."

"Callirhoe has been at that gate for nineteen years and she has never once let a stranger in. She let you in because of a story about her sister." Plotina looked up. "Was it true?"

"The sister was in a house on the Aventine for six years and died in the year of the fire," Antonia said. "Whether the rest of it is true, I do not know. I did not know her."

"No," Plotina said. "You did not. Callirhoe knows that, and she let you in anyway, and she has been in a state about it for a week." She walked a little way in silence. "I have read your petition. I have read it three times. The office has refused it, and the office has written nothing in the book, and there is a young clerk in the office who is going to be dismissed in the spring for a thing he did in August, and he does not know it yet."

Antonia's hands were cold.

"He gave me a copy," she said. "An unsealed copy."

"He gave you a piece of paper that had not been sealed, which is not a copy of anything, and he told you that the book is written up at the end of the quarter, which was true in the reign of Vespasian and has not been true since." Plotina stopped by the fig tree. "He is nineteen. He has a wife and a baby. He will be dismissed and he will not find another place in the palace, and he will go to the prefect's office, and the prefect's office will take him, because the prefect's office takes everybody."

"Domina."

"Do not." Plotina held up her hand. "I am not telling you this to make you feel guilty. I am telling you because you are about to walk into the senate house and say a great many true things, and I want you to know what a true thing costs in this city before you do it. It costs a nineteen-year-old clerk his place. It costs a tribune of the watch his cohort. It costs a midwife from Ficulea the goodwill of the men she delivers children for." She turned. "It will cost you your house, which is already burnt, and your name, which is the only thing you have that nobody gave you."

Antonia stood by the fig tree.

"You know about the senate house," she said.

"Gallus asked the consuls for the house to hear you, and the consul asked me whether the empress would be embarrassed by it, and I said that the empress would be interested by it." Plotina began to walk again. "The consul was relieved. He had expected me to say something else."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because I am going to make you an offer," Plotina said, "and I have been thinking about it for three days, and I am going to make it badly, because I have not made an offer to anyone in thirty years without an advocate in the room."

They came to the end of the walk, to the bench with the broken arm and the fountain with the green water, and the empress sat down on the bench and gestured to the other end of it, and Antonia sat.

"Your petition," Plotina said. "The office will not advance it. The office will not advance anything that has Nepos's seal on it, and Nepos has made a representation, and the prefect's office has written a letter, and the letter will sit in the file for a year, and in a year the emperor will be in the east and the file will be in a trunk." She looked at the fountain. "But the emperor can grant a rescript by his own hand, without the office, in a case that has been brought to his attention by a member of his household. It has been done. It is done more often than the office likes. And if a rescript of that kind is sealed before the emperor leaves Rome, it cannot be unsealed afterward, because a rescript is not a favor, it is a law."

Antonia looked at her.

"You are offering me the rescript," she said.

"I am offering you the rescript," Plotina said. "Sealed by the emperor's own hand, in the week before he leaves. Restitutio natalium. You would be freeborn, in law, from the day of your birth, and no man in Rome could say otherwise, and the register of the fourteenth region would be corrected, and the whole of the rest of your life would be a different thing."

Antonia sat on the bench with her hands in her lap.

"And in return?" she said.

"In return," Plotina said, "you will do what you were going to do anyway, and do it a great deal better. You will go into the senate house and you will tell them everything: the year 93, the stone room, the girl, the note, the household. And then you will tell them about the seals."

"The seals."

"The prefect's office is a machine for the selling of seals. It has been a machine for the selling of seals since the reign of Domitian, and every emperor since has known it and has done nothing, because the machine is useful and because the men who run it are freedmen and a freedman can be dismissed. But the emperor is going east, and the east is a war, and a war is won with grain and money and roads, and the grain and the money and the roads all pass through the prefect's office, and every seal that is sold in that office is a hole in the hull." Plotina's voice was quiet and entirely without emotion. "There is a freedman called Anicetus. There is a freedman called Felix, who carries messages and has a burn on his hand. There is a man called Oppius Nepos, who is not in the office and who has been paying for seals for twenty years, and who is not an equestrian."

Antonia did not move.

"You know about Nepos," she said.

"I have known about Nepos for four years," Plotina said. "His father was an auctioneer's clerk and a slave, and the manumission is in the guild's book, and the guild's book is in a strongroom that three men must open, and one of the three is a senator who has been bought by Nepos and one of the three is a freedman who is frightened of him and one of the three is an old man in a shop in the Vicus Tuscus who has no teeth and remembers everything." She looked at Antonia. "You have been to see him. In May, and again in June. He has written to me twice about it. He is a very conscientious old man and he does not approve of you."

Antonia laughed. It came out of her unexpectedly, and it was not a happy sound.

"Domina," she said. "Why have you not done it yourself?"

"Because a woman of my household cannot appear in the senate house, and because a prefect cannot be accused by the emperor's wife, and because the moment this is said by someone with an interest, it becomes a faction. If I move against the prefect's office, it is the empress against the prefect, and the prefect will say that the empress is meddling in the grain supply, and the emperor will have to choose between his wife and his prefect, and he will choose the prefect, because the prefect has the watch and the grain and the Guard." Plotina turned her head. "But if a freedwoman stands up in the senate house on the day before a case and says, in her own voice, that she was questioned for eleven hours in the year 93 and that the men who bought her silence are the men who sell the emperor's seals — then it is not the empress against the prefect. It is a wronged woman against a corrupt office, and no emperor in the world can afford to be seen to defend a corrupt office against a wronged woman."

The fountain ran in the green basin.

"Domina," Antonia said, "may I ask you a question that is not about the senate?"

"You may ask. I may not answer."

"Your family is from Nemausus."

"Your family is from Nemausus," Plotina said, "and my father was a magistrate there and a citizen of Rome and an equestrian, and there is not a drop of patrician blood in me, and the first time I walked into the palace the wife of the consul looked at my shoes." She smiled. "I have never forgotten the shoes. I was thirty-one years old, and I had been married to a soldier for eleven years, and I had bought the best shoes I could afford, and they were not the right shoes."

"And now?"

"Now I have a hundred pairs of the right shoes," the empress said, "and I have discovered that a woman in the right shoes is listened to with exactly the same attention as a woman in the wrong ones, and that the difference is entirely in the head of the man who is listening." She stopped by the fig tree. "Your petition has been refused for a reason that is not written in the file. The reason is that a freedwoman who is made freeborn by a rescript becomes a woman who can marry a senator. There is not a family in Rome that wants that door opened. The office did not refuse you because of the house of pleasure or the aediles or the forged papers. The office refused you because a door that opens once can be opened again."

"Then why are you opening it?"

"Because the door has been shut for a hundred years and it is not the only door," Plotina said. "And because I have spent thirty years being told that a woman cannot do a thing, and I have found that what they mean is that a woman cannot do it without a man, and I have found that a man can be found for almost anything if the price is right, and that the price of a man is always the same: he wants to be seen to have done it himself."

"You have been planning this for four years," Antonia said.

"I have been waiting four years for a woman with a grievance and evidence and nothing to lose," Plotina said. "I did not expect her to be a freedwoman with a burnt house and a book of names. I expected a senator's widow."

"Domina, I am a woman who keeps a house of pleasure."

"You are a woman who kept a house of pleasure, and it burnt down in September, and you have been living in a shed behind a fuller's yard for two months, and you have not asked anyone for anything except a dress and a chair to be carried in." Plotina looked at her. "I have been in this city for thirty years. I can tell the difference between a woman who has a house of pleasure and a woman who has a house of business. The difference is in the accounts."

Antonia sat on the bench with her hands in her lap and the fountain running.

"There is a girl," she said. "In the prefect's office. Her name is Dessa and she is a slave of my household, and she was taken from Ostia in July with a forged bill of sale and a genuine seal, and she is going to be questioned in December about what she saw in my garden in April."

Plotina was silent for a moment.

"The questioning has been ordered?"

"Yes."

"By whom?"

"By the prefect's office, at the request of a magistrate."

"Which magistrate?"

"I do not know the name. I know that the order exists, and that it was made in October, and that the girl has been in a cell since July."

Plotina looked at the fountain for a long time.

"If I move against the prefect's office," she said, "the office will know that I have moved, and the order will be executed before I can stop it. If I do nothing, the order will be executed in December anyway." She turned. "There is one thing I can do, and it is not a favor to the girl. It is a favor to the record. I can have the order transferred from the prefect's office to the office of the urban prefect, and the urban prefect is Petronius Celer, who is lazy and honest and will not order a slave questioned about a death that has already been written as an accident unless someone makes him. That will buy a month. Perhaps two."

"A month is not enough. The case is on the Nones."

"Then you will have to be quick," Plotina said, "and I will have to be quicker, and neither of us will be able to say afterward that we were not warned."

* * *

She walked Antonia to the gate herself.

At the gate, with Callirhoe standing at her stool with her hands folded, the empress stopped.

"There is a question I ask everyone who comes to this garden," she said. "Most of them lie. I would like you to try not to."

"Ask it."

"What do you want?"

Antonia stood at the gate of a garden on the Palatine in the fourth day before the Nones of December.

"Ten years ago I would have said the rescript," she said. "Five years ago I would have said my house. In March I would have said my daughter." She looked at the empress. "Now I want to be in a room where the truth is worth something. That is all. I want to say a thing in a room full of men and have it be worth more than the money they were paid to forget it."

Plotina looked at her for a long moment.

"That is a very dangerous thing to want," she said, "and it is the only thing worth wanting, and I have wanted it myself for thirty years and I have never once been in a room where I could say it." She held out her hand. "Come to the palace on the day after the senate. The emperor leaves for the east in the spring, and there is a great deal to do before then, and a rescript is a small thing in a large list, but it will be on the list. Bring the man from the guild. Bring the page from the dealer. Bring the record of the manumission and the decree of the praetor when you have it."

"Domina."

"And Antonia Cassia." The empress's hand was small and dry and surprisingly strong. "When you are in that room tomorrow, do not be modest. Modesty is a virtue for women who have something to lose. You have a shed and a cat."

* * *

Gallus came to the shed that night with a cold that had gone into his chest and a list of the rules of the senate house written out on three tablets.

"You will be brought in at the second hour," he said. "You will be brought in by me, and you will stand at the doors, not in the middle of the floor. You will not approach the consuls' bench. You will not raise your voice. You will not weep; if you weep they will decide that you are a woman and they will stop listening; if you feel it coming, stop speaking and count to ten and start again." He wiped his nose. "You will speak for no more than a quarter of an hour. The consul has agreed to a quarter of an hour, and a quarter of an hour in that room is a very long time; most men cannot fill five minutes without saying something they regret."

"Who will be there?"

"Three hundred men, more or less. The consuls. The consulars. The praetors. The tribunes of the plebs, who will be insulted that they were not consulted. The decemvirs, if they are sitting. The emperor's freedmen, in the gallery, taking notes, which is the only part of it that matters; whatever is written down by the ab epistulis is what the emperor will read, and everything else is noise." Gallus sat down heavily on the chest. "And Verus will be there, because he is a consular and he has the right. And Nepos will be there, because he has been buying himself a seat in that gallery for twenty years. And Attianus will be there, because Attianus is always there."

"What do I call them?"

"You call the consuls *consul*, and you call the rest of them *patres conscripti* if you must address the house, and you do not call any man by his own name unless you are accusing him, and then you call him by his full name, three names, slowly, and you look at him while you do it." Gallus's eyes were bright with fever. "And domina — there will be a moment, about halfway through, when a man at the back will say something, and another man will laugh, and the room will begin to move. They do it to everyone. They did it to me in the year 96. You will have about six seconds to decide whether you are a woman who is speaking to the senate of Rome or a woman who is being laughed at by a room full of men, and whatever you decide in those six seconds is what you will be for the rest of your life."

"What did you do?"

"I stopped," Gallus said, "and I looked at the man who had laughed, and I said, *I am speaking*, and I waited. It took eleven seconds. Nobody has ever laughed at me in that room again."

Antonia looked at the old man.

"You are very good at this," she said.

"I am a great deal better at this than I was at being consul," Gallus said. "In the consulship I sat in a chair and signed things and was told by a clerk what the things were. It was the most useless year of my life, and I have thought about it every day since, and I have come to believe that the whole of the Roman state is designed so that a man may hold the highest office in the world and never once be required to do anything." He blew his nose. "It is a very clever design, and it is why the empire works, and it is why nothing ever changes, and it is why a woman with a shed and a book is the most dangerous thing in the city."

"I am a consular of Rome with a fever and a shed to sit in," Gallus said, "and it has taken me fifty-nine years to find the one thing I am actually good at, and it is telling a woman how to be listened to by men who do not want to listen."

* * *

Myrtale came up from the fuller's yard at the tenth hour with a bowl of soup and a face that Antonia had learned to read.

"You have been crying," Antonia said.

"I have not."

"You have. Your eyes are red and you have been at the fuller's shed with the cat, and you have been thinking about tomorrow, and you have decided something and you are afraid to tell me."

Myrtale put the soup down.

"I am coming to the senate house," she said.

"No."

"I am coming to the senate house, and I am going to sit in the gallery with the women, and when you have finished speaking I am going to stand up and say my name, and let them look at me."

"Cassia."

"You have spent eight months telling me that the whole point of this is that a thing which is not said is a thing that belongs to the man who did it," Myrtale said. "And tomorrow you are going to say it, in front of three hundred men, and if I am not there, then it is a woman saying what was done to a girl who is somewhere else. And they will say, where is the girl? And the answer will be, in a shed behind a fuller's yard, being kept safe. And that is exactly what he did. He kept me safe for eighteen years."

Antonia stood in the middle of the shed with the soup going cold on the table.

"You are right," she said.

"I know I am right."

"You are right, and I hate it, and I am going to allow it, and there will be a condition." Antonia took her by the shoulders. "You will sit with Tullia. You will not stand until I have finished and the consul has thanked me. And when you stand up, you will say your name and nothing else, and you will not answer a single question that any man in that room asks you, because a girl who answers questions in the senate house is a girl who has agreed to be examined, and you are not there to be examined. You are there to be seen."

"Cassia," Myrtale said.

"Cassia," Antonia said. "Now eat the soup. You have not eaten, and tomorrow is a long day, and I am not going to have my daughter faint in the senate house before she has said her name."

* * *

The dress came from Marcella's own wardrobe, and it was dark green, and it fitted at the shoulder and was too long, and Rufina sat up half the night with a lamp and a needle taking up the hem.

The chair came from a man in the Vicus Tuscus who hired litters by the day, and it was not a litter, it was a chair with a leather roof and two poles, and the bearers were two brothers from the Transtiberim who had been recommended by Vindex and who did not ask questions.

The book came from Ostia, in a jar, in a sack of grain, on a boat, and Tullia carried it up the Clivus herself at the fourth hour of the morning and put it on the table in the shed.

It was damp at the edges and the oiled cloth had kept out most of the water, and Antonia opened it on the table with the lamp beside it and turned the pages.

Eleven years. Four hundred and six names. Loans and gifts and favors and dates and, beside some of them, in Tullia's small hand or her own, a word or two about what the man had been afraid of.

She turned to the page with the praetor's name. She turned to the page with the name of the man who had bought her silence in the year 95. She turned to the page where a knight had written, in his own hand, that he would be grateful for the rest of his life, and where she had written, in hers, *he will not be*.

"You cannot take all of it into the senate house," Tullia said.

"No."

"You will be asked to name names."

"I will name four."

"Which four?"

"Anicetus, who sells the seals. Felix, who carries them. Nepos, who buys them." Antonia turned another page. "And the man who bought my silence in the year 95, because he is on the emperor's council and he is going to be sitting in that room, and because a man who is sitting in the room when his own name is said is a man who will not be able to look at the floor."

Tullia was quiet for a moment.

"And Verus?"

"Verus will name himself," Antonia said. "He has been doing it for two months. I do not need to do it for him."

She closed the book.

"Now," she said. "Tell me about the girl."

Tullia's face changed.

"The order was moved," she said. "It was moved to the urban prefect's office on the day before the Kalends, and the prefect's clerk wrote to the prefect's office for the papers, and the papers have not come. The girl is still in the cells under the prefect's office, and the men there say that the order of the ides of October is still in force and that they have had no instruction."

"Then Plotina's month is not a month."

"Plotina's month is a piece of paper, domina, and a piece of paper is only as good as the man who carries it." Tullia sat down on the chest. "There is one more thing. The agent from the prefect's office came to the fuller's yard this morning and asked for you. He did not ask at the door; he asked at the yard, of the fuller's wife, and he said that he was instructed to say that the girl would be available to be seen, by you and by no one else, at the fourth hour of the afternoon of the day after tomorrow, at the office, and that she would be available to be seen in a condition that would make the visit short."

Antonia sat with her hand on the book.

"A condition that would make the visit short," she said.

"That is what he said."

"Then he is telling me that they are going to question her, and that they are going to begin before the senate, and that they want me to know it." Antonia stood up. "Which means they want me to come to the office and bargain, and the bargain will be the book, and the bargain will be offered by a man who believes that a woman will trade a book for a girl."

"Will you go?"

"Yes."

"Domina."

"I will go," Antonia said, "and I will not take the book, and I will not bargain, and I will look at her and I will tell her that the senate is the day after and that there is nothing in the world that they can do to her in one day that will make me stop." She was putting on her mantle. "And then I will go to the senate house and I will say her name in the first sentence, before the year 93, before Verus, before Nepos, before anything. Her name and what was done to her and the name of the office that did it."

"Domina, if you say her name they will know that she matters to you."

"They already know," Antonia said. "That is what they are counting on. So I will show them what it looks like when a woman tells the truth about a thing they thought was a lever."

She went out into the lane, and the Subura was dark, and the cat came out of the shed and wound itself around her ankles, and she stood there for a moment with her hand on the door.

"Tullia," she said.

"Domina."

"Tomorrow, when I am in that room, I want you to be in the gallery with the other women, and I want you to listen to every word, and I want you to write down what the men say when they are saying it to each other and not to me. Not the speeches. The other thing. The thing they say when they think no one is listening."

"I will need a tablet."

"You will need three."

Tullia looked at her.

"Domina," she said, "there is a thing I have never said to you in nine years, and I am going to say it now in case there is no good time after tomorrow."

"Say it."

"You are the most frightening woman I have ever met," Tullia said, "and I have been proud to keep your accounts, and if they destroy you tomorrow I will still be keeping them, and if they put you in the cells I will bring you bread, and if they sell you I will follow you to the ends of the earth and steal every one of your masters' account books until they let you go."

Antonia stood in the lane with the cat around her ankles.

"Thank you," she said.
