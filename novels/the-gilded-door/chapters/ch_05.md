They left before daylight, in a hired carriage with a driver who had been recommended for silence and a mule that had been recommended for nothing. The Ostian road ran south-west from the Porta Ostiensis between market gardens and rubbish heaps, and for the first hour the only things awake were the geese and the men coming into the city with vegetables on their backs.

Tullia slept. Antonia did not. She sat with her feet on a bale of wool and watched the road come out of the dark, and thought about a ledger.

At the seventh hour they came over the low ridge and saw the sea. Ostia lay below them, red roofs and white warehouses and the great brown mouth of the Tiber turning in its own mud, and beyond it, to the north, the lighthouse at Portus with its fire going out in the daylight, and the harbor full of masts so dense that from a distance it looked like a burned forest.

"There are three hundred ships in that harbor," said the driver, who had been silent for four hours and had evidently decided that the recommendation had been satisfied. "They say the grain fleet alone brings a hundred thousand tons a year."

"They say a great many things. Who is they?"

"The Greeks in the tavern."

"Then it is true. The Greeks in the tavern have no reason to lie about grain." Antonia looked at the water. "Turn left at the second milestone and take me to the house of Gaius Calpurnius Daphnus, on the street of the rope-makers."

The house of Daphnus was a warehouse with a room in it. The room had a bed, a table, a bronze lamp in the shape of a ship, and a view of a wall. The warehouse had four thousand modii of Egyptian wheat, a rat problem, and a small shrine in the corner with a fresh garland on it, and the shrine had three gods in it: one Roman, one Greek, and one that Antonia did not recognize and did not ask about.

Daphnus was already up and in a bad temper. "You are early."

"You say that every time, and every time I am exactly on time, and you say it because it makes you feel like a busy man." Antonia put her bag on the table. "The girl."

"The girl is where I said she would be. But you will not like it." He poured wine and did not offer her any, which meant he was serious. "Bassa is dead. She died six years ago, of an ulcer, in her bed, in a house she owned on the via della Foce. Her business went to a freedman called Secundus, who was her steward and is now a dealer in his own right. He has four rooms behind the Macellum and a yard, and he sells by the head, and he keeps better records than the treasury."

"And he will sell me a girl."

"He will sell you a girl. What he will not do is sell you a girl for the price of a girl, because he knows what you are and he knows that a woman who runs your house does not come to Ostia in March to buy a maid."

"Then he thinks I want something else."

"Half of Ostia thinks you want something else." Daphnus's mouth moved. "The other half knows you are looking for a paper and are too clever to say so, and they are more dangerous, because they will sell you the paper and also sell the fact that you wanted it."

"And which half is Secundus in?"

"Secundus is in the half that sells both." Daphnus set down the cup and took his cloak. "I have known him fourteen years. He is not a monster. He is a man with a shop. That is worse, in case you were wondering. A monster can be hated, and hatred is a sort of clarity. A man with a shop will sell you a child and give you a receipt, and he will go home and eat his dinner, and he will sleep."

Before that, the morning's business. Daphnus walked her along the quay to the square where the shippers had their offices, a colonnade with a mosaic floor of ships and dolphins and the names of every port from Alexandria to Gades. The men who ran the sea sat under the arcade on stools, with their clerks behind them and their account books open, and the noise was the noise of any market: money counted, credit promised, weather argued about.

A man called Aulus Caecilius was waiting for them, and he was sixty and had a face like a dried fig, and he owned two ships and had shares in four more, and he had the particular courtesy of men who have been ruined once and recovered.

"The Egyptian ships are late," he said, without preamble. "The grain comes in and the price drops and every shipper in the port says the same thing, and then next year the price rises and they say the other thing. You are not here to buy grain."

"I am here to buy grain."

"Then you are here to buy grain at a price that will look foolish in June."

"Then I shall be foolish in June." Antonia sat down on the bench beside him. "I want three thousand modii of the Egyptian wheat, delivered to the warehouse of Daphnus before the Kalends of May, and I want it at the Ostian price and not the Roman one."

"Impossible."

"Everything is impossible before breakfast."

"The Ostian price is for the man who has a ship. You have no ship."

"I have a warehouse, which is better than a ship, because a warehouse does not sink." She let a moment pass. "I have also the goodwill of a man in the corn office, who can tell me on any given morning how many ships are expected and how many have come in, and a warehouse in the fourteenth region where the grain can sit until the price is right. What I do not have is a man with a ship. So I have brought a bargain instead of a ship. You will deliver the grain to my man at the Ostian price, and when the price rises in June, as it will, I will sell it back to you at a price we agree today, and you will make a profit on grain that never left your warehouse."

Caecilius looked at her for a while. "That is a very old trick."

"It is. It works because men forget that it is old."

"It works if the price rises."

"It will rise."

"Why?"

"Because the emperor is going east, and the east takes legions, and legions eat grain, and the grain goes out of the province to feed them, and the price of grain in Italy rises in a war year the way it has risen in every war year since Sulla." Antonia turned her cup. "You knew that. You wanted to hear me say it, so that you could tell your clerk that the woman from Rome said it and so it must be true."

Caecilius's dry face cracked into something like a grin. "In June," he said, "I will say it was my idea."

"In June you may say it was the emperor's idea. Only deliver the grain."

The bargain was made the way bargains were made in the port: with two witnesses, a written tablet in duplicate, a deposit in silver, and a small offering of wine to the goddess of the harbor, which Caecilius made with the air of a man who had not believed in her since the year of the three bad harvests.


* * *

The Macellum of Ostia was a square of shops around a courtyard with a fish market in it and a shrine of the genius of the place at the corner. Behind it, in a lane too narrow for a cart, was a door painted a deep green, and behind the door was a yard, and in the yard were eleven people.

Antonia had been in such yards before. She had been in one at the age of nineteen, though not this one, and not in this town. She had learned that the trick of it was to look at the people and not at the thing the yard was: at the man from Britain with the scars on his back, at the two sisters who held hands, at the old woman with the cough who would be dead before the summer and would be sold cheap to whoever would take her.

Secundus came out with his tablets before he looked up. He was fifty-five, fat, clean, with a barber's hands. "Gaius Calpurnius said you would come. He did not say you would come yourself."

"I am here."

"You are here." He smiled. "The house on the Clivus. I have heard of it. A fine house. My father bought a girl from your man, years ago, a Numidian, and she went to a house on the Aventine — but that is business, and not to the purpose. What are you looking for?"

"A maid. Sixteen to twenty. She must be clean, and she must be able to be taught, and she must not be stupid."

"A maid is nine hundred sesterces."

"A maid of that description is four hundred."

"A maid of that description is nine hundred, because I am the only man in Ostia who can deliver one this week and you have come to me in March when every girl on the market is nineteen and ugly."

"Show me."

Secundus showed her. There were six women of the right age, and they were brought out and made to stand in a line in the yard, and told to say their names and their trades. Most of them had been taught to say these things before. One of them, a girl of about thirteen, was at the end of the line and had not been in the group with the two sisters; she had been sitting on a stone in the corner with her arms around her knees, and when the man with the whipcords came to fetch her she got up at once, before he touched her, which was a thing Antonia recognized and did not want to recognize.

"This one is small," Antonia said.

"She is Numidian. They do not grow tall."

"She is thirteen."

"Fourteen," Secundus said. "She has the certificate to say fourteen."

Antonia looked at the girl. The girl looked at the ground.

"What is your name?"

"Sabina," the girl said.

"Who named you that?"

"The last house."

"That is not a name. That is a list of three things that were done to somebody." Antonia turned. "How much?"

"Nine hundred."

"For a girl of thirteen who has been in three houses?"

"Nine hundred and fifty."

Daphnus, at the gate, was studying the sky. Antonia said, "Eight hundred, and I will buy the girl and the girl's certificate, and I will not tell the port officials about the yard's drainage. That is a great deal more than eight hundred."

Secundus did not look at the drainage, or at the yard. "Eight fifty."

"Eight twenty-five, and you will bring me the accounts of the woman Bassa, because I am told you keep better records than the treasury, and because a man who sells me a girl for eight twenty-five and then asks a favor of me next year is a man I can deal with. A man who gives me a girl for a fraction of her worth is a man who is planning something else."

Secundus's eyes moved. This was the moment. Antonia had seen it in a hundred negotiations: the moment when the man on the other side of the table realized he was not the cleverest person in the room and decided, instead of becoming smaller, to become larger.

"The accounts of Bassa," he said, "are in a trunk in my tablinum, and they are the only copy of eighteen years of business, and a man who wanted to see them would have to buy something worth his trouble."

"Then sell me something worth my trouble."

Secundus rubbed his jaw. "I will tell you what I would tell my own sister. There are men in Rome who have come down to Ostia asking about the child in the last month. Not one man. Two, and they did not come together, and they did not ask the same questions."

Antonia did not move. "What did they ask?"

"The first asked about a child sold to a house beginning with C. That was a man with a burned hand, in a good cloak, and he paid for his answer with silver and he did not write anything down. The second asked whether Bassa's ledger still existed, and whether it was for sale, and he said he was acting for a household that wished to be certain that its records were correct." Secundus spat on the ground. "I have been in this business thirty years. There is no household in Rome that sends a man to be certain about its records. A household sends a man to be certain that the records are gone."

"And what did you tell him?"

"I told him that Bassa's books were burned when she died, which is a lie, and that I only kept the accounts of the last five years, which is also a lie." Secundus smiled. "I keep them all, domina. I keep everything. A man who keeps everything in this trade lives to be sixty. A man who throws things away lives to be forty and dies of a complaint of the throat."

Antonia said, "The second man. What did he look like?"

"Tall. Greek, by his accent. Very clean. He had a ring with a horse on it and he paid in gold, which is a thing no one in this town does unless they wish it not to be remembered."

She felt the ground move a little under her, the way it does when you have been walking on a bridge and you suddenly see how high it is. Nepos. Or somebody behind Nepos. Either way, someone had been in Ostia before her, and had asked about the ledger before she had, and had been told a lie that would hold for about as long as it took to bribe the right clerk.

"Then I will pay you tonight," Antonia said, "and you will keep everything, and you will tell the next man who comes that the ledger was sold to a woman from Rome who paid in gold and gave no name."

"There is no such woman."

"There will be by the evening," said Antonia.



"What exactly are you looking for?"

"A child," Antonia said. "Bought in the year of the fire, sold out at three or four months old, and sold to a house with a name that begins with C."

Secundus looked at her for a long time. In the yard the man with the whipcords shifted his weight, and the two sisters held hands, and the old woman coughed.

"The year of the fire," Secundus said, "was a very good year for us, because there were a great many children with no one to claim them. I will need to think about the price."

"I have four hundred thousand in credit with Daphnus, and I have a boat tomorrow, and I have a great deal of patience." Antonia took the girl by the hand — the hand was small and cold and did not pull away — and put the coins in Secundus's palm, one by one, counting them out loud. "But I do not have the patience to stand in this yard all day. My father used to say that a man's price is written on his face, and if he will not read it out loud, someone else will take it out of his pocket, so he had better learn to speak. I never knew my father. But the man who owned my mother was a great one for saying it. So. Name a price, Secundus. Name it now."

Secundus looked at the coins in his hand and then at her. "The trunk is in the tablinum," he said. "Come at the sixth hour, when the market closes, and bring the girl, and I will tell you what a page is worth."

* * *

The tablinum of Secundus smelled of ink and of the stuff they use for moths. The trunk was iron-bound, and inside it were seventy tablets and thirty bundles of papyrus tied with string, and the smell when it opened was the smell of a whole trade: dust, sealing wax, the sourness of eight hundred names.

It took an hour. Tullia read, and Antonia read, and the girl Sabina sat on the floor by the door with a piece of bread and watched them, and the lamp was lit, and outside the market closed and the town went quiet in the way Ostia went quiet, which was never entirely.

The tablets were heavy and cold, and the writing on them was in three hands: Bassa's, large and round; her steward's, small and slanting; and a third hand that appeared only in the margins, in ink so faint it had to be held at an angle to the lamp. Tullia read the margins out loud, because Tullia had been a slave in a house where the steward wrote in the margins, and she knew what a margin was for.

"Here. *The Syrian, paid, do not sell again.* Here. *Boy with the bad eye, keep away from the senator, he asks for boys with both eyes.* And here —" Tullia stopped.

"Read it."

"*Girl with the token. Do not sell to the house of Verus. The price is not to be discussed.*"

Antonia took the tablet and turned it to the light, and read the words twice, and then a third time, as though a fourth reading might change them.

"Bassa wrote that?"

"Bassa, or her steward. It is not in the same hand as the other margins. It is in the large hand."

"Then Bassa knew that the sale was wrong, and she wrote a note to herself to be careful, and she sold the child anyway, and she took the mark of the infinity for it, which is what a woman writes when the sum is too large to write down." Antonia put the tablet on the table. "How much is a woman's silence worth, Tullia?"

"Less than a man's, and more than a horse," Tullia said.

"Write that down. I may need it one day."


Bassa had kept her books the way an accountant keeps them and the way a woman keeps them: in three columns, with the date on the left, the price in the middle, and the words on the right. In the year of the fire there were eleven entries for infants, and Antonia read all eleven, and her hand was steady on the tablet.

The ninth entry was in a shorter hand than the others, as though it had been written in a hurry or with the tablet held on the knee.

*Puella trimensis. Ex Iusta, obstetrix. HS ∞. Vendita domo C. Cassii Veri, per Chryseron, ser.* — a girl of three months, from Iusta the midwife, sold for the mark of the infinity sign to the house of Gaius Cassius Verus, through Chryseros, his slave.

The room was very quiet. Antonia heard the girl chewing her bread. She heard a dog outside. She heard the sea, a long way off, and the gulls, and the sound of a shutter going up somewhere.

"Domina," Tullia said.

"Read it."

Tullia read it, twice, and then set the tablet down.

"HS ∞," she said. "The infinity sign."

"It means the price was not money," Antonia said. "Or the price was never paid. Or the price was so large that Bassa did not want to write it. She used the same sign four times in eighteen years. Here, for a boy sold to a man in Capua who was her patron. Here, for a pair of twins sold to a temple. And here, for a girl sold to the Cassii." She turned the tablet. "The midwife's name is Iusta."

"The midwife at Ficulea."

"The same woman, or her cousin, or a woman she trained. There is only one Iusta who attends births in that district." Antonia put the tablet down. "The slave's name is Chryseros. He was the steward of the Cassii. He died in the year 96, of a fever, in his bed, which is the way all useful men die."

"Then the paper is worth what he paid for it," Tullia said. "It is a line in a dealer's book, and the dealer is dead, and the slave is dead, and the buyer is a consular who will say the hand is not his."

"It is worth what we do with it."

"Which is what?"

Antonia looked at the lamp for a moment. Then she looked at the girl on the floor, who had finished her bread and was watching her with the flat, careful attention of a child who has learned that adults are weather.

"First," Antonia said, "we pay him for the page."

Secundus named six thousand. Antonia paid four, and the man held out his hand, and she put the tablet inside her mantle and said, "You will make a copy tonight, in the same hand, and you will keep it in the trunk, and if anyone comes to you with money for the original, you will show them the copy and say that the original was destroyed by fire."

"And if they pay more than you have?"

"Then they will have bought a copy that says what I have already read, and you will have made an enemy of a woman who knows exactly how many girls were in your yard in March." Antonia smiled. "Do not go into the pouring business, Secundus. It is bad for the health."

Outside it had begun to rain. The lane ran with water the color of brick-dust, and Antonia walked in it with the tablet under her mantle and Sabina's hand in hers, and Tullia behind with the lamp, and none of them spoke until they came to the rope-maker's street.

"You are shaking," Tullia said.

"I am cold."

"You have not been cold since I met you."

"Then I am shaking," Antonia said.

Later, in the room with the ship-shaped lamp, she sat on the bed and looked at the tablet in her hands: a page of a dead woman's accounts, eleven words and a sign, and the whole of her life turned on it.

*Puella trimensis. Ex Iusta, obstetrix. Vendita domo C. Cassii Veri.*

They had not even given her a name in the book. She was a girl of three months, and she had been sold to a house, and the house had been the house of the father, and the man who had done it had a good name and a bad steward and a consular's chair, and in September his other daughter would be married in front of the whole city.

Antonia put the tablet down, and lay back on the bed, and cried in the way she had not cried since the year 94, which was to say almost silently, with her hand over her mouth, while the rain went on the roof and the lamp burned.

* * *

In the morning the girl Sabina went to Rome in the wagon with Tullia, with a cloak and a box of combs and a letter to Rufina; and Antonia went down to the harbor mouth and stood a long time looking at the sea, which was grey and enormous and did not care about anything, and then she went to find the ship's master who had brought the letter, and paid him to carry a message back up the river to Rome.

The message was for Gallus, and it was four lines, and it said that the petition was ready, and that she would bring it on the fifteenth day before the Kalends of April, and that she was grateful, and that she wished to tell him something that could not be written down.

Then she went to the warehouse and did the accounts, because the accounts did not stop for the past, and the wheat did not stop, and the rats did not stop, and at the sixth hour she was sitting on a bale with a tablet on her knee when a clerk came from the harbor office to say that a ship had come in with a load from Caesarea, and that among the passengers was a man who said he was a friend of Oppius Nepos.

Antonia looked up from the tablet.

"Did he ask for me?"

"He asked whether the house of Antonia Cassia had a venture in the harbor, and he asked it of the harbor office, and the harbor office told him that a woman from Rome had been in the town since Thursday."

"Then he knows I am here." She closed the tablet. "Where is he staying?"

"At the inn of the three anchors, by the theater."

"Send a boy with a jar of wine and a message that the house of Antonia Cassia does not receive friends of Oppius Nepos in warehouses, but that she will be at the baths at the ninth hour, and that if he wishes to speak to her he may speak to her there, in public, where he will have to be polite."

The clerk wrote it down and went away.

The man from Caesarea was called Menander and he was waiting at the baths when she came, and he was exactly as Secundus had described him: tall, Greek, very clean, with a horse on his ring. He had taken a private room and he had ordered wine and figs, and he received her with the courtesy of a man who has been sent to be courteous and has been given a budget for it.

"You are not bathing," he said.

"I am not. I am conducting business in a place where a man who raises his voice is thrown out by the bathman."

"Ah. That is very wise. In Caesarea we do the same, only we do it in the temple of the emperors, which is quieter and has better statues." He pushed the figs toward her. "Oppius Nepos sends his compliments."

"Nepos sends his compliments to everyone. It is his only expense."

Menander laughed. "He said you were amusing. He also said you were the most dangerous woman in Rome, but I think he says that to all the women who will not sleep with him."

Antonia looked at him. "What does he want?"

"You know what he wants."

"I want to hear you say it."

"He wants the ledger of the house of Antonia Cassia." Menander ate a fig, and wiped his fingers, and his eyes were bright and entirely without warmth. "Not the book of the house. The book of the guests. The names, the dates, the sums, the favors. He is prepared to pay a great deal for it, and he is prepared to pay a great deal more for it if it comes with a statement about the household of Manius Cassius Verus."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then he will buy it from someone else, and he will be less generous." Menander smiled. "You are thinking that there is no one else. There is always someone else. Every house has a steward, and every steward has a price, and every price is lower than the person paying it thinks."

Antonia stood. "Tell Oppius Nepos that the book is in a place he will not find it, and that the woman who keeps it has kept it for eleven years, and that in eleven years she has been offered money for it by four men, and has refused all four, and that the fourth offer was the largest and came from a man who is now dead."

She said the last words very pleasantly, and watched them land.

Menander's smile did not change, but his hands did: they stopped moving.

"That was a long time ago," he said.

"It was. And yet here we are."

She went out into the street. It was raining again, and the lamps were being lit along the via della Foce, and the sea was invisible beyond the roofs, and she stood under the awning of a wine shop and found she was breathing as though she had run up a hill.

Antonia would take the boat alone in the morning. She sat in the warehouse with the rats and the wheat and the smell of the sea, and thought about the man from Caesarea, and what he could have been sent to buy, and what she would be willing to sell.

At the door, the watchman's boy was lighting the lamp in the little shrine, and the three gods in it looked out at the warehouse with their faces worn smooth by the thumbs of everybody who had ever worked there. Antonia watched him for a moment, then put a coin in the box and did not say which god it was for, because it was better to let them settle it among themselves.

In the morning she would go up the river with the tide, and by evening she would be in Rome, and the paper would be in the cedar box with the token, and the two halves of her life would be in the same room for the first time.

She slept that night on the bed with the ship-shaped lamp, and dreamed of a door, and woke before dawn with her heart going and the taste of salt in her mouth.
