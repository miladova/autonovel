She had prepared for it the way she prepared for a banquet: by deciding, in advance, what she would not do.

She would not weep. She would not raise her voice before the slaves. She would not sit on the bench by the fountain, or look at the room behind the kitchen, or say the word father. She would not ask him whether he had ever thought of her. She would not, under any circumstances, take his hand.

She put on the dark blue stola and the mantle the color of wine, and the gold earrings she had bought in the year 103 with money she had earned in a single season, and no other ornament at all. She put the half-token in the fold of her sash, because she wanted it on her body. She looked at the ring with the cassia leaf in the cedar box and did not take it, and then at the last moment she took it and put it in the purse at her belt, and told herself it was for evidence.

And she took Rufina aside in the passage and said, "If I am not out of that house by dark, go to the station and find the tribune, and tell him that I am in the house of the Cassii and that he should bring men and a warrant and be very loud at the door."

"Domina," Rufina said, "you are going to a wedding contract."

"I am going to a house where I was a slave," Antonia said. "If you have never been in a house where you were a slave, you will not understand why I am telling you this. If you have, you will not ask me any more questions."

Rufina looked at her for a moment. Then she said, "I was in a house in Pompeii when the mountain went. I was sold four times after. The fourth time I came here."

"And?"

"And if you are not out by dark, I will burn the house down myself," Rufina said, "and the tribune may help."

* * *

The house of the Cassii had not changed in eighteen years. That was the first thing, and it was worse than any of the others.

The lane still smelled of the same things: stone dust, and the privy of the house opposite, and the fig tree at the corner that had grown over the wall and dropped its fruit on the paving where the ants found it. The door was the same door, with the same iron boss in the middle of it, and the same worn place at the bottom where the delivery boys kicked it. And the threshold stone was still black, a single block of basalt worn into a hollow by the feet of six generations, and when Antonia stepped over it she felt the hollow with her foot the way she had felt it ten thousand times when she was nineteen, and she stopped.

"Domina?" Marcella's steward was a thin man of forty with the face of a man who has been in service since he was eight. "The lady is in the garden. Will you come?"

"Will you give me a moment?"

The steward's face did nothing. "Of course."

She stood on the black stone in the fauces of the house she had been sold into, and the past came up around her like water in a cellar.

The walls of the fauces had been painted, eighteen years ago, with a garden scene: a trellis, a vine, a bird. The paint was still there, faded, and someone had painted over the bird at some point and then scraped the paint off again, and the bird was still there under the scraping, one wing showing, a green smear of vine behind it.

To the left was the passage to the kitchen. There was a door in that passage, and behind the door was a room with a pallet and a jug and a nail in the wall, and in that room a girl had slept for nine years, and in that room a man had come to her in the fourth year, and in that room she had learned to be silent.

To the right was the atrium. She could hear it: the small sound of the fountain in the impluvium, the shuffle of feet, a woman's voice giving an order about a chest.

Straight ahead, at the end, the garden, and beyond it the tablinum, and in the tablinum a man who had not spoken to her in eighteen years except across a garden full of roses.

Antonia stood on the threshold and looked at the bird.

Then she lifted her veil and walked in.

* * *

The atrium had the masks.

They were in the same cupboard, on the same wall, with the same bronze labels, and there were twenty-three of them now, and Antonia knew every name on every label because she had been made to learn them when she was sixteen, by a steward who had beaten her when she got the order of the consulships wrong.

She stood in front of the cupboard and read them, one by one, and the steward waited, and Marcella came out of the garden with her hands folded.

"You are early," Marcella said.

"I am punctual. It is a different vice."

"You said on the tablet that you would come at the fourth hour. It is the third."

"It is the third hour and a half."

"That is still early." Marcella came and stood beside her. She was in the blue wool she always wore, with her hair braided close, and here, in her own atrium, she looked smaller than she did in public, and harder. "You are looking at the masks."

"I was made to learn them. Caius Cassius Verus, consul in the year of the consulship of... I could recite the whole cupboard. It is the only thing I learned in this house that I have ever used."

"What did you use it for?"

"To know what kind of men I was dealing with," Antonia said. "There is a pattern in the cupboard. Every third generation produces a man who is clever and a man who is cruel, and the clever one always dies first."

Marcella's mouth moved. "That is a terrible thing to say in the presence of the family."

"They are not present. They are wax."

"They are more present than you think." Marcella turned. "Come into the garden. The slaves are cleaning the receiving room and there is dust everywhere, and I do not want to talk in the atrium where the whole household can hear through the passage."

They went out through the garden. It was smaller than Antonia remembered, and the vine was older, and the fountain was the same fountain with the same lion's head spout, and the bench where she had sat with a man who was going to be consul was still there, and she did not look at it.

"Your husband is not here."

"He is at the senate house. He does not know you are coming." Marcella sat on the bench. "You asked for me, and I have not told him, and I am going to have to decide what to tell him and when, and both of those decisions will be mine."

"Why?"

"Because I have been married to him for nine years," Marcella said, "and I have spent nine years being the woman who manages the money and the household and the wife's family and the daughter's marriage and the slaves, and in nine years I have not once been asked what I think about anything. So I am going to have this conversation, in my own garden, in my own house, and I am going to enjoy it, and then I am going to tell my husband whatever I decide to tell him."

Antonia sat down at the other end of the bench. "What do you want to know?"

"Why the wedding," Marcella said. "Why you, of all the people in Rome, are going to walk into this house in September and stand in the room with the shrine and eat my husband's dinner. You could have sent a man. You could have sent a letter. You could have gone to the praetor with whatever it is you have and never come near this house at all. But you asked for an invitation, and my stepdaughter wanted you there, and my husband forbade it, and I overruled him, which was the first time in nine years." She looked at Antonia. "So. Why?"

"Because I want to see the house again," Antonia said.

Marcella looked at her for a long time.

"Ah," she said.

"Yes."

"Then it is true. I had thought it was a story." Marcella's voice did not change. "I knew it was a story the day I found the page. In the year 104, when my husband's steward died, I went through the tablinum, and I found a bundle of tablets marked with the year of the fire, and I read them, and I found a page about a child, and a note in my husband's hand that said *for the child, from Bassa, and no more to be said.*"

Antonia did not move.

"I asked him about it," Marcella said. "Once. In this garden. And he said that it was a family matter from before our marriage, and that it had been settled, and that I was not to speak of it again. And I did not speak of it again for eight years." She turned her cup — there was a cup in her hand; Antonia had not seen her take it up. "I have thought about it every week of those eight years."

"What did you think?"

"I thought: a child was bought. And I thought: a child was bought because the mother could not keep her. And I thought: the mother is somewhere in this city, and she has not forgotten, and one day she will come to this door." Marcella looked at Antonia. "I did not think she would be the most successful woman in the Subura."

"She is not the most successful woman in the Subura."

"She is the most successful woman in the Subura. I have seen the household accounts of this city. Half the men on the Viminal owe money to a woman on the Clivus and do not know that they owe it to the same woman."

Antonia almost smiled. "Then you know my business."

"I know your business from the outside. I know what a house costs and what it earns and what a woman in it has to do to survive." Marcella put the cup down. "What I do not know is what you intend."

"I intend to free the girl," Antonia said, "and I intend to do it lawfully, in front of a court, with witnesses, and I intend her father to be the man who must answer for what was done. And I intend to do all of it before your stepdaughter's wedding, because after the wedding your husband will go to Asia with the dowry in his hands and the girl will be sold to a man in Formiae, and there will be nothing left of her to free."

Marcella's face went still.

"You are telling me this," she said slowly. "In my garden. With the door open."

"Yes."

"Because you know that I will not tell my husband."

"Because I know that you will tell your husband exactly what you choose to tell him," Antonia said, "and because I have made a study of you for fourteen years, Acilia Marcella, and I know that a woman who has read a page about a stolen child and has said nothing for eight years is not going to be on the side of the man who bought her."

* * *

The girl came into the garden to pour the wine.

Antonia saw her before she saw the token. She saw the way the girl came through the door with a tray and the way she set her feet, the way the slaves in a great house moved, as though the floor were glass and might crack and bring the whole house down; she saw the way the girl kept her eyes at the level of the bench, and the way her hands did not shake.

"Myrtale," Marcella said. "Put the tray here. And bring the white cup for the guest, not the red one; the red one has a chip."

"Yes, domina."

The girl set the tray down. She was close to Antonia, close enough to touch, and the two of them were within an arm's length of each other, and Antonia looked at her hands on the tray.

They were good hands. Long fingers, a narrow wrist, a small crescent mark on the inside of the wrist, and nothing else. No bruise, no brand, no collar. She was wearing a plain grey tunic and a belt of plaited leather, and her hair was bound up and held with a wooden pin, and around her neck, on a leather cord, was a lead token the size of a thumbnail.

The girl turned to fill the cup, and the token swung out from her throat, and the light caught it, and Antonia saw it clearly: a wreath on it, struck by a die that had made ten thousand others, and a break across the middle where the other half had been snapped off.

The world went white at the edges. Antonia heard, very distinctly, the sound of her own heart, and under it the little dry click of the token against the girl's collarbone, and under that, from a long way off, the sound of a room above a fuller's shop, and a woman saying, *the door half is yours, and when you have money, you will look for the wreath half, and you will find her.*

"Thank you," Antonia said.

"Domina." The girl glanced up, once, and her eyes were dark and careful, and she went back through the garden and into the house.

Antonia sat with the cup in her hand and did not drink.

"She does not know," Marcella said.

"No."

"You have not told her."

"No."

"Eighteen years."

"Eighteen years."

Marcella looked at the door the girl had gone through. "I have wanted to tell her," she said. "Four times in eight years I have come close to it. Twice I was prevented by my husband, and twice by myself. The first time I was prevented by the thought that if I told her, she would do something reckless and be sold, and it would be my fault. The second time I was prevented by the thought that she is happy." She turned. "She is not happy. But she has a room, and work, and my stepdaughter loves her, and a girl who is not happy but is safe is a thing a woman can live with. A girl who knows she has been robbed is a girl who will set the house on fire and be sold in the aftermath."

"You should have told her."

"Yes."

"Then why are you telling me this?"

"Because I am ashamed," Marcella said, and the word came out flat, and it was the first true thing she had said all afternoon. "I have been ashamed for eight years and there is nobody in this house I can be ashamed in front of. My husband does not permit it. My stepdaughter is a child. My stepson is drunk. The slaves cannot be told. And now the mother of the girl is sitting on my bench with a cup of my wine, and I find that I would rather confess to her than go on being the woman who said nothing."

Antonia put the cup down.

"What do you want me to do?" she said.

"I want you to take her out of this house," Marcella said, "and I want you not to tell anyone that I helped. Not the girl, not my stepdaughter, not my husband. If it fails, I will say I knew nothing, and I will be believed, because I am the wife and the wife is always the last to know. And if it succeeds, I will be the woman who did nothing, and I will live with that, because it is better than the alternative."

"What is the alternative?"

"Being the woman who did something," Marcella said, "and having my husband know it."

* * *

Verus came home at the sixth hour, and he came into the garden with his toga still on and his face grey with the heat of the senate house, and he stopped when he saw her.

"You," he said.

"Me."

Marcella rose. "I invited her."

"You did not."

"I did. She asked, and I invited her, and I am going to leave the two of you alone in this garden for a quarter of an hour, because I am tired, and because I have been the wife of this house for nine years and I am entitled to one afternoon of not being present at the thing I have arranged." Marcella went past her husband without touching him. "The slaves have been told to stay out of the garden. The steward has been told to keep the household in the kitchen until the seventh hour. And Manius — do not raise your voice. The whole lane can hear a raised voice in this garden, and half the lane works for people who would enjoy it."

She went in. The door closed.

Verus stood in the middle of his own garden with his hands at his sides.

"How did you get in here?" he said.

"Your wife invited me. You heard her. You were standing there."

"You have no right —"

"I have every right," Antonia said. "I was born in this house's service. Literally. In the room behind the kitchen, on a pallet, with a midwife from Ficulea and a woman from the kitchen holding my mother's hand. I was carried through that passage when I was three days old, and I was given to a nurse in the country, and I was brought back when I was six, and I slept in that room until I was twenty. I have more right to be in this garden than you have, because you have only owned it. I lived in it."

Verus took a step back. "How do you know about the midwife?"

"Because she is alive," Antonia said, "and she remembers a birth in the Subura in the year before the child was sold, and she remembers a token tied to a baby's wrist, and she remembers a man in a good cloak who came to her afterward and paid her to forget it, and she has not forgotten, because she is a woman who has delivered four hundred children and remembers every one of them."

"Antonia."

"Say her name."

"Antonia."

"Not mine." Antonia took a step toward him. "Say hers."

He did not move. The fountain ran. The vine moved in the wind.

"I do not know what you mean."

"You know exactly what I mean. I have seen the token, Manius. I have seen it on her throat in this garden, in the sunlight, on a leather cord, and it is the wreath half, and I have the door half in a cedar box in my house, and I have had it for eighteen years. And you have known since the day you arranged her put in that room behind the kitchen that I would come for her, and you have known that when I came I would not be polite."

Verus sat down on the bench. He sat down the way a man sits down when his legs have decided for him, and he put his hands on his knees, and he looked at the fountain.

"Her name is Myrtale," he said.

"Her name is not Myrtale. That is a name a slave is given. Her name is what her mother called her, and her mother called her nothing at all, because she was taken from her at three months and the mother was not allowed to name her." Antonia's voice was shaking, and she let it. "You had a daughter by a slave in your father's house, and you took the daughter and put her in the same house as a maid, and you watched her grow up at a distance of ten feet, and you never told her, and you never freed her, and you never let her go. Do not tell me her name."

Verus's face had gone the color of the wax in the mask cupboard.

"You have made a mistake," he said. "The child was bought from a dealer. The records say so. The dealer is dead. The slave who handled it is dead. There is no proof that the child was yours, and there is no proof that the mother was —"

"She was yours," Antonia said. "She was your father's slave and she was yours, and there is a book in my house that says so, page by page, in your hand and in your steward's hand and in mine. There is a page that says a child of three months was bought for ninety thousand sesterces, and beside it, in your own hand, *and no more to be said.* And there is a woman at Ficulea who attended the birth, and a piece of lead in a cedar box, and a slave called Chryseros who is dead and whose hand is on the page, and a woman standing in your garden who has spent eighteen years waiting for the day she could say this to you in your own house."

"Antonia."

"Do not say my name."

"What do you want?"

Antonia looked at him.

"I want my daughter," she said.

* * *

He was quiet for a long time. The shadows of the vine moved on the paving. Somewhere in the house a door closed and a woman laughed, and it was not Marcella.

"You cannot have her," Verus said.

"Why not?"

"Because she is mine."

"She is not yours. She is mine. She was born of my body in a room in this house, and you took her from me at three months, and the only thing you have ever given her is a lead token and a name."

"I gave her a life," Verus said. "I gave her a room and food and work and a house that is not a brothel. I gave her the run of the kitchen garden when she was eight and she used to sit among the beans and sing to herself, and I used to stand at the window of the tablinum and listen, and I never once let anyone see me do it. I have kept her from every man in Rome who would have bought her, and there have been four. I have paid her nurse's pension for twelve years. I have —" he stopped.

"You have what?"

"I have loved her," Verus said, "in the only way I was permitted."

Antonia looked at him for a long moment.

"You have loved her," she said, "in the way a man loves a thing he has put in a box. You have loved her from the window of a tablinum, at a distance of ten feet, without once telling her who she was. And when the box became inconvenient, you found a farmer in Formiae who will pay twelve thousand, and you arranged to have her sent away after the wedding, so that the bride would not have to look at her." She leaned down. "Do not say the word love to me in this garden. I have heard it before. You said it to me in this passage when I was nineteen, and I believed it, and six months later I was on a board with a rope in your hand."

Verus came up off the bench so fast that the cup went over, and for a moment his face was the face of the man in the passage, and Antonia did not step back.

"Hit me," she said. "There is no one in the garden. There is no one in the passage. There is no one in the tablinum to write it down. Hit me, Manius, and I will walk out of this house with a mark on my face, and by tomorrow every man in the Subura will know what kind of man the Cassii keep, and your wedding will be a wedding in a small room with the doors closed."

He stood there, breathing, with his hand half raised, and then he put it down.

"I did not do that," he said. "That was my father's doing. I was not in the room."

"You were in the room."

"I was not —"

"You were in the room," Antonia said. "You held the lamp."

The world stopped. The fountain ran. The vine moved.

"I held the lamp," Verus said.

"Yes."

"I have told myself for eighteen years that I was not there."

"You were there. You held the lamp, and your father asked the questions, and you did not stop him, and when it was over you untied me yourself and carried me to the room behind the kitchen and sat on the floor with your back against the door until morning." Antonia's voice was very quiet. "I remember your hands. I have spent eighteen years hating your hands."

Verus sat down again. He sat down slowly, and put his face in his hands, and his shoulders shook, and the sound he made was not a man's sound. Antonia stood over him with her hands at her sides and did not touch him.

"I have the ring," she said. "The one with the leaf. You gave it to me in the passage when I was nineteen, and you took it back the day the child was taken, and I took it back from your desk in the year 94 when I came for my daughter and the steward would not let me in. I have worn it once. I pressed it into wax on a letter to you and I sent it with the watch, and you did not answer."

Verus did not look up.

"What do you want?" he said again.

"Do you know what I did when they took her from me?" Antonia said. "I walked from this house to the room above the fuller's shop, and I sat down on the floor, and I did not get up for four days. On the fifth day the woman who kept the shop came up and put a bowl of soup on the floor and said that I owed her for the week, and that if I did not work I would be sold, and that I was free and freedom was a thing that could be taken away by a debt as easily as by a rope. And I got up. And I ate the soup. And I went down and washed clothes for eleven hours a day for a year and a half, and every night I walked to this lane and stood at the corner and looked at that door."

Verus's hands were over his face.

"Once I saw her," Antonia said. "She was six years old, and she was in the passage with the steward, and she was crying about something, and the steward took her by the arm and pulled her along. I stood at the corner and I did not move, because if I had moved the steward would have seen me, and he would have told you, and you would have had me taken up for loitering and put in the cells for a month. So I stood at the corner and watched a man pull my daughter along a passage by the arm, and I did nothing. And then I went back to the fuller's and washed clothes."

"Antonia —"

"You took a year and a half of my life with the debt, and eighteen years with the child, and I have had to build everything I have out of what was left. And you are going to sit on that bench and tell me that you loved her."

"I did love her."

"Then free her."

"I want you to free her," Antonia said. "In the praetor's court, in front of witnesses, by the rod, with a written record and a citizen's name. And I want an admission, in writing, that she was born of a slave of this household who was manumitted before the birth, and that she is therefore freeborn and has been held in slavery these eighteen years. And I want your signature and your seal on it, and I want it before the wedding."

"And if I do it?"

"Then you will have done the only decent thing you have ever done," Antonia said, "and I will not bring the rest of it to a court, and your daughter will be married in September, and your son will not be asked where he was on the night of the Floralia, and your house will go to Asia with its name intact."

Verus lifted his head. His eyes were red, and his face was wet, and he was still a man in a garden with an old name and a lot of debts, and Antonia saw him calculate, and she saw the moment he understood what she had just offered him.

"The night of the Floralia," he said.

"Yes."

"My son."

"Your son."

"What do you know?"

"I know that Decimus Iunius Gallus was in my garden at the sixth hour, and that he was dead before he went into the fountain, and that a man in the lane was wearing a gold ring with a leaf on it." Antonia held his eyes. "Your ring is not on your hand, Manius. I have been looking at your hand for a quarter of an hour. Where is it?"

Verus looked down at his own hand, and then at her, and his face did something that Antonia had never seen on it before.

"On my son's finger," he said. "It has been on my son's finger since the Kalends of May. He asked for it. He said a man should have a seal of his own."

The fountain ran. Somewhere in the house, a woman laughed again.

"Then we have a great deal to talk about," Antonia said, "and very little time."
