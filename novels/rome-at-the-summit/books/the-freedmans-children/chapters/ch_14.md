# How a Letter Moves

In the year between the wars he learned how a letter moved, and it was not the way he had thought.

He had thought, in Rufus's room with the window on the Palatine, that he had learned it: hand to secretary to slave to doorkeeper to steward to master. That was how a letter moved in a house. In Sura's house it did not move at all; it arrived, and was read, and became something else, and the something else was what moved. A letter from a governor in Asia about a bridge became, by noon, a sentence in Sura's mouth at the emperor's table about the cost of stone in the East, and by the next morning a note in the emperor's hand to the treasury, and by the week's end a different governor's letter from a different province asking why his bridge had been refused; and the first letter was in a box on the Aventine with a mark in the margin in Sura's hand and nobody who was not in the room would ever know that the bridge in Bithynia had been built by a man in Asia complaining about a quarry.

"That's the whole of it," Sura said, on one of the mornings. "A letter's a thing a man sends to be read by one person. The art is to read it as the other eleven would. The governor writes to me about his quarry. I read him as the treasury would, which is as a man who wants money; as the Senate would, which is as a man who wants a province renewed; as the emperor would, which is as a man who is complaining and should be found something to do; and as his enemies would, which is as a man who has put in writing that his province cannot cut its own stone. Four readings. One letter. The governor thought he was telling me about a quarry." Sura's face did not move. "You read faces across hillsides. Learn to read a sheet across a city. It's the same thing, slower, and nobody dies, mostly."

So he read sheets across the city. He sat on the stool by the lamp with the pile and read what Sura set aside, and told him one thing a morning, and in the afternoons he did the other work, the work that was not spoken of, which was to know where every sheet was. Sura's house held, by Celer's count in the second month, eleven thousand letters in boxes in four rooms, going back to the Rhine, and the man with the ring, whose name was Philon and who had been Sura's since Tarraco, knew where every one of them was and was fifty-eight and had a cough; and Sura, who arranged the world so that it came to him, had looked at the boy who counted and the man who coughed and had said nothing, and had let Celer find his way into the four rooms, box by box, as his father had let the girl find her way into a strong-room, because he would rather the boy read his papers than anyone else's, and because a thing that was known by two men was a thing that would survive one of them.

Philon did not mind. That was the surprise. He was a small neat Greek with a ring that had been Sura's father's and he watched the freedman's son go through the boxes with the look of a man watching someone else carry a thing he had carried a long time, and in April, in the third room, with the Rhine boxes, he said, without being asked: "You'll want to know about the adoption."

Celer looked up.

"Nerva's. Of the emperor. You'll have heard it was done in Rome, on the Capitol, by an old man who'd been frightened by the Guard. It was. The sheets are here." He touched a box. "And you'll have heard that the emperor knew nothing of it till the letter came to Cologne, and wept, and said he was unworthy. The sheets for that are here too." Another box. "And in this one —" a third, smaller, with the wax on its cord unbroken in six years — "is what Sura wrote to the old man's friends in Rome in the September before, from Cologne, about what the Guard would do in October and what the governor of Upper Germany would do about it if the old man had the sense to make it worth his while. Eleven letters. He let me seal the box. He's never opened it." The small neat face did nothing. "I'm telling you where it is because I'm fifty-eight and I cough, and because he's decided you're the one who'll know, and he'll never say so, and I'd not have you find it by accident and think it was a secret. It's not a secret. It's the thing the house is built on. Every house has one. Your father's has a stone with a small letter on it. This one has a box with the wax unbroken." He turned back to his own work. "Don't open it. Know it's there. That's what being in the tent is."

Celer did not open it. He knew it was there. He put it in the column with the depot and the Greek and the dated sheet about the ice, and he thought, that night, in the room with the window on the wall, that his father had been right about the tent and wrong about what was in it: it was not the war that was decided there. It was the question of what had happened, afterwards, when the boxes were opened, and who had sealed which.

* * *

The Spaniard with the accent came to Sura's house in May, in the morning, before the pile was done, and Celer read him, and did not like what the reading was for.

He was twenty-eight that year. He had been quaestor and had read the emperor's speeches to the Senate in a Baetican Latin that the Senate had laughed at, openly, in the house, and had gone away and practised, it was said, for a year, with a Greek, until the laugh stopped, and had come back and read them again; and he had been on the Danube in the first war with the Fifth Macedonica and had been given a decoration, and had been married, before the war, to the emperor's great-niece, the daughter of the woman who did her own hair, and was, by that, the only man in Rome who was in the family and under forty, and knew it, and had the look. He was tall, and thick through the shoulder in the Spanish way, with a beard — the new beard, the one that had come in with the emperor and that he wore, Celer thought, a shade fuller than the emperor's, as if to show that his was his own — and a face that had been handsome and had decided to be interesting instead. He came into the room where Sura read without being announced, which nobody did, and sat on the end of the couch without being asked, which nobody did either, and said in Greek, "Lucius. You've got a boy on a stool. Is that the one who reads?"

Sura did not look up from the sheet. "That's the one who counts. The reading's incidental. Hadrian, this is Hermes's son, from Puteoli. He found the depot and wrote the roads and he's the reason you got your decoration, because the granary your cohort took in July was on his Greek's list. Boy, this is the emperor's great-nephew by marriage, who is going to be tribune of the plebs next year if he can stop sitting on my couch, and who reads Greek better than either of us and wants it known."

The Spaniard laughed. It was a real laugh, and a loud one, and it filled the room the way his shoulders did, and he turned on the couch and looked at the freedman's son on the stool with a pair of eyes that were grey and very quick and did not match the laugh at all. "The roads," he said. "I read the roads. In the tent at Tapae. Somebody'd written *the garrison was promised land under Domitian and has not received it* in the margin of a granary in a hand like a tally-board, and I thought: that's not a soldier, soldiers don't notice what people were promised. Was that you?"

"Yes, sir."

"How old are you?"

"Seventeen, sir."

"God. Lucius, he's seventeen. I was reading Ennius at seventeen and being laughed at." The grey eyes did not leave Celer's face. "What was the Greek's name?"

Celer felt the room go very still around him, in the way the harbour went still before the wind changed, and he did not know why, and then he did: because the Spaniard had asked the one question that nobody on the staff had asked, in two years, about a man who was *the Greek* on every sheet and *died in the pen* in a margin, and had asked it the way Sura asked *why did you write that*, as a man hitting a wall to see if it was hollow.

"Apollonios, sir. Of Tomis."

"Of Tomis. On the Black Sea. Where Ovid was sent to die and wrote about the cold." Hadrian looked at him a moment longer with the grey eyes and the laugh gone out of his face entirely. "You remembered his name. Two years. Most of them wouldn't. Most of them would've said *the Greek* and been proud of the roads." He turned back to Sura. "Lucius, he reads. You can tell me he counts all you like. A boy who remembers the name of a dead Greek in a pen is a boy who reads, and I'd like him in my house when I'm tribune, and you'll say no, and I'll ask again when I'm praetor."

"I'll say no then too."

"Yes. You will." The Spaniard stood, which took the couch some time. At the door he stopped and said, to Celer, in a different voice, lower, in the Greek that he had practised for a year until the Senate stopped laughing: "The king's rebuilding. You'll know that; it's on the sheets. He's got the engines back from the men he was supposed to surrender and he's rebuilding the forts we razed and he's sent to the Iazyges and the Parthians and everyone else who'll take a letter, and the emperor knows, and is being clement about it, because he can afford to be, because somebody put a dated sheet in a box about the Parthian court two years ago. I read that one too." The grey eyes. "Next year, or the one after, we go back, and this time it's not terms, it's the end of it, and the staff will need a man who can write the inventory of a kingdom. Think about whose tent you want to be in when that's written. Lucius will be in it. So will I. The emperor will be in it. And afterwards, the man who wrote the inventory will be the only one who knows what a kingdom weighed." He went out.

Sura set the sheet aside. He said, without looking up, "Well? One thing."

"He's going to be emperor, sir."

A silence. The lamp hissed.

"That's not one thing," said Sura. "That's a thing nobody in this city says aloud, and you've said it on a stool, at seventeen, to the one man who's spent six years making it possible without once writing it down." He turned the next sheet. "You're right. It's the only sum there is. The emperor has no son and a wife who likes the Spaniard and a niece who's given him her daughter and no other man under forty in the family, and the Senate hates him, and the army will take whoever's put in front of it by a man they've crossed a bridge behind. It'll be him. In ten years, in fifteen. Unless the emperor makes someone else, and he won't, because he's a soldier and he doesn't think about dying; he thinks about the Danube." The knife-face lifted, finally, and looked at the boy. "Now you know the thing the house is built on and the thing it's building. Philon told you about the box. I know he did; I told him to. So you've both ends of it. You're seventeen and you've got the adoption and the succession in your head and no stone to put either on, and that's the most dangerous thing a man can be in this city, and I've made you it on purpose, because I'd rather you were dangerous in my house than useful in his." A pause. "He'll ask for you again. He'll ask when he's praetor and when he's consul and when he's the thing he's going to be. Every time, you'll say what I say, until I'm dead, and then you'll do your own sum. That's the arrangement. It's not a kindness. It's the only way to keep a boy who reads alive long enough to be a man who knows."

* * *

His father came to Rome in July, and Celer struck him, and it was over a sentence.

Hermes came up on the fast post and lodged at Bassus's house on the Caelian, as a freedman of the house lodged, in the rooms by the slaves' court that were kept for the coast people, and sent to the Aventine the first evening to say that he would be at the Laecanius door at the second hour on the following day and that his son would come, if Sura could spare him, because there were things to say that were better said in the patron's house than in a Spaniard's. Sura could spare him. Sura could spare him, Celer had learned, for anything that would tell Sura something, and a shipowner from Puteoli who had put four hundred thousand sesterces into a senator's debts and his son into a tent was a thing worth sparing a morning for.

So he went up the Caelian at the second hour, past the door with the bronze hand — he did not look at it; he had decided — to the door two streets up with no hand on it, and in through the atrium with the fish and the masks, and found his father in the small room by the slaves' court where the coast people were kept, sitting on a stool with a tablet on his knee, in a tunic that was better than the ones he had worn on the coast and did not fit as well, as though he had bought it for Rome and Rome had not yet agreed to it.

They did not embrace. They had never. His father looked at him — up and down, the hull-inspecting look, the toga Sura's steward had found him and the hair and the way he stood — and said, "You've grown. Sit. You're eating; Vibia will want to know you're eating."

"I'm eating."

"Good." Hermes looked at the tablet. "Bassus is dying. Not this year. The Greek physician says two, perhaps three; it's the water, it's in his legs. He doesn't know I know; Thallus told me, because Thallus wants me to know what he knows, so that I'll know what he's worth. Rufus is praetor-designate for next year. That cost the Spaniard son-in-law's uncle a word at the palace and me another hundred and fifty, which Rufus thinks was a loan and is not. So. The thing in the room off the peristyle, four years ago. The roll before twenty-five, on Rufus's praetorship, with his sponsorship, witnessed. He's praetor in January. You're eighteen in the autumn. I want it done by the time he's out of office. I want you on the roll, with the ring and the stripe, at nineteen, so that when the old man dies and the house goes to Rufus and Rufus's debts, you're already a thing the debts can't touch." He turned the tablet. "There's a problem. The census property qualification. Four hundred thousand, in land or its equivalent, in your name, not mine. I've the ships, but ships aren't land, and a freedman's ships in a freedman's name are a freedman's. I've been buying. In the Falernian hills, piece by piece, in your name, through a man at Capua who doesn't ask. I've three hundred and twenty. I'll have the rest by the spring if the *Isis* makes the bounty, which she will, because the new man at the grain-office is still cheap." He looked up. "That's where we are. That's thirty years. It's a year off, and I can see it from here, and I came to Rome to look at it with you in the room, because I've looked at it alone in a strong-room for long enough."

Celer sat on the other stool and looked at his father, and saw, for the first time, that the man was fifty-three, and that the hair had gone, and that the hand on the tablet had a thing in it that it had not had on the Capua road, a tremor, small, the kind the Greek's hands had had by the brazier in December.

"Yes, Father," he said.

"Don't *yes, Father* me. I'm not Bassus. Tell me what's wrong with it."

"Sura."

"Go on."

"Sura's kept me on his staff for two years on his own account. Not Rufus's. The arrangement was Rufus's staff and Rufus's sponsorship. If Rufus sponsors me to the roll as his praetorship's gift, Sura will read it as the Laecanii buying back a man he's been paying for, and Sura reads everything four ways and one of them is as his enemies would." He kept his voice level. "He'll let it happen. He'll say nothing. And he'll put a mark in a margin, and the mark will say that Hermes's boy has two masters, and a boy with two masters is a boy who can be asked by either which one he'd choose, and I'll have been made that in front of him by my own father's census."

Hermes was quiet for a moment. Then — Celer would remember this — he smiled, the small dry one, and said: "Good. That's right. That's exactly right. Sura reads four ways and so do I, and I've read that one." He turned the tablet again. "So Rufus doesn't sponsor you. Rufus *recommends* you, as a praetor recommends a worthy young man from his father's client-house, in a letter, to the emperor's secretary for the equestrian rolls, who is a man called Titinius Capito, who was Domitian's and Nerva's and is now this man's and has survived all three by never once sponsoring anyone. And Sura, who is Capito's friend, hears of the letter from Capito, and says a word, or doesn't, and either way the roll's Sura's doing and not Rufus's, and you've one master and a ring." He set the tablet down. "I've had the letter drafted since March. I wanted to see if you'd find the hole. You did. You're your father's."

"Yes."

"And now the other thing." Hermes did not look at him. "The girl."

The room went still in the way Celer had learned.

"She's in the widow's house two streets down. She goes up the hill on the Kalends and the Ides and does the empress's hair. Thallus tells me. Thallus tells me a great many things about that girl; he's got a man in Tertulla's kitchens and he's never once in his life stopped counting a thing he's had in his hand." His father's voice was the strong-room voice, flat, a manifest. "And she writes to you. Through Vibia, who thinks I don't know, and who I let think it, because she's a good woman and it costs me nothing. Four letters since the autumn. For the widow; she says so, at the top, *the lady Cornelia asks*, which is honest, and which Thallus's man in the kitchens has read, and which Thallus has therefore read, and which means that there's a steward on the coast who knows that Sura's secretary answers questions from the widow Tertulla through a freedwoman's bastard." He looked up. "I'm not angry. I'd have done it. I did do it; I sent the widow your manifest to show her what you were. I'm telling you that the thing's known, on the coast, and that the coast is Bassus's house, and that Bassus's house is going to be Rufus's house, and Rufus is going to be praetor, and a praetor's house that knows a thing about a man on Sura's staff is a thing Sura will one day read four ways." He paused. "Stop writing to her. Not for ever. For the year. Till the roll. A girl who does the empress's hair has gone up in the world, I grant you — she's done it the way her mother did, on her back, by a different name, and God knows I don't blame her, there's no other road for that sort — but she's a freedwoman's bastard in a widow's house and you're a year off the stripe and I'll not have the two of you on the same sheet in Thallus's hand when Capito reads the letter—"

Celer hit him.

He had not decided to. It was the ship coming about, the wind going round a point; he was on the stool and then he was standing and his open hand had gone across his father's face, hard, so that the sound of it went round the small room by the slaves' court and came back, and his father's head went sideways, and the tablet went off his knee onto the floor and split, and there was a silence in which Celer heard, very clearly, a slave in the court outside stop walking.

His father put his hand to his face. He looked up at his son with the mark coming up red across the cheek and the thin hair disordered and the eyes — the eyes were the thing; they were not angry; they were doing the sum.

"Well," Hermes said. Quietly. "Well. There's that, then."

"She didn't." Celer's voice was not steady, and he noted it, and did not care. "She didn't go up on her back. She went up with a slate, in a house where her mother was dying over the stairs and a steward came twice a month, and she didn't go up the stairs, not once, Thallus will have told you that too if he told you anything, and she kept the house from a slate for a year while I was in a tent writing lists, and she went to the widow's with a book under her gown and corrected a Greek on Strabo and got through the empress's door with a hairpin, and if you say *on her back* again in my hearing, Father, I'll — " He stopped. He did not know what he would. He stood in the small room with his hand stinging and his father's face marked and found that he had no end to the sentence, that he had never in his life said a sentence he did not know the end of, and that this was the first.

His father lowered the hand from his face. He bent, slowly, with the tremor, and picked up the two halves of the tablet, and looked at them, and put them together, and they did not fit, and he set them on his knee anyway.

"Sit down," he said.

Celer sat down.

"I said a thing I'd heard said. On the coast. By men who don't know her and never will, and I said it because it's what will be said in Capito's office when Thallus's sheet gets there, and I wanted you to hear it from me first, in a room, so that your face would do nothing when it was said by someone who meant it." Hermes looked at the broken tablet. "I didn't know you'd — well. I know now. That's a thing to know. A man should know what his son will hit him for." He was quiet. "I'll not say it again. Not because you struck me; because you're right, and I knew it when I said it, and said it anyway to see what you'd do, which is a thing I've done to you your whole life and will likely do till I'm ash, because it's the only way I've ever found of learning what a thing is made of. Now I know. Sit there. Breathe." A pause. "And stop writing to her for the year. Not because of what she is. Because of what Thallus is, and what Rufus will be, and what Sura reads. You know I'm right about that. You knew it before you stood up."

Celer sat. He breathed. He knew his father was right about that, and had known it before he stood up, and he said, after a while, to the floor, "Yes, Father."

"Good." Hermes stood, with the tablet halves, with the mark on his face that would be there a week and that he would, Celer knew, explain to nobody, not Thallus, not Bassus, not Vibia, because a man who had been struck by his son in his patron's house was a man who had something, and his father never let anyone know what he had. At the door he stopped. "I'm sorry," he said, to the door, in the strong-room voice. "For the sentence. Not for the test. I'd do the test again. I'll not do the sentence." And then: "She wrote *and?* at the end of her mother. Vibia told me. I didn't understand it. Vibia said you would. I've thought about it since, in the strong-room. I think I've got it now." He went out.

* * *

Philon taught him the dust in September, in the third room, with the lamp, because the archivist had decided that a boy who had seen the sealed box should know how to tell, without touching, which boxes had been opened since.

"Look. Don't touch. Never touch; a lid that's been touched is a lid that's been read, and a lid that's been read tells the next man who looks what the last man wanted." The old Greek held the lamp low along the shelf so that the light ran across the box-lids at a slant, and the dust on them stood up grey in it like frost on a field. "There. The fourth. The dust's whole. Nobody's had that lid off since Nerva's year; I'd not need the seal to tell me, the dust tells me. The seventh. See the edge, where it's thinner. Somebody's had a finger under the lid and not lifted it — tried the weight, wanted to know if it was full — and put it down. That was Sura, in June, when the Spaniard was here, checking a thing he already knew. He does that. He thinks I don't see it. The ninth —" the lamp moved — "the ninth's been off and on and off again in a month, and the dust's been put *back*. Brushed on from the shelf with a sleeve. That's a man who knows what dust says and has tried to make it say something else, and it's the one thing in this room that frightens me, and it's why you're here."

Celer looked at the ninth. He looked at it a long time, with the lamp held where Philon held it, and saw what Philon saw, and said, "Who?"

"I don't know. That's the point. If I knew I'd have told Sura and Sura would have done the thing he does, which is nothing, with his eyes open, until the man did it again. I don't know, and so I've taught you, because you count everything and in a year you'll be able to walk down this shelf with no lamp and tell me which lids have moved, and then there'll be two of us, and a man who's brushing dust back onto a box in the third room will have to get past two pairs of eyes instead of one old Greek's." Philon straightened, with the cough, and the ring of Sura's father caught the lamp. "It's not a figure. It's a thing you'll know when you see it and can't say how, like your father with a hull. He reads a ship from the quay and knows what's in her by how she sits. You'll read a shelf. It's the same god; different altar."

He walked down the shelf that night after Philon had gone, with no lamp, by the light from the door, and did it, and got the seventh and the ninth, and missed one, the eleventh, which had been off that week by Sura's own hand for the Spaniard's letters, and knew in the morning that he had missed it because Philon told him so, with a face that was pleased, because a man who had missed one and been told was a man who would not miss it twice. He put it in the column with the rest. He did not know, that September, that the lid he would one day most need to read would be a pine box in his father's strong-room with the seal broken and the dust gone from it entirely, because a girl of eleven had watched him lift it, and remembered.

* * *

He did not write to her for the year.

He told himself, in the room with the window on the wall, that his father was right, which was true, and that Sura would read it four ways, which was true, and that a sheet in Thallus's hand in Capito's office with both their names on it was a thing that would cost her more than him, because he had a father buying Falernian hills in his name and she had a paper under a hearth-stone, which was true; and under all the true things, in the column that did not balance, was the other one, which was that he had struck his father for a sentence about her and had then done exactly what his father told him, in the same quarter of an hour, and that this was what he was, and that he had known it since a warehouse and four hundred and twenty jars, and that she had known it since a tutor's room, and had said *all right*, and had let him carry the number.

The widow asked for three more things that year, through Phaedrus, through Vibia, in four flat lines, \*the lady Cornelia Tertulla asks*; and he answered the first two, because Sura said to — "let the widow have her small things; she's cheap at the price and one day I'll want to know what the empress said and I'll have it through her girl through you, and she'll have arranged it, and we'll both pretend otherwise" — and he answered them in four lines, in the square hand, with nothing under them, no smaller hand after the lamp was trimmed, and the third he did not answer at all, because it came in September, the week his father's letter from Capua said *the last parcel's bought; it's four hundred and six, in your name, in the Falernian hills, and the vines are old and the house has a roof and I've never seen it and I'll not; it's yours*; and he sat with the widow's four lines and his father's one in his two hands on the stool by the lamp and found that he could not write anything to that house that was not either a list or the thing on the mole, and he had been told not to write the one and had decided not to spend the other, and so he wrote nothing, and put the widow's sheet in the box with the rest, and the next morning told Sura one thing that was true of more than forty, and it was about the Iazyges, and it was used.

Hadrian asked for him again in December, when the tribunate was his. Sura said no. The Spaniard laughed, and looked at the boy on the stool with the grey eyes, and said in Greek, going out, "Apollonios of Tomis. I've not forgotten either. We'll talk about him one day, you and I, when Lucius is dead and you've done your sum." And Celer sat on the stool with his face doing nothing and thought that he was eighteen, and on the roll by the spring, and had the adoption and the succession and a dead Greek and a king's gold under a river and a stone on the Capua road and a girl two streets from the bronze hand who did an empress's hair, all in him, all uncounted, and no column that would hold any of it; and that his father had said *I think I've got it now*, about *and?*, in a doorway, and had gone out before Celer could ask him what he thought it was, and that this was the question he would most have liked to put to any man in Rome, and the one man he could put it to was the one he had struck, and he would not, and that was both.
