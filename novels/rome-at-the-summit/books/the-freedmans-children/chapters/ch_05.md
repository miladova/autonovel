# The Entry

The summons came as a tally of days, which was how a patron asked a freedwoman for anything: not *will you* but *you owe*.

Thallus brought it down himself in September, on the first of his autumn visits, and read it out in the common room with his hands folded on the paper, as though it were a thing that had happened to all three of them and not a thing he had written. The house of Laecanius in Rome required its people for the emperor's entry. The master would be receiving. There would be a dinner for sixty on the day and the house would be open to callers for the ten days after and the Roman household could not do it alone, and so the freedmen of the coast who owed days were called upon to give them, as was their duty and, the steward added without any change in his voice, their privilege. Hilara owed the house twelve days a year under the terms of her manumission, and had, by the steward's count, given none of them these four years, since the master had not asked. The master was asking. She would come up by the Kalends of October with whatever help she had and would be lodged in the house and fed and would work in the kitchens and at table and would be sent home when the callers stopped. Her own house she could shut, or leave with the girl upstairs, as she pleased; the lease would be excused for the month.

"Whatever help I have," her mother said.

"The girl. She's eleven. She can carry a dish." Thallus folded the paper. "I'd not have thought to name her, but the master's cousin is coming for the entry, a widow, the consular Tertullus's widow, and she keeps a great many women and has opinions about who serves her, and the Roman steward is short. A girl who can count and doesn't gape will be useful. I said we had one."

"You said."

"I said. To the master's son, who asked." He stood. "You'll travel with the oil. Hermes is sending a cart up on the twenty-fifth for the entry, with his boy on it to see the city; you'll go with that. It's a kindness of his. He's not doing it for you."

"I know who he's doing it for," her mother said. "Tell him we'll be at the warehouse at dawn."

When the steward had gone she sat for a long time at the table with the lamp. Then she said, not to Fausta, who was at the hearth, but to the room: "Twelve days a year for four years is forty-eight. He's forgiven thirty-six and called it a month. He wants me to notice that." And then, to her daughter: "Get the good gown out, the red, and see if it can be let out again. And your blue. We're going to Rome."

Fausta had the gown out already. She had got it out while the steward was reading. She found that she was not surprised by any of it, and that she had, in some part of herself, been waiting since the day on the mole for Rome to come down the hill and ask for her, and that it had, and that it had asked by the usual messenger, in the usual coin, and had called it a kindness.

* * *

The cart left the emporium in the dark, two mules and the oil-man's son driving, with twenty jars of the good Italian measure under a sail in the bed and three passengers on the board behind the driver: the boy, with a tablet in his belt and his father's letters in a satchel on his knees, and the freedwoman, and the girl. Hermes was going up separately, by the fast post, with Bassus's letters and his own; a man of the Augustales did not ride on an oil-cart. The boy, it appeared, did, when the alternative was that he should go up in his father's company and be seen arriving in it.

"He wants me to come in with the trade," Celer said, when the town was behind them and the Capua road had opened out white between its tombs. "So that when Rufus sees me I'm already there. He says a man who's seen arriving is a man people remember arriving."

"And your tomb?"

"We passed it. You didn't look."

"I looked. The mason's cut the ship's eye." She had looked; she had seen the bronze eye catch the first light, high on the stone, where the *Isis* was. "It's got a pupil now. It didn't in the spring."

He was quiet. Then he said, "He had it done in August. After he forbade me the house. I don't know why."

"Because he'd won," said her mother from the end of the board, with her eyes closed, "and a man who's won likes to see something finished." And she did not open her eyes, and the cart went on, and the mules' bells and the jars talked to each other under the sail, and the sun came up over the hills on the right hand and showed them Campania going by, the vines in their long rows and the villas and the cypresses and, once, far off, the sea again, and then no more sea.

It took four days. She would remember it as one long day with sleep in it. She would remember the Appian Way, when they came to it, which was not a road but a thing laid across the world — stone, flat and black and wide enough for two carts, going straight where the land went crooked, with the tombs on both sides thickening as the city came nearer until they were a street of the dead ten miles long, with the living going between them in a river of carts and litters and mules and soldiers and men on foot, all north, all for the entry. She would remember the aqueducts coming in from the hills on their arches like something walking. She would remember the smell, which began a day out and which she could not name until the boy said *that's the city* and she understood that a city could be smelled, like a man, before it was seen. And she would remember the moment on the fourth morning when the road came over a rise and there it was, all of it, filling the whole of the plain in front of them from one edge of the sky to the other, red and white and brown and gold under a haze of its own smoke, with the hills standing up out of it crowned with temples, and she had not known, she had not been able to know, that anything made by hands could be that size.

She did not say anything. The boy did not either. Her mother, who had seen it before, said, "Breathe. You've stopped."

* * *

The house of Laecanius in Rome stood on the Caelian, on the slope above the old temple of the deified Claudius, in a street of houses like it: high walls and a door and nothing else to show, so that you could walk the length of the street and not know you had passed the homes of six men who had been consul. Inside, it went back and back. She had thought the villa at Baiae was large. The villa at Baiae was a holiday; this was the thing itself, the machine the villa paid for, and it had a hundred and forty slaves in it when the family was in residence and eleven freedmen who came in daily and a steward who was not Thallus and had never heard of her.

His name was Eutyches and he had been Bassus's father's and he ran the house the way the sea ran the harbour at Puteoli, by being everywhere and never seen. He looked at Hilara and the girl in the slaves' court behind the kitchens on the morning they arrived, with the cart being unloaded in the lane and the oil-man's son shouting at a doorkeeper, and he said, "The Puteoli woman. Twelve days. Kitchens for the dinner, table for the callers. And this is the one Thallus wrote about for the lady Cornelia." He looked at Fausta for perhaps the time it took to blink. "Too young. She'll do for the water. Give her a clean tunic and put her with Phaedrus's lot; he's bringing the lady's people over this afternoon and he can have the sorting of her. You —" to Hilara — "the kitchens know you're coming. Don't argue with the cook; he's Gaul and he bites."

That was the whole of her reception. The city had been in sight for two hours and she had not yet seen a single thing in it but walls and a lane and a slaves' court, and already she had been looked at, priced, and put in a column. She found it steadying. It was the harbour again; it was the Dolphin with a hundred and forty rooms. The great were the great, but their stewards were stewards, and a steward was a man who had read one book very carefully, and she knew the book.

Celer she lost at the door. He had been taken off by a slave of Rufus's before the cart was unloaded, and had looked back once from the end of the lane with his satchel on his shoulder and his face doing the thing it did when it had been told to be a face, and then had gone; and she did not see him again for nine days, and when she did they were both in other people's clothes.

* * *

Phaedrus came over in the afternoon with the lady Cornelia's people, and the lady Cornelia's people were a thing to see.

There were thirty of them. Fausta had not known that a woman went visiting with thirty people. There were the litter-bearers, eight Cappadocians in matched grey; there were two women whose only work, she gathered, was the lady's hair, and a third whose only work was the lady's face, and a fourth who carried the lady's cosmetics in a case that had its own slave; there were readers, two, a Greek and a Latin; there were the secretary and his boy; there was a dwarf, whom nobody explained; and there were a dozen girls of between twelve and twenty who did, as far as Fausta could ever afterwards make out, nothing at all except be present in sufficient numbers that the lady was never in a room with fewer than four of them, and who had the look, all of them, of expensive things kept in a drawer. Phaedrus was their steward, a tall Alexandrian with a shaved head and a Greek so pure it sounded like water, and he looked at the Puteoli girl in her clean borrowed tunic and said, "Can you pour without touching the rim?" and she said, "Yes," and he gave her a jug and watched her, and said, "Yes. You can. Who taught you?"

"Lodgers."

"Lodgers." He considered this. "Well, it doesn't show. You'll stand behind the lady at the dinner and keep her water full and say nothing whatever and look at no one. If she speaks to you, you say *domina* and whatever's true. She doesn't like a lie and she can hear one through a wall. Can you read?"

"Yes."

"Don't let her find it out. Not yet. She collects girls who can read the way some women collect emeralds and she's got eleven and they hate each other." He took the jug back. "I'm telling you that as a kindness. Nobody told me."

* * *

She saw the emperor from a roof.

That was the whole household's arrangement; the family went down to the Forum in the dawn to stand with the senate, and the house's people, those who were not needed to prepare the dinner, went up onto the roofs of the Caelian, where the whole slope was black with the slaves and freedmen and clients of forty great houses, and looked down over the valley and the Colosseum's great curve and the Sacred Way beyond it, and waited.

They waited from dawn until the fourth hour. She stood at a parapet between two of the lady Cornelia's girls, who talked across her about a man, and did not look at the city, because if she looked at the city she would gape, and Phaedrus had said. So she looked at the roof-tiles, and at the backs of her own hands, and at the girls' hands, which had never carried a water-jar up a stair and showed it, and she counted the hours by the shadow of the parapet, and she listened. The city made a sound she had not heard before. It was not the roar of the fleet-day; that had been forty thousand people being glad. This was ten times that, or twenty, a sound so large it had stopped being a sound and become a condition, like heat; and it was waiting, and it moved, so that you could hear where the emperor was by where the roar was thickest, far off beyond the Caelian to the south where the Appian came in through the gate, and then nearer, and nearer, along the valley, until it was below them and the girls stopped talking.

He was on foot.

That was the thing everyone said afterwards, and it was the thing she saw: a man walking. He came along the Sacred Way through the middle of the roar with the senate behind him in their white and the Guard behind that, and he was not carried and he was not on a horse, he was walking, in a plain toga, with his head bare, a tall man with a soldier's shoulders and grey hair cut short, and as he came he stopped — she saw him stop — and a man came out of the crowd at the roadside, an old man in a senator's stripe, and the emperor took him by the arms and kissed him, and walked on, and stopped again, and did it again. The roar changed when he did it. It went up a note. She did not know what the note meant until one of the girls said, with something in her voice that was almost awe and almost contempt, "He's *embracing* them. He's letting them touch him. Domitian would have had you flogged for being on the same side of the street."

Fausta watched the man walk. She thought: he is doing it on purpose. She thought: he has a hundred thousand people on the roofs and he is making sure they see him stop. She thought, with the part of her that had learned in a yard and a tutor's room and a warehouse aisle to read the thing behind the thing: that is the Baetican jar. He is giving them less than an emperor and calling it a favour, and they are cheering him for the size of the jar.

And then she stopped thinking that, because behind him, a little way behind, with two women beside her and no litter and no canopy and no thirty people, there was another figure walking, and it was a woman, and she was walking as the emperor was walking, on foot, in a plain dress, with her hair done simply and her head covered, and she was not stopping to embrace anyone and nobody was roaring for her; she was simply walking into Rome behind her husband like a woman going to market.

"That's the empress," the girl on Fausta's left said. "Plotina. She's from Nemausus. Nobody knows anything about her." And then, lower, because she had been told something and could not keep it: "She said a thing on the palace steps. My cousin's in the Palatine household. She stopped on the steps and turned round to the crowd and said, *I enter here the kind of woman I should wish to be when I leave.* In front of everyone. The senate didn't know where to look."

Fausta watched the woman walk. She was too far off to see a face. But she could see how the woman held herself, which was very straight, and very still in the middle of all that noise, and she could see that the woman did not look up at the roofs, not once, though the roofs were black with people looking down; she looked at the road, and at the man in front of her, and at nothing else, as though the city were a thing that had been arranged for her to walk through and she had agreed to walk through it and that was the extent of the bargain.

*They didn't look at the boat*, Fausta thought. But it was not that. It was not the great not seeing. It was something else, that she did not have yet and would spend a long time acquiring: a woman who could see the boat perfectly well, and all the roofs, and every face on them, and had decided that the only answer to being looked at by a hundred thousand people was to give them a straight back and a covered head and a sentence they would be repeating in the slaves' courts by nightfall. It was not modesty. The girl beside her thought it was modesty. Fausta, who had grown up in a house where women arranged themselves to be seen for a living, knew the difference between modesty and work.

She watched the woman until the procession went round the end of the Palatine and out of sight. Then she looked down at her own hands on the parapet, which were a water-carrier's hands, and thought: *that one is the one to watch. Not the man. The man's a soldier and he's been told what the city wants and he's giving it. She's the one who told him.*

She did not know that this was true. She was eleven. But she put it where she kept things, in the front, where she could reach it.

* * *

The dinner was for sixty and went until the third watch and she stood behind the lady Cornelia's couch for the whole of it with a silver jug and did not touch the rim.

She did not, that first night, see the lady's face; she saw the back of her head, which was done in a style four years out of fashion and kept there, Phaedrus had said, on purpose, because the lady had been a beauty under Domitian and did not propose to be anything else under anyone. She saw the shoulders, which were broad for a woman's and held square. She saw the hand that came back, without turning, for the cup, and the cup go to the lips she could not see and come back, and she filled it, and the hand came back again, and she understood after the fourth time that the lady was not drinking but using the cup as a thing to do with her hand while she listened, and that she was listening to everything, and that the men on the couches round her — Bassus's guests, senators and their sons, a couple of the new Spanish faces that had come in with the emperor, Rufus, Hermes very far down the table in a place that was a great honour for a freedman and a precise insult to the freedman's hopes — were talking across her and past her and through her as men talked across a woman of fifty-five, and that she was letting them.

Bassus talked about liberty. The Spaniards talked about the Danube. Rufus talked about the Danube to the Spaniards and about liberty to his father, and did both well, and Fausta, filling the cup, thought that he was a man who had learned to pour without touching the rim and was pleased with himself for it. Hermes said nothing at all for two hours and then, when one of the Spaniards asked the table whether anyone knew what the Alexandrian fleet had carried this year, said the figure, to the modius, and the Spaniard looked down the table to see who had spoken, and looked away again, and Fausta saw the back of Hermes's neck do what it had done in the atrium at Baiae when the hand had rested on his shoulder.

She saw Celer once. He was with Rufus's people, standing behind Rufus's couch with a tablet, which was not a servant's work and not a guest's and was therefore exactly what his father had wanted: a boy who was visibly *something*, in a room where that was the whole point. He had a clean tunic and his hair had been cut and he looked, across sixty couches and the lamps and the smoke, like a stranger she had known once. He did not see her. He was watching Rufus's hands, the way she was watching the lady's, and she thought that between them they had the two most important people at that end of the table under observation and that nobody in the room knew it, and the thought gave her a small cold pleasure that she recognised, without liking it, as the first thing in Rome that was hers.

At the third watch the lady Cornelia set down her cup and said, without turning her head, in a voice that was low and dry and carried exactly as far as she intended it to, "Girl. Your hand's steady. Whose are you?"

"Domina. The house's. From Puteoli. My mother's a freedwoman of the master's."

"Hilara's." The lady said the name as though it were a place she had once been to. "Yes. I'd heard there was a daughter." Still she did not turn. "You've been watching the Spaniard on the third couch for an hour. Why?"

Fausta had not known it showed. She said, because Phaedrus had said *whatever's true*: "Because he asked about the fleet and didn't listen to the answer, domina. He was looking at who'd given it."

A silence. Then the lady's shoulders moved, very slightly, in a way that Fausta would learn, over years, to recognise as the nearest she came to laughing.

"Yes," said Cornelia Tertulla. "He was. That's Licinius Sura. He's the one who made this emperor, and he counts everything, and he's just counted your friend's father." The cup came back. "Fill that. And tomorrow when the callers come you'll stand behind me again, and you'll say nothing, as you have tonight, and afterwards you'll tell me who looked at whom. I shall know if you make it up." A pause. "Phaedrus said you couldn't read. Phaedrus lies to me about one thing a year to see if I notice. I always notice. Can you?"

"Yes, domina."

"Good. Don't do it in front of the others." The hand went away. "Go and find your mother and tell her the Gaul says the fish was adequate, which from him is love, and that she's to stay the month and not the twelve days, and that I've told Bassus so. He'll agree. He agrees with me about everything that doesn't cost him anything, and you don't cost him anything, you're already his."

* * *

Her mother was in the slaves' court behind the kitchens with her sleeves rolled and her hair coming down, sitting on an upturned basket in the dark with a cup of the Gaul's wine, and she listened to all of it without speaking, and at the end she said, "The month."

"Yes."

"She asked for you by name. The widow."

"She asked whose I was. I said yours."

"And she said the month." Hilara drank. In the dark her face was a shape and nothing else. "Listen to me. A woman like that doesn't ask a water-girl whose she is. She asks the steward, and the steward tells her, and she's known for a week. She asked *you* so you'd hear yourself say it. She wanted to know what you'd say. You said *the house's*. That was right. If you'd said *my mother's* she'd have put you in the kitchen with me and that would have been the end of it. You said the house's, and now you're the house's, and she's the house's cousin, and that's a door." She set the cup down. "I don't know yet if it's a door I want you through. I don't know what's on the other side. But I know what's on this side, and it's the Dolphin and Thallus on the Ides, and I'll tell you now, since we're in the dark and in Rome and nobody's listening: I didn't bring you up for the Dolphin. I brought you up to read a paper. If reading a paper takes you into a widow's house on the Caelian, then that's where it takes you, and I'll go back to the coast with my twelve days paid and my lease excused and say my daughter's in Rome, and see what Thallus makes of that."

"Mama —"

"Don't. Not here." Her mother stood up and shook out her gown. "Go to bed. You've callers at the second hour and the widow wants to know who looked at whom, and you'd better have it right, because she's a woman who's spent fifty years being looked at and she can tell a lie through a wall. That's not Phaedrus's phrase, by the way. It's Thallus's. He said it about her in the spring. He's been in this house too; everyone's been in this house. It's a small world, the house of Laecanius. It only looks big from the lane."

Fausta went to bed. The bed was a pallet in a room with eleven of the lady Cornelia's girls, who had been given the slaves' dormitory over the bakehouse and resented it, and who looked at the Puteoli girl coming in late with the lady's word in her and did the arithmetic, and some of them said something and most of them did not. She lay down among them. She could not sleep. She lay and listened to the house, which was a thing she did, and found that she could not; it was too large; it did not come to her as an instrument came but as the city had come over the rise, all at once, without edges, a hundred and forty people breathing and the Gaul swearing in the kitchen and a cart in the lane and the roar, still, very faint, from somewhere down in the valley where the city had not yet stopped being glad.

So she thought instead about the woman walking. The straight back, the covered head, the eyes on the road. *The kind of woman I should wish to be when I leave.* She thought about the lady's hand coming back for the cup, and about Sura's eyes going down the table to find who had said the figure, and about Celer behind Rufus's couch watching Rufus's hands, and about her mother in the dark saying *that's a door*. And she thought, before she slept, the first thought she ever had in Rome that was neither fear nor wonder nor arithmetic, and it was this: that everyone in this city was watching someone, all the time, and that the whole of the roar she could hear from the valley was only the sound of a million people trying to work out who was watching them.

It was, she would come to think, not a bad first thought. It was wrong in only one particular, and she would find out the particular from the lady Cornelia, in due course, and at a price.

* * *

The callers came for ten days, from the second hour until noon, and she stood behind the lady's chair in Bassus's atrium with the jug and watched them, and every noon in the small room off the peristyle that Phaedrus had made the lady's own, she said who had looked at whom.

She was wrong at first. Not in the seeing; in the telling. She said, the first noon, that the senator with the limp had watched the door the whole hour, and the lady said, "Yes, and why," and she did not know why, and the lady said, "Because his wife's brother was due and didn't come, and he's lent the brother money, and the brother's gone over to Sura's people and won't be seen in this house again. You saw a man watching a door. I need to know what the door is. Find out. Phaedrus knows everything and lies about one thing a year; ask him, and work out which thing." So she asked Phaedrus, and worked it out, and the second noon she said who had looked at whom and why, and was right four times in six, and the lady said nothing, which Phaedrus told her afterwards was the lady's highest praise and that he had had it twice in nine years.

By the fifth day she had the shape of it. The atrium at the second hour was the harbour at the first: everyone who came in had a cargo and a port and a price, and the whole of the business was to read the manifest off the face before the man had opened his mouth. The Spaniards came, and were given the front. The old senators came, Bassus's friends, Nerva's friends, men who had kept their doors shut on the night of the Guard, and were given the middle, and knew it, and smiled. The freedmen came last and stood at the back where her mother had stood at Baiae, and Hermes among them, every morning, in his toga, saying nothing, being seen. And through it all the lady sat at her cousin's left hand with her cup and her four-years-old hair and let the room talk across her, and once a day, perhaps, said a sentence, and the room stopped.

On the ninth day Celer came into the small room with a tablet for the lady from Rufus, and found her there.

He stopped in the door. She was standing at the lady's shoulder with the jug, as she stood; he was in a tunic of good undyed wool that was not his, with a clerk's case at his belt that was not his either, and his hair cut to the Roman length, and they looked at each other across the lady Cornelia's head for the length of a breath, two children from the same street in other people's clothes, and neither of them moved a muscle of the face, because they had both, in nine days, learned where they were.

"Rufus's boy," the lady said, without turning. "Give it to the girl. She'll read it to me; I've a headache and your master's hand is a crime against the alphabet."

He gave it to her. Their fingers did not touch; he saw to that, or she did. She read it. It was nothing — a note about the seating for the tenth day, who was to be moved up and who was to be allowed to notice. She read it in the voice Phaedrus had taught her, which was no voice, and the lady listened with her eyes shut, and when it was done said, "Tell him yes to the first and no to the second and that I don't care about the third. And boy —" Celer, at the door, stopped. "Your father stood at the back of my cousin's atrium for nine mornings saying nothing, and on the sixth morning Sura's secretary asked Eutyches his name. That's what he came for. He has it. Tell him he can go home."

Celer said, "Yes, domina," and went, and did not look at Fausta, and she did not look at him, and the lady Cornelia opened her eyes and looked at neither of them but at the wall opposite, where there was a painting of a garden, and said after a moment in the dry low voice:

"You grew up with that one."

"Yes, domina."

"He didn't look at you. You didn't look at him. That was well done, both of you, and it was a mistake, and I'll tell you why, once, because nobody told me. Two people who don't look at each other in a room are the only two people in it that everyone else can see." She reached for the cup. "Next time, look. Look the way you look at the Spaniards. It costs nothing and it's invisible. The thing that shows is the effort." She drank. "Your mother goes home on the Kalends. You'll go with her. I'm not ready for you and you're not ready for me, and I've eleven girls who can read already and they'd have you in the Tiber by the Saturnalia. But I'll write to Bassus. In a year. Two. When you've bled and can be put in a room without a man thinking about it, or at any rate not thinking first." She set the cup down. "Don't thank me. I'm not doing it for you either. Nobody in this city does anything for anyone; it's the one thing about it that's restful. Go and tell the Gaul I'll have the eggs."

She went and told the Gaul. And in the lane behind the kitchens, where the oil-cart had stood nine days before and stood again now, empty, waiting for the Kalends, she found Celer sitting on the tail of it with the clerk's case on his knees, looking at nothing, and she sat down beside him without asking, and they looked at nothing together for a while.

"She said to look," Fausta said. "Next time."

"I heard. The door's thin." He turned the case over in his hands. "My father's got his name on Sura's list. He's going home tomorrow. He's — I've never seen him like it. He walked into Rufus's rooms and didn't say anything and sat down. He sat *down*. In Rufus's rooms." A pause. "He's leaving me. For the winter. With Rufus. To see the city, he says. To be seen in it."

She had known. Phaedrus knew everything. She said, "And you?"

"I don't know what I am yet. Something between a clerk and a nephew. Rufus hasn't decided and I don't think he will till the war." He looked at her, then, properly, for the first time in nine days, the way the lady had said: the way you looked at a Spaniard. "You're going home."

"On the Kalends. She's not ready for me." She heard how it sounded and let it stand. "A year. Two."

"Then we'll both be here."

"We're both here now."

"No," he said. "We're not. You're the house's and I'm Rufus's and we're sitting on an oil-cart in a lane. That's not here. That's where people wait for news of it." And he said it without bitterness, as a fact, in the voice that she had heard him use about the Baetican jars and the harbour at Ostia, and she understood that he was telling her, as exactly as he could, where he had got to, so that she would know where to look for him; and she thought that it was, in its way, the most honest thing anyone had said to her in Rome, and that it was a thing a man said who had already begun to count the lane as somewhere he would not be coming back to.

"Look at me," she said. "The way she said."

He looked. It cost nothing. It was invisible. The effort, she thought, was the thing that showed, and neither of them let it; and a slave came out of the kitchen door with a basket of eggs for the Gaul and walked between them and saw two children on a cart and nothing else, and went in.
