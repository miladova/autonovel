# The Year of the Tongue

She did not speak in the room for a year, and the year taught her what a room was.

It was twenty-four mornings. She counted them, as she counted everything, and set them out afterwards in the place where she kept things like the Rhodian's bill, one under another: Kalends, Ides, Kalends, Ides, July to June, the comb and the box of pins and the woman on the couch with the cloth on or off and the Spaniard's wife on her stool and Marcia at the shoulder and the lady on the bench outside and the long wall and the door. Twenty-four mornings of being furniture with hands. And she learned, in them, the thing that Nicias had said was the only thing he had owned in fifty-five years, and that she had thought, at ten, she understood: that a woman who was not spoken to was a woman who could not be lied to, and that in a room where everyone was performing for everyone, the one person nobody performed for was the one who could see the whole of the play.

She saw Matidia. The Spaniard's mother-in-law, with the book and the plain hair done by her own hand, who turned a page when the legate's wife laughed, and who was, Fausta understood by September, the only woman in the room the Augusta did not manage. She did not need to. They had made the thing together, the two of them, the way her mother and the egg-woman's mother had made the thing in the back room at the Dolphin: one with the hands and one holding the lamp, and no need afterwards to say which had been which. Matidia had given her daughter to the Spaniard. The Augusta had made the Spaniard worth giving a daughter to. Between them they held a man in a legion in the Dacian hills the way two women held a sheet to fold it, each with her corners, and the man knew, and wrote four-sheet letters about hill-forts in good Greek, and did not come to the room, because he did not need to; he was in it.

And she saw the daughter.

Vibia Sabina was twenty-two that year, and had been married five, and came to the room on the Ides, not the Kalends, because the Kalends were her mother's and she would not share them; and she sat on a stool by the window that was not her mother's stool, with no book, with her hands in her lap, and looked at the room with a face that had been given everything a face could be given on that hill — the blood, the marriage, the future — and had decided, somewhere, to find all of it insufficient. She was beautiful. It was a thing that was said about her and it was true and it did not help; she had her grandmother's long bones and her mother's stillness and something of her own that neither of them had, a kind of dry disappointed intelligence that sat in the beautiful face like a tenant who had not chosen the house. She did not speak to the benches. She did not speak to the Augusta, who was her husband's patroness and her great-aunt by marriage and who had made her a wife at seventeen to a man who read her poems about other people. She spoke, when she spoke, to Marcia, in a low quick voice, about nothing, and Marcia answered about nothing, and the room watched them do it and understood that this was the one person in it who was not owned by anyone and hated it.

On the Ides of October she spoke to Fausta.

It was after the hair. The Augusta had the cloth on and had said *that's it, that's my mother's* and had turned her face to the wall, and Marcia had gone out to the benches with the comb, and Fausta was by the door with the box of pins waiting to be let through it, and Sabina, on her stool by the window, said without turning her head, in the low quick voice: "You're the one who doesn't speak."

Fausta said nothing. She had been told.

"Yes. I heard. A year, and then a thing, and if not, Tertulla's son." The beautiful face did not move. "She does that. She gave my husband a year once, before the marriage, to stop writing to a boy in Athens, and he stopped, and she gave him me. That was the thing." A pause. "I'd have taken the son."

Fausta looked at the floor. She felt the long game in her like a stone held in the mouth.

"You can't answer. I know. I'm not asking you to; I'm telling you, because you're the only person in this room who can't repeat it, and I've wanted to say it to someone for five years and the slaves repeat everything and the women repeat it better." Sabina turned her head, finally, and looked at the girl by the door with the long bones and the dry tenant intelligence, and it was not an unkind look; it was the look of a woman examining a wall to see if it was load-bearing. "You've got a face like a shutter and a nose like a consul and you do an old woman's hair the way her mother had it and you've not said a word in four months in a room where I've never once heard the truth, and I thought: that one's not performing. Everyone else is. My husband is. My mother is. *She* is —" the smallest movement of the head toward the couch, where the cloth was — "she's been performing a headache for twenty years and it's the best thing on this hill. You're not. You're just standing there, seeing it." The beautiful face went back to the window. "I wanted you to know somebody'd noticed. Go on; Marcia's waiting with her comb and her one thing a Kalends. Tell her I said nothing. It'll be true of more than forty."

She went out. She told Marcia nothing. Marcia's fingers moved a pin and the round face said, "Sabina spoke to you."

"I didn't answer."

"No. You wouldn't. That's the year." The comb went into the box. "She speaks to no one. She's spoken to me and to a Greek girl who died and now to you, and the Greek girl died of a fever and not of anything else, before you ask, but the Augusta had her moved to Laurentum first, where the air's better, and where she couldn't be spoken to by the Spaniard's wife in a room with the door shut." Marcia did not look at her. "That's not a threat. I don't make them; she does, and she's made you one already, and she doesn't make two. It's a thing to know. The one person in that room who isn't owned is the one person it's not safe to be seen by. Don't let her choose you. She's lonely and she's clever and she'll make you the only true thing on the hill, and then the hill will have to do something about it."

* * *

Marcia asked her about Puteoli in February, with the comb, in the Nemausus Latin, so lightly that she almost did not hear it was a question.

"The war eats wheat," the freedwoman said, to a pin. "Four legions on the far bank and the Guard and the Syrian's bridge-builders and the horses, and the Danube depots can't feed it; it comes from Egypt, round by sea, into the Italian ports and up the roads and across. The grain-office pays by weight. The Augusta's had the figures from the prefect — she has everything from everyone; it's what the headache's for — and she's looked at them, which the prefect didn't expect, because emperors' wives don't look at modii, and she's found a thing." The fingers moved. "The Puteoli carriers claim the bounty for early delivery nine years in ten and are docked for short weight never, and the Ostia carriers claim it three in ten and are docked twice a season. Same ships. Same sea. Same Egyptian wheat. Different port. Different scale." A pause the width of a stylus. "You grew up a street from a man with three hulls in that trade. The Augusta said: *Tertulla's girl kept a lodging-house book at thirteen; she'll have read every slate in the lower town.* I said: she's not speaking this year. The Augusta said: *then she'll have a year to decide what it's worth.*" The comb went into the box. "I've not asked you a question. The year holds."

Fausta stood by the wall with the pins and the shutter to and the thing in her that she had read at eleven in a strong-room while a boy counted jars — *pondere, non mensura*, the fourth line of the second column. The sail that was wet on the day the prefect's man weighed, and *the baker in the Subura gets a loaf that's a tenth water and nobody counts that because the baker's a baker* — and she did not move, and she did not speak, and she thought, in the slate way, very fast: *they've found it. Not from me. From the figures. They've found it the way Celer found the depot, from a column that wouldn't reconcile, and they've come to the only person on the hill who could tell them what the column means, and they've come in a year when I can't speak, and they've told me that the year's a mercy.*

And under that, where she kept the things that were not sums: *it's his father. It's the house with the columns that aren't marble. It's the man who sent the widow his son's hand in a manifest and said "the daughter of my patron's freedwoman" so she'd think of us as a pair. It's the four hundred thousand and the olives and the vines in the Falernian hills with an F on a stone.*

And under that: *it's not Celer. He's on the far bank with a ring, writing the inventory of a kingdom. He's never once in his life done a thing by weight that he could do by measure. But he's his father's; he said four hundred and twenty in a tutor's room and looked at me while he said it and I said all right.*

She said nothing. The year held. She went down the long wall behind the comb.

In the litter the lady said, "Well?" and she said, "The Augusta's looking at the grain figures, domina. Marcia told me so. She said it to a pin so it wasn't a question," and the lady was quiet for a street and then said, "Yes. I'd heard. Sura's people have been at the prefect's office since the autumn; it's a war, and wars are paid for by somebody, and the somebody's usually a man with ships who's been doing a thing for ten years that nobody counted till the bill came in." The shoulders moved. "Your Hermes."

"I don't know, domina."

"No. You don't. And you'll not know, this year, because you can't speak and I'll not ask, and that's the only kindness I've got for you, child, and I'm giving it because I can see what it's costing you to hold your face and I was fifteen once in a room where the truth would have hanged a man I'd eaten with." The lady did not look at her. "But the year ends in June. And in June they'll ask, and you'll have to decide whether a man's hulls are worth more to you than a woman's door, and I'll tell you now that I know which I'd choose and that I was wrong, at your age, and it took me thirty years to be glad of it."

* * *

There was a hairpin on Phaedrus's table in March, in the slaves' court, in the morning, lying by itself on the wood where the day's lists should have been, and it was silver, and the steward was standing well back from it.

It came through Phaedrus, which was how the son sent anything to his mother's side of the house: a thing left on the steward's table in the slaves' court in the morning with a word, *for the Puteoli girl, from the stable wing, with compliments on her advancement*, and Phaedrus brought it to her in the corridor nobody used, where Momus sat at the end, and put it in her hand and stood back to watch her look at it.

It was ivory. It was old — older than the house, Momus said afterwards, turning it in his short fingers; Greek, from the islands, the kind of thing a consul's grandfather brought back from a province and that lay in a box for a hundred years until somebody opened the box. It had a head carved as a woman's face, very small, very fine, with the hair done the old way, plain, as a woman on a coast built on ash had it, as the Augusta's mother had had it. And it was, she saw, turning it in the corridor's bad light, broken: the shaft had been snapped, once, long ago, and mended, with a thread of gold wound round the break, so that it held, and showed.

"He's telling you three things," Phaedrus said, in the Greek like water, very low. "He's telling you he knows what you do on the hill and for whom, because the hair's the old way and he's never seen the Augusta's hair in his life; someone's told him, carefully, which means he's asked, carefully. He's telling you he can put a thing in your hand through me, in his mother's house, and I'll carry it, because I will, because a steward who refuses the son a hairpin is a steward who's declared a thing, and I've twelve years in this house and no wish to declare anything. And he's telling you the third thing with the gold." The Alexandrian looked at the mended shaft. "A pin's a small thing. It breaks. It can be mended, and look better for it, and be worth more. He's patient. He's saying so, in ivory, with compliments."

She stood with it in her palm. The small carved face looked up at her with the plain hair and the expression that faces carved on pins had, which was nothing, which was the shutter, which was hers.

"What do I do with it?"

"You wear it." Phaedrus did not hesitate. "On the Kalends. In the Augusta's room. In your own hair, where Marcia will see it, and ask, with two fingers, and you'll say *a gift from the lady's house*, which is true. Marcia will know within the day whose, because Marcia knows what's in every box on this hill, and then the Augusta will know, and then the thing he's put in your hand through me in his mother's house will be a thing the Palatine has seen in the Palatine, and he'll have told the one woman in Rome he can't count that he's sending her hair-girl ivory." He stepped back. "That's what you do with a pin from a patient man. You make it public, and you make it *hers*. It's the only move there is. He'll know you've made it; he'll have expected it; that's why it's broken and mended and not whole — he wants you to wear it knowing he knows you'll wear it to the hill. He's playing the long game, girl. He had it from his mother and she had it from this house. You're not the only one who's been given a year."

She wore it. On the Kalends of April, in the Augusta's room, in her own hair, at the side, where the lamp caught the gold on the break; and Marcia's fingers came to it and stopped, and the round brown face did nothing, and the freedwoman said, to the pin, "That's island work. That's older than the hill. Where?" and Fausta said, "A gift from the lady's house," which was true; and the fingers went away; and on the couch the woman with the cloth over her eyes said, to the ceiling, in the voice she used for metre, "Marcia. What's she wearing?"

"Ivory, Augusta. Old. Mended with gold. From Tertulla's house."

A silence under the cloth.

"From Tertulla's *house*." The Augusta did not move. "Well. He's a man who knows what a thing looks like from the outside; I'll say that for him, it's the one thing his mother gave him that took. Girl. You've two months left of your year. You've worn a patient man's pin into my room so that I'd see it, which is the cleverest thing you could have done with it and which you were told to do by an Alexandrian who's been twelve years in this house not declaring anything, and it's worked, because I've seen it, and now it's mine, the way everything in this room is. Keep wearing it. Every Kalends. Let him hear that you do." The cloth did not move. "And when the year's up, I'll give you the thing I said, and it'll be a thing he'll understand better than a pin, because he's his mother's and his mother was raised on paper."

He found her in the peristyle on the Nones, by the fountain that had stopped and been started again, in the hour after the lady's rest, and he did not come close this time; he stood on the far side of the basin with the water between them and his hands behind his back, in the tunic from the stable wing, and looked at the pin in her hair.

"You wore it to the hill."

"Yes, domine."

"On the Kalends. Where the Augusta's woman would see it. And the Augusta asked, and now it's hers." He said it the way a man said *the roof held* after a storm he had built the roof for. "Phaedrus told you to."

"Yes, domine."

"Good. I'd have thought less of him if he hadn't, and less of you if you'd not done it." He looked at the water. He was thirty-one and the heaviness was in the shoulders now and not yet in the face, and the face was his mother's, narrow and patient and raised on paper, and it had on it the expression of a man who had made a move he had expected to be answered and was pleased, in a way that had nothing to do with her, to have been answered correctly. "You understand what I did."

"You told the Palatine you were sending me ivory, domine. Through me. So that I'd be the one who'd told it."

"Yes. And now it's known, on the hill, that Tertulla's son has an interest in Tertulla's girl, and that the girl wore it, and that the Augusta saw and said *keep wearing it*, which Marcia will have told the benches by now, because it's the kind of thing Marcia tells." The slow turn of the head. "Which means that when my mother dies — not this year; she's strong; but she's sixty-four and she's had the cough since the winter and the doctor from Laurentum looks at her the way doctors look — when she dies, and the house comes to me, and you with it, there'll not be a woman on that hill who's surprised. It'll be the thing everyone already knew. That's what the pin was for. Not you. The *knowing*." He took one hand from behind his back and rested it on the basin's rim, where it had rested a year ago when he had said *lamp lit*. "I'm not a man who takes a thing by force, Puteoli girl. I'm not a soldier. I've never been north of Ariminum. I'm a man who puts a thing where it'll be seen and waits for the seeing to do the work. I had it from her. She had it from this house. You've learned it yourself, in a year, from the Alexandrian and the Nemausus woman and the one with the headache, and you've just played it back at me across a fountain and played it well. I'm telling you so you know I'm not angry. I'm the opposite."

She stood with the water between them and her feet under her and did the sum, and it came out where it had come out in November on a lamp: that he was telling her the truth, which was the worst of him. That there was a road he had been walking up for a year and a half with his hands behind his back and that he had just told her the name of the house at the top of it and that it was his mother's death.

"Domine," she said. "The Augusta said a thing."

"I'm sure she did."

"She said the pin was hers now. The way everything in that room is. She said to let you hear that I wear it every Kalends." She kept her voice flat. "I think she meant you to hear the first part, domine. Not the second."

The patient face did not change. The hand on the basin's rim did not move. But something behind the face moved, the way a slate did when a figure was carried from one column to another, and she saw it, and he saw her see it, and he smiled, slightly, for the first time in her knowledge of him, and it was not a pleasant thing and it was not an unpleasant one; it was a man acknowledging that the piece he had moved had been moved back.

"Yes," he said. "She would. That's her game; mine's only a copy." He took the hand off the rim. "Then we both wait, you and I. She's fifty-five and I'm thirty-one and my mother's sixty-four with a cough, and one of those numbers will move before the others, and when it does we'll see whose the pin is." He turned to go, and at the colonnade's edge turned back, with the heaviness in the shoulders. "Keep wearing it. I mean that as she did. Let *her* hear that you do. It's the only thing we'll ever agree on, the Augusta and I, and I'd not waste it."

He went. She stood by the fountain until the sound of him was gone down the colonnade and the water had gone back to being water, and then she went in by the corridor nobody used, and Momus, at the end of it, looked up at her face as she passed and said, "Ah. He's told you what he's waiting for," and she said, "Yes," and the dwarf said, "Then you've a number to beat, and it's the lady's cough, and I'll tell you now for nothing that I've heard that cough on three women in this house and buried two," and went back to his stool.

* * *

The year ended on the Ides of June, and the Augusta gave her the thing, and it was paper, and it was more than she had asked for on a mole or a wall or a last clear morning with the sea in the glass, and she did not weep, because she had decided.

The room was empty. That was the first thing; the door at the end opened and Marcia stood in it and said to the benches, "The Augusta thanks you and is engaged," as she did, and the room stood and bowed and went, the lady with it, with a look back at her girl that was a question and a permission at once; and Marcia said to the girl by the wall, "Not you," and the room emptied. She went through the door with the box of pins into a room with one woman in it, on a couch, sitting up, with no cloth and no book and no Spaniard and no daughter and no Matidia, and the window open on the June morning and the whole city below it going about its noise.

"Sit," said the Augusta. "There. The stool. Sabina's. She'll not mind; she's not here, and if she were she'd mind everything, so it's the same."

She sat on Sabina's stool. She had never sat in the room. She put the box of pins on her knees and her hands on the box and her face did nothing and her feet were under her.

"A year," the Augusta said. "Twenty-four mornings. You've not said a word in this room since you told me I'd chosen a man and didn't love him and that was why it would work, which was true, and which I'd have had you drowned for if you'd said it twice. You've done my hair the way my mother had it. You've worn Tertulla's son's ivory where I'd see it. You've been asked about the grain by Marcia with a pin and you've said nothing to her and nothing to Tertulla but the fact of the asking, which Tertulla told me, because Tertulla tells me everything she thinks I don't know in order to find out which things I do." The plain face that showed exactly what it decided to was deciding, Fausta saw, to show her something again, and this time it was not the thing from a year ago, the thing that was not kindness; it was something she had not seen on it, and did not have a word for, and would decide, much later, was the nearest the woman ever came to being tired. "And Sabina's spoken to you, and you've not told anyone what she said, and I know what she said, because she says it to everyone she thinks can't repeat it and they all can, and you haven't. So. You've held your tongue in the one room in Rome where it's hardest and you've done it for a year and I said I'd give you a thing, and here it is." She reached beside her on the couch and took up a sheet, folded, with a seal on it that Fausta did not know and would come to know better than her own, and held it out. "Read it. Then I'll tell you what it is, because what it says and what it is are not the same, and you're a girl who's known that since a slate."

She took it. She unfolded it. It was a copy, in a clerk's hand, of a thing she had by heart.

*Fausta, daughter of Hilara, freedwoman of Lucius Laecanius Bassus, born free in the house by the harbour at Puteoli on the fourth day before the Ides of May, in the consulship of —* and the names, and the witnesses, the Rhodian's father and the egg-woman's husband. The Puteoli magistrate's seal drawn in, and under it, in another hand, a line: *Exemplified from the register of the basilica at Puteoli at the instance of the Augusta and lodged in the archive of the prefect of the city, this day,* and a date, which was the Kalends of June, and a seal, which was the prefect's.

She read it twice. She looked up.

"That's what it says," said the Augusta. "What it *is*, is this. There's a steward on the coast who asked you a question in front of a praetor's man at a funeral so that it would be on a sheet. I had that from Marcia who had it from Tertulla's dwarf who had it from you, in a litter, on the way down the Caelian. He asked it because his master owes a shipowner a villa and he needed a thing the shipowner's son would pay for, and he'd worked out that you were it, and he was right about that and wrong about how. His sheet says a question was asked. It doesn't say what the answer was. A sheet like that sits in a box till a man with a reason picks it up, and the reason comes when the shipowner's son is something and the steward is old and wants a thing from him, and on that day the steward goes to Puteoli and finds an egg-seller's husband who's sixty and drinks and asks him, with a coin in his hand, whether he remembers signing anything. The egg-seller's husband remembers whatever the coin says." The plain face did not move. "Somebody's been paying the egg-seller's husband a quarter for nothing since February of last year. I know that too. It's a good move and it's a boy's move; it buys a man's memory till the next coin. This —" she nodded at the sheet — "buys the register. The register's been copied. The copy's in the prefect's archive under my seal. Nobody counts a thing I've filed; it's the one use of a headache. The steward can go to Puteoli with every coin on the coast and the egg-man can forget his own name and it won't matter, because the paper's not in Puteoli any more. It's here. On the hill. Mine." She sat back. "That's the thing. You asked a boy for the egg-man and he gave you the egg-man. I've given you the hill. Which is what a woman gives, when she's got it to give. We don't pay quarters. We move the box."

Fausta sat on Sabina's stool with the sheet in her two hands and the box of pins under it and the ivory in her hair with the gold on the break and the paper under the hearth-stone against her skin, which she had carried for four years and had by heart and had not burned and did not, now, need, because the thing it said had been said again by someone nobody would ask *why did you think you'd need it*; and she felt — she would be honest, later, in another room, to a woman with a comb who laughed — she felt her feet, and under her feet, for the first time since a shutter that would not bar, the floor.

"Augusta," she said. It was the first word in a year. "What does it cost?"

The woman on the couch looked at her for a long moment with the face that showed what it decided to, and the thing on it that was nearly tiredness went, and the other thing came, the thing from a year ago, and it was not kindness.

"Good," she said. "Good. That's the right first word. Tertulla said you'd say *thank you* and I said no, that one'll ask the price, she was raised in a lodging-house." She folded her hands. "It costs this. Marcia asked you about the grain with a pin and you said nothing. In a month, she'll ask you again, without the pin, and you'll say what you know. All of it. The weight and the measure and the sail and the name. And it'll go to the prefect, and to Sura's office, which has the war to pay for and a young man on a stool who counts, and the house with the columns that aren't marble will pay for ten years of water in the Subura's bread, and you'll have done it. The boy on the stool will know you did, because he reads every sheet Sura sets aside and this one Sura will set aside for him to read on purpose, because Sura's cruel in the only way that's any use, which is that he lets a man see the figure." The Augusta did not look away. "That's the price. Your street for your paper. I've had the paper moved already; you've got it in your hands; I'm not a woman who takes a thing back. You could walk out of this room with the hill in your gown and tell Marcia nothing in a month and I'd not have you drowned, because I said I'd give you a thing and I have. But you'd have taken a gift from me and given nothing for it, and you'd know, and I'd know, and Marcia would know. The long game would be over before you'd played a piece, because the one rule on this hill is that nothing's free and the people who pretend it is are the ones who end up in Laurentum with the good air." She lay back, finally, and reached for the cloth. "Go and think. You've a month. It's more than most get. I had a night, with Nerva's letter, and a husband in Cologne who didn't know yet what I'd decided he was."

Fausta stood. She folded the sheet along the clerk's folds and put it in her gown with the Strabo and the letters and the thing she had by heart, and picked up the box of pins, and went to the door, and at the door she stopped, because she was her mother's and there was a thing to be said and it was true and she had decided on a mole never to pretend about what was true, only about what it cost.

"Augusta. The boy on the stool."

"Yes."

"He's never once in his life done a thing by weight that he could do by measure. The sail was his father's. The fourth line of the second column was his father's. He read it to me when he was eleven and looked at me while he did it and I said *all right*." She kept her voice level. "When Sura sets the sheet aside for him, it'll have my name under it, and his father's over it, and he'll read it four ways, because he's been taught, and one of the ways will be true and it won't be the one he picks. I'm telling you so that you know what I'm paying. It's not the street. The street's ash; it's been ash since the Ides of March four years ago. It's him. It's the only paper I had that nobody could burn, and you've asked me to put it in Sura's box with the wax unbroken."

The woman on the couch did not move the cloth.

"Yes," she said, under it. "I know. I knew when I asked. That's why I gave you the month." A breath. "Marcia picked a woman with a headache. You've picked a paper over a boy, or you will, in a month, because you're not a fool and you've felt the floor. It's the right choice. It's the one I'd make. It's the one I *did* make, with a different boy and a different paper, in Nemausus, when I was seventeen, and I've not been sorry a day in thirty years, and I've not been anything else either." The cloth did not move. "Go and do Tertulla's hair. She'll want to know what the thing was. Tell her it was paper. She'll understand; she was raised on it."
