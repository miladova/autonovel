# The Count

Her letter reached him in October, in the mountains, in the rain, and it told him to count the merchant, and he did.

He went down to the pen the same evening. It was a thing he had not done since the counting; he had kept away from it as his father kept away from the Dolphin, by deciding to, and had found that a decision held in the mountains as well as it held on the coast, which was to say exactly until something arrived in a packet. The pen was below the camp in a fold of the hill with a ditch round it and a palisade and two cohorts' worth of guards who hated the duty, and in it were the four hundred and six less the thirty-one who had died of their wounds and the nineteen who had been sent down the river to be sold and the eleven whom nobody could account for and whom the quartermaster's tablets described as *escaped*, which Crescens said was a word that covered a great deal of ground in a camp. So, three hundred and forty-five. He counted them again from the gate, by habit, and got it, and then he did the thing he had not done the first time, which was look at the faces.

The merchant was by the palisade on the east side, where the smoke from the camp fires came down and there was a little warmth in it. He was perhaps fifty. He had a Greek face gone Dacian round the edges — the beard, the way he sat — and he was thinner than he had been in September and had lost the look of a man who expected to be useful; he had the other look now, the pen look, which was the look of a tally-board waiting to be reconciled. He saw the boy at the gate and did not get up.

"You're the one who counts," he said in Greek. "You came back."

"I was told to."

"By whom? Your centurion with the nose?"

"By someone on the coast of Italy who thinks you know the roads." Celer crouched by the palisade so that their eyes were level. He had not planned that; it was a thing he had learned over a chandler's and had not known he had learned. "Do you?"

The man looked at him for a long moment.

"My name is Apollonios," he said. "Of Tomis, on the Black Sea, where the Greeks have been since before your city had walls, and where I was born, and where I had a warehouse until I had one at Sarmizegetusa instead, because the king paid in gold and Tomis paid in fish. I have gone up and down the Olt and the Mureş and the passes between them for thirty years with mules. I know which fords hold in March and which do not. I know where the king's granaries are, the real ones, not the ones he lets you find. I know the names of the men who hold the passes and what each of them was promised and what each of them actually got. Yes. I know the roads." He turned his head and spat, neatly, into the mud. "I told your centurion that. He said everyone knows the roads. He is a soldier; soldiers think a road is a thing you walk on. A road is a thing you *pay* on, boy, every mile of it, to someone, and I know who. That is what I am worth. That is what you were sent to count."

Celer stayed crouched. The rain came down. Behind him the guards talked about a woman in Viminacium.

"If I take you to the quartermaster," he said, "and you're lying, it's my name on the tablet."

"Yes."

"And if I don't, you'll be sold at Viminacium in the spring with the rest for the price of a mule, and the roads go with you."

"Yes." Apollonios looked at him with something that was almost interest. "You've done this sum before."

"I've done every sum before. It's what I'm for." He stood up. His knees cracked; the rain had got into everything. "Tell me one thing I can check. One ford, one granary, one name. Something the quartermaster can send a rider to by tomorrow night. If it's there, I'll bring you up. If it's not, I never came."

The merchant thought. Then he said a name, and a place, and a number — a granary of the king's in a side-valley two days west that the maps did not show, with so many thousand modii in it and a garrison of forty under a man who had been promised land in the Banat and had got a felt cap — and Celer wrote it on his tablet in the rain with his back to the guards, and went up the hill, and gave it to Lucilius, and said nothing about where it had come from.

The rider went. The granary was there. The forty surrendered when they saw the standards, and the man with the felt cap asked, through an interpreter, whether the offer about the Banat was still open.

Lucilius sent for the boy. He was a heavy grey man with ink to the elbows who had been a clerk at Ostia with Hermes thirty years ago and owed him a thing, and he sat in his tent with the granary's figure on the table in front of him and looked at the freedman's son for a long time, and said: "Where?"

Celer told him.

"The Greek in the pen. The one who's been saying for a month that he knows the roads." Lucilius rubbed his face. "Crescens told me. I said everyone knows the roads. I say that about everyone; it's true of most of them." He looked at the figure again. "Bring him up. Put him in your tent; you've one to yourself, God knows why, and he's your find. Feed him. Get everything out of him you can before the snow, and write it down, in Latin, with the places spelled the way the maps spell them, because I'll not have the staff saying they couldn't read it. And boy." Celer waited. "If he runs, or lies, or turns out to have sent word to his king from your tent, I'll not remember whose find he was. Your father's thing from thirty years ago covers a tent. It doesn't cover that. Understand me."

"Yes, sir."

"Good. Then go and get your Greek. And tell Crescens I said he was wrong about the roads. He'll enjoy that; he's not been wrong since Nerva."

* * *

So Apollonios of Tomis lived in his tent for the winter, and taught him the roads.

It was the first thing Celer had ever learned that was not from a Laecanius. He would think about that, afterwards: that he had been taught his letters by his father's borrowed slave and his figures by his father and his Greek by a man who belonged to his patron and the city by the patron's son, and that the first teacher of his life who was nobody's was a prisoner in a pen with a Greek face who owed him his life and knew it and did not, as far as Celer could ever tell, hold it against him.

The merchant talked. He talked the way a man talked who had been three months in a pen with nothing to do but rehearse what he was worth, and he talked in the Greek of the Black Sea, which had Scythian in it and Thracian and the slow vowels of the fishing towns, and Celer wrote it down in Latin with the places spelled as the maps spelled them, and the map of the kingdom in his head grew, over the winter, from a thing with mountains on it to a thing with people. The passes and who held them. The fords and when. The king's granaries, real and false, and the king's gold, which was not where anyone thought, Apollonios said, it was under a river, the king had had the river turned, and nobody who had worked on it was alive, and he knew this because he had sold the mules that carried the dead men's tools. The nobles with the felt caps, the *pileati*, and which of them had been promised what, and which were tired, and which had sons in the Roman pen, and which had sons who had been sent to Rome as hostages after Domitian's peace and had come back speaking Latin and wanting things. And under all of it, in the way the man talked about it, a thing Celer had not expected and did not at first recognise, because he had never heard it from anyone about anywhere: the merchant loved the country. Not the king. The country. He talked about the Mureş in spring the way Crescens talked about a woman in Córdoba, and once, describing a valley above the king's city where there were apple trees that had been planted by somebody's grandfather and were still bearing, he stopped, and looked at the tent-wall, and said in the ordinary voice, *they'll cut those for the siege-works, your people, when they get there; they cut everything*, and went on with the ford.

Celer wrote it all down. He did not write that.

"You don't ask questions," Apollonios said once, in December, with the snow coming down outside and the brazier between them. "You write. I've had Romans ask me questions for three months and they ask like men hitting a wall to see if it's hollow. You just write. Why?"

"Because if I ask, you'll tell me what I asked. If I don't, you'll tell me what you know." Celer did not look up from the tablet. "It's how my father counts a warehouse. You don't ask the man in the aisle how many jars. You read his board and watch his hand."

"And what does my hand say?"

Celer looked up then. The merchant was holding out his hands to the brazier, both of them, with the fingers spread, and they were a merchant's hands, soft once, and the three months had taken the soft off them, and they shook, slightly, all the time now, which they had not in September.

"It says you're afraid of the snow," Celer said. "Not of us. Of the snow. You've started talking about the river freezing. You say it every day and you don't know you're saying it."

Apollonios took his hands back. He looked at the boy across the brazier for a long time, and then he said, quietly, in the Black Sea Greek: "Yes. I'm afraid of the snow. Because when the river freezes the king will cross it. He's done it before. He did it under Domitian, when the Romans thought a river was a wall. He'll come across in the dark with the Roxolani, the horse-people, the ones with the scale coats, and he'll not come here, to the mountains, where the army is; he'll go where the army isn't. He'll go to the depots." He nodded toward the tent-wall, south, toward the river. "I've told your quartermaster. I told him in November. He said: the depots are garrisoned. I said: so was the granary with the forty men and the felt cap. He wrote it down. He writes everything down, your people; it's how you don't have to think." He put his hands between his knees to still them. "Write that, if you like. *The Greek says the king will cross on the ice.* Then when it happens there'll be a tablet with it on."

Celer wrote it. He put it at the top of the day's sheet, in the clear Latin, with the date; and he took the sheet to Lucilius, who read it, and sighed, and put it on a pile.

* * *

The king crossed on the ice in the second week of January, in the dark, with the Roxolani, and went where the army was not.

Celer was not there. He would carry that for a long time in a particular pocket, next to the stair at the Dolphin: that the thing he had been warned of and had written down and had carried up a hill to the man whose job it was, happened eighty miles from where he was sitting with a brazier and a Greek, and that he heard of it, as he heard of everything, as a list. The news came up from the river on the fourth day by a rider who had killed two horses: the depots at the river were burned, three of them, and the fourth was holding, and the legate of the lower province was dead or taken, and the Roxolani were across in strength and moving east along the bank toward the towns, and the emperor was to come down out of the mountains at once with whatever could march on ice, and Viminacium was —

"Standing," Lucilius said, when Celer got to the tent. The grey man was already in his cloak with the tablets being boxed around him. "Viminacium's standing. The new depot's gone, the one we built in the autumn on the far bank. The old one in the town's behind walls and it's holding; it was built for Domitian's war and they built it right, God rot them, it's the only thing they did." He shoved a box at a slave. "We go down tonight. All of it. The emperor's taking the Guard and the cavalry across the pass on foot in the dark, which is a thing nobody's done, and the Seventh goes behind with the baggage, and you go with the Seventh with every tablet in this tent, because when this is over the staff is going to want to know who knew what and when, and I want to be the man with the sheets." He stopped. He looked at the boy. "Your Greek."

"Sir."

"He said January. In November. It's on a sheet with the date." Lucilius's face did a thing that Celer had seen his father's do over a tally that would not reconcile. "And he's been in your tent since October with a brazier and all the roads in his head, and the king crossed exactly where the roads say the ice is thickest, and went straight to the new depot, which is not on any map the king could have had, because it was built in the autumn after the pen was full." He let that sit. "I'm not saying he did it. I'm saying that's what the sheet will look like to a man on the staff who wasn't here, and the man on the staff will want a name, and I've told you already whose name it won't be."

Celer stood in the tent with the slaves boxing the tablets round him and the snow coming in at the flap and the cold going through him that had nothing to do with the snow.

"He couldn't have," he said. "He's been in the tent. There's a guard. He's not had a word with anyone but me and the—"

"And the guards, who go into Viminacium on their days, and drink, and talk, and the women they talk to have brothers across the river, and that's how a depot that's not on a map gets to be known on the ice." Lucilius pulled his cloak straight. "I don't say he did. I don't think he did; I think he's a frightened Greek who knows the roads and told us the truth and we were too busy writing it down to read it. But I've been a clerk forty years and I know what a sheet looks like when it wants a name on it, and that one wants his or it wants mine. Crescens is taking the pen down tonight. Ask him what he's doing with it. Then do what he says. He's a soldier; he knows what's done with a thing you can't carry."

* * *

Crescens was at the pen with two centuries and torches and the snow coming down on all of it, and the three hundred and forty-five, less the winter, were being got into a column, and the ones who could not walk were being got to one side, and Celer, coming down the hill with the quartermaster's words in him, saw what was being done with them before he was close enough to hear it, and stopped, and made himself go on.

"The boy who counts," Crescens said. He had a torch in one hand and nothing in the other, and the broken nose was red with cold, and his voice was the cart on gravel. "Good. Count them. The walking ones. I want a figure for the sheet and I want it from you, because the staff believes your figures and they don't believe mine." He did not look at the other side of the pen, where the ones who could not walk were, and where the sounds were. "Two hundred and ninety, I make it. Give me the real one."

Celer counted. He got two hundred and eighty-eight. He said so. Crescens nodded and said it to his optio, who wrote it, and then the centurion turned, with the torch, and looked at the boy, and said: "Your Greek."

"He's in my tent. He's — Lucilius says he's to be —"

"Lucilius says to ask me what's done with a thing you can't carry." Crescens's face did not move. "He's told you. He's told you the way clerks tell things, by telling you where to go to be told. All right. I'll tell you straight, because I'm not a clerk and I've got two centuries in the snow. We're marching tonight across a pass on ice with the baggage and two hundred and eighty-eight prisoners who can walk and we're leaving the rest. Your Greek can walk. But your Greek's name is on a sheet that says he knew when the ice would hold, and in three weeks when the emperor's got the Roxolani back across the river and the staff sits down with the tablets, somebody is going to pick up that sheet, and the quartermaster who wrote *garrisoned* under it in November is going to need the Greek to be the reason, and the Greek will be. He'll be the reason whether he walks across the pass or not. And if he walks across it, he'll be the reason in front of the emperor, in a camp, with questions, and the questions will go to the tent he was kept in and the boy who kept him." He moved the torch. "Or he doesn't walk across it. And the sheet's got a name on it that's nobody's problem, and the quartermaster's covered and so are you, and there's one fewer Dacian who knows where the gold is. That's the sum. I've done it. Lucilius has done it. Now you do it, because he's yours, and in this army what's yours you see to."

The snow came down. Over on the other side of the pen the sounds had stopped.

"I brought him up," Celer said. His voice was steady. He noted it. "He told the truth. Every road he gave us was there. The granary was there."

"Yes."

"And you want me to—"

"I don't want anything. I want to get two centuries over a pass. I'm telling you what's done." Crescens reached into his belt and brought out a thing and held it out, and it was the knife, the ordinary one, the soldier's, that he had given the boy in April across the river and that Celer had worn ever since and had, he realised, left in the tent that morning on the table beside the brazier, next to the tablets, where a Greek could have picked it up at any time in three months and had not. "You left this. I took it off your table when I went to look at him. He was asleep. He's still asleep, probably; he sleeps in the day now, he's frightened of the nights." The centurion put the knife in his hand and closed his fingers over it with his own, which were very cold, the way Thallus had closed them over the denarius. "You can give it to me and I'll do it and you'll have had it done for you, and you'll be a boy who had a thing done for him, and I'll know it and so will you. Or you can do it, and be a man who saw to what was his, and that'll be known too. Nobody will make you. Nobody in this army makes anyone do anything; that's not how it works, you've been here long enough to see that. They just tell you what's done. And then they watch what you do."

He stood in the snow with the knife in his hand.

He thought of his father saying *be useful; be the one in the tent*. He thought of Lucilius saying *whose name it won't be*. He thought of a girl on a step saying *count the merchant too*, and of the merchant's hands held out to the brazier, shaking, and *they'll cut those for the siege-works*, and *you don't ask questions, you write*. He thought, very clearly and from a long way off, that he was fourteen years old and that he had been sent to a war to hold a tablet and that there was a tablet, somewhere, in a box going down a hill in the snow, with a sheet in it in his own hand that said *the Greek says the king will cross on the ice*, dated, and that he had written it to be safe and that it had made him the opposite, and that this was the thing his father had never told him about counting: that the figure you wrote down to cover yourself was the figure that, in the end, wanted a name.

"He's asleep," he said.

"He's asleep."

"Then he'll not—" He could not finish it. He did not need to. Crescens's face, in the torchlight, did a thing that was not kindness and was not its opposite, and the centurion said, quietly, for the first time without the gravel in it: "No. He'll not. That's the one mercy there is in it, boy, and you'll be the one giving it, and you'll be the only one who knows you did. Go on. We march at the second watch. Be at the baggage with the tablets."

* * *

He went up the hill. He went into the tent. The brazier was down to a glow and the Greek was asleep on the pallet by it on his side with his hands between his knees and his mouth a little open and the beard gone grey at the edges in a way it had not been in September, and he did not wake when the flap moved, because he slept in the day now; he was frightened of the nights.

Celer stood over him for a time that he did not measure, with the knife.

He would not afterwards be able to say what he thought. He would be able to say what he saw, because he had decided to look at everything and he looked: the pallet, the hands, the grey, the mouth, the sheet of the last day's roads still on the table with the Latin place-names spelled the way the maps spelled them. He would be able to say that it was not hard, in the way of the body — Crescens had shown him, once, in April, on a straw target, where, and the merchant did not wake, and it was quick, as the centurion had said, and the sound was less than he had thought and the rest was more. He would be able to say that he did not look away. He would be able to say that when it was done he stood for a moment longer and then did the thing that was in him deeper than anything, deeper than the lamp over the chandler's or the mole or the stair, which was that he went to the table and picked up the day's sheet and read it through, the last of the roads, and folded it, and put it in his tunic with the others, because it was true and had been paid for and would be wanted.

Then he wiped the knife on the pallet and put it in his belt and boxed the tablets and went down to the baggage.

He did not write to her. Not then; not in February, from Viminacium, behind the walls that had been built for Domitian's war, with the Roxolani being driven back across a river that was breaking up under them so that you could stand on the wall and watch men in scale armour go through the ice like coins into water and not come up; not in March, when the emperor came back from the eastern bank with the title of the river on him and the army cheered him in a voice that had the whole winter in it, and he tore no cloak, because there was linen now, and the men cheered that too. He did not write because she had said *count the merchant*, and he had, and she had said *I'll work out the true part*, and he had decided, on the hill in the snow with the knife, that she would not; that this was the first thing he had that would not go in a packet, not as a list and not as anything, that it would go where the thing on the mole had gone, into the column that did not balance, and stay there, and that he would carry it north and south and for the rest of his life as a man carried a knife he had been given and had not needed, which was to say every day and close to the skin and never once taken out to be looked at by anyone.

Lucilius sent for him in April, when the river was open again and the army was going back up into the mountains for the second year, and said, without preamble, holding a tablet: "The staff's read the sheets."

"Sir."

"All of them. The depot, the granary, the roads. The one from November about the ice, with the date." The grey man did not look up. "There's a note in the margin in Sura's hand. It says: *the Greek?* And under it, in the hand of the man who took the sheets up to him, who is that secretary with the ring: *died in the pen in the January withdrawal; see the centurion's count.* Two hundred and eighty-eight. Your figure." He turned the tablet over. "And under that, in Sura's hand again: *the boy who wrote the roads — send him up. L.S.*" He held it out. "You're wanted on the staff. Not Rufus's. Sura's. The man who made this emperor wants the freedman's son who counts, and I'm to send you with the sheets and a letter, and I've written the letter, and it says you're honest and you're quick and you were nowhere near the pen in January, which is true, because I had you at the baggage; I made sure of it." He looked up then, finally, with the clerk's eyes that had been forty years at this. "Your father's thing from thirty years ago. You've never asked."

"No, sir."

"It was this. At Ostia, in the year Vespasian died, I made a mistake in a grain tally that would have cost a man his name, and your father, who was the junior clerk in the room and had nothing to gain, took the sheet off the pile and rewrote it in the night with the figure I should have put, and never spoke of it, and let me think for ten years he'd forgotten. That's the thing. That's what covered your tent." Lucilius put the tablet in his hand. "I'm telling you because you've just done the same, in a way, for me, and I'd not have you think it was free. It wasn't free for him and it's not for you. He's carried mine thirty years and I've carried knowing he has. Now you've got one of yours. Go up to Sura. Count his things. And boy—" Celer waited. "Don't ever write the date on a sheet you'd not want read back to you in front of the emperor. That's the only lesson in this war, and you've had it, and it's cost one Greek, which is cheap. I've known it cost whole cohorts."

He went up to Sura with the sheets. The man with the ring took them from him at the door of the staff tent and looked at him, once, with the look of a man who noticed who noticed, and said, "Hermes's boy. Yes. The roads. He'll want you in the mornings, early; he reads before the emperor's up. Don't speak unless he does. Don't write anything down that he hasn't told you to. And don't bring the knife into the tent; nobody carries iron near him, it's not a rule, it's just not done."

Celer took the knife out of his belt and gave it to the man with the ring, who looked at it, and at him, and put it on a shelf by the door with a dozen others, and he never saw it again, and found that he did not need to. He had the weight of it. That was the thing about a denarius, his father had said, on the Capua road, in another life; you kept it, not for what it bought, but so that you would remember you had been paid.
