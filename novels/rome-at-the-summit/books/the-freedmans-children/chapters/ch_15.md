# The Witness

She knew the patron was dead an hour before his cousin did, and spent the hour deciding what the hour was worth.

It was Marcia who told her, on the Kalends of February, in the outer room, with the comb. The door at the end was shut that morning; the Augusta had the head, and was not receiving, and the benches sat and talked across the room at each other in the dry voices and did not look at the door, and Fausta stood by the wall with the jug and the box of pins that was hers now, and the freedwoman came down the long wall, as she did, and lifted two fingers to a pin, and said to it, in the Nemausus Latin: "Laecanius Bassus died in the night. In his bed, on the Caelian, of the water in his legs. The priests of the deified Titus were sent for at the third watch because he held their flamen's place and the place has to be filled before the Ides, and the priests' secretary sent up here at dawn because the Augusta likes to know when a priesthood's empty before the Senate starts wanting it. His own house hasn't sent to the widow yet. His son's in the house. His son's steward's in the house. They're reading the will." The fingers moved. "I'm telling you because you've his nose and you'll want your face arranged, and because the widow will have it from her own people within the hour, and I want to know what you'll do with the hour. Do what you like with it."

Fausta stood against the wall and looked at the jug.

She thought — fast, the slate way — about what the hour was. It was a thing the empress's freedwoman had given her that the empress's freedwoman had not needed to give, which meant it was a test, and the test was not whether she would tell the lady; it was *how*. She could go to the bench now, with the box of pins, and touch a pin that was not loose at the back of the lady's four-years-old hair and say it to the pin, and the lady would have it before the room did and before her own house did, and the room would see the Puteoli girl do it and would know, by the Ides, that the widow's hair-girl got things from the empress's hair-woman, which was true and which nobody yet said. Or she could stand against the wall for the hour and let the lady's man come up the hill with it and have the lady hear it in front of five women in a public voice, and the lady would have been the last on her own bench to know that her cousin was dead, and would know afterwards, from Momus, that the girl had known, and had stood.

She went to the bench. She did not hurry. She lifted two fingers to a pin and said to it, very low: "Domina. Your cousin died in the night. The priests have it; the Augusta has it; the house hasn't sent. They're reading the will. Rufus is there, and Thallus."

The lady did not move. The shoulders did not move. The dry carrying voice went on, to the consular's wife beside her, about a dinner, for the length of a sentence, and finished the sentence, and then the lady said in the same voice, not lower, so that the whole of the bench and half the other heard it: "My cousin Bassus is dead. Marcia's just had it from the priests and been kind enough to send it down the wall to me by my girl, which is more than my cousin's own son has thought to do. Fausta, you'll go down to the house with Momus and find out what's in the will before Thallus has finished improving it. I'll stay; the Augusta may want to know who she's giving the Titus priesthood to and I'd like to be in the room when she asks." A pause. "And child. You've just told the whole of this room where you get your news. On purpose, I think, since you could have whispered. Yes?"

"Yes, domina."

"Good. It's time they knew. Go on."

She went, with Momus. Going down the long wall past the door at the end, Marcia was standing by it with the comb, and did not look at her, and said to the comb as she passed: "You told her so the room would hear. That's not what I'd have done. It's what *she'd* have done, at your age, before she learned to whisper." A breath. "Which means you've read her better than I have, and I've had her since she was a bride. Go and read the will."

* * *

The house of Laecanius had its door open, which it never did, and the atrium had the pool covered and the masks out of their cupboards, all twelve of them, the wax faces set up on stands round the walls so that the consul under Nero and the praetor under Claudius and the rest looked down at the bier in the middle with the expression of people who had seen it done before; and on the bier, under a purple cloth, with his feet to the door and coins on his eyes and his fine white hair combed, was the man who had stood in a square of sun and said *my people*, and who had looked along a row of girls with a lamp once and stopped, and who was, by every arithmetic she had ever done on a stair or a wall or a fishing boat, her father, and whom she had never once in seventeen years spoken to.

She stood at the edge of the atrium behind Momus and looked at him. She looked at the nose. It was hers. It was more hers dead than it had been alive, because the softness had gone out of the face in the night and left the bone, and the bone was the bone she saw in the bronze; and she waited to feel the thing she had been told she would feel, by Antiope, by the eleven girls, by the poets the lady's Greek read aloud, and it was like the morning of the linen: there was nothing. There was a dead man on a bier who had put her mother on a ship. There was a face that had stopped at a row. She thought, *yes, and?*, and found that it was not defiance, this time; it was simply the sum, done, with nothing left over.

"Don't," Momus said, very low, at her elbow, not looking at her. "Don't go looking for it. I did, at my old master's. Stood by the bier for an hour waiting to feel something about the man who'd bought me at nine and I felt my feet. That's all there is for people like us. Feet." He nodded, barely, toward the tablinum. "They're in there. The son and the steward and a man from the praetor's office with the will. Walk behind me. Nobody looks at what's behind a dwarf."

They were in there. Rufus, on his father's chair, which he had never sat in and sat in now as though it had been waiting, in a dark tunic with his hair unoiled and the boxer's shoulders gone soft in four years of Rome and a look on his face that she had seen on Antiope coming down from the villa: *he's not cruel; he's a man who's been told what he's worth*. Thallus, standing, at his shoulder, where he always stood, with his hands folded and the bald head shining, reading over the praetor's man's arm. And the praetor's man, a freedman with a bad cough, unrolling, by the lamp, a thing that had been in a box behind a lock since before she was born.

She did not hear it read. She was behind a dwarf in a doorway; she heard the shape of it, the way she had heard the house at the Dolphin from under the stairs, by its silences. The estate to Rufus, entire, with the debts. The Baian villa to Rufus. The Falernian land — a bequest to the Spaniard son-in-law, which made Thallus's hands tighten on each other. A sum to the priests of Titus. Sums to the freedmen, by name, the Roman household first, in order of their usefulness, each with the degree of thanks their usefulness had earned, so that she could have told, standing in the doorway, which of them had been loved and which merely kept. The manumission of eleven slaves, by name, Eutyches at the head of them, who had been promised it for thirty years and now had it from a dead hand and would not, she thought, be grateful. And then the coast freedmen, briefly, as a list; and then —

"*To Hilara, my freedwoman, of Puteoli,*" the praetor's man read, in the cough-voice, "*the house by the harbour at the sign of the Dolphin, in which she lives, for her life, and twenty thousand sesterces, in gratitude for her long service. Should she have predeceased me, the house to revert to the estate and the sum to lapse.*" He coughed. "The steward informs me she has predeceased."

"She has," said Thallus. "Three years since."

"Then it lapses. Next: *to Nicias, my Greek—*"

And that was all. That was the paper in the box behind the lock that her mother had talked about on a wall at dusk and on the last clear morning with the sea in the glass; that was the thing that could be taken out and put in a fire by any man who held the key; and it had not been burned, it had been kept, and read out, in front of witnesses, with a praetor's man's cough, and it said *long service* and *twenty thousand* and *lapse*, and her mother had been three years in the ground with a coin in the goddess's saucer from the man who was standing at the dead man's son's shoulder with his hands folded, and the twenty thousand lapsed, and the house had a Greek woman in it, and the paper had been true all along, and it did not matter, because the one thing a paper could not do was arrive in time.

She stood in the doorway behind the dwarf and did not move. Her face did nothing. She felt her feet.

Then Thallus looked up.

He looked up from the praetor's man's arm, across the tablinum, past Rufus's chair, to the doorway, where there was a dwarf of the widow Tertulla's and, behind the dwarf, a girl with a box of pins at her belt; and his face did what it always did, which was nothing, and his eyes went still the way she had watched them go in a common room over a slate, which was go down a column feeling for the place where the hand changed; and he said, in the ordinary voice, to the room: "The widow's girl is here. The lady Cornelia will want to know what's in it. She may tell her it's what was expected." And then, to the girl, directly, which he had never done in front of anyone of rank: "Your mother's remembered. You heard. It lapses. The master's generosity was real and his timing was his own, as it always was." The small hands did not move. "There's a thing I've never asked you, in all the years of sums. I'll ask it now, since the master's dead and the house is the young master's and I'm the young master's steward with the young master's debts to think of, and a steward with debts to think of has to count everything in the house, even the things that have gone to the Caelian. Your birth-year. Your mother gave one for the lease. Was it before the ship, or after?"

The room was quiet. Rufus, in the chair, looked up for the first time, at the girl in the doorway, with the pricing look, and she watched him see the nose, and watched him do the arithmetic, which he had never had occasion to do, and watched him arrive, and watched something go across the soft handsome face that was not kindness and was not its opposite and was, she thought, simply a man discovering that his father's house had one more item in it than the will had listed.

"After, sir," she said. "By six months. It's registered at the basilica in Puteoli. With witnesses."

"Is it." Thallus inclined his head, the width of a stylus. "Good. Then there's nothing to count. I only like to know where the money's going." He turned back to the praetor's man. "Go on. The Greek. He's dead too, but the master would have wanted it read."

She went out behind the dwarf. In the atrium she passed the bier, and the nose, and the twelve wax faces, and she did not look at any of it, because she had decided, and because her feet were the only thing she could feel and she needed them for the street.

* * *

Celer was in the street.

He was coming up it from the Aventine side with his toga on and Sura's steward's boy behind him, because Sura had sent a man of his house to stand at a consular's bier for the length of a cup of water, which was the degree of attention a dead flamen of Titus with eight hundred thousand in debts earned from the man who made emperors; and the man Sura had sent was the dead man's freedman's son, because Sura read four ways and one of them was *let the Laecanii see what they sold*. He saw her come out of the door behind Momus. He stopped. She stopped. They were twenty feet apart in a street of blind walls on the Caelian in February with a dead man between them in a house and a dwarf and a steward's boy watching, and neither of them had looked at the other since a room with three couches two years ago and a door, and he had not answered the third letter.

She looked at him. The proper look. It cost nothing.

He had grown again. He had the ring — not yet; the roll was in the spring, Vibia had written; but he had the look of a man who had been told he would have it, which was a different thing from Rufus's look, because Rufus had been told what he was worth and this one had been told what he had bought — and he had something else on him that she could not name and that she would see again, years on, on other men, and would learn was what the Palatine did to a face when the face had been near it long enough: a stillness that was not Thallus's and not the lady's but was the thing both of them had been practising toward.

"The will's read," she said, across the twenty feet, in the eggs voice, so that the steward's boy would hear a servant reporting. "My mother's remembered. The house for her life and twenty thousand. It lapses."

He did not move. "Yes," he said. "It would."

"Thallus asked my birth-year. In front of Rufus."

A pause; the length of a breath. "What did you say?"

"The truth. After. Registered. With witnesses." She watched his face, which did nothing, perfectly, and she looked under the nothing, as she had at the foot of the third couch, and found — she was not sure. For the first time since a yard and a cake she looked under and was not sure what was there; it was as if the column had been closed and the box sealed and she was looking at the wax. "You didn't answer the third."

"No."

"Your father?"

"Yes. And Sura. And the roll." He said it the way he had said *the contract's by weight*: a fact, laid between them, for her to pick up. "It's a year. He said a year."

"And after the year?"

He looked at her for a long moment, across the street, with the dead man's door open behind her and the dwarf and the steward's boy and the February light. And he said, in Greek, very low, so that it went no further than it was meant to: "After the year I'll have a ring and a stone with an *F* on it and four hundred thousand in Falernian vines I've never seen, and you'll have the Augusta's hair and the widow and the room, and Thallus will have Rufus and a question about a birth-year that he's asked once in front of a praetor's man so that it's on a sheet somewhere. That's after the year. I don't know what's after that. I've stopped — " He stopped. "I've stopped doing that sum. It doesn't come out."

She stood with the box of pins at her belt and the paper under the hearth-stone against her skin.

"No," she said. "It wouldn't." And then, because it was both, and because he had said *stopped* and she had heard what it cost: "Go and stand by the bier. Sura sent you. Stand for a cup of water and look at the nose and come away. It's all any of us can do for him." She turned to go, and turned back. "Celer. The witnesses. At the basilica. One's dead. The other's the egg-woman's husband, and he's sixty, and he drinks, and Thallus has the Dolphin. If you've a hand on the coast that's not Thallus's — "

"Vibia."

"Vibia. Tell her the egg-woman's husband is to be paid, by someone, something, every quarter, for nothing, so that when a steward comes to ask him a question he's a man with a reason to remember the answer." She heard her mother in it and let it stand. "That's not for the widow. That's for me. It's the first thing I've asked you for me since the mole. You can not answer it. You're good at that."

He did not say anything. He looked at her, and then he nodded, once, the way the steward had on the harbour steps when she was ten, ticking a thing on a list; and he went past her, at the civil distance, into the dead man's house to stand for a cup of water, and she went down the Caelian behind a dwarf, and at the bottom of the street Momus said, without turning his head, in the voice that went no further than it was meant to: "You asked him for the one thing that matters and made it sound like eggs. Your mother did that. She asked the old master for a lease and made it sound like oil." The dwarf's short legs went on. "He'll do it. He's the kind that does the thing you ask and not the thing you want, and you've just learned to ask for the right one. That's the whole of men, girl. Took me sixty years and a bath-water carrier."

* * *

Phaedrus taught her the hand in May, in the steward's room, at the table where he kept the house's book, because the lady had said *the child writes like a lodging-house and I'll not have a lodging-house writing to Sura's office under my name*.

"Not your mother's. Your mother's is a good hand for a slate; it's fast and it's true and it looks like a woman who's been owed money. That's not what a consul's widow's letters look like." The Alexandrian laid a sheet in front of her and a reed and sat on the other side of the table with his own, and wrote, slowly, so she could see the reed go, *the lady Cornelia Tertulla asks*, in a Latin that had no haste in it anywhere and no ornament either, the letters upright and even and spaced as if each had been paid for separately. "That. The lady's house has written like that since her father, because her father had a Greek who'd been a copyist in Pergamum and the Greek taught the lady and the lady taught me and I'm teaching you, and in thirty years you'll teach someone, and the hand will go on after all of us, which is what a hand's for." He turned the sheet. "Again. Slower. You're making the *a* as if it owed you a quarter's rent."

She made the *a* again. It took a month. She wrote the lady's Latin in the steward's room every afternoon the lady rested, until the reed went where the hand wanted without her watching it, and she found, somewhere in the third week, that the hand had changed the Latin as well; that a thing written slowly in letters that had each been paid for came out as a different sentence from the same thing on a slate, not because the words moved but because the hand would not let her hurry past what they cost. She wrote *the lady asks* and it meant *the lady asks*; and underneath, in the same hand, when the lady's request was done, she found that she could write a line of her own that said what the asking was for, in the lady's letters, and that the letters made the line a thing a man on a stool across the city would have to read slowly too.

"There," said Phaedrus, on the last day, looking at a sheet. "That's the house. That's thirty years of not declaring anything, in a hand. Nobody reading that will know a lodging-house girl wrote it." He put it down. "And Fausta. The line at the foot. The one that's yours. Keep it to one, two at most. A line in that hand under the lady's request is a thing a man will read twice, because it costs him something to read it at the pace the hand insists on, and a man who's read a line twice has been *told* something, whether or not you meant to tell him. You'll mean to. I can see it in the *a*. Mean it carefully."

* * *

Marcia gave her the long game in June, on the Ides, through the door, with the empress on the couch and no cloth over her eyes.

It was the first time she had been in the room when the Augusta was not ill. She had gone through the door with the comb perhaps twenty times by then and the woman on the couch had always had the head, or had said she had, and Fausta had begun to understand, somewhere in the spring, that the head was a thing the Augusta had when she wished to receive someone without receiving them, and that the cloth was a door, and that she, Fausta, had been let through it by being a girl who did hair and did not exist. On the Ides of June the cloth was off. The Augusta was sitting up, in the plain gown, with the plain face that was not plain at all when you were three paces from it, because it was a face that had decided a long time ago exactly how much of itself to show and showed precisely that; and Matidia was in the room on her stool with her book, and did not turn a page; and Marcia was at the Augusta's shoulder with the comb; and the Spaniard was there.

He was sitting on the end of the Augusta's couch in a toga with the tribune's stripe, which he had worn since December, with his beard and his shoulders and a scroll in his hand that he was not reading, and he was talking to the Augusta in Greek about a poem, and the Augusta was listening with the look of a woman who had heard a great many poems from this man and intended to hear a great many more; and Fausta stood by the wall with the box of pins and did her work, which was to be furniture with hands, and did not look, and saw.

She saw what she had seen from the roof. That was the thing; it was the roof again, five years on, from the inside. The Augusta was looking at the Spaniard the way she had looked at the road, walking into Rome: straight ahead, at a thing that had been arranged for her to walk through and that she had agreed to. Not love. The eleven girls would have said love and been wrong, as they were wrong about modesty. It was *work*. It was a woman of fifty with no children and a husband who thought about the Danube, looking at the only man in the family under forty, and having decided, somewhere, in a room, perhaps this one, that this was the one, and that it was her business to see that he was the kind of man who deserved it, since nobody else on the hill was going to make him so; and the Spaniard knew it, and read her his poem, and was, Fausta thought, three paces from him with a pin in her hand, the most owned man in Rome after the emperor, and the only one who had chosen it.

Matidia turned a page. Marcia's comb went through the grey hair. The Spaniard finished the poem and the Augusta said a thing about the metre that made him laugh, the real one, the loud one, and he got up, which took the couch some time, and kissed her hand and went out with the scroll, and did not look at the wall, and the room let out its breath.

"Well," the Augusta said, to the ceiling, with the cloth off. "Tertulla's girl. You've been in this room twenty times and that's the first time you've seen anything in it worth telling her. What will you tell her?"

Fausta did not look up from the pin. "That the tribune reads Greek well, Augusta, and that you corrected his metre."

"That's what you'll tell her. What did you see?"

She thought of the wall at dusk and *I want your face to do nothing*. She thought of a room off a peristyle and *the street's not for sale; the hand is*. She thought that this woman could see through a cloth and had taken it off, and that a lie to her would be the one Phaedrus had warned about, the one about the only person it was not safe to lie to, and that the thing on the couch was not a test of her reading but of what she would do with it, which was the only test there had ever been, from the slate on.

"That you've chosen him, Augusta," she said, to the pin. "And that he knows. And that you don't love him, and he doesn't need you to, and that's why it will work."

The room was very quiet. Matidia did not turn a page.

Then the Augusta laughed. Not the sound under the cloth; the real one, short, dry, a laugh like the lady's shoulders moving but with breath in it, and she said, "Marcia. Did you hear that?"

"I heard it, Augusta."

"Twenty times with a comb and on the twenty-first she tells me the thing you've been forty years not saying. Where did Tertulla find her?"

"Behind a chair, Augusta. Watching Sura."

"Yes. Well. Sura's got a boy who does the same for him, from the same street, I'm told; they breed them on that coast like fish." The Augusta looked at the girl by the wall, finally, directly, with the plain face that showed exactly what it decided to, and it was deciding, Fausta saw, to show her something, and the something was not kindness. "Girl. You've just said a true thing in a room where true things are not said, to a woman who could have you in the Tiber by the Kalends for it, and you said it because you worked out that I'd asked. Good. That's reading. Now here's the other half, which Marcia will teach you, because I haven't the time and she has the patience: a true thing said at the right moment buys you a place in the room. A true thing said at the wrong one costs you your tongue. The art isn't seeing. Every clever slave sees; that's what slaves are for, Nicias will have told you, Tertulla says he had you. The art is *waiting*. I've waited twenty-three years for that man to be the thing I needed him to be, and I've not said so once, and I'll not say so till my husband's dead and perhaps not then. You said it in a breath. You were right. Don't do it again for ten years." She lay back. "Marcia, teach her the long game. She's got the short one. Tertulla's girl, you'll do my hair on the Kalends and the Ides and you'll not speak in this room again until I ask you a question, and I'll not ask you one for a year, and at the end of the year if you've held your tongue I'll give you a thing, and if you haven't I'll give you to Tertulla's son, who I'm told is patient." A pause. "Yes. I know about that too. Everyone on this hill has a son. Go on."

She went on. She went down the long wall behind Marcia with the box of pins and her face doing nothing and her feet under her, and at the door the freedwoman stopped, with the comb, and said to it, in the Nemausus Latin, very low:

"She's given you a year and a threat. That's more than she gave me; she gave me twenty and no threat, because she didn't think I'd need one. You will. You've the short game and it's fast and it's good and it'll get you killed in a house like this before you're twenty-five." The fingers moved a pin. "The long game is this. You've a paper under your gown — don't; I'm not asking what; everyone's got a paper — and a steward on the coast who asked a question in front of a praetor's man so it's on a sheet, and a boy on Sura's stool who nodded when you asked him for a thing and will do it, and a widow who'll sell you to the Palatine the day the price is right and thinks she's doing you a kindness, and a man over the stables who's patient. Five things. None of them's yours. That's the board." The comb went into the box at Marcia's belt. "The long game is to make one of them yours, outright, so that the other four have to go through it. Not the widow; she's never been anyone's. Not the steward. Not the man. The boy, or the paper. Pick one. Then wait ten years and make it true." She opened the door. "I picked a woman from Nemausus with a headache. Took me from a stool to a door. Go and do the widow's hair; she'll want to know what the tribune read."

Fausta went and did the widow's hair. She told the lady the tribune read Greek well and the Augusta corrected his metre. The lady's shoulders moved the width of a stylus and she said, "And?" — the lady's *and*, not her mother's; a woman asking for the rest — and Fausta said, "And nothing, domina. I've been told not to speak in that room for a year," and the lady looked at her in the bronze for a long moment and said, "Have you. By whom?" and Fausta said, "By the room," and the lady said nothing at all, which was praise, and was, Fausta understood, the first time the widow Tertulla had been told by her own girl that there was a thing she would not be given, and had let it stand.

That night, in the dormitory over the bakehouse, with the twelve breathing and the Strabo and the paper against her skin, she took out the last sheet of the Rhodian's papyrus and wrote, under the thing she had not sent: *Five things, none of them mine. Pick one and wait ten years. She said the boy or the paper. I know which I'd pick if picking were a thing I were allowed. I know which one's already stopped doing the sum.* And then she did not write any more, because the long game was waiting, and she had been told, and she was her mother's, and could carry a thing once it was decided; and she put the sheet away with the others, and lay in the dark, and felt her feet.
