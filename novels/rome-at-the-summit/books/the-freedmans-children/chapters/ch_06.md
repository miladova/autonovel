# The Betrothal

Rufus's rooms were on the second floor of his father's house, over the garden, and from the window of the one he gave Celer you could see the whole of the Palatine across the valley, and Celer spent the winter learning that this was not an accident.

Nothing in Rome was an accident. That was the first thing the city taught him, and it taught it the way his father taught, by letting him find out. The rooms were on the garden side because the garden side was quiet and the street side was for clients and the family did not sleep where it received. The window faced the Palatine because the house had been built by a man who wanted to see the emperor's lamps from his bed, and had put the bed accordingly, and every man who had slept in it since had woken to the same view and had taken it, as Celer took it that first grey morning in December with the rain coming down on the valley and the palace standing up out of the murk like a ship, as a kind of instruction.

Rufus was not often in them. Rufus was out. That was what a man of twenty-nine did in Rome in the winter of a new reign when he wanted to be something: he went out at dawn to stand in other men's atria, and he came back at noon to let other men stand in his. He went out again in the afternoon to the baths, where the real business was done, and dined out, and came home at the third watch smelling of Syrian scent and somebody's wine and slept four hours and did it again. He took Celer to all of it. Not as a companion; as a thing that was carried. The boy went behind him with the tablet and the case and stood against walls and was not spoken to, and Rufus, who had said on the terrace at Baiae that a man on a staff needed people who could keep their mouths shut, found out that winter that he had been given one who could also keep his eyes open, and did not, Celer thought, entirely like it.

"You were watching Sura's man," Rufus said once, in the litter, coming back from a morning at the house of a consular on the Quirinal where the whole of the senate's younger half had stood in the rain for two hours to be nodded at. "The secretary. The Greek with the ring."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because he was the only man in the room who wasn't watching Sura."

Rufus was silent for the length of a street. Then he said, "Nicias said you'd no Greek style. He didn't say you'd that." And after another street: "Don't do it where Sura's man can see you doing it. He notices who notices. It's his whole use."

So Celer learned not to be seen noticing, which was a different skill from not noticing and a harder one, and he practised it on the city as his father had made him practise the Baetican ratios, until it came out the same three times. He learned the hills and what lived on each. He learned that the Caelian was old money that had kept its doors shut and the Quirinal was old money that had opened them a crack and the Esquiline was new money and the Aventine was nobody's and the Palatine was the thing all the others were facing. He learned how a letter moved: from the hand that wrote it to the secretary who sealed it to the slave who carried it to the doorkeeper who took it to the steward who read it, because every steward read everything, to the master who was told what was in it by the steward and then read it himself to see what the steward had left out. He learned that Rufus owed, by his own count made in a strong-room that was not his with a lamp that was, three hundred and twelve thousand sesterces to nine men, and that two of the nine were Hermes, under different names. That Rufus knew this, and that Rufus did not know that Celer knew it, and that this was the first thing he had ever held in his hand that could hurt a senator's son, and that he would have given a good deal not to be holding it.

He did not write to the girl. He had said he would and he did not, and he knew every day that he did not, and he told himself that it was because the post went by the house's slaves and the house's slaves were Eutyches's and Eutyches read everything; and this was true; and it was not the reason. The reason was that he did not know what he would say. *I am being carried about Rome behind a man who owes my father money and I am learning to see without being seen and this morning I stood against a wall for two hours and was not spoken to and it was the most important thing that has ever happened to me* — he could not write that. He could not write anything that was true without writing that, and he had learned, from Nicias, that a letter which was not true was a tally-board, and from his father, that the people who could read your papers were the people who could hurt you; and so he wrote nothing. The winter went by, and the rain came down on the Palatine, and he woke every morning to the instruction in the window and took it.

* * *

The family went down to the coast on the Ides of April, and the villa was opened, and the slipped tile was still there; and in June, when the roses were out on the second terrace and the senators' sons had come down for the season and the Dolphin, he supposed, was full, Laecania Paulla was betrothed.

She was seventeen. He had seen her perhaps four times in his life, at the back of rooms: a tall girl with her father's height and her brother's shoulders and a face that had been told from birth it was the family's and had, in consequence, never been asked what it was itself. She was being given to a Spaniard. That was how everyone said it, on the terrace and in the kitchens and in the lower town — *the Spaniard* — though the man had a name, which was Fabius Catullinus, and a family, which was from Baetica and had been in the senate for a generation and was, by every measure that could be made in a strong-room, poorer than the Laecanii and younger and of less account; and had one thing, which was that Fabius Catullinus's mother's brother had served under the emperor on the Rhine and dined with him in the palace twice a month. That in the summer of the second year of Trajan this was worth more than a consul under Nero and eight hundred thousand sesterces of debt.

"It's a good match," Rufus said, on the terrace, the morning the Spaniard's people arrived, with the same flatness he used for sums. "She'll be unhappy and he'll be rich and in ten years my nephew will have an uncle who was consul under Trajan and a grandfather who was consul under Nero and nobody will remember which of them paid for which. That's what a sister's for." He looked at Celer. "Write that down. No. Don't. I said it; that's enough."

The betrothal was done in the atrium with the fish and the masks, before witnesses, with the Spaniard's uncle standing in for the emperor's goodwill the way the masks stood in for the dead, and the girl between her father and her future husband with her eyes on the floor, and the whole of the family's people — the Roman household, the coast freedmen, the Augustales, Hermes at the front in his toga and the Dolphin's woman at the back in her red — ranged round the walls to see it done. The words were said. The ring was put on. The Spaniard kissed the girl on the forehead and she did not move, and Bassus raised his hand in the blessing-or-beckoning and said something about the gods and the house and the future that Celer, standing behind Rufus with the tablet, did not write down because he had stopped listening to Bassus in the winter and had not found a reason to start.

He was listening to Thallus. Thallus was at Bassus's shoulder, where he always was, with his hands folded, and Thallus was not watching the girl or the Spaniard or the masks; Thallus was watching the back of the room, the way a steward did at a sale, counting the house. And Celer, following the steward's eyes because he had learned that winter that the man who was not looking at the thing was the man to look at, saw them go across the freedmen and stop, as the master's had stopped in the atrium two Aprils ago, at the red gown; and then move, a hand's breadth to the left, and stop again.

The girl was there. He had known she would be. He had not looked for her, because he had learned not to be seen looking, and because he had not written, and because it had been eight months. She was standing at her mother's shoulder in the blue that had been let down again and would not let down any further, and she had grown, and her hair was up, and she was looking — not at the ring, not at the girl, not at Bassus. She was looking at Thallus looking at her. Steadily, the way the lady Cornelia had told her, the way you looked at a Spaniard. And Thallus, who noticed who noticed, had noticed, and his face did nothing at all, and his eyes moved on; and Celer, with the tablet against his chest and his heart going in a way he did not care for, thought: *she has got better at it than I have. She has been practising on the stairs.*

* * *

He found her on the stair between the second terrace and the third, in the hour after the ceremony when the house's people were being fed in the garden and the family and the Spaniards were being fed inside and the two feasts had, between them, emptied the staircases; she was sitting on the fourth step with a dish of something she had been sent for and had not taken back, looking out at the bay. She did not turn when he came down, though he had made no noise.

"You've grown," she said.

"So have you."

"I'm twelve. I'm supposed to." She moved the dish so he could sit. He sat, two steps above, so that they were not side by side and so that anyone coming up from the garden would see a clerk resting and a kitchen-girl resting and nothing else; it was the kind of arithmetic they both did now without noticing. "You didn't write."

"No."

"I know why. Eutyches reads everything. The post goes through his hands." She said it evenly, offering it to him, and he understood that she was giving him the reason the way the lady Cornelia had given her the month: as a door he could go through if he chose.

He did not go through it. He found that he could not, with her; he had spent a winter learning to be a face and the thing would not work on her, had never worked on her, and he was tired, suddenly, in a way that had nothing to do with the hour. "That's not why."

"No," she agreed. "It's not." She looked at the bay. "You didn't know what to say."

"I didn't know what was true."

She was quiet. Then she said, "That's better. That's a reason." And she picked a fig off the dish and ate it, and gave him one, and they sat on the stair in the warm shade with the slipped tile above them and the roses below and the two feasts going on out of sight, and he told her — because the thing would not work on her and he had stopped trying — about the window that faced the Palatine, and the atria, and standing against walls, and Sura's man with the ring, and the litter and *don't do it where he can see you*, and she listened as she had always listened, with the whole of her face, and asked once, "Did Rufus mind?" and he said, "Yes," and she nodded as though that were the point.

He did not tell her about the three hundred and twelve thousand. He noticed himself not telling her, and noticed that it was the first thing he had ever kept from her on purpose, and that it was not his to tell, and that this was true, and that it was not, entirely, the reason.

"Thallus looked at you," he said instead. "In the atrium. You looked back."

"I always look back now. The widow said." She finished the fig. "He comes to the Dolphin twice a month. He didn't use to. He says it's the lease, since Rome; he says the master's cousin has taken an interest in the house and he needs to know what the house is worth. He sits in the common room and asks me sums. My mother goes up and comes down and doesn't sleep after, and in the mornings she's slow, and she was never slow." She said all of this to the bay, flatly, as a manifest. "She's not well, I think. She says she is. Antiope says it's nothing, it's the summer, it's what happens to women who were pretty, and Antiope's not been down from the villa since the Ides so I don't know what Antiope thinks any more." A pause. "I'm not asking you for anything. I'm telling you what's in the house, because you asked once what I wanted and I said the house, and you should know what it's made of."

He sat with it. The bay went on being blue. Below them somebody in the garden laughed, a high senator's-son laugh, and was shushed.

"My father's going to buy me a place tonight," he said. "In the room off the peristyle. Secretary, with the name, when the war comes. The equestrian roll before I'm twenty-five. Against money he's been holding since January for the purpose. He's going to let Bassus think it's a dowry matter." He heard himself say it and did not know why he had, except that she had given him the house and he had nothing else of equal weight. "I'll be in the room. He wants me to see it done."

She turned, then, and looked at him; the proper look, the one that cost nothing.

"Then you'll have been sold in front of witnesses," she said, "and I'll have been priced in a common room by a steward, and we'll both know our number." Her mouth did the thing it did that was not quite a smile. "That's more than most people get. Most people have to guess." She stood, and took up the dish. "Don't look for me after. I'll have gone down with my mother; she doesn't walk the coast road in the dark any more, we go at dusk. And Celer —" He looked up. "Write or don't. But don't do the thing where you decide what's true first. You'll be at it till you're forty." And she went down the stair toward the garden with the dish, and did not look back, and he sat on the fourth step with the taste of fig in his mouth and found that he was shaking, very slightly, in the hands, and could not have said whether it was the stair or the room he was about to go into or the other thing, the thing she had said about the house being made of a woman who had stopped sleeping, which he had not asked to be given and now held, and could not put down, and would be carrying, he understood, into the room off the peristyle, and north, and for a long time after.

* * *

The business was done afterwards in the room off the peristyle, with the lamp, and he was in the room, because his father wanted him to see it done.

There were four of them. Bassus on the couch, with wine. Rufus standing at the window with his back to it. Hermes on a chair, which was a thing a freedman was not given in that room and had been given, and sat on very straight. And Thallus by the door, with the box.

"The Spaniard's people want the dowry in coin," Bassus said. He said it to the wine. "Not land. Coin. Six hundred thousand, by the Kalends of August, and the rest in Falernian and furniture, which God knows we have. Six hundred thousand in coin." He drank. "I have told them they shall have it."

Nobody said anything. The lamp hissed.

"Hermes," said Rufus, from the window.

"Patron." Celer's father did not move on his chair. "The house of Laecanius has my name on its lintel and my ships at its service and such coin as a shipowner has, which is less than people think, because a shipowner's coin is on the water nine months of the year. I can find two hundred thousand by the Kalends. Not six. Not without selling a hull, and the man who sells a hull in June to pay in August is a man who has no hull in September when the fleet goes home."

"Two hundred," said Bassus, to the wine.

"And a further two in the spring, when the *Isis* is paid for the return carry. Against the Baian oil-rights, which are nothing to the house and something to me." His father said it as if he had just thought of it. He had been thinking of it, Celer knew, since December, when the first rumour of the Spaniard had come down the coast; he had had the figure in the strong-room under the lamp in January, and the oil-rights beside it, and had said to his son, *when he asks, and he'll ask, I'll give him a third and ask for a thing he doesn't value, and he'll think he's won.* "The remaining two hundred the patron will find among his other friends. He has many."

"Fewer than in Nerva's time," Rufus said drily.

"Then the patron has fewer friends and one of them is sitting in his study offering him four hundred thousand sesterces against a hillside of olives." Hermes looked at Bassus for the first time. "I don't ask for the olives, patron. I ask for what I asked for in the spring of the Spaniard's first year, on the terrace, when you were kind enough to put your hand on my shoulder before the house. The boy."

Bassus looked up.

"He's on Rufus's staff," he said. "He's been in Rome all winter. What more —"

"He's carried a tablet behind Rufus all winter, patron, with respect, and been very useful, and Rufus will say so. That's not a place. That's a favour, and a favour can be withdrawn." His father's voice did not change. "I want the place. I want it written. When the war comes — and it comes, the whole coast knows it comes, there were Dacian envoys at the emperor's camp last autumn and nothing came of it but a silence that's cost somebody already — when it comes, my son goes north on your son's staff as his *secretary*, with the name, and the pay, which I'll remit, and the standing in the camp, which I can't buy anywhere else. And when it's over and your son is praetor, which he'll be, because a man who's been on the Danube with this emperor will be praetor if he can write his name — when he's praetor, my son is on the roll of the equestrian order before he's twenty-five. With your son's sponsorship. In writing. Witnessed." A pause. "Four hundred thousand, patron. In coin. By the Kalends and the spring. And the olives, which nobody in this house has walked through since your father died."

The lamp hissed. Rufus, at the window, had turned round. Thallus, by the door, had not moved at all, and his face was doing the thing it did, which was nothing, and Celer, watching it, saw the nothing go very slightly tight at the corners, the way a sail did when the wind came on it from a point it had not expected, and understood that the steward had not been told, and that his father had made sure of it.

Bassus looked at the freedman on the chair for a long time.

"You've thought about this," he said at last.

"Every day for thirty years, patron."

"Yes. I suppose you have." The old man set his cup down. He looked, for a moment, very tired, and very large, and not at all like the man who had stood in the square of sun and said *my people*; he looked like a man who had just been shown the bill for a dinner he had thought was a gift. "Rufus."

"Father."

"Is the boy any use?"

Rufus looked at Celer. It was the pricing look from the terrace, two years older and with a winter in it, and it went on for longer than was comfortable, and then it stopped.

"He's the only person in my household who knows what I owe," Rufus said, "to the sestertius. He worked it out in December from the strong-room and he's never once let me see that he knows it. Yes. He's some use." He turned back to the window. "Write it, Thallus. Secretary, with the name. The roll before twenty-five, with my sponsorship, on my praetorship. Against four hundred thousand and the olives. Witness it yourself and have the Spaniard's uncle put his seal to it tomorrow; he'll do it, he thinks it's a dowry matter. Then nobody in this room can ever say it didn't happen."

Thallus wrote it. He wrote it standing, on the box, in the small quick hand that Celer had seen on a hundred tallies, and he did not look at Hermes while he wrote. He did not look at Celer; he looked at the paper, and when it was done he sanded it and brought it to the couch for Bassus's seal, and to the chair for Hermes's, and to the window for Rufus's, and then, last, with the ink still wet, he held it out to the boy by the wall.

"Read it," he said. "You're the subject of it. You should know what you've been sold for."

Celer read it. It was four lines. It was his life. He read it twice, as he read everything, and gave it back, and said, "Yes," because there was nothing else to say and because his father's eyes were on him and had in them a thing he had never seen there before and would see only twice again in his life. It was not pride, exactly; it was the look of a man watching a hull he has built go down the ways and into the water, and finding that it floats.

* * *

That night his father took him down to the lower town and gave him a woman.

It was done, as everything his father did was done, without a scene. They walked down from the villa in the dusk along the coast road with the bay going dark on their right hand, the two of them, no slave, which was itself a signal. His father talked about the Spaniard's uncle and the price of Falernian and the olive-rights, which were worth, he said, about a quarter what the house thought and twice what he had let Bassus believe; and then, where the road came down into the town by the baths and divided, he turned not toward the house above the macellum but the other way, toward the harbour, and said in the same voice: "There's a thing a man of our name does for his son at thirteen, and I'm doing it tonight, because you're going north inside the year and I'd rather it was done here by someone I've paid than there by someone who hasn't washed."

Celer said nothing. He had known. He had known, in the way one knew things in that house, since the spring, when his father had bought a girl at the Puteoli market from a dealer who did the Baian trade and had not put her in the kitchen, and the stepmother had looked at the loom and said nothing for a week.

"Her name is Chione. She's Bithynian, nineteen, cost me the price of a mule and she's cleaner than most of what's at the villa. She's in the room over the chandler's, which I've taken for the month. She knows what's wanted and she's been told to be kind about it, and if she isn't you'll tell me and I'll know why." His father walked on. "You'll not be ashamed. There's nothing in it to be ashamed of. Every man in this town has done it or had it done, and the ones who say otherwise are lying to a wife. You'll go up, and you'll come down, and tomorrow you'll be the same boy with one thing fewer to think about, and that's all it is. It's a jar. It's the measure. It's what's done."

They had come to the chandler's. There was a stair at the side, outside, going up to a door with a lamp over it. His father stopped at the foot of it.

"I'll be at the warehouse," he said. "Don't hurry. Don't go to the Dolphin after, you'll want to and you'll not; it's the wrong house for tonight and the woman there would know by your face." He put his hand on the back of Celer's neck, the hull-inspecting hand, and took it away. "Go on."

Celer went up.

* * *

He came down an hour later, or less, and the lamp over the door had gone out and the street was dark, and he stood at the foot of the stair for a long time.

He would not, afterwards, be able to say much about the room. He would be able to say that it was small, and clean, as his father had said, and had a bed and a lamp and a window on the harbour. That the girl had been sitting on the bed when he came in, and had stood up, and had been — this he would remember — exactly as tall as he was, so that when she came to him their eyes were level, and hers were brown, and had in them a thing he had seen in Antiope's on the stairs and in the girl's in the boat-house at Baiae and had not, until that moment, been on the receiving end of: a complete, practised, almost gentle attention that was looking at him and seeing a task. She had been kind. His father had paid for kind, and had got it. She had said his name, which she had been told, and had asked him nothing, and had taken his hands and put them where they went, and had been, throughout, the one in the room who knew what was happening; and he had been thirteen, and a body, and then not; and it had been over. She had got up and brought him water and said, in a Greek with the sea in it, *there, now, that's done, you'll find it's easier than counting*, which meant that his father had told her what he was, or she had read it off him, and he had not been able to tell which was worse.

He stood at the foot of the stair. The harbour was in front of him with its lamps. Up the hill the Dolphin would have its shutters barred, and the woman at the hearth, and the girl in the room under the stairs with the curtain drawn, listening to the house; and he found that his father had been right, that he wanted to go there, and that he would not, and that the not going was the first thing he had done since he came down the stair that he had chosen.

He did not feel ashamed. His father had said he would not and he found, examining it, that he did not; it was a jar, it was the measure, it was what was done. What he felt was something else, something that had no name in his father's strong-room and that he turned over now in the dark the way he had turned over the denarius on the harbour steps. It was this. The girl in the room had cost the price of a mule. She was nineteen. She had been told to be kind and had been kind, as a slave in a warehouse aisle was told to count and counted; and she had done it well, and had said *that's done*, and he had come down the stair with one thing fewer to think about and she was still in the room, with the lamp. The window on the harbour, and would be in it tomorrow, and the day after, for the month his father had taken it, for whoever his father sent up; and he had not asked her anything. Not where in Bithynia. Not how long she had been in Italy. Not whether the window was a kindness or a cruelty, to a girl from the sea. He had been in a room with a person and had treated her, exactly as he had been told to, as a thing that was done, and she had let him, because that was what she was for; and the thought that went through him at the foot of the stair, cold and exact and unwelcome, was that he had just learned, in an hour, over a chandler's, the whole of what his father had spent thirty years trying to teach him about the house of Laecanius, which was what it felt like from the inside to be the master.

And he had liked it. That was the thing he would not say to anyone, then or ever. Not the act, which had been brief and strange and mostly her. The other thing. The being, for an hour, the one in the room who was not a task.

He went up to the house above the macellum by the long way, past the baths, so as not to pass the Dolphin's lane. His stepmother was at the loom with a lamp. She looked up when he came in, and looked at his face, and he saw her read it, all of it, the room and the stair and the thing at the foot of it; and she did not say anything, and she did not look away either, and after a moment she said, in her even farm voice:

"There's bread on the table. He said you'd not have eaten." And then, as he went past her to the table: "Her name's Chione. He'll have told you. He'll not have told you she asked me for a needle and thread in the spring, to mend her own tunic, because the kitchen wouldn't give her one. I gave it her. You might remember that she sews."

He remembered it. He remembered it for forty years. And he went north inside the twelvemonth with it among the things he carried, and it was, of all of them, the only one that had been given to him by a woman, and the only one he never once took out and counted, because he knew already what it was worth and did not want to know to whom.
