# The Stone

His father could not say his name, and wrote it instead, on a wax tablet, in figures, which was the only hand he had left.

Celer came down the Appian road in March on the horse he had bought with the vines and sat better now, with Chrestê's bread in a bag and Crescens's stick in the saddle-roll because he had not been able to leave it in a box in Rome and did not know why, and came over the shoulder of the hill above the town in the afternoon with the Gulf opening below him grey and flat and the mole out in it and the new work beyond the mole where the Syrian's man was pushing it two hundred feet further into the sea, and the lodging-houses along the front half down already, so that he could not have said, from the hill, which gap had been the Dolphin; and he did not stop to work it out. He went down through the upper town past the house where a Greek had died by a brazier and along the slope past the house of a merchant called Granius where a woman he owned had kept the books, and in at the door between the columns that were not marble, and his stepmother was in the atrium, and had been, he understood from her face, since the boy had seen the horse on the hill; and she put her arms round him, which she had never done, and said into his shoulder in the Nola Latin, "He's in the strong-room. He'll not be moved. He sleeps there, on a pallet, with the lamp, and the Lares; he'd not have them brought to him, he had himself brought to them. Go in. He's been writing your name since the Nones and I can't read it."

He went in. The strong-room was as it had been when he was eleven: the lamp, the table, the pine boxes on the shelves with the seals, and one box with the seal broken and the lid set beside it, which was the *Isis* year and the sail-makers' bills, and which nobody had put back; and on a pallet against the far wall, with a blanket and the Lares on a shelf above him and a tablet on his knee, his father.

The left side of the face had gone. That was the first thing, and he made himself look at it, because he had decided on a beach that a man was his face and this was his father's now: the left eye half-shut and the left corner of the mouth down and the left hand lying on the blanket like a thing that had been set there, and the right side of the face his father's, exactly, the shipowner's face, hard and reasonable and tired, with the right eye on him, and the right hand with the stylus. Hermes of Puteoli looked at his son from the pallet with half a face and made a sound, which was not a word, and then stopped trying, and looked down at the tablet, and wrote.

Celer knelt and read it. It was a column. It was in the house's shorthand, which only two living people could read and one of them was on the Palatine; and it was:

*406.*
*480.*
*2 hulls.*
*1 paper (R.)*
*1 stone.*
*1 son.*

And under it, a line, and under the line, nothing.

He read it twice. He looked up. The right eye was waiting.

"The sum's wrong," he said.

The right side of the mouth did something.

"You've left out the villa paper's worth, which is the headland and the atrium and sixty years of walking up it, and you've left out Vibia, and you've left out the man in the prefect's office you've had since Nerva, and you've left out the Lares from Nola, which I've carried across the Danube twice and fed in a tent on the far bank with bread while a king's city burned." He kept the voice level; the manifest voice; his father's. "And you've put the four-eighty on the wrong side. It's not a loss. It's a figure you paid for ten years of a thing you did with your eyes open, and you said so in a letter, and I believed you. Put it in the other column."

The stylus moved. Slowly, with the right hand, badly, the way a man wrote who had written ten thousand sheets and now had to think about the shape of a four. He crossed out the 480. He wrote it again, to the right, in another column that had not been there. And under it, in the first column, he wrote, in letters, not figures, which took a long time:

*F.*

And looked up.

Celer knelt by the pallet in the strong-room with the lamp and the open box and his father's half-face waiting, and did the sum, and it came out where it had always come out, which was that there was a thing on the tablet that could be read four ways and that he would have to pick one, and he picked.

"The stone," he said. "You mean the stone. Yes. I'll stand by it. Tomorrow, if Vibia says you can be carried. That's what I came for."

The right eye held his for a long moment. Then it closed, and opened, which was a nod, and the stylus went down on the blanket, and Hermes of Puteoli lay back on the pallet with the Lares above him and the sail-makers' box open on the shelf and let his son be right about what the letter meant, which was, Celer would understand much later, on another hill, the last thing his father ever gave him, and the only lie.

* * *

They carried him to the stone on the Ides, four of the house's men with a chair, up the road out of the town between the tombs, in the morning, with the Gulf behind them and the vines on the hill above still bare; and Celer walked beside the chair with his hand on its arm and his stepmother behind with the boy who had been outside the door, and nobody else, because his father had written *nobody* on the tablet and underlined it, and the Augustales who would have come had been told the family was private and had understood, because they had all walked off ships once and knew what it was to want a thing to be yours for an hour before the town had it.

The tomb was finished. He had not seen it finished; he had seen it as a plan, and a plinth, and a plinth with a stone on it; and now it stood by the road on its own ground with a wall round it and a gate and a cypress somebody had planted and that had taken, and it was, he saw, coming up the road, exactly what his father had said it would be in every letter for eighteen years, which was *the biggest on the road*, and it was, and it was also — he had not expected this and did not know what to do with it — handsome. Plain. Travertine, not marble, because his father had priced marble and said *no, a freedman in marble is a joke and I'll not be one on my own stone*; good travertine, dressed square, with the relief above the inscription that showed a ship, a corn-hull, with the sail set, going somewhere, and under it the words, which he had read as a plan and read now cut:

*C. LAECANIUS HERMES, FREEDMAN OF LUCIUS, AUGUSTALIS, SHIPOWNER, BUILT THIS FOR HIMSELF AND FOR VIBIA HIS WIFE AND FOR C. LAECANIUS CELER HIS SON, EQUES ROMANUS, AND FOR THEIR FREEDMEN AND FREEDWOMEN AND THEIR DESCENDANTS.*

*Eques Romanus.* It had been cut in the winter, after the Ides of March two years ago, by a mason who had been told to leave the space and had left it, and the letters were newer than the rest and had not weathered, and his father had had them cut a hand's breadth taller than the rest, so that a man going by on the road to Capua would see that before he saw anything else, and his father had said so, in a letter, *a hand taller, so they see it from a horse*.

They set the chair down in front of it. Celer stood beside the chair with his hand on its arm. His father looked up at the stone with the right eye, for a long time, in the morning, with the cypress moving a little and the Gulf behind and the vines above, and then the right hand came up off the blanket and found the son's wrist and held it, hard, with what was left, and the half-face turned up and the mouth tried, and made the sound, and stopped, and tried again; and Celer bent, and put his ear by his father's mouth, as a woman at a loom had told him you did with the gods, so they could see a face; and his father said, with the half-mouth, in a voice that was not a voice, two words, which were the first since the Nones and the last, and which were:

"Not nothing."

He straightened. He stood with his father's hand on his wrist and the stone in front of him with his name on it a hand taller than his father's, and the sum went through him and came out, and he said, "No. Not nothing," and his father's hand let go, and lay on the blanket, and the half-face went back to looking at the stone, and that was all.

They carried him back down. He died nine days later, on the pallet, in the strong-room, in the night, with the lamp and the Lares and the open box, and Vibia woke the son in the room with the window on the wall and said *it's done*, and he went in, and his father was dead with the right eye open on the shelf where the gods were, and the tablet on the blanket, and on the tablet, in the figures hand, one line, which the stylus had been in the middle of and had dropped from:

*and*

No mark after it. Not a question; not a stop. The word, and the wax smooth.

He stood and looked at it. Then he picked up the stylus from the blanket where it had fallen and, under his father's *and*, in his own square hand, in the shorthand only two living people could read, wrote the answer his father had worked out in a strong-room two years before and written him in a letter: *the sum's done and there's nothing left over*; and then crossed it out, because it was not true, because there was a tomb and two hulls and a paper and a woman at a loom and a son with a ring and a girl on a hill who was holding a door shut, and wrote instead, under the crossing, the thing he had never said to his father living and said now to the wax, which was: *the villa, and me, and I'm not spending it either. — C.* And closed the tablet, and put it under his father's right hand, and went out to tell Vibia what was to be done, because he was the house now and that was what the house did.

* * *

Rufus came to the funeral.

He had not been asked. The Augustales had been, this time, because the house was Celer's and Celer had decided, in the strong-room with the tablet under his father's hand, that a man who had built the biggest tomb on the road had meant it to be walked to by the whole town, and that *nobody* had been for the stone and not for the fire; and so the whole town came, on the ninth day, up the road between the tombs behind the bier, the Augustales in their best with the fasces they were allowed and the ship-masters and the sail-makers and the port-man's son who had kept the scale, who had the grace to walk at the back, and the Alexandrian who had bought the *Isis*, and the Rhodian who had drunk eleven at the Dolphin and was seventy now and walked with a stick and wept, because the Rhodian wept at everything; and at the front, behind the bier and before the son, where the patron walked if the patron came, Lucius Laecanius Rufus, senator, in a toga, with his father's nose and his father's walk and none of his father's weight, and Thallus a pace behind with the map of a face, back from Rome a week.

Celer did not look at him. He walked behind the bier with Vibia on his arm and the stick in his belt, which was a thing nobody but a centurion wore at a funeral and which he had decided to wear and had not explained, and at the tomb he said the thing that was said, in Latin, in the square voice, badly, because he had only heard it said once, over Nicias, and had not been listening then, and then the fire, and the town stood, and the smoke went up over the vines, and the Rhodian wept, and it was done.

Afterward, at the house, in the atrium between the columns that were not marble, with the town going through the door and the slaves with the wine and Vibia being what a widow was, Rufus found him.

He was thirty-six. He had been praetor and was waiting to be something else and had, Celer saw, close, for the first time in five years, the look of a man who had sat on a stool in a strong-room and been shown a paper and understood it in his legs; it had gone into the face, under the nose, and would not come out. He stood in front of the freedman's son in the freedman's atrium with the toga and the senatorial stripe and said, in the voice that was his father's voice with the certainty taken out: "Celer. I'm sorry. He was — my father said he was the only man on the coast who ever made him feel he'd been *counted*. I didn't understand that when he said it. I do now."

"Thank you, sir."

"Don't." Rufus looked round the atrium. "I've come to say a thing and I'd rather say it standing, because the last time I sat down in this house it cost me more than I had." He lowered his voice. "The paper. The villa. It's yours now. You know that; you've been in Sura's boxes five years, you know what a paper is. You could call it on the Kalends and I couldn't pay and the headland would be yours and my father's atrium with it, and every freedman on this coast would spit when they said your name, and you'd be the richest man in Puteoli and never asked to dinner." He held the freedman's son's eyes with his father's nose. "I'm not asking you not to. I'm telling you I know you could. That's — that's the only thing I've got to give that your father would have valued, I think. That I know."

Celer looked at him. The town went through the door behind.

"I'm not calling it, sir."

"No. He said you wouldn't. Thallus had it from him, in the winter, on the tablet: *the boy'll hold it and never call it and that'll be worse for Rufus than calling, and better for the boy, and I'll not be here to see either.*" Rufus's mouth did something that was nearly a smile and was the family's and he had not earned it. "He was right. It's worse. I'll walk up that headland every April for the rest of my life knowing whose it is and so will my sons, and you'll never say a word, and that's — that's a freedman's revenge, and it's better than any I've seen in the Senate." He straightened the toga. "There's one other thing. I'm told you wrote the finding on the fine. With your name and your ring."

"Yes, sir."

"Then hear this, from the patron, in front of your father's gods, since I'm here and it's the only use I'll be." He raised his voice, slightly, so that the men nearest heard, which was the point. "The fine was just. The sail was weighed. My father's dock was leased to a man who did a thing and the thing was found and paid for, and the man's son did the figure himself, straight, and signed it. That's the house of Laecanius's view and it'll be said in the Senate if anyone asks, and if anyone on this coast says otherwise about Hermes's son, they'll say it to me." And then, lower, to Celer alone: "That's for the ring. It's not nothing; a senator's word on a freedman's son's figure is a thing that goes on a roll. It's the only thing I've got that doesn't cost me the headland, and I'd like, once, to have given this house something instead of sat in it."

He went. Thallus went behind him, and at the door the steward turned, as people did at doors, and looked at the freedman's son in the atrium with the stick in his belt, and said nothing, and went; and Celer stood with the town going by and understood that he had just been given, by the patron, in his father's atrium, the one thing his father had wanted for eighteen years and had not lived nine days to hear, which was a man of the house saying *just* about a freedman's figure in front of the town, and that it had been given because the man could not afford anything else, and that his father would have said *that's the and*, and that it was.

* * *

He went down to the mole on the evening after the fire, alone, because the house was full of the town and the town was full of his father, and he wanted, once, before he went back to Rome, to stand at the end of a thing and look out.

He could not. That was what he found. The old end was gone under the new work, the Syrian's man's stone going out another two hundred feet into the Gulf with a gang on it and a crane and a tower begun, and where two children had sat in March in the dark with the villa lamps on the water there was a wall of new blocks higher than his head and a man on top of it with a plumb-line who shouted down in Greek to get back; so he got back. He stood where the old mole met the new, with the fish-market behind him and the gap in the front where the Dolphin had been and the evening coming down over Capri the colour it came, and looked at the Gulf through the gap between the crane and the tower, and the Gulf was the Gulf, and it had no manifest, and he stood anyway.

"You're Hermes's."

An old woman. With a basket, empty, going home from the market; small and brown and bent, with a face that had been doing one thing for sixty years and had the shape of it. He did not know her. He knew what she was before she said it, because his father had taught him to read a hull from the quay by how she sat.

"I settled my eggs with the girl," the old woman said. "At the Dolphin. When she was ten and kept the book on the stair. Every week for four years. She never once passed the higher figure. I'd put it to her sometimes, to see — a dozen for eleven — and she'd look at me and write eleven and say nothing, and I'd think, that one's going somewhere, and she has." The old woman looked at the gap in the front. "My man's paid. A quarter, for nothing, since the year the old master died. He drinks it. I know who pays and I know why and I've told him he's to remember what he signed, if he's asked, and to say so, and he says he will, and he will, because I've told him." She shifted the basket. "I saw your father's fire from the hill. I'd have come down but I'm not Augustales and I'd not have been let in the atrium. He was a hard man and he weighed a sail and he sent oil to a centurion every year and never said, and that's three true things about him and I've known men with fewer." She looked at the tall man in the road with the stick in his belt. "You've her nose. Not the consul's; the other one. The one that goes somewhere. Tell her the egg-woman's man remembers. Tell her that's paid for, and she needn't send any more coin, and I know she will anyway, because that's her, and I'll not stop her, because he drinks it and it keeps him quiet."

She went. He stood at the join of the old mole and the new until the man with the plumb-line came down off the wall and the gang went home and the crane stood alone against the evening, and then he went back up through the town past the house of Granius and the house where a Greek had died by a brazier to the house with the columns that were not marble, which was his, and full of the town, and in which his father's stool was by the loom and empty.

* * *

Vibia was at the loom when the town had gone, in the room off the atrium where she had always been, with the lamp, and the shuttle going, because that was what her hands did when the rest of her could not.

He stood in the door. She did not stop. She was forty-four and had been a freedwoman of a Nola wool-merchant and then a shipowner's wife and now a shipowner's widow, and she had the Nola face, broad and brown and unsurprised, and she had put her arms round him in the atrium and would not again, he understood, because once was what it had cost her and she had paid it.

"Sit," she said, to the loom. "There. His stool. He sat there in the evenings this winter when he could still walk, and watched me, and said nothing, which was new. Thirty years I'd wanted him to say nothing for an hour and when he did I'd have given the house to have him back at the sum." The shuttle went. "You wore the stick."

"Yes."

"Good. He'd have liked that. He didn't know Crescens — met him once, on the quay, the day the *Pelican* came in — but he sent the oil every year and he said to me, the year you went north, *that man's doing the thing I can't, which is standing next to him*. He was jealous of a centurion. He'd not have said so. He's not here now so I can." She reached for the next thread. "I've a thing to tell you and then a thing to ask, and then I'll not speak of either again, because I'm from Nola and we say a thing once."

"Go on."

"The letter. In the box behind the gods. You'll find it tonight because you'll go through the strong-room tonight, you're his; so I'll tell you now how it came. It came on the Nones of March, from Rome, by the house's post, the day after Thallus had been up the hill there to see her, and it was in the shorthand, and I can't read it, and he could, with the one eye, and he had me hold it for him because the right hand had the stylus and the left was gone. I held it. I watched his face." The shuttle stopped, for the first time. "I've watched that man's face over a letter for twelve years, Celer. Over the emperor's letter about the *Pelican*. Over yours about the ring. Over the fine. I know what it does. And I'll tell you what it did over that one, because you'll want to know and he can't say and I'm the only one who saw." She looked at him, finally, with the broad brown unsurprised face. "It did nothing. For a long time. And then the right side of it — the side that was still his — smiled. Not the way he smiled at a figure. The other way. The way he smiled at you on the quay the day you came off the *Pelican* with your hand on your own knife. And then he made the sound, the one that's not a word, and I thought he was trying to say your name, and he wasn't; he was trying to say *hers*; I could see the shape of it; and he couldn't; and he took the stylus and wrote on the tablet, and I've kept what he wrote, because I can't read it and I thought you should."

She reached under the loom and brought out a tablet and gave it to him.

In the figures hand, badly: *She's mine. I said so. I was right. Tell the boy she's not holding him. She's holding* — and then the stylus had failed, or the hand, and there was a mark that was not a letter, and then, lower, smaller, as if written later with the hand rested: *the door. Not him. The door. He'll not see it. I didn't, at his age. Tell him.*

He read it. He read it again. The loom did not start.

"I don't know what it means," said Vibia. "I've never known what you two were saying to each other in that hand; it was the one room in the house I wasn't in. But I know what his face did, and I've told you, and now I've given you the tablet, and that's the thing I had to tell."

"And the thing to ask."

"The woman. In Rome. Over the wine-shop." She started the loom again, and did not look at him. "Your father knew. The steward's boy writes to Thallus's man who writes to Thallus who told Hermes everything, because that was their game, the two of them, for forty years, each telling the other what the other already knew so they'd both know they knew. He knew you'd bought a Puteoli merchant's woman in the Subura and paid without bargaining and that she kept your slate and called you by your name. And he said to me, on the stool, in the winter, when he could still speak: *he'll free her. He's mine. I freed you.*" The shuttle went. "I was his for six years before he did. I'd kept his house and his bed and his books when he was at sea and I was twenty-eight and I'd stopped expecting it, and he came in one evening from the Augustales' dinner, not drunk, and said *Vibia, I've been to the magistrate, you're free, I'd like you to stay and I'll not blame you if you don't*, and went to bed. I stayed. I stayed because he'd said *I'll not blame you*, which no man on this coast had ever said to me about anything." She looked at him, once. "So that's the thing to ask. Not whether. When. Because there's a difference between a man who frees a woman because he's going to and a man who frees her because he's just stood by his father's stone and found out what a thing weighs, and I'd rather you were the second, and I'm asking you to be, because I'm from Nola and I'd like someone in this family to do a thing before they've done the sum on it."

He sat on his father's stool with the tablet in his hands and the loom going.

"When I'm back," he said. "The first day. Before I go up the hill to Sura."

"Good." The shuttle. "And Celer. When you do it. Say the thing he said. *I'll not blame you.* It's the only thing a woman in that place has never heard and the only thing worth saying, and he knew it, and he'd not have told you, because he thought it was a weakness, and it was the best thing he ever did."

* * *

He opened the strong-room on the last night, alone, with the lamp, because the house was his and someone had to count it.

He went through the boxes. He did not need to; he had Philon's habits now and knew what was in a box by the dust on its lid; but he went through them anyway, because it was the thing his father would have done on the last night of a house, and because a woman over a wine-shop had said *count it in the morning* and he was counting it at night, which was the only time he had. The *Isis* year. The *Pelican* year with the Rhine run and the emperor's letter, which he read, standing, *the man who sent the Pelican is the man I want fed by*, and put back. The villa paper, in its own box, with the Spaniard son-in-law's seal and Rufus's, and he looked at it a long time and put it back without unfolding it, because he had told the patron he would not call it and a thing you did not call was a thing you did not read, either, or it began to read you. The Lares from Nola, which he would take; the Lares of the house, which stayed. The tablet with the figures, under his father's hand still, because Vibia had not let anyone move it and he had not either; and he took it now, and read the column again, and his own two lines under it, the crossed and the kept, and closed it, and put it in the box with the stick and the forty-one days and his father's last letter, which was the box he carried, and which was getting heavy, and which he understood he would carry until he could not, as his father had carried a sail-maker's bill for ten years because he was the one who wrote things down.

And at the bottom of the last shelf, behind the Lares, in a box with no seal and no dust, which meant it had been opened often and recently, a thing he had not known was there.

Letters. Not many. In his own square hand, the four lines each, from Vetera, from the far bank, from the tent, from the Aventine: *the roll, the Ides, Capito's office, the ring*; *Crescens, the second wall, a hand-stone, it was quick*; all of them, every letter he had ever written his father, from the first at fourteen that said *the Rhine is wider than the Gulf* to the last, kept, in order, tied; and under them, in the box, in another hand, one more, which was not his, and which he unfolded, and which was in the house's shorthand, and was not addressed, and said:

*Hermes. The paper's in the prefect's archive under the Augusta's seal. I don't know if you'll have heard; I'm telling you so you've a figure and not a story. The steward's sheet is dead. I'll not come back to the coast. I know what you said in the strong-room, about how I came up; Thallus told me, on the hill, in March, with a German at the door. I'd like you to know that it was by my feet, and that I'd have been proud to have been yours in another house, because you were right about that, and because you taught me what a thing weighs with the lid off, and I've never forgotten the lesson or the man. The sail was the price of the hill. I paid it. You'd have paid it in my place; you said so. I left him off the sheet. You'll know why; it's the same reason you've not called the villa. — F.*

He stood in the strong-room with the lamp and his father's gods and the letter in his hand that a girl on a hill had written to a man who could not read it, in March, after Thallus, after the stroke, knowing he could not read it, in a shorthand only two living people could read; and one of them was dead now, and the other was standing in his strong-room with it, which was, he understood, the whole of why it had been written in that hand, to that man, at that time: so that it would be found here, by this lamp, by the one person left who could read it, after the one it was addressed to was past reading; and that this was either the cruellest thing she had ever done or the kindest, and that he had been in that place before, on a stool, with a prefect's sheet, and had not been able to tell, and could not now, and that his father had written *F.* on a tablet and he had said *the stone*, and his father had let him.

He read it again. *The same reason you've not called the villa.*

He put it in the box he carried, with the stick and the tablet and the forty-one days and the rest. He closed the strong-room. He fed the Lares from Nola in his old room with the window on the wall, with Chrestê's bread, which was stale now and was what he had, and said to them, out loud, in the house's shorthand, because they liked to see a face and there was nobody else in the house who could read it: "She's holding a paper on me. He said so. And she's just told him she knows it, and she's told him why, and the why's a thing I'd have had to do the sum for and she's done it and left it in a box for me to find. I don't know if that's the kindest thing or the cruellest. He'd have known. He'd have said it's both, and that both's a figure, and that I'd better learn to carry it, because he's not here to tell me what it weighs."

In the morning he rode back to Rome with the box on the saddle and the stick in the roll and the house in his stepmother's hands, because she had kept it for a shipowner for twelve years and could keep it for his son; and on the shoulder of the hill above the town he stopped, once, and looked back, not at the Gulf or the mole or the gap where a lodging-house had been, but up the road to the right, where a travertine stone stood on its own ground with a cypress and a ship on it going somewhere, and a name cut a hand taller than the rest, so that it could be seen from a horse; and he saw it; and rode on.
