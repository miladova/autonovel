# The Terms

Sura read before the emperor was up, and Celer learned to be awake before Sura.

That was the whole of the first month on the staff: a tent at the top of the camp where the ground was driest, and a man in it at the first grey of the morning with a lamp and a pile, and a boy at the flap who was not to speak unless spoken to and was not to write anything down that he had not been told to, and who stood there, in the cold, with his hands behind his back, and watched a man who had made an emperor read.

Lucius Licinius Sura was fifty-five. He was a Spaniard from Tarraco with a face like a good knife, long and plain and kept sharp for use, and a way of sitting very still that Celer had seen before only in Thallus and in the lady Cornelia and had come to recognise as the posture of people who had arranged the world so that it came to them. He had been consul. He would be consul again; everyone knew it; he had been offered it this year and had said that the emperor needed him in the mountains and that the fasces would keep. He had, it was said in the camp, the whole of the emperor's correspondence in his head and most of the emperor's opinions, and had had them there since the Rhine, where he had, in a tent like this one, with a pile like this one, persuaded a governor of Upper Germany that an old man in Rome was about to adopt him and that he had better be the kind of man who deserved it. He did not shout. He did not, in a month, raise his voice once. He read, and set a sheet aside, or did not, and the sheets he set aside became the war.

"Hermes's boy," he said, on the eleventh morning. He did not look up. "The roads. Come here."

Celer went.

"This granary." A finger on a sheet. "The one in the side-valley, west, with the forty and the felt cap. Your Greek gave it you in October. You wrote: *the garrison was promised land in the Banat under Domitian's peace and has not received it.* Why did you write that?"

"Because he said it, sir."

"He said a great many things. You wrote this one. You didn't write that the commander was tall, or that he had a wife in the king's city, which your Greek also said, according to the centurion who went." The knife-face did not move. "You wrote the thing about the land. Why?"

Celer thought. He thought the way he had learned to in a warehouse aisle, which was not about the question but about what the question was for. "Because the forty surrendered when they saw the standards, sir. And asked if the offer was still open. A man who's been promised a thing and not had it is a man who's still waiting for someone to keep the promise. It doesn't matter who. I thought — I thought it was the kind of thing that would be true of more than forty."

Sura looked up then. It was the first time, and the eyes in the knife-face were not what Celer had expected; they were tired, and amused, and very old, in a way that had nothing to do with fifty-five.

"Yes," he said. "It's true of the whole kingdom. It's true of every man in a felt cap between here and the Black Sea; the king has promised them land and gold and the Roman tribute for ten years and he has given them a war, and they're tired, and they're waiting for someone to keep a promise, and it doesn't matter who." He set the sheet aside. "Lucilius said you were honest and quick and nowhere near the pen in January. He's a careful man. He didn't say you could *read*. Not figures; anyone can read figures. The thing under the figures. The thing the man in the aisle leans on." He picked up the next sheet. "You'll stop standing at the flap. You'll sit there —" a stool, by the lamp — "and you'll read what I set aside, after me, and you'll tell me, each morning, one thing in it that's true of more than forty. One. If you tell me two I'll send you back to Rufus. Go on."

He sat. He read. He told Sura one thing each morning, and the war went up into the mountains for its second summer with the staff behind it, and by August the thing he had said about the felt caps was being said in the emperor's tent by the emperor, and the king's nobles were coming in, one valley at a time, with their hands out, asking if the offer was still open; and nobody said *Hermes's boy* about any of it, because that was not how a staff worked, and he had not expected them to, and he found that it did not matter. He had been in the tent. That was the thing his father had said: the tent is where the war is decided. He had been in it, on a stool, with a lamp, and he had watched it be decided, and he had said one true thing a morning, and some of them had been used.

He did not write to her. He told himself it was the staff: that Sura's post went through the man with the ring and the man with the ring noticed who noticed. This was true. It was not the reason, and he knew it was not, and he put the knowing in the column with the rest.

* * *

The king came down out of his city in the first days of October with his nobles behind him and knelt in front of the emperor on a hillside, and Celer wrote the terms.

He had not expected to be there. He had expected to be in the tent with the pile, two miles back, where his father had said he would be, and had been content with it; it was Sura who said, the night before, without looking up, "You'll come up tomorrow. With the tablets. You'll stand behind the secretary and when the terms are read you'll write what's agreed, in your hand, as it's said, and the secretary will write it in his, and afterwards we'll compare, and if they differ, yours is the one I'll read first, because you've no style and he has, and I want to know what was said before I know how it sounded." And so he had gone up, in the grey morning, with the staff, and stood on a hillside above a river with the whole of the army drawn up on the slopes below in its iron and its colours, which he had never seen all at once and had not known was so much, and watched a kingdom end.

The king was a big man. That was the first thing; Celer had expected, from the way the camp talked of him, something else, a creature, and he was a big grey-bearded man of sixty in a felt cap and a cloak with a gold clasp, who walked up the hill between two lines of Roman iron with his nobles behind him in their caps and did not look to either side, and stopped at the distance that had been agreed, and looked at the emperor, who was standing, bare-headed, with Sura at his shoulder, and then did the thing that had been agreed, which was to go down on his knees on the wet grass and put his hands out, palm up, and lower his head, and stay there.

The army made a sound. It was not a cheer. It was the sound of forty thousand men seeing a thing they had been told they would see and finding it was true, and it went along the slopes like wind in barley and was gone.

The emperor let him kneel. Celer counted — he could not help it; it was what he was — and got to thirty before Trajan stepped forward and put a hand under the king's elbow and raised him, which had also been agreed, and said something that Celer could not hear and that the interpreter made into Dacian, and the king answered, and the interpreter made it into Latin, and it was: *The king says he has been the enemy of Rome and is now its friend, and asks the emperor's clemency for his people, who have suffered.* And the emperor said — this Celer heard; the man had a voice made for hillsides — "Tell him his people have my clemency. Tell him the terms are the terms. Tell him I'll not say it twice."

Then the secretary read the terms, and Celer wrote them.

He wrote: *The king to surrender his arms and engines of war and the makers of them. The king to raze his fortresses. The king to withdraw from all territory occupied since the peace of Domitian. The king to receive a Roman garrison in his capital. The king to surrender all Roman deserters and to receive none hereafter. The king to have the same friends and enemies as Rome. The king to send envoys to the Senate to have these terms confirmed.* He wrote it as it was said, in Latin, in the square hand, without style, and he looked up between the clauses, because he had decided to look at everything, and he saw the king's face while they were read to him in Dacian, and it did nothing. It did nothing at all. It was a face that had been arranged a long time ago to show nothing and had set that way, and Celer, watching it, thought of Thallus, and of a lodging-house, and of a man who had never frightened anyone on purpose because it was a waste, and thought: *he's not beaten. He's a steward. He's heard the terms and he's doing the sum, and the sum is how long.*

He did not write that. He wrote the terms. And when the thing was done and the king had gone back down the hill between the lines of iron with his nobles behind him and had not once looked to either side, and the army had made its sound again, and the staff had turned to go down to the tents, Sura stopped beside the boy with the tablet and said, low, in the voice he used for one true thing a morning: "Well?"

"He'll break them, sir."

"Which?"

"All of them. The arms first; he'll give up the engines and keep the makers. Then the fortresses; he'll raze the ones we can see. The deserters he'll send, because they're no use to him. The friends and enemies—" Celer stopped.

"Go on."

"He's already sent to the Parthians, sir. Apollonios said. In the winter. He said the king had a man at the Parthian court asking what Parthia would give for a war on the Danube that kept Rome's legions off the Euphrates. He said the man came back with a promise and no gold, which is what Parthians give." He kept his eyes on the tablet. "It's not in the sheets. I didn't write it. I didn't — the man was dead by the time I'd have had reason to, and it wasn't a road."

Sura was silent for a moment on the wet hillside with the army going down past them.

"No," he said. "It wasn't a road." And then: "You'll write it tonight. All of it. Everything your Greek said that wasn't a road. Every name, every promise, every man at every court. Date it the day he said it, not today, and bring it to me before the lamp's out, and I'll put it where it goes." He began to walk. "The emperor has clemency, boy. He has it for all of them, the king and the caps and the forty and the thousands; it's the thing he's best at and it's real, I've watched him have it on three frontiers and it's never once been a performance. And I have the other thing, which is what clemency needs standing behind it so that it can afford to be real. You've just told me the king will break the terms. I knew. I've known since the Rhine. What I didn't have was a dead Greek's account of the Parthian court with a date on it, and now I shall, and in three years when the king breaks the terms and the Senate asks why we were not warned, there will be a sheet." He glanced sideways. "That's what you're for. Not the roads. That. You read a man's face across a hillside and told me the sum. Keep doing it. Never do it where he can see."

* * *

They went down to Rome in December by the roads the army had built, and the city gave the emperor a triumph, and the name of the river, and Celer stood on the Palatine side of the Sacred Way among Sura's people in a toga that Sura's steward had found him and watched it go by, and thought about a roof on the Caelian.

He had been on the roof three years ago. He had not; she had. He had been behind Rufus with a tablet in a house on the Caelian watching Rufus's hands, and she had been on a roof watching a man walk; and now he stood at the foot of the Palatine among the staff with the whole of the city on its roofs above him, black with people, a hundred thousand faces, and he looked up at them, which was a thing he had learned not to do and did anyway, and thought that somewhere in that was a face that had been on a roof three years ago, and that it was looking down, as it had looked down then, and that it could not pick him out, as he could not pick out the Dolphin from the villa wall, and that he had known this before he looked.

The triumph went by. The spoils went by on carts, the arms and the engines and the gold that had been found, which was not the king's gold, which was under a river, which the staff knew and the city did not. The prisoners went by, in their caps, and he counted them, because he could not not, and lost the count at four hundred and did not pick it up again. The emperor went by, in the chariot, with his face painted red as the god's and a slave behind him holding the crown over his head and saying the thing the slave said, *remember you are a man*, which the city could not hear and knew was being said; and the staff cheered, and Celer cheered, and found that he meant it, which surprised him, and put the surprise with the rest.

Afterwards, in Sura's house on the Aventine, which was large and plain and had no masks in its atrium because the Suras of Tarraco had not been anybody until this one, there was a letter waiting that had been waiting since May, from his father, and inside it another in his stepmother's hand, and he read them in the room he had been given, which had a window on nothing, a wall, which he took also as an instruction.

His father's said that the house was well, that the *Isis* had made the return carry at the bounty, that Felix at the grain-office had been expensive, as foreseen, and had been paid, and was no longer at the grain-office, and that the man who had replaced him was a Spaniard who did not yet know what he was worth and was therefore cheap; that Bassus had been ill in the winter and was recovered; that the Spaniard son-in-law had been made quaestor; that Rufus's debts were being spoken of on the coast; and that Thallus had taken the lease of the lodging-house by the harbour on the death of the tenant and had put a Greek woman in it. *The girl went to Rome,* his father wrote, in a line by itself, *to the widow Tertulla's, on the Caelian, as her mother had arranged. I tell you so that you know, and so that you understand that she is in a consular's house two streets from the patron's and is, by that, a thing the patron's house will notice. You are on Sura's staff. I do not need to tell you what Sura's staff does not do on the Caelian. You have done well. Better than I planned. Do not spend it.*

Vibia's said that Hilara had died in the window room four days after the girl sailed, with the old Rhodian midwife by her, and had asked for nothing, and had said one thing at the end, and that she, Vibia, had written it to the girl and would write it to him, because he had sat on her step and would want it, and it was *and?* And under that, in the even farm hand: *I do not know what it means. I expect you do. The Lares are fed. Chione sews. Your father goes to the tomb on the Kalends and stands, and comes back, and does not say. He has had the ship's eye gilded. I think he is proud of you in the only way he has, which is to add to a stone. Come home when you can. Not for him. For the gods, who like to see a face.*

He sat in the room with the window on the wall and the two letters on his knee and did not move for a long time.

*And?* He knew what it meant. He had stood in the burning fields and heard her say it to a corridor voice with the steam going up between them and had laughed until his eyes ran, and he had told her to say it to Thallus, and she had, and his mouth had moved the width of a stylus, and she had written him that, and he had read it in a tent in the rain in another life with a Greek asleep by the brazier; and now the woman who had taught it her had said it to death, in a window, with the sea in the glass, and it had been written down by a farm-wife and sent north in a packet and had waited six months in a Spaniard's house for a boy who had killed a man in the snow and not written since.

He did not cry. He had not, since the chandler's stair, and had decided on the mole that he would not, and a decision held. But he sat with the letters until the lamp went down, and when it had gone down he got up and went to Sura's steward in the dark and asked whether the staff would be dining out in the spring, when the city opened again, and the steward, who was a Tarraco man and did not ask why boys wanted things, said that the staff dined where Sura dined and Sura dined where he was asked, and that in the spring he was asked everywhere, and that the Caelian was on the list, the widow Tertulla's among them, because the widow went up to the empress on the Kalends and the Ides and Sura liked to know what the empress had been told.

"Why?" the steward said then, because he was a Tarraco man but not a fool.

"My father's patron is her cousin," Celer said. "Two streets up. I'd not want to be in her house and not know the shape of it."

The steward looked at him in the dark for a moment. "You're the one who reads faces," he said. "Sura says. Read mine." And when Celer said nothing: "It says *that's not why and I don't care.* Go to bed. The spring's a long way off and the city's full of people who'd like to know what the staff wants and you've just told one of them. Learn not to."

* * *

Sura dined at the widow Tertulla's on the third day before the Ides of April, with six of his people, and Celer was one, and she was in the room.

He had known she would be. He had known it the way he had known the four hundred and twenty jars and the three hundred and twelve thousand sesterces and the king's face on the hillside; he had done the sum and it came out the same three times, and he went up the Caelian in the litter behind Sura's with the sum done and his face arranged, and he went in through the door with the bronze hand on it, and through the atrium with the masks, and into the dining-room with its three couches and its lamps and its painted garden, and took the place at the foot of the third couch that was a secretary's, and looked at nobody, as he had been taught; and she was behind the lady's chair with a jug, and he saw her the way you saw a thing you had been looking at for five years with your eyes shut.

She had grown. That was all, and it was not all. She was fifteen and she had the lady's house on her the way he had Sura's tent on him, in the way of standing, the way of holding the jug, the way of being in a room and not in it; she had her hair up in the Roman way, plain, and a gown that fitted, which he had never seen her in, and her face was doing nothing, perfectly, the shutter to, and she was looking at the lady's cup. She did not look at him. He had expected that. The lady had told her once, in a small room, with the door thin, *next time, look*, and she was not looking, and he understood, in the first breath, that this was not disobedience; it was that the lady's lesson had been for a room where the two of them did not matter, and this was not that room.

The dinner began. Sura talked to the lady about the empress, and the lady talked to Sura about the Senate, and both of them told the other things that the other already knew and watched to see which things were new, and Celer, at the foot of the third couch, watched Sura's hands, as he was there to do, and did not watch the jug.

He watched the jug. He could not not. He watched it come forward to the lady's cup and go back, and come forward and go back, and he watched the hand on it, which was steady, which had been a water-carrier's and was not now, and once, when the lady reached back without turning and the jug was there before the hand had finished reaching, he saw Sura's eyes go to it, for the width of a stylus, and come back; and he thought, cold, *he's counted her. He counts everything. He's counted the girl behind the widow's chair who knows where the cup will be, the way he counted my father down a table,* and he found that he did not know whether what went through him was fear for her or something else that he had no column for.

And then, in the second hour, the lady said — to Sura, in the dry low voice that went exactly as far as she sent it — "Your secretary is my cousin's freedman's boy. Hermes's. The one who found the depot. I had him in this house three years ago behind Rufus's couch with a tablet and he's come back behind yours with a better one. Puteoli breeds them, it seems. I have one of its daughters behind me now. They were children together, in the same street. Did you know?"

Sura did not look at Celer. He looked at the lady, with the knife-face, and said, "I know what's in my own house, Tertulla. I'd not presume to know what's in yours."

"Then I'll tell you, since you've come up the hill to find out what the empress said and I'd rather give you something true than watch you guess. She's the girl who watched you. Three years ago, at the dinner for the entry, in Bassus's house, you asked who knew the fleet's figure and looked down the table to see who'd given it, and a water-girl of eleven behind my chair saw you do it and told me what you'd done and why, and I've kept her since, for that." The lady's shoulders moved. "And he's the boy who'd given it, or his father had, and you wrote his father's name in the margin of a list and he's been earning it since in a tent. So there you are. Two of Bassus's freedmen's children, in a room with the two of us, and neither of them has looked at the other once in two hours, and it's the only thing in this room anyone with eyes could see." A pause. "Boy. Look at her. Girl. Look at him. Do it now, in front of us, and get it done, so that the rest of us can eat."

He looked.

She looked.

It was across a room with lamps and three couches and the man who had made the emperor and the woman who faced the Palatine back and a dwarf on a stool and the lady's girls along the wall and a slave at his elbow with the wine; and it lasted the time it took to breathe twice, and he had been told once, on a mole, in the dark, that he could not read her, and he could not read her now. Her face did nothing. It did it perfectly. It was the face she had practised on a stair and a steward and a corridor voice in a field of steam, and it had been made for exactly this, for a room full of people who noticed who noticed, and it was flawless, and he thought — in the second breath, with the whole room watching him think it — *she has become the thing she said on the mole she would be. The paid number. The wax. She's picked it, and she's it, and the board can say what it likes.* And under that, where he kept the figure he could not find: *and she's looking at me the way she said she'd look at a Spaniard. At no cost. And it's costing her, I can see it costing her, and nobody else in this room can, and that's the only thing I've got.*

Then the lady said, "Good. That's done. Sura, the empress said this—" and the room let out its breath, and the jug went back to the cup, and he went back to the hands, and the dinner went on for another two hours and he could not afterwards have said one thing that was said in them.

In the atrium, going out, with the litters in the street and Sura already in his and the lady's people lining the walls, she was at the end of the line with the jug, by the door, because that was where the water-girl stood when the guests went, and he went past her at the distance that was civil, and did not stop, and did not turn his head, and said, to the door, in a voice that went no further than it was meant to, in Greek: "Vibia wrote me."

And she said, to the jug, in the same Greek, in the voice her mother used for eggs: "Then you know what it means."

"Yes."

"Then don't write it down." And then, as he was through the door, so low he was not sure afterwards that he had heard it and not made it: "You stopped. In January. Two letters, and then you stopped. I worked out the true part. You didn't need to send it."

He went down the steps to the litter. He got in. Sura, in his, across the street, was looking at nothing, with the knife-face, and as the bearers lifted he said, through the curtain, without turning his head, in the voice for one true thing a morning: "Well? You've looked. Tell me the sum."

Celer sat in the litter with his hands on his knees and the Caelian going by in the dark and the bronze hand on the door behind him, and he found that he had one, and that it was not the one he had done on the way up.

"She's the widow's," he said. "All the way through. She's picked it. There's nothing in that house she doesn't see and nothing she'd tell, and the widow knows it, and that's why she's behind the chair and not in the kitchen." He looked at the curtain. "And the widow told you about her on purpose. In front of me. So that you'd count her. So that when the widow needs a thing from your office, you'll remember there's a girl on the Caelian who can read a room the way I can read a sheet, and that she's not yours."

A silence from the other litter. Then: "Yes. That's the sum." And, after a street: "She'll be on the Palatine within the year. Tertulla's grooming her for it; that's what the jug is, it's a girl learning where a cup will be before the hand that wants it knows. The empress likes that in a woman. She has it herself." The bearers' feet went on the stones. "You'll not go to that house again unless I send you. Not because of the girl. Because of the widow, who has just shown me she can make two of my people look at each other in front of me and call it a kindness, and I'd rather she did that to someone else's." A pause. "And boy. Whatever it was she said at the door, in Greek, that you think I didn't hear. Don't write it down."

"No, sir," Celer said. "I'd been told."
