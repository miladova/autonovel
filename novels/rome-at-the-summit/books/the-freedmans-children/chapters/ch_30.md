# A Hundred and Twenty Feet

He had been in the hut since the end of the fourth hour of the night, because the alternative was his own room, with the slate on the table and the lamp in the stair-window that he had not lit and had not been able to stop looking at.

Blaesus was asleep across the door on a sack, as he slept, with his mouth open and no sound coming out of it, since he could not hear himself snore and so, Apollodorus said, had never learned to. Celer stepped over him. The hut smelled of lime and tallow and the Syrian's wine. He lit the one lamp from the banked brazier and sat on the rod-chest and opened the day's sheet and did not write on it. The figure for the footing was already in his head and had been for a week; what the Syrian wanted was the figure on a sheet in the hand the Senate could read, and the hand the Senate could read needed a flat surface and a steady lamp and a man whose attention was on it, and he had the first two.

He got up and did what he did when a figure would not sit. He checked the plumb-lines. There were eleven, coiled on their pegs along the back wall, each with a lead weight the size of a hen's egg and the Syrian's mark cut into the lead, and he took each one down and ran it through his fingers from the peg to the weight for a kink or a fray, the way his father had run a ship's cable through his hands on the mole at Puteoli with his eyes shut, and hung each one back. None was frayed. He had checked them the morning before. He checked the rods: the twelve-foot, the six, the three, the bronze pins tight in the seasoned oak, the oak itself for a warp against the lamp. None was warped. He put the lid on the chest and sat on it again.

Through the hut's one window the sky over the Esquiline had begun to be a colour. Not light; the thing before light. The cut was below him in it, a hill's height of nothing with the string-lines across the floor, square after square, pale in the dark where the lime had dried on them. At the far end, by the shoulder, the ladder stood against the face where he had left it.

He went down.

The engineers' stair first, cut in the tufa, forty-one steps, which he had counted the first morning in November and not since; and then along the floor of the cut in the near-dark with the string-lines under his feet, stepping over each where it crossed, because you did not walk on a Syrian's line; and the walls going up on either side of him a hundred and twenty feet to a sky that was now, when he looked, grey. The tufa breathed cold. There was the smell it had, which he had not been able to name in seven months and which Blaesus, who could not smell it, said was the smell of the inside of a hill that had never been outside before. His boots were loud. At the far end the shoulder of the Quirinal came down sliced like a cheese, the strata in it showing, brown on grey on brown, and the ladder against it, and he put his back to the ladder and his face to the length of the cut and stood, with the stick in his belt and his hands at his sides, and the light came down the face behind him a foot at a time, and he waited, as he had waited every morning for seven months for a thing he had told himself he was not waiting for.

He heard her before he saw anything. Sand, on the edge above, where there had been no sand yesterday.

He did not turn round, and she stood a pace to his left where she had sat at twelve, and the light was in the bottom of the pit, and he found that the thing he had been told to have after the question was not a thing he had, and that he was going to have to make it, out loud, on the edge of a hole, in a hand only one other person could read, with that person beside him.

"You left me off the sheet," he said. To the pit.

"Yes."

"Why."

She did not answer at once. Below them the Syrian's string-lines ran across the floor of the cut in the grey, square after square, where a basilica would be.

"A man in the prefect's office would have asked whether the boy knew," she said. "And when. And what the vines were bought with."

"That's the figure. I asked why."

"You've a freedwoman who kept a merchant's books. She'll have told you."

"She told me you took the lower figure and looked at the woman while you did it. She said she couldn't tell if it was the cruellest thing or the kindest."

"No," said Fausta. "Nor can I." And then, after a moment, as if it were a smaller thing: "I've a book now. I've written it both ways, on facing leaves. I read them. It doesn't help."

He stood. He had come with a list and the list was going, item by item, into the pit, the way the dust went in the evening.

"I wrote the finding," he said. "Four hundred and eighty. My name under it, and the ring."

"I know."

"He said it was just. In a letter. He said *that's the and*." He stopped. "He had the stroke doing the sum of what was left, and he wrote *F* on a tablet, and I said *the stone*, and he let me."

She turned her head then — not round; a degree, toward the shoulder of the Quirinal, where the light was. "Vibia wrote me. She drew the letter. She couldn't read it."

"You wrote to him. In the hand. After. Knowing."

"Knowing you'd find it." The ivory at the side of her head took the light and he did not look at it. "There's nobody else alive can read that hand."

"*The same reason you've not called the villa.*"

"Yes."

"I don't know what that is."

"Yes you do."

The dwarf on the chest did not move. Somewhere behind them in the hut a deaf clerk was waking, and would not hear, and the pit filled by degrees to the floor.

"My mother said *and?*," she said. "I took it for a reckoning. It wasn't. She was asking." She let the shorthand carry it; she had never been able to say a thing in Latin that she could say in the hand. "So. I'm asking. You said once you'd nothing after it, and burned the sheet. Chrestê told the wine-shop. The wine-shop told a dwarf." A breath. "Have something this time."

He had been told, by a woman over a slate, to stop being good at nothing for one morning. He had been told by a man at a table with the sky on it that there was always a next. He had not been told what to say, and he found, on the edge of a hole a hill's height with the morning in it, that he had no column for it and was going to have to do it the way he had done a bill at eleven, with his eyes on the thing and not on her.

"Not for nothing," he said. "You said it. I said *all right*, and put it in the column, and I've been six years doing the other thing because I thought *all right* was the sum." The light had reached the floor. "I'll go to Hadrian when Lucius is dead. You'll be in the room. He'll look at you and you'll say your sentence and he'll laugh, and ask again. That's the hill's sum and it's right; I've done it a hundred times on a stool." He breathed. "I'd like, once, to turn round. I know nobody on a mole does. I'm asking what it costs."

She was quiet so long that he thought the hour had gone. Then, in the shorthand, very level:

"The hill."

"Say the figure."

"The day either of us is the other's, we're priced. She keeps us because she can't price us. She's said so, in her way; she's given me back a letter this morning to see what stays true without it." She did not look at him. "By noon they'd all know — she'd know, he'd know, Sabina would see my back. And then you're Sura's clerk who went to the cut with the Augusta's hair, and I'm the hair, and he's a man who learns." A pause. "That's the figure. I've paid worse. I'd not pay it for *once*."

He had known the sum before she said it. He had asked anyway, which was new, and she had answered as a manifest, with no *I* in it, and under the manifest, where he could read because he had been taught by her mother's slate and his father's margin, was a thing that was not *no*.

"Then when."

"When the column's up." She said it the way the Syrian said the time. "A hundred and twenty feet. He says six years. Then it's a column and not a hole, and she's sixty-two, and he's whatever he is by then, and we're a thing the hill's tried to price for six years and couldn't, and that's the only kind of thing up there that's ever let be its own." Her voice did not move. "That's not *no*. You can read it."

"Six years."

"I counted five for a man in Africa. I can count six for a column."

Six years. He would be twenty-six. He had been taught the long game in a strong-room with the lid off and had never once played it for anything that was not a figure.

"Then I'll measure the column," he said. "Every course. I'll be at the far end before the gangs. It's where the man stands who did the figure."

"I know. I've a terrace."

The light lay on the pit's floor, flat and grey and whole.

"Chrestê's gone to a fuller's," he said.

"I heard." A pause. "I liked her over a slate when I was fourteen. I'd not have said so." She did not move. "There's a thing I'll give you. Not the arm. A paper." Her hand went to her gown and came out and held it between them, at the height of the pit's edge, without turning: a folded sheet, soft at the folds, a brass fish in the wax. "Tingis. In my hand. Asking. She gave it back this morning." The shorthand, level. "You've a shelf. Don't read it. You know what it says."

He took it. It weighed nothing. He put it inside the tunic against the skin, and his hand touched hers on the paper for the length of a breath and then did not, because the paper was given and the arm was not; and it was the arm, he understood, and the length of it, and it was shorter than it had been.

And then he turned round.

Not to her. Away from the pit, toward the near end of the cut, because there was a sound there, which was a Syrian with lime on his arms coming along the edge with four engineers and a deaf clerk carrying a rod, shouting, in the Greek, "There. The weigher. And — ha. The dwarf's measure." He arrived. He looked at the two of them a pace apart on the edge of the pit with the morning on them, and whatever he thought about it stayed under the lime. "One at each end. I'll not set a thing a hill's height on one pair of hands. Then she goes up and you come to the hut and I've seen a rod." He held it out. "Take it."

* * *

She took the rod.

That was what she would keep of it, in the book, that night, on the cold floor, with Sabina asleep and her hair wrong: not what had been said, which was in the hand and would stay there; the rod. Twelve feet of seasoned oak with the Syrian's marks on it in bronze pins, that Blaesus put into her hands at one end as if she were an engineer, because he was deaf and had not been told she was anything else, and that the shipowner's son took at the other, and they went down into the pit by the ladder the gangs used — she went first; he did not offer the hand and she did not look for it — and stood on the floor of the Syrian's footing a hundred and twenty feet below the city with the morning coming down the sliced shoulder onto them and the rod between them, and measured.

Apollodorus called it from the edge. The side; the diagonal; the depth from the string to the floor; the offset from the shoulder's foot. She held her end to the mark and he held his and the Syrian read the bronze pins through the glass he had and Blaesus wrote in the loud flat hand of a man who could not hear what he was writing, and the figures were right, each one, because the Syrian had dug the pit to them and was measuring only to be told so by two pairs of hands that had kept a book; and between the calls, on the floor of the pit, twelve feet apart, with the rod level between them, they did not speak. The morning came down the sliced shoulder onto the two of them a hill's depth below the city, and a man above read what it weighed.

"Level," the Syrian called. "Good. Hold it. Last one." And then, lower, to the engineers, in the Greek he thought the pit would not carry, and the pit carried everything: "Look at that. Neither of them's moved. I've had legionaries on that rod who couldn't hold it for a plumb-line."

"Level," said Blaesus, loud, writing.

"Then that's the course." Apollodorus straightened. "Bring it up. The gangs are at the Curia; I can hear them. Up, both. You" — to her — "to your hill, by the new road; the Guard will not have seen you. You" — to him — "to the hut, with Blaesus; I want the footing's figure on a sheet by the third hour and the first course set by the sixth, and the emperor will come at the ninth to see a stone where a hill was."

She went up the ladder. He held the foot of it, because that was what was done with a ladder, and she did not look down, and at the top the dwarf was on the chest with his legs out and the seamed face doing nothing, and he said, as she came over the edge, in the voice that was for the end of a corridor, "I counted. I didn't hear." He got down. "Come on. Sabina's hair."

She went along the edge. She did not look back at the pit. She had held one end of a rod level for a quarter of an hour and the other end had not moved, and she was going up a hill now to a room with one window and a woman with a cloth who had decided not to know, and would know, and would say nothing.

At the near end, where the new road went down past the Curia, the gangs were coming up. Three hundred men with baskets and mattocks in a column, with the overseers, going to take a hill away a basket at a time as they had every morning for a year but one; and she stood aside with the dwarf to let them by, and they went past her into the cut without looking, because she was a tall girl with her hair tied back and a pin and a dwarf and the Syrian had people at the pit every morning; and they went down the ramps onto the floor and the first basket went into the first barrow and the first mattock went into the red, and the dust came up.

It came up as it did, all at once, as a thing breathed out, from three hundred men and a year's practice, and stood over the cut in the still June air the way it had stood every morning she had watched from the west terrace, and went up, and spread, and began to settle on everything, on the Forum and the Curia and the Capitol's steps and the Palatine and the Augusta's one window, which would have to be shut, and on the far end, where a man was climbing out of a pit with a rod over his shoulder and a deaf clerk behind him and a folded paper with a brass fish on it against his skin, and did not look up at the near end; the Syrian had said *the hut by the third hour*, and he had six years, and she could see from the set of the shoulders, even through the dust, what he had decided to spend them doing.

"Girl," said Momus, beside her.

"Yes."

"You've a face on you like a slate that's come out."

"It hasn't." She turned up the new road toward the hill. "There's a thing left over."

"There always is," said the dwarf, and fell in beside her on the short legs, and they went up through the dust, which was the colour of the cut, and of the sand on the Sacred Way the morning after a triumph, and of the ash a mole was built on.

* * *

Marcia was on the covered stair, halfway, where neither end could see, and stood up, and did not ask, and looked at the girl's face in the light that was light now, and at the hair tied back, and at the dust on the hem of the gown, which was the colour of the cut and had not been there when she went down.

"You've been in it," the freedwoman said. "Not at it. In it. There's tufa on you to the knee."

"I held a rod."

"Did you." Marcia looked at the hem a moment longer and then at the face, and the round brown face came unarranged, as it had once in a steward's room with Germans in the atrium, which was not be arranged. "I'll not ask. I said. But I'll tell you one thing, for nothing, which is the first for-nothing in forty years and I'd like it marked: the Augusta woke at the first hour and lay with her eyes open and did not call for the cloth, and at the half she said to the ceiling, *Marcia, is it dusty*, and I said *not yet, Augusta, the gangs aren't in*, and she said *tell me when it is*, and turned over." The freedwoman sat back down on the step. "So I'll go up now and tell her it's dusty. That's all she's asked. It's the only question she's got left that she's allowed herself, and I think it's the one she means, and I think you know what it means, and I'd not have you go into that room without knowing she's asked it."

She went up. The courts. The fountain that worked. The north rooms, where Sabina was at the glass with her hair down and the beautiful disappointed face turned to the door before it opened, and looked at the girl who came through it, and at the hem, and at the hair tied back, and at the face that did nothing, perfectly; and said nothing; and turned back to the glass; and said, to it, in the low quick voice, after a while: "Your back's different. I said I'd know. I do." A pause. "Don't tell me. The new way. The pins on the left; he's coming from the library at the third hour and I want him to not know me, and then to know me, and then to look past me at the corner, and I want to see what his face does when the corner's got tufa on its hem."

She did Sabina's hair. The new way. And at the third hour the Spaniard came from the library in the Greek cloak with no dust on it, because he had been in a room with the door shut, and stood in the door of the cold rooms and looked at his wife in the glass and did not know her for a moment, and then did, and then looked past her to the corner; and the grey eyes found a tall girl with a box of pins and her own hair tied back and the Syrian's hill on her hem to the knee, and rested; and the face did not do the thing. It did the other, the one from the library, across four scrolls: it simply looked, as a man looked at a column that he had decided not to know the figure for and had been handed the figure anyway, by a hem.

"Puteoli," he said, in the Greek. "You've been to see the Syrian's hole."

"Yes, Hadrian."

"Was it as deep as he says?"

"A hundred and twenty feet. I held one end of the rod. The Syrian read it. It was right."

"One end." The grey eyes did not move. "And the other?"

She stood in the corner with her feet under her and the floor there, and it was not paper, and she had decided on a terrace to stop pretending about what was true, only about what it cost, and she knew what this cost, because she had said the figure to a hole an hour ago with no *I* in it, and she said it anyway, because the doors on this hill were labelled and this one said *Hadrian* and she would not begin six years by lying to the one man who would spend them learning.

"The boy who weighed the gold. The Syrian wanted two pairs of hands. He said he'd not set a thing a hill's height on a figure made by one."

The Spaniard stood in the door. Behind him, at the glass, Sabina had gone very still with a pin in her hand. And the face did, at last, the thing — interesting, and a little short of what it might have been — and then, under it, where she had learned in a low chair to read, the other thing, the one that was not quite patience and was what patience was made of.

"Two pairs of hands," he said. "Yes. He'd say that. He's a Syrian; he counts in pairs." He turned from the door, and stopped, and said to the corridor, not to the room, in the Greek: "I was in the library. From the first hour. The door was shut. I'd like that known. I read Sappho; the bad copy; the apple on the topmost bough that the pickers couldn't reach. I've read it a hundred times and this morning I noticed, for the first time, that she never says whether anyone got a ladder." He went. "Sabina. Plotina's room. Bring the girl. The window's being shut; it's dusty; she's asked for her hair done again because the first was for a box and this is for a receiving day, and she says she wants it done by someone who's been down the hill this morning and can tell her what it weighs."

The Augusta had the cloth on. The window was shut, and the room with one window was close already in June with eleven women on the benches outside the door and the dust standing beyond the glass in the light like a thing breathed out; and Marcia was at the shoulder with the comb and said *the old way*, as she did, and Fausta put the box down and began, and the woman on the couch under the cloth said nothing for the whole of it, which was the thing, and when the last pin was in and she had turned her head once to the left and once to the right and said *that's it, that's my mother's*, she said one more thing, to the ceiling, in the voice that was not for metre and that Fausta had heard perhaps five times and would hear, she understood now, once a year for as long as they both lived, on the morning after whatever the year's sum had been.

"Is it dusty, girl?"

"Yes, Augusta. The gangs went in at the second hour. Three hundred. It'll be on everything by noon."

"Good." The cloth did not move. "Then the hill's being taken away and I've decided not to know by whom, and I'll have the window shut for six years, and Marcia will tell me each morning that it's dusty, and that's the whole of what I'll ask. Six years. The Syrian says. Then it's a column." A silence under the cloth that was the silence of a woman who had, for the first time in thirty years, a thing in a column she could not price and had decided to keep it there. "Go and sit with Sabina. The room may come in. And girl. The hem. Have it brushed before the benches see it, not because I mind — I've decided not to mind; it's new — but because Tertulla's son's letters from Tingis are getting longer and I'd not have the fourth bench write to Africa that the Augusta's hair came in from the cut with the Syrian's hill on it. He's patient. Let him be patient about nothing."

She went down the long wall behind the comb. The benches rose.

* * *

He did not go back to the hut until the figure was on the sheet.

He did it at the Syrian's table with Blaesus at his elbow reading each line as it went down and nodding, loud, as he nodded, and when it was done he signed it with his name in the hand the Senate could read, and Apollodorus took it and looked at it and put it inside his tunic against the lime without a word, and that was the base of a column seen to. He went up the engineers' stair. Forty-one. He went out past the Curia, where the Guard who had let him through at the first hour of yesterday looked at the stick and not at the face, and up the Sacred Way through the sweepers, and the Velabrum, and the long climb of the Aventine, and the stair.

The room was as he had left it. That was the first thing, and he stood in the door and had it: the table, the slate on the table with the Kalends figure in her hand and the line under it and nothing after, the stool, the second stool, the bed, the shelf. The lamp in the stair-window, unlit. And the things that were not there, which he counted, standing, because counting was what he had: the comb that had been on the shelf, the red cloak on the peg, the Corinthian pot she had kept her pins in. The sandals under the bed. The second cup. She had taken what was hers and nothing that was his, down to a knife she had bought with her own money and used on his bread, and the shelf where the comb had been had a clean place on it the length of a comb, and the rest of the shelf had dust.

He fed the gods. Hermes's two, and the one of his own that he had bought on the Aventine in the first month, from the boxes under them: barley, a little oil, a splash of the wine from the jar which was two days old and had not been poured since the feast. The small bronze faces took it as they took everything. He stood a moment with the dish in his hand and looked at the boxes under the shelf, the eleven boxes, with Hermes's figures in them in the hand he had been taught, and the twelfth box, which was empty and had a lid, and had been empty since April. He did not open any of them.

Then he sat down at the table and took up the stylus and turned the slate over to the clean side and wrote, in the Latin hand, for nobody, *VIII Id. Iun. — footing figure to the Syrian; course set; measure level,* and under it, after a while, in the other hand, the one only one other person could read, a thing that was four strokes long and that he looked at for the time it took the light to move from the table's edge to the slate, and then rubbed out with the ball of his thumb; and he turned the slate back over to the side with her figure on it, and the line, and nothing after, and left it so, and went down the stair with the lamp unlit to find a cook-shop that was open and eat something, because he had not since the feast and a man who was going to walk into Sura's house in the morning and be told what was next needed to have eaten.

On the stair, at the turn, where the window was, he stopped and looked out at the city with the dust standing over the Forum in the middle of it, and the hill beyond the dust, and the terrace on the hill, which was too far to show a figure on it, and was not too far to know there was one. Nobody looked at her hem, because nobody on that hill looked at a hair-girl's hem, and that was the hill, and she had decided it was a house.

On the west terrace, that evening, she opened the book on her knees at the leaf after the triumph and sat with the ink and did not write. Momus, on the parapet, watched her not write for the time it took the light to go off the Capitol, and said nothing, and when she closed it with the leaf blank he said, "Good. Some things you keep by not putting them down. I've a whole corridor of them."

And on the west terrace, that evening, with the dwarf on the parapet and the city below going about its noise and the cut beyond the Forum with the first course of a column's base set in it on a figure two pairs of hands had held level, the dust that had gone up at the second hour was coming down, slowly, in the long June light, on everything, on the Forum and the Capitol's steps and the Augusta's shut window and the box on its posts and the sand of the Sacred Way and a hut where a man was writing a footing's figure on a sheet with a deaf clerk and a folded paper with a brass fish on it against his skin; and it was the colour it was; and it would come down every evening for six years on everything in Rome; and when the column stood, and the dust stopped, people looked up at it and said *a hundred and twenty feet* and did not ask who had measured, because nobody on that hill thought a rod had two ends.
