# A Hundred and Twenty Feet

He did not turn round, and she stood a pace to his left where she had stood at thirteen, and the light was in the bottom of the pit, and he found that the thing he had been told to have after the question was not a thing he had, and that he was going to have to make it, out loud, on the edge of a hole, in a hand only one other person could read, with that person beside him.

"You left me off the sheet," he said. To the pit. It was the first thing, and he had read it four ways on a stool and a fifth over a slate and he said it as a figure, with no *I* that could be left out, because that was how she had said the sail and it was the only voice either of them trusted.

"Yes."

"Why."

"You were the higher figure." The eggs voice; the mole; the shorthand. "A man in the prefect's office would have asked whether the boy knew, and when, and whether the vines were bought with the sail. I took the lower and looked at her while I did it, so she'd know I'd seen the higher was there." A breath. "Chrestê told you that. In the wine-shop's words. She was right. She watched me do it to Granius's factor for a year."

"She said it was the cruellest thing or the kindest and she couldn't tell."

"Neither could I. I've a book now. Sabina gave it me. I've written it both ways on facing leaves and I read them and I still can't."

He stood. The pit was full of light to the bottom and the sliced shoulder above it was the colour it was, and behind them the dwarf on the chest did not move.

"I wrote the finding," he said. "The figure. Four hundred and eighty. Sura made me and I'd have made me in his place. I signed it with my name and the ring. It's on the roll under my father's fine and Capito's put a line under that in his own hand about the rock." He kept the voice. "He said the fine was just. My father. In a letter. He said *I knew what I was doing and so did every carrier in this port and I'm the one who wrote it down because I'm the one who writes things down.* He said *that's the and.* He thought it meant the sum's done." He stopped. "He had a stroke doing the sum of what was left and wrote *F.* on a tablet and I said *the stone* and he let me. It was the only lie he ever told me and he told it by nodding."

"I know. Vibia wrote me. Not the lie; the tablet. She can't read the hand and she drew the letter."

"You wrote to him. After. In the shorthand. Knowing he couldn't read it."

"Knowing you'd find it." She did not look at him. Her hair was tied back like a girl's with a slate and there was a thing in it at the side, ivory, with gold on a break, that he had never seen and that caught the light coming down the shoulder, and he did not look at it either, because he was on a mole. "I'd nothing else to write it in. You and I are the only two people alive who can read that hand. I wanted it read by someone who'd know what it cost to write and the only one was you and the only way to get it to you without the hill seeing was to send it to a dead man's strong-room." A pause. "That's the cruellest thing or the kindest. Leaf three."

"*The same reason you've not called the villa.*"

"Yes."

"I don't know what that is."

"Yes you do. You've known since a mole. You said it at thirteen with a hull going to ash in front of you and I said *all right* because I thought *all right* was the price." Her voice did not change; the eggs voice did not; it was the voice she had told a dying woman's slate to an Alexandrian in and a sail to a comb and she was using it now, he understood, because it was the only one that would hold. "It wasn't the price. It was the thing left over. My mother said *and?* and I thought for nine years she was telling. She was asking. She'd done her sum and there was a thing left over and she wanted to know what it would do next." She stopped. "So. I'm asking. You said once you'd nothing after it and burned the sheet; Chrestê told the wine-shop; the dwarf told me. Have something this time."

He stood on the edge of a hill being taken away a basket at a time with a dead centurion's stick in his belt and the question in the air between them in a hand nobody else could read, and did the sum, and it came out, and he did not say the figure. He said the thing that was left over, because he had been told by a woman over a slate to stop being good at nothing for one morning, and by a man at a table with the sky on that there was always a next, and by a dying Spaniard that a mole was *side by side, looking at the same thing*, and the same thing was a hole a hundred and twenty feet deep with the morning in it, and she was looking at it, and so was he.

"Not for nothing," he said. "I said it at thirteen. I've been nine years doing the other thing because you said *all right* and I thought that was the sum. It wasn't. It's this." He did not turn his head. He had never been able to say a thing to her face; he had read a bill to her with his eyes on the bill. "I'll go to the Spaniard when Sura's dead, with my eyes open, as you told the Augusta, and hate it, as you told her, and be the best he's got and the only one he'll never own. You'll be in the room. Sabina's, and the Augusta's, and the library in the afternoons in a low chair, and he'll look at you, and you'll say *what does it cost* and he'll laugh, and he'll ask again, because he's patient, and one day you'll have to decide what you are. That's the hill's sum and it's right and I've done it a hundred times on a stool." He breathed. "And there's a thing left over. There's two people from a coast who read the same bill at eleven and have never once in nine years stood in the same place and said the true thing to each other's faces, because they were taught by a shipowner and a dying woman not to, and because the one time they tried it was on a mole and one of them held the other off at the length of her arm. I'd like to not do that. I'd like, once, to turn round. I know nobody on a mole does. I'm asking what it costs."

The dwarf on the chest did not move. The light stood in the pit. Somewhere behind them, in the hut, a deaf clerk was waking, and would not hear.

She was quiet for a long time, and he did not look, and then she said, in the eggs voice, to the hole, with no *I* that could be left out: "It costs the hill."

"Say the figure."

"The Augusta bought two people from a coast for a sail and a man in Africa and she's decided not to know what she paid for because for the first time in thirty years she can't do the sum. The thing she can't do it on is that we're nobody's. *The only one you'll never own*; she's kept the sentence and given me back the other, this morning, on a stair, through Marcia. She wants a thing in the room that hasn't been priced. The day either of us is the other's, we're priced. We're furniture. She'll know by noon — she's decided not to know and she'll know anyway, because the hill is her — and the Spaniard will know, because he's in his library with the door shut deciding not to know, and Sabina will know because she'll see my back, and Sura will know because he'll ask the one question he's said he'll never ask, and the two people nobody owns will be a shipowner's son and his girl from the same street, which is a thing the hill has a word for and a box to put it in. And then I'm Tertulla's hair-girl who went to the cut with a freedman's boy, and you're Sura's clerk who went to the cut with the Augusta's hair, and the Spaniard's a man who learns, and he'll learn that, and he'll not look at me in a low chair again, he'll look at you, and not the way he does now." She stopped. "That's the figure. It's the whole of what we've got. I've paid worse. I've the receipts. But I'd not pay it for *once*. I'd pay it for the thing that's left over, and I don't know yet what that weighs, and neither do you, because we've never stood in the same place long enough to find out, and we've got an hour, and it's half gone."

He stood. The sum was right. He had known it was right before she said it, because he had done it on a stool a hundred times, and he had asked anyway, because he had decided to be the man who asked, once, with his eyes open; and she had answered as a manifest, with no *I*, and the manifest said *not yet*, and under the manifest, where he could read because he had been taught by her mother's slate and his father's margin, it said something else, and he read it, and it was not *no*.

"Then when."

"When the column's up." She said it without weight, as the Syrian said the time. "A hundred and twenty feet. He says six years. Then it's a column and not a hole, and the Augusta's sixty-two, and Sura's dead, and the Spaniard's whatever he is by then, and Sabina's had six more years of a slate on a floor, and you've been the best man he's got for five of them and I've said *what does it cost* in a low chair till he's stopped laughing, and then we'll both be a thing the hill can't price *because* it's tried for six years and failed, and that's the only kind of thing on that hill that's ever allowed to be its own." Her voice did not move. "That's not *no*. You can read it. I wrote it so you could."

"Six years."

"I counted five for a man in Africa. I can count six for a column."

He did the sum. Six years. He would be twenty-six. Sura would be dead and the boxes would be the Spaniard's and he would have read the third room to a man who read for eleven, and she would have been in a low chair, and the hill would have had six years to put a word to what they were and would have had, every morning, a freedman's son at the far end of the cut with a rod, measuring, because the Syrian had asked, and nobody on the hill had anything to say about a man who measured; and it was the long game; and it was hers; and he had been taught it by his father in a strong-room with the lid off and had never once played it for anything that was not a figure.

"Then I'll measure the column," he said. "Every course. For six years. I'll be at the far end before the gangs. It's where the man stands who did the figure. Nobody on the hill has anything to say about it."

"I know. I've a terrace. I can see the far end from it." The light had reached the pit's floor and lay there flat and grey and whole. "Momus counts you in."

"Chrestê says."

"Chrestê's gone to a fuller's."

"Yes."

"She's clever. I liked her over a slate when I was fourteen and I'd not have said so. She told the wine-shop everything you'd never say so I'd know it without you, and then left so she'd not be the woman on the stair. That's a kind I know. We keep the receipt." She did not move. "There's a thing I'll give you. Not the arm. Not yet. A paper." Her hand went to her gown, and came out, and held it between them at the height of the pit's edge without turning: a folded sheet, soft at the folds, with a brass fish in the wax. "Tingis. In my hand, to Marcia, asking. It's the price of a man in Africa. The Augusta's given it back this morning so she can see if the sentence she's kept stays true without it. I've decided not to keep it on me. I've decided you've a shelf." The eggs voice. "Put it under your gods with the others. Don't read it. You know what it says. It's a receipt and you're the one who writes things down."

He took it. It weighed nothing. He put it inside the tunic against the skin where his father had carried a sail-maker's bill in his head for ten years, and his hand touched hers for the length of a flame leaning, on the paper, and then did not, because the paper was given and the arm was not; and he understood, with the sum done and six years in it and a receipt against his skin, that she had just told him the only true thing she had never said on a mole, which was that she trusted him to hold the thing she had paid, and that this was the arm, and the length of it, and that it was shorter than it had been.

And then he turned round.

Not to her. Away from the pit, toward the near end of the cut, because there was a sound there, which was a Syrian with lime on his arms coming along the edge with four engineers and a deaf clerk carrying a rod, shouting, in the Greek, "There. The weigher. And — ha. The dwarf's measure. Lucius said. Good. *Good.* You've both looked at it; now you'll hold the rod, the two of you, one at each end, because I'll not have a thing a hill's height set on a figure made by one pair of hands, and Blaesus can't hear a plumb-line; and when the course is set you'll go, her up and you to the hut, and I'll have seen nothing, because I'm a Syrian and I don't do the thing where a man lets other men find out." He arrived. He looked at the two of them standing a pace apart on the edge of the pit with the morning on them, and the Damascus face did a thing that was not quite a smile and was the look of a man who had cut a hill in half and knew what it cost. "Well. You've turned round. One of you. Lucius owes Hadrian a bet and he'll pay it from his couch and die happy. Take the rod."

* * *

She took the rod.

That was what she would keep of it, in the book, that night, on the cold floor, with Sabina asleep and her hair wrong: not what had been said, which was in the hand and would stay there; the rod. Twelve feet of seasoned oak with the Syrian's marks on it in bronze pins, that Blaesus put into her hands at one end as if she were an engineer, because he was deaf and had not been told she was anything else, and that the shipowner's son took at the other, and they went down into the pit by the ladder the gangs used — she went first; he did not offer the hand and she did not look for it — and stood on the floor of the Syrian's footing a hundred and twenty feet below the city with the morning coming down the sliced shoulder onto them and the rod between them, and measured.

Apollodorus called it from the edge. The side; the diagonal; the depth from the string to the floor; the offset from the shoulder's foot. She held her end to the mark and he held his and the Syrian read the bronze pins through the glass he had and Blaesus wrote in the loud flat hand of a man who could not hear what he was writing, and the figures were right, each one, because the Syrian had dug the pit to them and was measuring only to be told so by two pairs of hands that had kept a book; and between the calls, on the floor of the pit, twelve feet apart, with the rod level between them, they did not speak, and did not need to, and she understood that this was what Sura had meant and the Spaniard had not: not standing side by side looking out, which was a thing children did on a mole. Holding the two ends of a thing level, with a hill's height of morning coming down on you, while a man above read what it weighed.

"Level," the Syrian called. "Good. Hold it. Last one." And then, lower, to the engineers, in the Greek he thought the pit would not carry, and the pit carried everything: "Look at that. Neither of them's moved. I've had legionaries on the ends of that rod who couldn't keep it still for a plumb-line, and those two have kept it level for a quarter of an hour without looking at each other once, and I don't know what they are, and I'm not going to ask, because Lucius says it's the only thing on the hill the Augusta can't do the sum on, and I'd not take that from her; she's got little enough."

"Level," said Blaesus, loud, writing.

"Then that's the course." Apollodorus straightened. "Bring it up. The gangs are at the Curia; I can hear them. Up, both. You" — to her — "to your hill, by the new road; the Guard will not have seen you. You" — to him — "to the hut, with Blaesus; I want the footing's figure on a sheet by the third hour and the first course set by the sixth, and the emperor will come at the ninth to see a stone where a hill was, and he'll ask who measured, and I'll say *the boy who weighed the gold*, and he'll say *good*, and nobody will ask who held the other end, because nobody on that hill thinks a rod has two."

She went up the ladder. He held the foot of it, because that was what was done with a ladder, and she did not look down, and at the top the dwarf was on the chest with his legs out and the walnut face doing nothing, and he said, as she came over the edge, in the voice that was for the end of a corridor, "I counted. I didn't hear. A quarter of an hour with a rod level and the Syrian talking Greek to a pit. That's a thing I'll not see twice." He got down. "Come on. Sabina's hair. The new way. She'll want to be told nothing."

She went along the edge. She did not look back at the pit, because she was on a mole and had decided on a terrace by a sea to stop pretending about what was true, only about what it cost; and what was true was that she had held one end of a rod level for a quarter of an hour on the floor of a hole a hill's height and that the other end had not moved, and that she had given a man a receipt to keep under his gods and told him six years, and that the arm was shorter than it had been and was still the arm; and what it cost was that she was going up a hill now to a room with one window and a woman with a cloth who had decided not to know, and would know, and would say nothing, which on that hill was the loudest thing there was.

At the near end, where the new road went down past the Curia, the gangs were coming up. Three hundred men with baskets and mattocks in a column, with the overseers, going to take a hill away a basket at a time as they had every morning for a year but one; and she stood aside with the dwarf to let them by, and they went past her into the cut without looking, because she was a tall girl with her hair tied back and a pin and a dwarf and the Syrian had people at the pit every morning; and they went down the ramps onto the floor and the first basket went into the first barrow and the first mattock went into the red, and the dust came up.

It came up as it did, all at once, as a thing breathed out, from three hundred men and a year's practice, and stood over the cut in the still June air the way it had stood every morning she had watched from the west terrace, and went up, and spread, and began to settle on everything, on the Forum and the Curia and the Capitol's steps and the Palatine and the Augusta's one window, which would have to be shut, and on the far end, where a man was climbing out of a pit with a rod over his shoulder and a deaf clerk behind him and a folded paper with a brass fish on it against his skin, and did not look up at the near end, because he was on a mole, and because the Syrian had said *the hut by the third hour*, and because he had six years to look and had decided, she could see from the set of the shoulders even through the dust, to spend them measuring.

"Girl," said Momus, beside her.

"Yes."

"You've a face on you like a slate that's come out."

"It hasn't." She turned up the new road toward the hill. "There's a thing left over."

"There always is," said the dwarf. "That's what the column's for. So nobody forgets there was a hill." And he fell in beside her, on the short legs, and they went up through the dust, which was the colour of the cut, and of the sand on the Sacred Way the morning after a triumph, and of the ash a hull went to on a mole in February when two children stood and watched it and one of them said *not for nothing* and the other said *all right*; and behind them the Syrian's first course went down into the footing on a figure two pairs of hands had held level, and would stand, a hundred and twenty feet, when everyone who had stood at the end of a cut that morning was dead, so that nobody would forget; and nobody did; and nobody, looking up at it, ever once asked who had held the other end of the rod, because nobody on that hill thought a rod had two, and the two who knew had six years to count, and had begun.
