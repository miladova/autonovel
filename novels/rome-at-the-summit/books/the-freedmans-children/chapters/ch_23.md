# The Hearth-Stone

She taught the Spaniard's wife a lodging-house book in the mornings, and the Spaniard's wife was quick, and it was the first thing in Fausta's life that she had given away for nothing and watched be taken.

They did it on the floor. Sabina would not have a table; she said tables were for men with sheets, and sat on the cold floor of the north rooms in a gown worth the Dolphin's lease ten times with a slate on her knees, as a girl had sat on a lodging-house's bottom stair, and Fausta sat across from her with the second slate and made up a house. Eleven rooms. A Rhodian who drank. A sail-maker who paid in canvas and called it coin. A merchant's woman who came once a month with a factor's figure that was what he wished he'd drunk. And Sabina did the sums, in a hand that had been taught by a Greek to write Homer and had never written a number that meant anything, and got them wrong, and got them right, and looked up with the beautiful disappointed face lit from inside by a thing that was so plainly new to it that Fausta looked away, the first time, as from something she had not been meant to see.

"It *comes out*," Sabina said, the third morning, with the slate. "You see? I've done the Rhodian's week and the sail-maker's canvas at what it's worth and not what he says and the factor's figure at the lower, and it comes out. There's nothing left over." She looked at it. "Nothing on this hill ever comes out. There's always a thing left over and the thing's always a person."

"That's what my mother said, domina. Vibia. That's what she said with the last of her breath."

"Then your mother was the only honest woman between here and Puteoli and I'd have liked her." Sabina put the slate down. "Again. Make me a harder house. Put a man in it who's been told to pass the higher figure and knows I know."

She made her a harder house. And in the evenings, when the Spaniard came in from the cut with the dust on him and stood in the door and did not know his wife for a moment, and then did, and then looked past her to the corner, Fausta stood in the corner with the box of pins and let him look, because a dead woman had said *let the seeing do the work* and because Sabina had said *I'll have arranged it*, and because she had decided, on a terrace by a sea with no manifest, that she would stop pretending about what was true, only about what it cost; and what was true was that the grey eyes rested on her now, each evening, for the length of a flame leaning, and that the face did the thing, interesting and a little short, and that she did not know yet which she was, and that neither, she thought, did he.

He spoke to her once in April. Not in the north rooms; in the Greek library, the old one, Augustus's, on the south side, where she had been sent by Sabina with a tablet to find a line in Sappho that the Spaniard's wife wanted for a reason she would not give, and where the Spaniard was, alone, at a table, with four scrolls open and the Greek cloak over the chair and no staff, reading; and he looked up when the girl came between the shelves and said, in the Greek, without surprise, "Puteoli. What does she want?"

"Sappho, sir. The one about the apple."

"On the topmost bough. The one the pickers forgot; no, not forgot, couldn't reach." He did not get up. He pointed with the stylus. "Third case, second shelf, the roll with the red tag; it's a bad copy but it's the one she'll have read as a girl and she'll want the bad copy, she always does." The grey eyes did not go back to the scroll. "She's teaching you Sappho and you're teaching her a slate. Plotina told me. Plotina tells me most things; the rest I read." A pause. "Which is the better bargain?"

"Hers, sir."

"Why?"

"Because she'll never need the slate and I'll need the Sappho, sir. A thing you'll never need is a gift. A thing you'll need is a price."

The Spaniard looked at her for a long moment across the four scrolls, in the old library with the light coming grey through the high windows onto the cases where Augustus had put the Greeks so he could say he had them, and the face did the thing, and then — she saw it; she kept it — it did another thing, which it had not done in the north rooms, which was to stop finding her interesting for the length of a breath and simply *look*, as a man looked at a column he had not expected to reconcile.

"Hermes's son said a thing like that on a rock," he said. "About a bridge. That it wasn't for crossing, it was for *after*. Lucius says you two were raised in the same street and read the same bill at eleven. I'd not believed it till now." He turned back to the scroll. "Take her the red tag. And Puteoli — when you've done with the slate, and she's done with the Sappho, and Plotina's done with your hair, come back here. Not for anything. I read alone in the afternoons and I've never had anyone in this room who knew what a thing cost, and I'd like to find out whether it's as tiresome as I think it'll be."

"Yes, sir."

"Hadrian. When the door's shut. Vibia's told you the same about *domina*; I know, she tells me what she's told you so I'll not undo it. We're a family that labels its doors." He did not look up. "Go on. She'll be counting the time you've been gone and putting it in a column."

She took the red tag. Sabina was counting. Sabina looked at the roll and at the girl's face and said, in the low quick voice, "He spoke to you," and Fausta said, "He said to come back to the library when the slate's done," and Sabina was quiet, and then laughed, once, short, dry, at the glass, and said, "Already. I'd given it till the summer. Well. He's quicker than I thought or you're better, and I'll find out which by watching, and either way it's a stone laid, and I laid it, and don't you forget that, Puteoli, when you're in that library in the afternoons. I laid it." And then, differently, with the slate on her knees: "Make me a house. A hard one. I don't want to think about the library and I'd rather not think about him and the only thing that stops me thinking is a Rhodian who drinks."

She went back to the library on the fourth afternoon. Not because she had been told to; because Sabina had said, with the slate, *go, or he'll think I kept you, and I'd rather he thought I sent you*, and because a dead woman had said *let the seeing do the work* and she had decided to find out what the work was.

He was at the table. He did not look up. She stood between the cases with her hands empty, because she had not brought a tablet and had not been sent for a line, and after a while he said, in the Greek, to the scroll, "Sit. There. Not across; beside. I don't talk across tables; it makes a thing of it." She sat. The chair was his staff's and too low. He read for a time, and she sat, and the light came grey through the high windows, and she found that she did not mind it, the sitting, which was new; on the Caelian and in the Augusta's room sitting had been a thing you were permitted and here it was simply a chair.

"Lucius is dying," he said, eventually, to the scroll. "You'll know. Everyone does. He coughs in the mornings now where the clerks can hear and he's stopped pretending it's the damp. A year, the Greek says; two if he goes to Baiae, and he'll not go to Baiae, because the emperor's here and the boxes are here and Lucius has never in his life been anywhere the boxes weren't." He turned a column. "When he dies, his office goes to someone. The emperor will say it goes to his freedman Phlegon, who's a good clerk and can't think, and Plotina will say it goes to Capito, who's a good friend and can't be spared from the rolls, and in the end it'll go where the boxes go, and the boxes will go where the man is who's read them." Now he looked up. "Hermes's son has read them. All of them. The third room too; Lucius showed him the sealed box in his second year, which he's done for nobody else, not even me. When Lucius dies that boy will know more about how this emperor was made than anyone alive but Plotina, and he'll be twenty, with a ring, and a father's fine on the roll with his own name under it, and a woman he's freed, and no patron but a dead Spaniard. He'll need one." The grey eyes rested. "I'm telling you because you'll be asked. Not by me; by her. She asks the people who know a man what the man will do, and she'll ask you, because you read the same bill, and I'd rather you'd had time to think about the answer than gave her the one she wants."

"What does she want, sir?"

"Hadrian."

"What does she want?"

"She wants him on my staff. Where I can see him. Where the boxes are, when they're mine." He said it without weight, as a man said the time. "She's wanted it since he was fourteen and said a thing on a stool about who'd be emperor. I've wanted it since a rock. Lucius has said no for six years because Lucius is the only man on this hill who's ever loved anything that wasn't useful, and the thing he loves is that boy's figure, and he'll not give it to a man who'll use it. He's right. I will." The face did the thing. "So when she asks you whether Hermes's son will come to me when Lucius is dead, you'll want to say yes, because yes is what the hill says. And I'd like you to say what you think. Because I've read your sheet, Puteoli, and your sheet had no *I* in it and left a boy out on purpose, and a woman who can do that is a woman who can tell Plotina a true thing about him, and there's not another on the hill who could."

She sat in the low chair with her hands in her lap and her feet under her and the cases going up into the grey light, and did the sum, and did not pick the first way it came out, because a boy on a stool had taught her that by doing it wrong on a mole.

"He'll come, sir. Not because the boxes are yours. Because Sura will tell him to, with his last breath, in the Spanish, and he's a man who does what he's told when the telling's right." She did not look away. "And he'll hate it. Not you. The being used. He's read the sealed box; he knows what a man looks like who writes letters for eleven readers; he said so to Sura and Sura told him to watch for it, and he's watched, and he's seen it in you, and he's seen it in himself, and he'll come to you anyway because he's got nowhere else to put his figure, and he'll do it with his eyes open, and he'll be the best man you've got and the only one you'll never own." She stopped. "That's what I think. It's not what she wants. She wants a man who's hers. He's not. He's been nobody's since he was eleven in a strong-room with the lid off. It's the only thing we've got in common that isn't a bill."

The Spaniard looked at her for a long time. Then he laughed — once, short, surprised, the way Sabina laughed at the glass, so that she understood for the first time where the wife had got it — and put the scroll down.

"*The only one you'll never own.*" He shook his head. "Lucius said you'd say something I'd not have thought of. He said it on the rock, with lime on his boots, about both of you. *They were raised in the same street by a dying woman and a shipowner and the one thing neither of them learned is how to be had.*" He stood, and took the Greek cloak from the chair. "Come tomorrow. Bring the Sappho; I'll show you where the bad copy's wrong and why it's better. And Puteoli." At the door of the library, where people on that hill turned. "When she asks — and it'll be this week — say it to her exactly as you said it to me. The whole of it. *The only one you'll never own.* She'll not like it. She'll remember it. And in a year, when she's deciding whether you're the thing she thought you were, she'll weigh that sentence, and it'll be heavier than the hair."

The Augusta asked on the Ides, under the cloth, after the hair, with the room emptied and Marcia at the shoulder and Matidia's page turning.

"Hermes's son. When Lucius dies. Will he come to Hadrian?"

She said it exactly. The whole of it. *The only one you'll never own.*

The cloth did not move for a long time.

"Yes," the Augusta said at last, to the ceiling. "That's what Lucius says too, and Lucius is dying and tells the truth now because it's cheaper. I'd hoped you'd say otherwise; I'd have known you were lying and I'd have known why, and it would have been a thing I could use." The voice for metre. "Instead you've told me the one true thing about him that I can't use, which is that he's nobody's, and you've told me that you're the same, in the same breath, which I'd worked out, and now you've said it in my room, where things that are said become so." A pause. "Marcia. Write it down. *The only one you'll never own.* Put it in the box with the girl's letter about Tingis. When she's thirty I'll want to read them together and see which one was true."

"Augusta."

"I've not finished. Here's your instruction, since Hadrian's given you his and Sabina's given you hers and you're a girl who likes to have the whole column before she adds it." The cloth came off. The plain face sat up and showed what it decided to, and what it decided to was the thing that was nearly tiredness, and was not. "You'll go to the library in the afternoons. You'll let him look. You'll say true things to him that nobody else on this hill will say, because that's what he's short of and what he'll come to you for and what'll keep you in the room when the hair's grey and done by someone else. You'll not go to his bed, this year or next; he'll not ask, and if he does you'll say *what does it cost* in the voice you've got for it, and he'll laugh, and not ask again for a while, because a man like that wants the one thing in the room that's not been priced and the day you're priced you're furniture. You'll be Sabina's, and she'll love you, and you'll let her, because it's the only shield on this hill that he can't take down without taking her down, and he can't do that, because I made her the price of him. And when Lucius dies and the boy comes to Hadrian's staff with his eyes open, as you say, and hates it, as you say, you'll be in the room. Both of you. The two people on this hill that nobody owns, in the same room, with the same man looking at you, for the rest of my life and most of his." The face did not change. "That's what you're for. I told you a year ago it was in five years or ten and I was wrong; it's this summer. Sura's cough has moved the day. The triumph's in June. You'll be in the box with Sabina. He'll be in the procession with Lucius, on a horse, with the staff. And you'll see each other, across the Sacred Way, with a hundred thousand people between, and I'll be watching your faces, both, because I've bought the two of you from a coast for the price of a sail and a man in Africa and I'd like, once, to see what I've paid for."

* * *

Marcia told her Hermes was dead on the Kalends of May, at the shoulder, with the comb, in the Nemausus Latin, so lightly that the Augusta under the cloth could pretend not to hear.

"The shipowner. Puteoli. On the pallet in his strong-room; nine days after they carried him to the stone. The son's back; he came up the Aventine on the Ides with a box and a centurion's stick and went to the magistrate's before he went to Sura's, which is a thing the wine-shops have noticed." The comb moved. "He freed the woman. The Corinthian. First day. The steward's boy says he said a thing to her in the Latin and not the Greek and she sat down on the floor, and then got up and went on keeping the house, and that she's called Laecania Chrestê now and has a wage and a room with a door of its own and he's not been through it since." The fingers found a pin. "That's all the hill has. I tell you because it's the fifth door and I said I'd not wait ten years."

Fausta's hands did not stop. The Augusta's hair was fine and grey and plain and her hands did nothing with it, perfectly, and the room was quiet, and under the cloth the Augusta said, to the ceiling, in the voice for metre, "Marcia. What's the girl's face doing?"

"Nothing, Augusta."

"Good. Then she's heard it." The cloth did not move. "Girl. I'll say one thing about it and then it's not mine, it's yours. A man who frees a woman on the first day back from his father's stone is a man who's just learned what a thing weighs and wants to put one down. That's not kindness. It's a man emptying a box so he can carry a heavier one. I've watched my husband do it with provinces." A page turned; it was Matidia's. "You'll not ask me what the heavier one is. You know. You left it off a sheet."

She did the hair. She went down the long wall behind the comb and across the courts past the fountain that worked and into the north rooms, where Sabina was on the floor with a slate and looked up and saw the face that was doing nothing and said, "Go away. Not away; go and stand somewhere. You've a thing in you that's not for my rooms and I'll not have it in them. Momus. Take her to the terrace. The one on the west; nobody uses it; it looks at the Capitol and the cut and it's where I go when I want to be furniture." And she went.

The terrace looked at the city. Not the sea; the city, from the top of its own hill: the Capitol with the temple gold in the May light and under it the old Forum with its roofs and beyond it the raw red wound of the cut, where the Syrian was taking a hill away a basket at a time, and the dust standing over it in the still air like a thing that had been breathed out and not yet in. Momus climbed onto the parapet's bench, where he could see, and sat with his short legs out, and said nothing, which was the thing he was for.

She stood with her hands on the parapet. The price was in her, and she looked at it, and it had moved, as it had moved in a room with a German at the door when a steward had said *open hand, across the face, with the Lares watching*; and she did the sum, slowly, in the eggs voice, inside, and it came out where it had been coming out since a terrace by a sea: that she had put the father on a sheet and left the son off it, and had told herself it was the lower figure, and had told the Augusta it was *him, the only paper I had that nobody could burn*, and had told a dead woman on a terrace nothing, and had written a letter to a dying man in a hand only the son could read, so that it would be found; and that all of these were true and none of them was the figure; and that the figure was that a boy on a mole had said *not for nothing* and had been right, and that she had held him off at the length of her arm ever since because the arm was the only thing on the mole that had been hers.

"Momus."

"Girl."

"The paper. The false one. Under the hearth-stone in the room off the lady's; I brought it up the hill with the other. It's in my gown now. I've carried it five years." She did not turn. "The true one's in the prefect's archive under the Augusta's seal. The steward's dead to it; he said so with his own mouth. The egg-man's paid. Hermes is dead. The only people who ever knew it was false were my mother and Thallus and the magistrate's clerk who took the coin, and my mother's in the ground and the clerk's drunk it and Thallus has decided." She took it out. It was soft with five years of being against skin, and the fold had gone through, and the seal was a smear. "There's no one left to show it to."

"Then burn it," said Momus. "There's a brazier in the Augusta's outer court that's kept lit for the Germans' hands. I'll walk you. Nobody stops a girl with a dwarf; they think I'm hers and she's mine and they're half right."

She did not move.

"Girl."

"If I burn it," she said, to the city, "then I'm what the Augusta's paper says. Born free, in the house by the harbour, with witnesses. And that's true, now, because she's filed it, and nobody counts a thing she's filed." Her hands were on the parapet. "And I'll have nothing left on me that says what it cost. The Strabo's a book. The brass fish is a lease that's sold. His letters are small things from Sura's office with postscripts. This is the only thing I own that says: *your mother bought you a name at a magistrate's for the price of a quarter's rent, with an egg-seller's husband for a witness, and sent you up a hill with it, and said don't come back.*" She looked at the smear of the seal. "I don't want to be a woman who burned the price. I've seen them. They're on the benches. They've all got a true paper somewhere and none of them remembers what it cost and they sit in that room and perform."

Momus was quiet on the parapet for a long time, with his legs out, looking at the cut.

"I was sold at four," he said. "To a man who made us. You know what I mean; you've seen the ones who didn't survive it, at the games, on sticks. He had a cage. You grew in it the way it let you and then you were worth something. I was in it two years and then I was Tertulla's father's, and then hers, and I've been on a stool at the end of a corridor for forty years counting people in and out, and I'll tell you, girl, as the only person on this hill who's been in a cage and come out shaped by it, that there's two kinds of people who keep the price. There's the ones who keep it so they'll remember. And there's the ones who keep it so they'll have a thing to hold onto when the floor goes." He turned the walnut face to her. "You've felt the floor. You told me. On Sabina's stool, with the Augusta's sheet in your hands. You've not had it since you were ten and a shutter wouldn't bar and you've got it now, and you're standing on it, and you're holding a soft bit of paper with a smear on it because you're afraid that if you let go the floor was only ever the paper." He looked back at the cut. "It wasn't. The floor's her seal and your feet and a man in Africa and a sail. You bought it. It's yours. Burn the receipt."

She stood a while longer. The dust hung over the cut. Somewhere under it, in a hut, with a deaf clerk, a man she had known since she was five was doing a figure for a Syrian, and she could not see him from here and had not expected to and stood anyway.

"Walk me," she said.

He walked her. Through the courts, past the fountain, along the long wall, into the Augusta's outer court where the Germans stood and the brazier was, for their hands, lit in May because Germans were cold in any month; and the two by the brazier looked at the tall girl with the pin and the dwarf and stepped back, because she was the Augusta's hair and the dwarf was a gift, and she stood over the coals with the soft folded thing and did not say anything, because there was nothing that was said, and put it in.

It went slowly. Five years of skin had made it damp. It curled, and browned, and the smear of the seal went first, and then the magistrate's drawn stamp that a drunk clerk had been paid to draw, and then the names, the Rhodian's father, the egg-woman's husband, and last, because it was in the fold where the paper was thickest, *Fausta, daughter of Hilara*; and she watched it, as she had watched a hull go to ash from a mole with a boy's arm under her hand, to the length of the arm and no further; and when it was gone she stood a moment longer with her hands empty and her feet under her, and found that the floor was there, and that it was, as the dwarf had said, not the paper, and that she had known that, and had wanted to be told by someone who had been in a cage.

"There," said Momus. "Now you're what the Augusta says. Born free. Witnesses. It's a terrible thing to be and you'll get used to it." He looked up at her. "What's left on you?"

"The Strabo. The fish. His letters." She did not touch her gown. "And the one from the prefect's archive, with her seal."

"Then that's your paper. And the letters are the door." He turned for the courts. "Come on. Sabina's counting. She'll want to know what you did on the terrace and she'll not believe *nothing*, so tell her *burned a thing*, and she'll make you a harder house to stop her thinking about it, and you'll do it, and that's the hill; that's the whole of it; a woman on a floor with a slate and a woman at a brazier with a receipt and neither of them saying what the other knows."

* * *

Her birthday was the fourth day before the Ides of May, and she was nineteen, and the Spaniard's wife gave her a book.

Not a scroll. A book — the new kind, cut and sewn, leaves of parchment between boards, that the Spaniard had brought back from Athens when he was a boy and that nobody on the hill used because they looked like account-books; and that was why, Sabina said, with the beautiful face doing its nearly-smile, handing it across the floor between the slates. "It's blank. Every leaf. I had the binder on the Argiletum make it and he asked what it was for and I said *a lodging-house* and he thought I was mad." She sat back. "You've taught me a book. I've nothing to teach you that you'll need except Sappho and you're getting that from him, in the afternoons, in the library, and I'll not compete with a man who reads better than I do. So here's a thing you'll not need. A book with nothing in it. For whatever you keep. I know you keep things; you've a face like a shutter and a shutter's for keeping. Keep them in that. Not the slate kind. The other kind. The kind that doesn't come out."

Fausta held it. It was heavy, for its size; the boards were oak. She opened it and the first leaf was blank and the second and all of them, and she sat on the cold floor of the north rooms with a book with nothing in it on her knees and understood that she had just been given the only gift of her life that had not been a price in advance, and that she did not know what to do with it, and that the Spaniard's wife was watching her not know with the dry tenant intelligence and was, Fausta saw, pleased, in a way that had nothing to do with the hill.

"Thank you," she said. It was the wrong word on the Augusta's side of the hill and she said it anyway, because it was Sabina's side and the doors were labelled.

"Don't. I'll start to like it." Sabina picked up her slate. "What will you put in it first? Don't tell me. I'll guess. The sail."

"No."

"The man in Africa."

"No."

"Then what?"

Fausta looked at the blank first leaf. Her hands, which had done a dying woman's hair and an empress's and had held a soft paper over coals, were still on the oak.

"A mole," she said. "In the spring. Two children on it, counting ships. One of them says *not for nothing* and the other one says *all right*. That's the first thing. The rest comes after it, in order, and it's long, and it doesn't come out, and I've been carrying it in the place where I keep the Rhodian's eleven for nine years and I'd like, once, to put it down somewhere that isn't me."

Sabina was quiet. The slate lay on her knees.

"Then that's the fifth door," she said at last, in the low quick voice, to the glass. "Marcia's five. She told me; she tells everyone; she thinks a thing's not a secret unless she's said it. The paper, the sheet, the widow, the son, the boy. Four shut, one yours." She did not turn from the window. "Write it in the book, Puteoli. And when you've written it, read it, and then you'll know which it is — the thing you're keeping or the thing you're holding. Momus told me what he said on the terrace. He tells me everything too; it's a house of tellers. He's right about the receipt. He's wrong about the boy. The boy's not a receipt. He's the one line in your book that's not a price, and you've known it since a bill in a strong-room, and that's why he's not on the sheet, and that's why you'll not burn his letters, and that's why you'll go to the library in the afternoons and let my husband look at you, because a woman with one true line in her book needs a great many false ones round it to keep it from being read." The beautiful face turned, finally, and was not performing, and was not kind, and was, Fausta thought, as near as it could come to either. "I know. I've one too. His name doesn't matter; he's in Athens and married and I've never written it down. Make me a house. A hard one. It's your birthday and I'd like to get something wrong."
