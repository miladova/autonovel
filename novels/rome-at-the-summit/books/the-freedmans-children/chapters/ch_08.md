# The Mole

The war came as a manifest, which was how Celer would always afterwards think of wars: not as a trumpet but as a list.

It came to Rufus's rooms in January, in a leather case with Sura's seal on it, carried by the Greek with the ring, and it was forty sheets of papyrus in the hand of the grain-office setting out what the Danube legions would need between the spring and the autumn if the emperor were to go north with four of them and the Guard and the auxiliaries of three provinces: so many thousand modii of wheat, so many of barley for the horses, so much oil, so much wine of the kind soldiers drank, so many hides, so much iron. The ports it would come through and the roads it would go up and the depots on the river where it would wait. Rufus was to read it and report to Sura whether the figures from Puteoli and Ostia were sound, because Rufus had a shipowner among his father's people and was understood, in Sura's office, to know about ships.

Rufus did not know about ships. Rufus gave it to Celer, in the room with the window on the Palatine, and said, "Tell me what it says and whether it's true and what I should say about it that will make me look as though I'd thought of it," and went to the baths.

Celer read it. He read it the way he read everything, twice, and then a third time with the tablet; and on the third reading he began to see what it was, which was not a list of what the army needed but a list of who would be paid to supply it, written by men who already knew the answer and were arranging the question to fit. The Puteoli figures were his father's figures. He knew them; he had carried them in his head since the strong-room, and here they were, the *Isis* and her sisters down to the modius, with the rate per carry that had been fixed under Nerva and that Felix had countersigned, and a note in the margin in another hand that said *confirmed, L.S.* — and he sat with that for a long moment, in Rufus's room, with the rain on the window, and understood that his father had been at the back of Bassus's atrium for nine mornings in the autumn of the entry for exactly this. That Sura's secretary asking Eutyches his name had been the whole of the transaction, and that the four hundred thousand and the olives had been, in his father's accounting, the price of being in the margin of a list in Sura's hand before the war was declared.

He did not write that down. He wrote, for Rufus, three things that were true and would make Rufus look as though he had thought of them: that the barley figure assumed the horses would be fed through the winter on the river and not sent south, which meant the staff expected to hold the far bank; that the wine was costed at the Campanian rate but routed through Aquileia, which was a thing someone would make money on and Rufus might wish to know who; and that the Puteoli carry was sound to the modius but that the depot at Viminacium was listed with a capacity it had not had since Domitian's war, when the Dacians had burned it, and somebody in the grain-office was working from an old map.

Rufus took the three things to Sura. He came back in the evening with a look on him that Celer had not seen before, which was the look of a man who has been praised by someone whose praise he had not expected to want.

"He asked who'd found the depot," Rufus said. He stood in the door of the room with his cloak still on and the rain on it. "I said my secretary. He asked your name. I told him. He said, *Hermes's boy; yes, the Puteoli figures*, and wrote something, and then he said —" Rufus stopped, and took the cloak off, and threw it at the bed, and said to the window, in his sum-reciting voice: "He said I'm to have the Seventh Claudia. Tribune of the broad stripe. Viminacium. We go with the emperor on the twenty-fifth of March." He turned. "That's eleven weeks. You'll go down to the coast tomorrow and tell your father and bring back what he's promised, in coin, because a tribune's kit costs what a dowry costs and I've no sister left to sell. And you'll come back by the Kalends of March with it and whatever else he thinks I'll need, and then we'll go north, you and I, and find out what you're for."

Celer said, "Yes, sir." He had not said *sir* to Rufus before; it had not seemed to be wanted. It was wanted now. He could see it go into the man and settle, and he thought, watching it, that he had just learned the first thing about war, which was that it made men need to be called things.

* * *

He went down on the fast post because Rufus paid for it, and came into Puteoli on the fourth day in a sleet that had followed him from the Alban hills and turned, as the road came down to the bay, into a thin cold rain that the sulphur-smoke could not get up through, so that the whole town lay under a lid of yellow and grey and the harbour was a blur and the mole was a line and the Dolphin, when he passed the end of its lane with his hood up and his face doing nothing, was a door with the bar down and a lamp behind the shutter.

His father was in the strong-room. He was always in the strong-room now; the stepmother said he slept there some nights, with the box for a pillow, and Celer had believed it as a figure of speech until he came in out of the rain and found his father at the table with the lamp and a blanket round his shoulders and the contract unrolled and his eyes shut.

They opened. They looked at the boy in the door with the road on him, and his father said, "The Seventh."

"How —"

"Thallus wrote. He's in Rome; he has a man in Sura's house who tells him what Sura's house has for dinner. He wrote the same day." His father did not move. "I knew before Rufus did. I knew before you did, probably, unless Rufus told you in the litter. Sit down. You look like something the tide left."

Celer sat. The lamp hissed. His father looked at him for a long time across it with an expression he could not read and then, unexpectedly, reached across and put a hand flat on the boy's wet head, as a man might on a dog that had come back, and took it off, and said: "The twenty-fifth. Eleven weeks. You'll want coin for his kit, which I have, and a letter to the quartermaster at Viminacium, which I've written, because the quartermaster at Viminacium is a man called Lucilius who was a clerk at Ostia when I was a clerk at Ostia and who owes me a thing from thirty years ago that I've never asked for. You'll give him the letter and you'll not ask what the thing was. And you'll want what I'm going to tell you now, which is the only thing I've got that's worth more than the coin, so listen."

He listened.

"You're going to a camp. A camp is a town with one man in it. Everything else is clerks. The tribune's a clerk, the legate's a clerk, the quartermaster's a clerk, the centurions are clerks with sticks. The one man is the emperor, and you'll never see him, and nothing you do will ever reach him, and that's not the point. The point is that every one of the clerks between you and him wants something, and most of them want the same thing, which is to not be blamed. That's the whole of a camp. It's not courage and it's not iron. It's forty thousand men trying not to be the one whose name is on the tablet when the thing goes wrong." His father leaned forward. "You can count. You can write a manifest that's true. In a camp, that makes you the most dangerous man in the tent, because you're the one who knows whose name goes on the tablet. Use it. Not to hurt anyone — not yet; you're fourteen and you'll be killed for it. To be *needed*. Be the one who knows where the barley went. Be the one who can tell the legate on the day the depot's short that it was short in Domitian's war and here's the old map. A boy like that doesn't get sent across the river with a shield. A boy like that gets kept in the tent, and the tent is where the war is decided, and the men who were in the tent are the men who come home with the land." He sat back. "That's it. That's thirty years. Rufus will be brave, probably; his sort are; it's the only thing they're taught. Let him. You be useful. One of you has to come back, and he's got a father who can buy another son."

Celer sat with his hands between his knees and the rain running off his hair onto the table. He said, after a while, "And Mother?"

His father looked at him blankly for a moment. Then he understood. "Vibia's at the loom. She'll say what she says. She's been saying it to the gods since Thallus's letter came and I've let her; it keeps her hands busy." A pause. "The other one's in the kitchen. Chione. I kept her; she's cheaper than a cook and the cook ran off at the Saturnalia. She asked after you. I didn't answer. You can go and see her or not. It's nothing to me either way and it should be nothing to you."

It was not nothing to him. He had carried it to Rome and back. He said, "Yes, Father," and went up out of the strong-room into the house, and found the stepmother at the loom, and stood by it. She did not look up, and said: "Eleven weeks," and he said, "Yes," and she said, "Then you'll have the lamb at the Ides, and the gods will have the rest of it, and you'll go to the tomb with him before you go and let him show you the stone, because he needs to and you can bear it." And then she did look up, with the long farm face and the pale eyes, and said: "And you'll go to the lodging-house. Once. Properly, by the door, in daylight, and I'll not tell him, and he'll know anyway and say nothing, because he's not a fool and you're going to a war. There's no point in being careful about a boy's name when the boy may not come back to use it. I've told him that. He didn't answer either. He's not answering anything this winter; it's how he's frightened." She went back to the thread. "Go on. Not tonight. Tomorrow, when it's stopped raining. She'll want to see you dry."

* * *

He did not go the next day, or the one after. He went to the warehouse with his father and counted what Rufus would need and put it in boxes and sealed the boxes. He went to the tomb and stood in the rain in front of the blank space under the ship where he would go, *F* not *L*, and let his father show it to him, and said what was wanted. He went to the harbour and arranged the carriage of the boxes to Rome by the house's cart. He went to the kitchen, once, at an hour when he knew it would be empty, and it was not; the Bithynian girl was there with her back to him at the hearth, and she turned, and they looked at each other across the room with the pots between them, and she said, in the Greek with the sea in it, *you've grown*. He said, *so have you*, which was not true and which she let pass, and then neither of them had anything, and he said, *my mother says you sew*, and she said, *your mother gave me the needle*, and he said, *yes*, and went out; and that was all, and it was enough, and he never spoke to her again in his life, and remembered it.

And on the ninth day, which was dry, with a wind off the land that had cleared the smoke and left the bay standing up blue and hard as a painted wall, he went down the hill to the Dolphin by the lane in daylight and knocked on the door with his face doing nothing, and the woman who opened it was not Hilara.

She was a Greek woman he knew by sight, the egg-seller's daughter, in an apron, and she looked at him and knew him and said, "She's upstairs. The mother. She's — she's lying down. The girl's in the yard." And stood back from the door, which was a thing nobody had ever done for him at that house, and he understood from the standing back more than from the words that something had changed in the Dolphin that the lane did not yet know about.

The girl was in the yard. She was sitting on the step by the shrine with a roll open on her knees, and she looked up, and her face did nothing — he watched it do nothing, and was, in some small cold part of himself, proud of her — and then did something, briefly, that he did not have a word for, and then was still again.

"You came by the door," she said.

"My mother said to."

"Vibia." She closed the roll. "She's the only one in that house with any sense. Sit down. You'll want to know about my mother and I'll tell you once and then we'll not speak of it, because I've had three months of speaking of it and I'm done." He sat. "She's ill. Since the Saturnalia. It's her side; there's a hardness, Antiope's felt it, the Greek woman's mother's felt it, they look at each other over her and say nothing. She can't do the stairs. She can't do Thallus." Flat, a manifest. "I do the house. I do the slate and the lodgers and the Rhodian's eggs and I pay the Greek woman out of the box to do the beds. Thallus came at the Ides and I gave him the quarter and the accounts and he sat in the common room and looked at me and said *and the rest?* and I said *there's no rest, she's ill*, and he said *we'll see*, and went away. He'll be back on the Kalends. I don't know what he'll do. I don't think he knows. A man like that doesn't know what to do with a thing he can't price." She looked at the shrine. "The widow hasn't written. It's winter; they don't. The Rhodian put it in the bronze hand himself. So that's where we are. That's the house."

He sat on the step beside her in the hard blue light and found that he had nothing. He had come down the hill with eleven weeks and the Seventh Claudia and a letter to a quartermaster and the whole of his father's thirty years in a sentence about tents, and he sat beside her and it was all paper, it was all a manifest in another man's hand. The thing in front of him was a girl on a step with her mother dying above her and a steward coming on the Kalends, and he could not count it, and he could not make it come out the same three times, and he understood, for the first time, what his father had been doing in the strong-room with his eyes shut: not sleeping. Hiding from the thing that would not add.

"I'm going on the Kalends," he said. "To Rome. And then north on the twenty-fifth. The Seventh, at Viminacium. Rufus has the broad stripe." He heard it come out of him in the corridor voice and hated it and could not find the other one.

"I know. Thallus told the whole common room. He was proud of it; he said it as if he'd arranged it." She turned her head and looked at him, the proper look. "You'll go. You should go. There's nothing here you can do and your father's right that one of you should come back and it had better be the one who can count." A pause. "Come to the mole tonight. After the lamps. I want to see the water and I'll not go alone and the Greek woman will sit with her. That's all I want. Don't bring anything. Don't say anything you've practised. Just come."

"Yes," he said. "All right."

"And Celer." He waited. "You said you'd write. From the Danube. What you saw."

"Yes."

"Write it to the widow's house. Not here. By the summer I'll be there or I'll be dead or the house will be Thallus's, and whichever it is, a letter to the Dolphin's a letter to him."

* * *

The mole at night was a different thing. He had been on it a hundred times in the day with the porters and the boys and the fishermen and the whole town looking east, and he had never once been on it when it was empty. It was empty now: a road of stone going out into the dark on its arches with the sea moving under it, black, with the lamps of the fleet at Misenum far off on the left and the villa lamps on the right climbing their hill, and at the end of it, where they sat with their legs over the edge as they had sat to watch the news-ship, nothing but the bay and the stars and the sound.

They did not say anything for a long time. She had said not to and he found that he did not want to; the corridor voice had gone somewhere on the walk out and he did not go looking for it. He sat with the stone cold through his tunic and the sea moving under his feet and her beside him, close, not touching, and listened to the water on the arches, which was a sound he had heard his whole life and had never once, until that moment, sat still long enough to hear all of.

"Strabo says it's built on ash," she said eventually. "Not sand. Nicias gave me the book. He says that's why it stands."

"My father says it stands because the Augustales pay for the repairs."

"Both." She leaned forward and looked down between her knees at the black water. "Everything's both. That's what I've worked out this winter, doing the slate. Every number's two numbers. The one that's owed and the one that's paid. The one on the board and the one in the wax. And you can't live in the gap, you have to pick one, and whichever you pick is a lie to someone." She sat back. "I'm going to pick the paid one. When I go to the widow's. I've decided. I'm going to be what's in the wax and let the board say what it likes."

"And if the widow doesn't write?"

"Then I'll pick it here. With Thallus. I've worked that out too." She said it without any weight at all, and he heard the weight in it anyway, the way you heard a hull's draught in how the water moved against it, and he turned and looked at her in the dark, and she was looking at the sea.

"Fausta."

"Don't. I said not to say anything you've practised."

"I haven't practised this. I don't know what it is." And he did not; he sat on the end of the mole with the whole of the sea in front of him and found that the thing in his chest had no number, had never had one, had been there since a yard and a cake and had been given no figure because a boy who was raised to count knew better, and that he was going to the Danube on the twenty-fifth and might not come back and that if he did not the thing would go into the river with him uncounted, which was the only way he had ever been able to keep anything safe, and that it was not safe, it had never been safe, it had been on the mole the whole time where anyone could see it. "I don't know what it is," he said again. "I know it's not a jar."

She turned then.

He did not decide to. That was what he would say to himself, later, for years, in tents and strong-rooms and rooms with windows on palaces: that he had not decided, that it had happened the way the ship had come about on the news-day when the wind went round a point, all at once and with no one at the tiller. He put his hand on the side of her face, where the lamp from the villa hill caught it, and she did not move. He leaned in and kissed her, badly, with his eyes open, the way a boy of fourteen kissed who had been in a room over a chandler's and learned nothing there about this; and for the space of a breath, or two, or something that was not measured in breaths, she kissed him back, with her hand come up and closed in the front of his tunic, hard, the way she had held his hand at the lip of the burning fields, and he felt the whole of the sea stop.

Then she stopped it.

Not a push. She took her hand out of his tunic and put it flat on his chest and held him there, away, at the length of her arm, and her face in the villa-light was doing nothing, nothing at all, it was the shutter gone to, and she said, very low and very level, "No."

He sat back. He felt the cold come into the place where she had been.

"No," she said again, and it was not to him; it was to the sea, or the mole, or the stone under them. "Not that. Not like that. I'll not — I've seen where that goes. I've seen it every morning of my life coming down the stairs doing the sum. I'll not be a thing a man stopped at."

"I didn't — that's not —"

"I know it's not. I know what it is. I know better than you do; you've no idea what it is, you said so." Her voice did not rise. "That's why. Because you don't know and you're going to a war and I'm going to a widow's house or a steward's bed, and whichever of those it is, I'll not go into it having been that, on a mole, in the dark, with the one person in the world who's never once looked at me as a thing. I'll not spend it. Not here. Not for nothing. Not when it's the only — " She stopped. She took her hand off his chest. "Not when it's the only paper I've got that nobody can burn."

He did not understand. That was the truth. He would know it as the truth for a long time before he knew what it meant: he sat on the mole with the cold in him and heard her say *no*, and *I'll not be a thing a man stopped at*, and heard it as a girl refusing a boy, as the market disagreeing, as the answer to a sum coming out wrong; and he did not hear, because he was fourteen and had spent the winter in a room with a window on a palace learning to be a face, the other thing under it, which was a girl whose mother was dying above a staircase that a steward climbed twice a month, who had done the sum on the wall and knew exactly what she was made of and what it had cost and who was holding the one thing she owned outright away from the only person she would have given it to, with her arm out straight, because she had seen what being given did to women.

He heard *no*. He was his father's son. He put it in the column.

"All right," he said. His voice came out steady, which he noted with the distant interest he always noted it with, as if it were someone else's. "All right. I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be — " She shook her head. "Just don't be sorry. Sorry's the thing Rufus is good at." And she stood up, on the end of the mole, with the sea behind her and the villa lamps on her face, and looked down at him, and he could not read her, and it was the first time since the yard with the cake that he could not, and he would remember that longer than the kiss. "Come on. The Greek woman's got to go home and I've the shutters. Walk me back. Don't say anything. We've both said it, whatever it was. Let it be both."

He walked her back. He did not say anything. At the end of the lane, where the Dolphin's lamp was behind its shutter, she stopped, and did not look at him, and said to the door: "Write what you saw. I'll work out the true part. I said I would." And went in, and the bar came down, and he stood in the lane with his hands at his sides for the length of a hundred counted, and then went up the hill the long way, past the baths, so as not to pass anything.

* * *

The mason was at the tomb on the last day of February, and his father had sent him up to see the space.

It was on the road out of the town between the other tombs, on its own ground that his father had bought from the Augustales' college for more than it was worth so that the college would remember who had paid; and the plinth was up, in travertine, dressed square, and the stone was on it, and the mason was on a trestle in front of it with a chisel and a mallet and a boy holding the lamp against the grey, cutting letters. *C. LAECANIUS HERMES, FREEDMAN OF LUCIUS, AUGUSTALIS, SHIPOWNER.* The mason was at the second line. He was a Campanian with stone-dust in the creases of his face and he did not stop when the boy came up the road, because a man cutting a letter did not stop for anyone, and when the letter was done he sat back on the trestle and looked at the tall boy in the road and said, "You're the son."

"Yes."

"He's told me to leave a space. Here. After *son*. Two words' worth, in letters a hand taller than the rest, and I'm to leave it blank and go on with the freedmen and the descendants under, and when he sends me the two words I'm to cut them." The mason looked at the blank. "I've cut tombs thirty years. I've never been told to leave a hole in one. A hole in a stone's a thing people read. They'll walk past this every day to Capua and see a freedman's tomb with a gap in it a hand high and they'll say *what's that for* and somebody will tell them. He knows that. He's a man who knows what a stone says before it's cut." He picked up the chisel. "I'm not asking what the two words are. I'd not cut them any better for knowing. I'm telling you the space is there and I'm leaving it, and that a space on a stone's heavier than a letter, because a letter's done and a space is waiting."

Celer stood in the road and looked at the blank. Two words' worth. A hand taller. He knew what they were; he had known since he could read; his father had never once said them to him and had never once not said them. He stood with his hands at his sides and looked at the place where they were not yet, and found that it was the first thing in his life he had looked at that had a figure he did not want to count. That he was counting it anyway, because he counted everything, and that the figure was *not yet*, and that he was going north in two days with a girl's *no* in his column and his father's two words in a space on a stone and both of them open.

"Tell him it's left," said the mason. "Tell him I've left it exactly the width he said, and that it looks like what it is, and that if he wants my advice, which he's not asked, he'll send me the words before he's dead, because I've cut a lot of spaces filled by sons and they never get the letters right."

* * *

He went on the Kalends, in the house's cart, with the boxes, in a thin March rain.

His father walked beside the cart as far as the amphitheatre and did not speak and at the amphitheatre put his hand on the tail-board and stopped, so that the cart went on and the hand came off it. That was the goodbye; Celer looked back once and saw him standing in the road in his cloak with the rain on him, not waving, a man watching a hull go down the ways, and then the road bent and he was gone. The stepmother had said what she said at the door, and had given him a thing wrapped in cloth that was bread and a small bronze of the Lares from her father's farm, and had said, *the gods don't care which house, they only care that someone's feeding them, see that someone is*, and he had put it in his satchel with the letter to the quartermaster and the denarius. The Bithynian girl had not come to the door. He had not expected her to and had looked anyway.

The Dolphin's lane he passed with his hood up. He did not look. He had decided on the mole that he would not, and he was a boy who, once a thing was decided, could carry it the way he carried a number; and so he looked at the mules' ears and the rain on the road and the tombs beginning, and he did not see the shutter on the upper window of the Dolphin open the width of a hand as the cart went by the end of the lane, and a face at it. The shutter close; and she did not see him not look, because she was not looking at him, she was looking at the cart, and the boxes, and the sail over them with the rain running off it, and counting, and she got to a hundred and forty before the sound went under the sound of the harbour, and then she closed the shutter and went down to her mother, who was awake, and wanted water.

He had the tomb on his right going out. The ship's eye caught what light there was. The blank space under it was still blank. He looked at that; he let himself look at that; and he thought, with the rain coming down on the Capua road and the Danube somewhere in front of him past a city and a mountain and a river he had seen only on a map that was out of date, that his father had been wrong about one thing in the strong-room. It was not that the clerks wanted not to be blamed. It was that everyone, down to the last mule, wanted to be the thing that was not spent. And he had spent nothing, on the mole, in the dark, because she had held him off at the length of her arm and said *no*, and he had put it in the column; and he sat in the cart with the boxes and found, when he went looking, that the column did not balance, that there was a figure in it he had not entered and could not find. That it was the first sum of his life that would not come out the same three times, and that he was going to a war with it open.
