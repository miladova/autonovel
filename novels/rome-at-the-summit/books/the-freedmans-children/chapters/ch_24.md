# I'll Not Blame You

He went to the magistrate before he went up the hill, as he had told a woman at a loom he would, and it took less time than buying her had.

The praetor's clerk in the basilica by the Forum had seen a thousand of these and looked at the tall young man with the ring and the dust of the Appian road still on him and the woman beside him in a good plain gown with a long face arranged, and said, "Name," and he said, "Chrestê, Corinthian, twenty-three, bought in the Subura in the consulship of —" and the names, and the clerk wrote, and said, "Manumission before the magistrate, by the rod; the lictor's there; touch her with it when he says and say the words; she's a citizen when you've said them and a freedwoman of your house and takes your name; fee's on the table. Next." And the lictor came with the rod, bored, and touched her shoulder, and Celer said the words, which were old and short and had been said by his father in this basilica or one like it for a woman from Nola, *I wish this woman to be free*, and the clerk made a mark, and it was done, and the ring had weighed less than a denarius and the words weighed less than the ring and she was standing in the basilica of the Roman people a citizen, with his name, *Laecania Chrestê*, and the clerk was already saying *next*.

They walked back along the Forum toward the Aventine. She did not speak. He did not. At the foot of the hill, by the wine-shop, with the stair going up to the three rooms, she stopped, and he stopped, and she looked at him with the long face arranged and said, in the Gulf Greek, "Well. I'm free. What now? You've not said. You've walked me to a magistrate and said four words and walked me back and you've not said what now, and I've kept your slate for two years and I'd like to know what the next line is."

He had thought about it on the Appian road for four days, with a tablet in a box that said *the door, not him*, and a loom going in his head; and he said it, in the Latin, not the Greek, because his father had said it in Latin to a woman from Nola, and because the Greek was the language he had bought her in.

"The rooms are yours to keep, if you want them, at the wage, as a freedwoman keeps a house. Your own room, with the door; I'll not come through it. The slate's yours; I've never checked it and I'll not start. If you want to go — Puteoli, Corinth, anywhere — there's two thousand at the banker's on the Sacred Way in your name as of this morning, which is what my father gave Vibia, and it's yours whether you stay or not." He stopped. He made himself look at her face while he said the rest, because a man was his face and this was the only thing he had ever been told by his father that he knew to be the best thing his father had done. "I'd like you to stay. And I'll not blame you if you don't."

She looked at him for a long moment, at the foot of the stair, in the April noon, with the wine-shop's noise behind her and the long face arranged; and then it was not arranged, and she sat down.

On the step. All at once, as a sack went down, with her gown in the dust of the Aventine and her hands in her lap and the long face turned up to him with nothing on it at all, not the sum, not the pen-look, not the thing underneath; nothing; a woman of twenty-three on a step who had been sold with the furniture and had just been told the one thing in the world she had decided at fourteen she would never hear and had arranged her face for nine years so as not to be seen waiting for it. She did not weep. She sat. People went past on the stair and round her.

"Say it again," she said, after a while, in the Latin.

"I'll not blame you."

"No. The other."

"I'd like you to stay."

She sat a moment longer. Then she got up, and shook the dust out of the gown, and arranged her face, and it was the long plain Corinthian face he had bought in the Subura and it was not, because something under it had moved and was not going back, and she said, in the Gulf Greek again, briskly, as a manifest: "Then I'll stay. At the wage. In my room, with the door, and you'll not come through it, and I'll not come through yours, because a freedwoman who goes to her patron's bed the week she's freed is a woman the wine-shop has a word for and I've been called it. I'll keep the slate. I'll keep the rooms. I'll keep *you*, as much as a woman can keep a man who's nobody's; I've watched you two years and I know what that is now." She started up the stair. At the turn she stopped, and looked down, and the thing that had moved was in her eyes and she let him see it. "And the two thousand stays at the banker's. I'll not touch it. It's the first money I've ever had that wasn't a price and I'd like to look at it now and then and not spend it. Your father's woman did the same; I'd wager on it. We're a kind, us. We keep the receipt."

* * *

Sura was on the couch, and the couch had been moved to the window, because the man wanted to see the hill while he coughed.

Celer came in with the box under his arm and the stick in his belt and stood, and Sura looked at him from the cushions with the knife-face gone to bone and the eyes exactly the same and said, in the Spanish, "You freed her first. Before you came to me. The steward's boy had it from the wine-shop before you were up the Aventine." A breath, with a sound in it. "Good. Your father would have done it second and hated himself. You're better than him by one place in the order, which is the most a son gets." He moved a hand. "Sit. Not the stool. Here, where I can see you without turning; the Greek says turning's a thing I've done enough of."

He sat. The hill was in the window, the Palatine, across the valley where the Circus was, with the palace roofs and the pines and, if he looked, the north side, where the Spaniard's wife's rooms were, with three windows, cold.

"Your father."

"On the pallet. In the strong-room. Nine days after the stone." He did not say the rest. "Rufus came. He said the fine was just, in front of the town, and that the house of Laecanius would say so in the Senate."

"Did he. Well. That's the first useful thing Rufus has done since he was born and he did it because he couldn't afford the other." Sura coughed, into the cloth, and looked at it, and this time did not put it away, but held it, in his lap, as a thing he had stopped hiding. "It'll go on the roll. Capito's already had it; I sent it the day the boy told me. A senator's word on a freedman's son's figure, in a patron's atrium, in front of Augustales. It's the kind of thing that makes a roll a roll and not a list." He looked at the window. "You've done it, you know. The thing he wanted. You're on the roll at four hundred and six, with a patron's word, and the fine paid, and a freedwoman of your own house, and a stone with your name a hand taller. That's the whole of it. That's what a man walks off a ship at nineteen and builds for forty years. It's done. How does it feel?"

Celer sat with the box on his knees and the hill in the window and did the sum, and it came out, and he said it.

"Like a clerk, and a box, and *next*, sir."

Sura laughed. It turned into the cough, and the cough went on, and when it was done he lay back and said, "Yes. That's it exactly. That's what the consulship felt like, and the second, and the house, and the day I wrote the eleventh letter from Cologne and knew the thing was made. A clerk and a box and *next*. Nobody tells you. They can't; you'd not believe them; you'd think it was a rich man's sorrow and you'd be right, and it would still be true." The eyes came back from the window. "I've a thing to say and I'll say it now, because I've not got the breath to save it for a better day and there's not one coming."

"Sir."

"When I'm dead — and it's this year; the Greek says two with Baiae and I'll not go to Baiae — the boxes go to Hadrian. Not by my will; a man can't will the emperor's letters. By the fact. He's read what I've let him and he knows there's a third room and he's the one who'll be standing when the door's opened, because Plotina will see to it, because she's been seeing to it since he was fourteen. And he'll want the man who's read them, because a box is nothing without the man, and the man is you." A breath. "I've said *until I'm dead, then do your own sum*. I've said it to him and I've said it to you and I meant it both times. I'll say the sum now, since I've done it, and you can do yours after and see if it comes out the same."

Celer waited.

"Go to him. The day I'm burned. Go to him with the box under your arm and the stick in your belt and say *Sura said*, and he'll take you, and he'll use you, and you'll be the best man he's got and he'll know it and you'll know it and neither of you will ever say so. He'll be emperor. You said it on a stool at fourteen and you were right and I've known it since and I've kept you from him for six years because — " Sura stopped. The knife-face did a thing Celer had not seen it do, which was hesitate. "Because I'm a man who's spent forty years making things useful and I wanted, once, to have a thing that wasn't. That's all. That's the whole of why. Your figure. The way you look at a bridge and say *it's for after*. I wanted it in my office and not his for as long as I had an office, and that's a vanity and I've had worse, and it's done now, and you'll go to him."

"Yes, sir."

"And you'll hate it."

"Yes, sir."

"Good. Hate it with your eyes open. It's the only honest way to serve a man like that; the ones who love him are furniture and the ones who hate him are dead and the ones who do it with their eyes open are the ones he keeps, because he's clever enough to know the difference and vain enough to want the hardest kind." Sura turned his head, slowly, as if it were a thing he had been told not to do, and looked at the freedman's son. "She said the same. Did you know? The girl. Plotina asked her, on the Ides, under the cloth, in the room, whether you'd come to him when I'm dead, and she said — Hadrian told me, laughing; he'd not laughed since the rock — she said: *he'll come, because Sura will tell him to with his last breath, and he'll hate it, and he'll be the best man you've got and the only one you'll never own.*" The eyes held. "*The only one you'll never own.* Plotina had Marcia write it down. It's in a box on the hill with another letter of the girl's, the one about Tingis, which you'll know about by now because the wine-shops do. She said it about you and she said it about herself in the same breath and the Augusta heard both and wrote both down, and in ten years one of those two sentences will be true and the other won't, and I'll not be here to see which, and I'd have liked to." He lay back. "That's the sum. Go away now. Come back in the morning with the Syrian's figure; the Senate's asked again."

Celer stood. He stood with the box under his arm and the hill in the window and the Spaniard on the couch with the cloth in his lap and did not go.

"Sir. One thing."

"One."

"You showed me the sealed box in my second year. You've shown nobody else. Not him." He kept the voice level. "Why?"

Sura looked at the hill for so long that Celer thought he had not heard, or had decided not to.

"Because you'd have found it," he said finally. "You count everything. You'd have counted the boxes in the third room and found one that didn't balance and you'd have opened it, because you're your father's and your father never let a lid stay on, and then you'd have known the thing by accident, which is the worst way to know a thing, because you'd have had to decide what to do with it alone. I'd rather you knew it on purpose. From me. With the wax looked at and not broken." The eyes closed. "That's all. It's not a kindness. It's an old man who's made one emperor by letter and wanted the boy who'd read the letters to know he'd been *let*. Go and do the figure."

* * *

Philon set him to the third room the week after, because Sura had said *the boxes go where the man is* and the man would need to know what he was carrying.

The archivist was sixty-one now and the cough he had had at fifty-eight had not killed him and had not gone, and he stood in the door of the third room with the lamp and Sura's father's ring and watched the freedman's son go along the shelves with a tablet, as he had watched him in the first year, and said, when the boy reached the end of the second shelf, "You've not opened one."

"I don't need to, sir. The dust."

"No. You don't. I taught you that and you've learned it better than I know it." Philon coughed. "Open the fourth on the third. The pine one with no seal. It's not Sura's. It's mine. I've kept it since the year the Caelian was barred and I'd like you to see it before the Spaniard's people are in here with their lists and it goes in a cart with the rest and nobody knows what it was."

He opened it. It was letters. Not many: a year's worth, perhaps, on the cheap papyrus a widow's household used for small things, in a clerk's Latin, headed, each one, *the lady Cornelia Tertulla asks*; and under the heading, the request, a small thing from Sura's office, a date, a name, a sum; and at the foot of each, in a different hand, a line, sometimes two, in the Latin a consul's widow had taught a lodging-house girl, saying what the lady wished to know and why. He knew the hand. He had seen it at eleven on a slate and at fifteen on a shutter and at seventeen in a postscript that said *I know the sum, do the other thing*. He had never seen a year of it together.

"She wrote them," Philon said from the door. "Not the clerk; the lines at the foot. Sura had me keep them separate from the Caelian's other correspondence because he said the lines were worth more than the requests. He said: *there's a girl on the Caelian who's learned to ask a thing so that the man she's asking knows exactly what it costs her to ask, and that's a thing I've seen three men do in forty years and no woman, and I want it in a box.*" The archivist coughed again. "You answered two in three. No postscript. He noticed that too. He said *the boy's been told to do nothing and he's doing it with his hand on the pen, which is the hardest way*."

Celer stood with the box open and a year of her hand in it. He read one. *The lady wishes to know whether the priesthood is still refused, because she has promised Bassus's daughter an answer and will not give her a false one.* He read another. *The lady asks for the name of the man who took the Nola grain-office because her freedwoman's cousin's husband was passed over for it and she would like to be able to say by whom.* He read a third, the last in the box, from the spring of the year his father had said *on her back*: *The lady asks nothing this month. I am writing because the Kalends have come and the lady's house sends a letter on the Kalends and I would not have Sura's office think the house had stopped. — F.*

He put them back. He closed the lid.

"Keep it," said Philon. "Not in the cart. Take it to the Aventine. Sura's said so; he said *the boy'll know what it weighs, and the Spaniard would only read it*." The archivist turned in the door with the lamp. "I'll be dead by the winter; the Greek's said. I've kept the boxes of two Spaniards for forty years and I've never once in that time taken a thing out of them for myself, and I'd like, before the cart, to have given one away. That one. It's the only box in this house that was never useful to anyone, and that's why he kept it, and that's why I'm giving it to you, and that's all the philosophy an archivist has."

He took it to the Aventine. He put it beside the other, the one with the stick and the tablet and the forty-one days, and did not open it again that spring, and Chrestê, who saw everything, looked at the two boxes side by side on the shelf under the Lares and said nothing about them for a week, and then, over the slate, one evening, with her door open: "Two boxes. One's your father's and one's hers. You've put them under the gods where you'll see them when you feed them. That's not nothing, Gaius; that's an altar. I kept a merchant's books and I know what a man's shelf says." And went back to the slate.

The Spaniard came to the house on the last day of April, to see Sura, as he did now every third day, and found the freedman's son in the outer room with the Syrian's figure and stopped.

"Hermes's son. He's sleeping?"

"Since the sixth hour, sir. The Greek gave him the poppy."

"Then I'll wait. I'd rather wait here than in the room; he hates to be watched sleeping, he thinks it's a thing you do to the dead." Hadrian sat, uninvited, on the clerk's bench, in the Greek cloak, with the dust of the cut on it, and looked at the tall young man with the tablet, and the face did the thing. "You've a freedwoman now. Chrestê. The wine-shop says you said a thing to her at the foot of a stair and she sat down in the road. I've had that from four people and none of them knew what the thing was and all of them wanted to."

"It was my father's, sir. Not mine to tell."

"No. Nor would you." The grey eyes rested. "Puteoli says the same about you. *Not mine to tell.* She said it to Sabina about the sail — Sabina asked whether you'd read it to her as a boy and she said *that's his to tell*, and Sabina, who gets everything out of everyone, got nothing, and told me, which is how I know." He leaned back against the wall. "She sits in the library in the afternoons. In the low chair. Did you know? I read and she sits, and now and then I ask her what a thing costs and she tells me, and it's never what I'd have said, and it's always right. Lucius says you do the same with bridges. I've begun to think the two of you were made by the same man."

"The same woman, sir. Her mother. She kept a book."

"Ah." The Spaniard was quiet. "Yes. Plotina said. *A dying woman and a shipowner.*" He looked at the door of Sura's room, which was shut. "When he's dead you'll come to me. He's told you. He's told me he's told you. I'll say one thing about it now and not again, because it's not a thing I say. I'll use you. You know that; she said so, in the room, *he'll hate it*. But I've read the sheet you signed with your father's figure on it, and I've read the Syrian's figure you did in a hut with a deaf clerk, and I've read a girl's line at the foot of a widow's letter that Lucius keeps in a box, and I'll tell you, Hermes's son, that I've never in my life been short of men who'd say yes to me, and I've been short all my life of men who'd tell me the figure, and I've found two, and they were raised in the same street by a woman who kept a book, and I'm not fool enough to think that's an accident or wise enough to know what it is." He stood. "That's all. Tell Lucius I came. Tell him I'll come on the Nones. Tell him I said the Syrian's figure is right; I checked it myself, in the hut, against the Senate's copy, at night, because I'm that kind of man, and there wasn't a line wrong, and I was almost sorry."

* * *

He took a sheet to the library on the Nones, in the afternoon, because the Spaniard had asked for the Syrian's figure in his own hand and Sura had said *take it yourself; he'll want to see the man who made it, and you'll want to see the room*.

The old Greek library on the south side, Augustus's, with the cases going up into the grey light from the high windows and the Greeks on the shelves where an emperor had put them so he could say he had them. He had never been in it. He went between the cases with the sheet and came to the table, and the table had four scrolls open on it and the Greek cloak over the chair and no one in the chair, because the Spaniard had been called to Plotina's room an hour before and had left the scrolls as a man did who meant to come back to them; and beside the table, a pace from it, a second chair, low, a staff's chair, the kind a man sat in to be read to, with nothing on it.

He stood with the sheet. The room was quiet in the way rooms were that had been quiet for a hundred years. The low chair had been sat in; the cushion had the shape of someone in it, lately, that afternoon, a shape that was tall and had sat upright with its hands in its lap and its feet under it, because that was how the cushion had taken the weight, evenly, at the back and not slumped, as a woman sat who had been taught on a Caelian floor that a chair was a thing you were permitted; and on the table, by the Spaniard's scrolls, a fifth, smaller, with a red tag, rolled, that was not the Spaniard's hand on the tag but a copyist's from Pergamum, and was Sappho, because the tag said so, and was the bad copy, because a man who read for pleasure kept the bad copy by him for someone else.

He put the sheet on the table where the Spaniard would find it. He did not sit in the low chair. He stood beside it for the length of time it took the light to move across one case, which was a thing he could count, and looked at the shape in the cushion, and did the sum, and the sum was that she had been here an hour ago, in this chair, a pace from a man who read for eleven, saying what a thing cost, and that she had gone up to Sabina's rooms when the Spaniard was called, and that he was standing in the one room on the hill where she sat every afternoon and she was not in it and he had been sent here by a dying man on purpose, because Sura sent nobody anywhere by accident, and because Sura had said *you'll want to see the room*, and had meant the chair.

He went out between the cases. At the door, where people on that hill turned, he did not turn; he had learned from a steward's girl at a dinner that you told a person how far up the room you were by what you did not do. He went back down to Sura's and said, "The figure's on his table, sir. He wasn't there," and Sura, on the couch at the window, said, "No. Plotina had him. I knew she would; it's the Nones; she has him on the Nones," and looked at the freedman's son's face, and read it, and said nothing else, which was the kindness, and was not one.

* * *

Capito sent for him on the Kalends of May, to the old palace below the Palatine, to the room with the clerk and the box where he had been given a ring, and the clerk was not there; Capito was, at the clerk's table, with the ink on his own cuff, which was the thing about Titinius Capito, that he had been secretary to three emperors and still did his own ink.

He was sixty. He had the face of a man who had been in the room when Domitian was killed and had not been asked how he felt about it and had never said; and he looked at the freedman's son across the table where a ring had been and said, in the Latin, without preamble, "Sura's dying."

"Yes, sir."

"He's written me. About you. Four lines; he's not the breath for more. *The boy. The figure. Hadrian when I'm dead. Until then, give him something to do that isn't watching me cough.*" Capito turned a sheet. "So. The triumph's in June. The Kalends or near it; the Syrian says the road's clear enough through the cut for the procession to come round the Capitol the new way and the emperor's said yes, because he wants the city to see the hill gone before they see the gold. There'll be the gold. All of it, your forty-one days, on carts, with the figure on a board beside each cart in letters a hand high, because the emperor's said he'll have a triumph that's a figure and not a story and the man who weighed it is to see the boards are right." He looked up. "That's you. You'll walk with the carts. Not ride; walk. Behind Sura's horse, with the staff, in the stripe, with the stick if you want it, Sura says you've a centurion's stick and the emperor's asked why and been told and said *let him*. You'll have the boards. Every board. If a figure's wrong on the Sacred Way in front of a hundred thousand people and the Senate, it's your name on it, as it was on the fine." He put the sheet down. "That's what Sura's given you to do that isn't watching him cough. It's not nothing. It's the biggest sum anyone in Rome will ever see written and it's yours, and in twenty years when the Syrian's column's standing with the war cut round it in a spiral, men will look up and say *and the gold, how much was it*, and the answer will be a figure, and the figure will be the one on your boards."

"Yes, sir."

"And one other thing, which Sura didn't ask for and which I'm doing because I was secretary to Nerva for sixteen months and Nerva said to me, on the day the letter came from Cologne, *Capito, a man who's made by another man's letter owes the next one*, and I've never forgotten it." Capito took a second sheet from under the first. "The roll. Your entry. *G. Laecanius Celer, son of Gaius, Falerna, four hundred and six thousand in land.* I've added a line. Rufus's word on the fine; I've entered it as a patron's attestation, which is unusual, and under it a thing of my own, which is more so." He turned the sheet so Celer could read it. Under his name, in Capito's own hand, with the ink still dark: *Weighed the Dacian treasure at the Sargetia, by the emperor's order, forty-one days, the figure accepted by the Senate without amendment.*

Celer looked at it.

"That's a thing that goes on a stone," said Capito. "Not your father's; yours, one day, wherever it is. I've put it on the roll so that whoever cuts the stone will have it from the record and not from a story." He drew the sheet back. "You'll not thank me. Sura says you don't; he says you ask what a thing costs. It costs nothing. That's why I've done it. In this building almost nothing is free and I've been here thirty years and I'd like, once before I'm as old as Sura, to have written a true line on a roll for a man who didn't pay for it."

He walked back along the Forum with the box under his arm. The cut was ahead and to the right, the red wound in the saddle with the dust standing over it, and the gangs going down, and the Syrian somewhere in it shouting; and he stopped at the edge of the old Forum where the new road came round, and looked at it, and did the sum, as his father would have, on the last night of a house.

A ring, which weighed less than a denarius. A roll, with four lines on it now, which was more than most. A patron's word in an atrium. A freedwoman in three rooms with a door he would not go through. A dying Spaniard who had *let* him. A living one who would use him. The boards, in June, with his figure on them a hand high in front of a hundred thousand. A stone on a road with a cypress. A stick in his belt. A box under his arm with a tablet in it that said *the door, not him*, and a letter in it in a hand only he could read that said *the same reason you've not called the villa*.

It was his father's plan. The whole of it. Every line. *Eques Romanus*, a hand taller, seen from a horse. It was done, and he had done it, and his father had been nine days short of hearing a patron say *just*, and it had cost a sail and a fine and a stroke and a tomb and a girl's year of silence and a man in Africa and a woman sitting down on a step, and he stood at the edge of the Forum with it done and felt what Sura had said, which was a clerk, and a box, and *next*; and under that, where he kept the things that were not figures and had been keeping them since a beach, he felt the other thing, the thing he had not had a word for since a mole and had now, because his father had given it him on a tablet with the stylus failing: *not nothing*. That was the sum. It was done and there was something left over, and the something was not the ring or the roll or the boards or the stone; it was that he was standing at the edge of a hill being taken away a basket at a time, and somewhere on the other hill, across the valley, in a cold room with three windows, a girl who had been eleven in a strong-room was teaching an empress's niece a lodging-house book and saying to a dying man's wife in a room with the cloth on that he was the only one nobody would ever own; and that she had said it about herself in the same breath; and that the Augusta had written both down; and that he was going to walk down the Sacred Way in June behind a cart of gold with his figure on a board and she was going to be in a box with Sabina, and they were going to see each other across a hundred thousand people, and a woman with a headache was going to be watching both their faces to see what she had paid for.

He fed the Lares that night with Chrestê's bread, which was fresh, because she had baked, in her own room, with the door open, which it had not been before. He said to them, in the shorthand, out loud: "Done. Every line. Father, it's done. And?"

And from the open door, in the Gulf Greek, not unkindly, because she had been listening, as she always had: "That's the wrong word. The girl on the hill's got the right one; the wine-shop says so; she says *what does it cost*. Learn it. You'll need it in June."
