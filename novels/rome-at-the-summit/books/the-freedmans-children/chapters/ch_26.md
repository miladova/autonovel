# The Boards

The emperor came to see the boards on the Ides of May, alone, which was a thing he did, and which nobody on the staff had ever learned not to be frightened by.

They were in a shed behind the Temple of Peace, where the carts were, forty of them, under canvas, with the Guard round the shed in a ring that did not look like a ring; and in the shed, along the wall, the boards: forty, of pine, a man's height, whitewashed, with the figures on them in black in letters a hand high that a sign-painter from the Argiletum had done and Celer had checked, each one, against the tablet from the sheep-rock, which he had in the box, which was on the trestle by the door with the stick lying across it. Blaesus the deaf clerk was on a stool at the end of the shed with a brush, touching up a nine. Celer was halfway along with the tablet, at the twenty-second board, reading it for the third time, when the light from the door changed, and he looked up, and it was the emperor.

He was fifty-three. He had the face that was on the coins and the face was not a lie; it was simply that the coins left out the thing the face did when it was looking at a figure, which was become, for the length of the looking, entirely still, as a man's face did who had learned on a frontier that a figure was a thing men died of. He came down the shed in a plain tunic with no one behind him and stopped at the first board and read it, and went to the second, and read it, and Celer stood at the twenty-second with the tablet and did not move, because a man who was reading did not want to be spoken to and this man least.

He read all forty. It took the time it took. Blaesus, who was deaf and had not heard the door, went on touching up the nine and did not look round, and the emperor, reaching the fortieth board, looked at the clerk on his stool with the brush and then at the boy with the tablet, and said, in the slow Spanish Latin that had made Hermes of Puteoli stand up in an inn, "He's deaf."

"Yes, Caesar. Apollodorus keeps him for it."

"Sensible. A man who can't hear can't be told what to write." He came back along the boards, not reading now, and stopped in front of the freedman's son, and looked at him, which he had done once before in a tent with a dead Greek on the floor and once on a sheep-rock from thirty feet, and never from three. "Sura's boy. Hermes's. The one who weighed it."

"Yes, Caesar."

"Forty-one days on a rock. Capito showed me the roll; he's put it on, in his own hand, which he does for nobody." The emperor looked at the board behind Celer's shoulder, the twenty-second, and then at the tablet in his hand. "You're checking them against the original. Third time, Sura says. He says you'll do it a fourth on the morning and a fifth on the Sacred Way with your lips moving." The still face did not change. "Good. I've asked for a triumph that's a figure and not a story and the Senate thinks I'm being modest, and I'm not; I'm being a soldier. A story's a thing men argue about in twenty years. A figure's a thing they can't. In twenty years when I'm dead and my nephew's doing whatever he's going to do with what I've left him, somebody in the Senate will say *the Dacian gold was never what Trajan said*, and somebody else will say *the boards were checked by a boy from Puteoli five times against a tablet from the river and Capito put it on the roll*, and that'll be the end of that." He turned to go. He stopped. "Your father died."

"In April, Caesar."

"Sura told me. I stood up for him in an inn once; the *Pelican*. I'd stand up for him again. The fine was just and the man was good and both of those are true and I've never met anyone who could hold both at once without wanting to put one down." The coin-face looked at him a moment longer. "You're holding both. I can see it. Hold them till the Nones and then put the boards down and go and find out what's next. That's an order, since I'm the only man who can give you one Sura hasn't. There's always a next. It's the one thing I know that Sura doesn't; he's never had to march the morning after."

He went out. The light in the door changed back. Blaesus finished the nine, and looked round, and saw the boy standing with the tablet and a face on him, and said, in the loud flat voice of the deaf, "Who was that?"

"The emperor."

"Oh." Blaesus looked at the door. "Did he like the nines?"

"He didn't say."

"Then they're right," said Blaesus, and went to the next board.

* * *

Vibia's letter came on the Nones of May, in the Nola hand that made its letters as if they were threads, and it was the first she had ever written him that was not about the house.

*The Rhodian's dead. The one who drank eleven and wept at your father's fire. In his bed, the week after, with his stick beside him, and the egg-woman's husband found him, who goes in now and then because he's paid a quarter for nothing and has decided he ought to do something for it. I thought you'd want to know. You'll not remember him. She would.*

*They've taken the front down. All of it, from the fish-market to the old customs-shed, for the Syrian's man's new hall. I went to look. I don't know why; I'd never been in the Dolphin in my life; your father had the lease in his books for twenty years and I never once walked past it. There's nothing. A line of foundation-stones and a stair going up to no floor, and a hearth-stone in what was the kitchen, lifted, with the earth under it dark where something was kept. I stood where the stair was. There was a woman behind me, old, with a basket, and she said "that was Hilara's," and I said "I know," and she said "the girl's on the Palatine, they say," and I said "I know," and she said "she kept the book at ten, I settled my eggs with her, she never once passed the higher," and went on. I think it was the egg-woman. I didn't ask.*

*They've begun on the mole. It goes out another two hundred feet, in stone, with a tower. The old end's gone under the new work; you can't stand where the children stood. I tried. There's a gang on it and a man with lime on his arms who shouts in Greek, which the town says is the Syrian's man and I think may be the Syrian's brother, they all look the same, and he told me to get back, so I got back.*

*That's all. I'm writing because the house is quiet and your father's stool is by the loom and I sat on it last night and it was the first time, and I thought: he'd want the boy to know the Rhodian's dead and the mole's gone. He kept a figure for both. I've kept his place in the book. — Vibia.*

*The Lares have the bread. I say your name to them and hers, both, because he did, the last winter, with the one side of his mouth, and I can't read what it meant but I can say what I heard.*

He read it in the shed, by the twenty-second board, with Blaesus touching up a four. He folded it. He put it in the box with the rest. Then he stood a while with his hand on the lid and did a thing he had not known he was going to do, which was count: the mole, the Dolphin's stair, the hearth-stone, the Rhodian, the egg-woman with her basket, the shed where a dying woman had been carried to see the sea in a glass; and the number was the number of things on that coast that had been there when two children stood at the end of a mole and were not there now, and it was high, and the only two left that he could put a hand on were the stone with the cypress and a girl at an empress's shoulder with a pin, and one of those was travertine.

"Who's dead?" said Blaesus, loud, from the four. He had not heard the letter; he had seen the face.

"A Rhodian. He drank."

"They do," said Blaesus, and went on with the four.

Rufus came to the shed on the Ides, in the toga, with no steward, because Thallus was on the coast counting what was left, and stood in the door the way the emperor had and was not the emperor, and knew it, and came in anyway.

"Celer."

"Sir."

"I'll not keep you. I've seen the boards from the door and I've not the head for them; my father had it and I've his nose and not his head, which everyone on the coast has known since I was ten." He stood with his hands in the toga's folds. "I've come to tell you a thing before you hear it in the Senate tomorrow, since you'll be walking past it. I've moved a motion. On the *Pelican*. The Rhine run, the winter of the first war; the emperor's letter, *the man I want fed by*. Sura's office entered a credit against the fine for it, and I've moved that the credit be doubled, on the grounds that a shipowner who sent a hull up the Rhine in December for a legion he'd never see is owed more than a shipowner who's weighed a sail is docked, and the Senate's voted it, because it's the week of the triumph and the Senate votes anything that has the Rhine in it this week." He did not look at the boards. "It's eighty thousand. Back to the estate. To you. It's not the fine; the fine stands and it was just and I've said so in the Senate and I'll not unsay it. It's the other half of the sum, which Sura's office left out because Sura's office is a Spaniard who thinks a man's made by the worst sheet, and I'm a patron who thinks — " He stopped. "I don't know what I think. I think my father sat in your father's strong-room on a stool and that I've sat there too, and that a house that's had two generations on a freedman's stool owes the freedman's son one line in the Senate's record that's not about a sail. That's all."

Celer stood with the tablet.

"Sir. Thank you."

"Don't. Sura says you ask what a thing costs. I'll tell you. It costs me nothing, because I can't pay anything, and I'd like you to know I know that too." Rufus turned in the door, as people did at doors, and looked at the stick lying across the box on the trestle. "That's Crescens's."

"Yes, sir."

"He carried you across the Rhine. My father sent him oil for it every year and never told anyone, because he thought it was a weakness. I found the receipts in the strong-room after." The consul's son's face did a thing that was the family's. "He'd have liked that you wear it. He'd have liked the whole of this, the boards and the roll and the stick, and he'd have said nothing to you about it and told everyone else, and I'm the only one left who knows that about him, and I thought you should have it, since it's the one thing in my father's estate that didn't go to debts."

* * *

Chrestê told him on the Kalends of June that she had told the wine-shop on purpose, and she told him over the slate, with her door open, and did not look up while she did it.

"The shelf. The two boxes under the gods. I said it to the steward's boy and the wine-shop's woman and the baker's wife on the stair, in that order, in a day, in the Greek, and I said *it's an altar*, because that's the word that goes up a hill. It was on the Palatine by evening. Marcia had it at the Augusta's shoulder on the day before the Nones — not today, it's not a Kalends up there this week, they've moved the hair for the triumph — I'm telling you now so you've the day before and not the day after." The stylus moved on the slate. "You'll want to know why."

"Yes."

"Because you'd never have told her. You've a box of a year of her hand under your father's gods and you'd have fed the gods in front of it every night till one of you was dead and never once said so to anyone, because you were raised by a man who wrote things down and never said them, and she was raised by a woman who said one word and died, and the two of you have been not saying things across a valley for five years, and I've kept your slate for two of them and I'm free now and I've decided I'll not keep it for a man who's an altar and won't say so." She looked up. The long face was arranged and under it the thing had moved and was not going back. "So I've said it for you. In a wine-shop. Where it costs you nothing but being known, which you hate, and which is the only price a man like you ever pays for anything. You can be angry. I'd not blame you."

He stood in the outer room with the day's tablet. He was not angry. He looked for it and it was not there, and what was there instead was the thing he had felt at the foot of a stair when a woman had sat down in the road: that a thing had been said that he could not have said and that it had been said *for* him, which was a thing nobody had done since a girl had taken his hand on a mole and held his arm to its length so that he would not go to the edge.

"No," he said. "I'm not."

"Good. Then sit down and eat; you've been in the shed since the first hour and your face is grey." She pushed the bread across. "And Gaius. She knows now. The whole of it, as much as a wine-shop can carry: the box, the shelf, the gods, the far end of the cut every morning since November. Marcia will have given it her as the one true thing, and the Augusta will have said something under the cloth, and the dwarf will have said something on a parapet, because that dwarf sees the cut from the west terrace and he's been counting you in at the shoulder for seven months, the steward's boy says, through the wine-shop's gossip, the same as I've been counting her." She picked up the stylus. "That's all. I've done the thing I could. The rest is two people on a mole, and I was never on it; I only settled the factor's slate there once a month and watched a girl take the lower figure."

* * *

Sura sent for him on the eve.

Not to the couch at the window. To the third room, where Philon had the lamp and the boxes were in rows now with the Spaniard's lists pinned to them, and where Sura was, in a chair, carried there by two of the house's men because he had said he would see the room once more while he could still count it; and he sat in the chair in the middle of the third room with the cloth in his lap and the ring of his father's on Philon's hand beside him, and looked at the freedman's son in the door with the stick in his belt, and said, "Shut it."

Celer shut it.

"Tomorrow," said Sura. "I'll ride. The Greek says I'll not and I've told him I've ridden with a fever on the Danube and I'll ride down the Sacred Way behind the man I made with a cough, and if I fall off, I fall off in front of the Senate, which is where I've done everything else." A breath with a sound in it. "You'll walk behind me with the carts and the boards. Capito's told you. I've a thing to add and it's not Capito's to tell."

"Sir."

"The box. The imperial box, on the Sacred Way, at the turn below the Palatine, where the road comes out of the cut. Plotina will be in it, and Matidia, and Sabina on the left with her hair done the new way, and a girl at Sabina's left shoulder, standing, with a pin." The knife-face, gone to bone, did not change. "Plotina told the girl, with the cloth on, in front of Marcia, that she'd be watching your faces. Both. To see — her words — *whether either of you turns round*. She said it on purpose, so it'd come down the hill to you by the wine-shop, and it has, or you'd not have that look. She wants it seen. Which means she wants it *not done*. If either of you turns round on the Sacred Way in front of a hundred thousand people and the Senate and her, it's a thing on the hill, and a thing on the hill is hers, and she'll have bought the two of you for a sail and a man in Africa, and the price will have been right."

"Yes, sir."

"So you'll not turn round. You'll walk behind the twenty-second cart with the stick and your lips moving over a figure and you'll not look at the box, and she'll stand at Sabina's shoulder and not look at the road, and Plotina will see that, and it'll go in her box with the two sentences, and she'll have been right about you both, which she'll like, and wrong about what it means, which she'll not know for ten years." Sura coughed, and held the cloth, and went on. "And then the day after. I'll tell you what the day after is, since I'll not be at the Syrian's forum and you will. The day after, the gold's in the treasury and the boards are kindling and the carts are hired back to the men they were hired from and the city's drunk and the Senate's voted the games and the Spaniard's in his library and I'm on my couch, and there's nobody on that hill with a thing to say about a freedman's son who goes to the far end of the cut before the gangs, as he's done since November, because that's where the man who made the figure stands. And there's nobody on that hill with a thing to say about a girl who goes to see a Syrian's hole in the ground because a Syrian was told *one day*, because the Augusta's said yes to it already, in April, in the library, and the girl said *not before the triumph*, and the triumph's over." The eyes held his. "I'm not telling you to go. I'm telling you where the far end of the cut is on the morning after, because you've stood there seven months and never once seen a litter, and I'd not have you stand there an eighth not knowing that the one morning there might be one is the one morning you'd have a reason not to go."

Celer stood in the third room with the boxes in their rows and the Spaniard's lists on them.

"Sir. Why?"

"Why what."

"Why tell me. You've said *do your own sum*. You've kept me from him six years for a thing that wasn't useful. This isn't useful to you. You'll be dead."

Sura looked at him for a long time, in the lamp, with Philon at his shoulder and the cloth in his lap.

"Because I wrote eleven letters from Cologne in a month," he said, "and made an emperor out of a soldier and a widow out of a girl from Nemausus who'd have been a governor's wife in a province and happy, and I've never once been sorry, and I've never once been anything else. And because I've read a year of a lodging-house girl's lines at the foot of a widow's letters and kept them in a box because they were worth more than the asking, and I've watched a shipowner's son answer two in three with no postscript because his father said *do nothing* and he's good at it. And because I'm a man who's spent forty years making things useful and I'd like, once, at the end, to have told two people where a hole in the ground is on the morning after and let them do with it what people on a mole do, which Hadrian says is *not turn round*, and which I say is *stand side by side and look at the same thing*, and I'd like to know which of us was right, and I'll not, and that's the and." He raised a hand, slightly. "Go home. Feed your gods. Check the boards a fourth time in the morning. And Celer." At the door, where people in that house did not turn, the freedman's son turned. "The stick. Wear it. The emperor said *let him*. I'd like the Senate to see a centurion's vitis in a freedman's son's belt behind a cart of a king's gold and not know what it means, and have to ask, and be told. That's a story. I know I said the triumph's a figure. A man's allowed one."

* * *

He did not sleep. He had not expected to. He lay in the room with the shelf under the Lares and the two boxes on it and the stick across them, and the window open on the June night and the river beyond the wall and the city making the noise a city made on the night before a thing, which was the noise of a hundred thousand people not sleeping either; and some time after the middle of it he got up and dressed and went down the stair past Chrestê's door, which was open, and in which she said, from the dark, not asleep, "The cut."

"Yes."

"It's not the morning after. It's the morning of."

"I know."

"Then go and stand there anyway. You've done it seven months without a reason; you can do it once with the wrong one." A pause in the dark. "And Gaius. Tomorrow, on the road, behind the cart, when you're not turning round. Don't not turn round the way your father didn't call the villa. Don't make it a thing you're *holding*. Just walk. Read your boards. Let her stand. That's all a mole is, the dwarf says, and he'd know; he's stood at the end of a corridor forty years not turning round, and he's the only one on that hill who's happy."

He went down the Aventine in the dark, and along the river, and through the Forum Boarium with the cattle-smell and the first carts already moving toward the Campus where the procession would form, and past the Circus and under the Palatine's shoulder where the imperial box stood built out over the Sacred Way in timber and hangings with the Guard already at its foot, and did not look up at it, and came out along the Sacred Way into the old Forum, where the stands were up and the sweepers were out and the sand was down, and through it, and past the Curia, and up to the edge of the cut.

The far end. The shoulder of the Quirinal, sheer where the Syrian had sliced it, with the pit at its foot where the column would go, dark in the red, a hill's height measured out in a hole. Nobody was there. The gangs would not come today; the Syrian had given them the triumph, as the emperor had given the city. The dust had settled in the night with no baskets to raise it and the cut lay bare and raw and quiet in the grey before the light, and he stood at the far end of it with his hands at his sides and the stick in his belt and the box left on the shelf for once, because today the figure was on forty boards and not in a box, and he looked at the hole where the column would go, and did what he had done every morning since November, which was nothing.

He did not expect a litter. He had not expected one for seven months. He stood and the light came, grey, and then not grey, over the Esquiline behind him, and touched the top of the sliced shoulder first, and came down it, and the red went from black to brown to the colour it was, and the pit at the foot stayed dark a while longer because it was deep; and he thought, as he had not let himself think on a stool with a sheet or a rock with a king's gold or in a strong-room with a tablet, the whole of the thing, in order, the way he kept things: a mole, and a hull going to ash, and an arm held to its length, and a wall and a shutter and a false paper and a lamp, and a dinner on the Caelian where she had not looked at him and he had understood that she was telling him exactly how far up the room she was, and a street where she had asked him for the egg-man, which was the only thing she had ever asked him for herself and which he had done, and a sheet he was left off of, and a letter to a dead man that said *the same reason you've not called the villa*, and a box of her lines under his gods that a Corinthian had told a wine-shop about so he would not have to, and a dying Spaniard in a third room saying *side by side and look at the same thing*.

He did the sum. It came out. There was something left over, and he knew what it was, and it was not a figure, and he had known since a mole, and he said it, out loud, to the cut, in the house's shorthand, in the voice his father had used for the gods, because they liked to see a face and there was nobody else awake in Rome who could read it: "Not for nothing. I said it at thirteen. I was right. I've been doing the other thing for nine years because you said *all right* and I thought that was the sum." He looked at the pit. "It wasn't. It was the thing left over. She'd have known. She said *and?* and you thought she was telling you and she was asking."

The light reached the bottom of the pit. He turned — not round; away, toward the Campus, where the carts were, and forty boards with a figure on them, and a Spaniard who was going to ride with a cough behind the man he had made, and an emperor who had said *there's always a next* — and went to check them a fourth time, because that was what he was for, and because he had been told, by the only man who could give him an order Sura had not, to hold both until the Nones and then put the boards down.

Behind him, at the far end of the cut, where nobody was, the Syrian's hole lay full of morning, a hill's height, so that nobody would forget.
