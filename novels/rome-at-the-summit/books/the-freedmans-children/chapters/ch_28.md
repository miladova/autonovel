# The Twenty-Second Cart

He walked behind it with his lips moving, as Sura had said he would, and the figure was right forty times, and on the twenty-second his lips stopped.

The Campus at the first hour had been the whole army of the thing in a field in the grey, forming: the Senate at the front in a white mass with the lictors trying to make it a column, the carts behind in their forty with the canvas off now and the Guard round them and the boards on their poles, the pictures on their frames with the paint still tacky where the Syrian had touched the bridge up at midnight, the prisoners in their chains sitting on the ground because nobody had told them to stand, the white horses being walked, the legions beyond in their cohorts with the eagles cased until the gate, and the sound of it, which was not yet a sound, which was forty thousand men and animals and wheels in a field being quiet. He had checked the boards a fourth time by lamp. He had put the tablet in the box and the box on a trestle in the shed with Blaesus sitting on it, because Blaesus had asked to and because a deaf man on a box was the safest archive in Rome. He had the stick. He stood behind the twenty-second cart's tailboard with his hands at his sides and the stripe on and the sandals the Argiletum had sold him for this and waited for the horns, and when they came he walked.

The gate. The Circus Flaminius, the Velabrum, the Forum Boarium with the cattle-smell under the flowers, the Circus Maximus with its hundred and fifty thousand on the stands making the first real noise, and then the long turn under the Palatine's flank toward the Sacred Way, with the sound coming up ahead of them the way the sea came up a beach; and he did not look up. He read the board. Twenty-two carts ahead of him twenty-one boards had gone by and he had read each as it came out of the shed and again at the gate and again at the Circus and they were right; and the twenty-second was his, the one he walked behind, *twenty-two pounds four ounces* in the eleventh line of its column, bar, forty-first, with the line of a wing in it that nobody on the Sacred Way would see because it was under eleven hundredweight of other bars, and he read the board, in letters a hand high, as the cart came round the turn below the Palatine where the road came out of the cut and the box was.

He knew where the box was. He had walked past it in the dark at the first hour with the Guard at its foot. He did not look. He read the board. And the sound did the thing, stopped, all of it, for the length of a breath, as the first cart came into the light below the Capitol, and then came back as a roar that he felt through the sandals and the road, and the twenty-second cart rolled on into it with its board on its pole and he walked behind with his lips moving over a figure he had by heart and had been told by a dying man and a freedwoman and a dwarf he had never met not to make into a thing he was holding.

And his lips stopped.

Not because he looked. He did not look. Because the roar, which was a hundred thousand people being shown a figure, had a shape, and the shape had a hole in it, and the hole was above him and to the right, at the turn, where the box was, and it was the shape of a dozen people in a box on posts *not* making a noise while a city did; and he felt the hole go over him as the cart went under the box the way he had felt a sail go over his face on a sheep-rock, and in it, for the length of a breath, with his eyes on the board, he knew where she was standing as exactly as he had known on a Caelian couch where she was in a room he had not looked at, which was at the left shoulder of a woman in a chair, a pace back, with a pin, reading his board.

Then the cart was past the turn and the hole was behind him and the roar was whole again and his lips moved, *twenty-two four, bar, forty-first*, and he walked on toward the Capitol with the stick in his belt, and did not turn round, and found that it was not a thing he was holding. It was a thing he was walking beside. Chrestê had said *just walk* and the dwarf had said *that's all a mole is* and Sura had said *side by side and look at the same thing*, and the same thing had been a board with a figure on it that he had made on a rock and she had read from a box, and they had both read it and neither had looked at the other and that was, he understood on the Sacred Way with a king's gold in front of him and an empress's box behind, the first thing they had done together in nine years.

* * *

Sura did not fall. He rode the whole of it, behind the fortieth cart, upright, with the cloth in his left hand and his eyes on his horse's ears, and at the Capitol, where the emperor got down and went up the steps to the god and the Senate followed and the prisoners were taken away to the thing the road ended in, the two consuls' lictors had to lift him from the saddle, and he let them, and stood, and said in the Spanish to the nearest, "There. In front of the Senate. Tell the Greek," and was carried to a litter.

Celer found him in it, at the Capitol's foot, in the shade of the arch, with the roar still going on above and the gold being counted into the treasury by Capito's clerks with his boards beside them; and Sura looked out of the litter's curtain with the knife-face gone grey under the bone and said, "Well?"

"Forty boards, sir. All right. Capito's men have them against the tablet now."

"Not the boards."

"No, sir." He stood with the stick. "I didn't turn round."

"No. Hadrian's man on the roof says not. He says your lips stopped at the turn. He says hers did too, or her eyes; he's not sure which; he was a long way up." Sura coughed, and it was bad, and went on, and the litter-bearers looked at each other. "Plotina's seen it. She'll say something tonight to the girl in the box, because she's a woman who can't leave a sum alone once it's come out, and the girl'll put it in a book, Marcia says there's a book now, Sabina gave it her. And tomorrow." The eyes closed. "Tomorrow I'll be on my couch at the window and I'll not know. For the first time in forty years there'll be a thing on that hill I've not had from someone by noon. I've decided to find out what that's like. Plotina's decided the same, Marcia says; she told the girl *I've decided not to know where you go*. Two of us. Not knowing. On purpose. It's the strangest thing that's happened on this hill since Cologne." The curtain began to fall. "Go to the feast. The emperor's asked for you. He'll ask you a thing. Answer it. Then go home and don't sleep, and in the morning do what you've done since November, and don't tell me. Ever. If I'm alive in the autumn I'll not ask and if you tell me I'll have you sent to Capito."

The feast was in the Syrian's basilica, which was not finished and had no roof, because the emperor had said *I'll dine in it with the sky on and the Senate can look up and see what the gold's for*; and the Senate looked up, and dined, and the gold was in the treasury with forty boards beside it, and some time after the second course a Guardsman came down the long table to where the staff were and said to the freedman's son, "Caesar," and he got up and went.

The emperor was at the high table under the open sky with the laurel off and the paint washed off and the face that was on the coins doing the thing it did when it was looking at a figure, and the figure, this time, was a tall young man with a stick in his belt, standing. The Spaniard was on his left, in the Greek cloak, with the grey eyes on his cup. The Augusta was not there; the Augusta did not dine with the Senate; the Augusta was in a room with one window, or a box, or wherever a woman went who had just watched her sum come out.

"Sura's boy." The slow Spanish Latin. "I said I'd give you an order. Hold both till the Nones; then put the boards down; then find out what's next. It's the Nones. The boards are in the treasury. I'm asking." The still face did not move. "What's next?"

He stood under the open sky in the Syrian's unroofed basilica with the Senate behind him and the Spaniard's grey eyes on a cup, and did the sum, and did not pick the first way it came out, and said it.

"Sura's office, Caesar, until Sura's dead. Then the Spaniard's, because Sura's said so and the telling's right. The forum, because Apollodorus has asked and I can count what it weighs. The roll, because Capito's written a line on it in his own hand and I'd not have it be the last." He stopped. He had been asked what was next and he had been taught by a woman at a loom to say a thing once and by a woman over a slate to say it in the voice. "And a thing that's not Caesar's, and not Sura's, and not the Spaniard's, and that I'll not say at this table, because it's not a figure, and the tables on this hill are for figures."

The emperor looked at him. The Spaniard's eyes came up off the cup and rested, and the face did the thing, interesting and a little short; and then did not; and simply looked.

"Not a figure," said the emperor. "At my table. On the day of my triumph. From the boy who weighed the gold." The coin-face did a thing the coins left out, which was, Celer would think afterwards, nearly amusement and was not; it was the face of a man who had marched the morning after a great many things. "Good. There's always one. Nobody tells you. Sura didn't tell me; Plotina did, in a letter from Cologne, the eleventh, at the end, after the sum, in a line I've never shown anyone: *and there will be a thing left over, and it will be you, and I have decided to carry it.*" He picked up his cup. "Go home, Hermes's son. You've an order. Find out what's next. It's not here."

Capito stopped him on the way back down the long table, with the ink on his cuff even here, and a tablet.

"Yours. From the rock. The treasury's done with it; the boards matched, every line, and the clerks have sworn to it on a sheet I've put on the roll under your name, because I said I'd write one true line for a man who didn't pay for it and I find I've written two." He held it out. "Keep it. The Senate's figure is the Senate's now; it's in the treasury's book in a clerk's hand and nobody will ever read the original again. But a man should have the thing he did the sum on. I've my own, from Nerva's year; it's a wax tablet with sixteen months on it and I look at it when I need to remember I was in a room." He let go. "And Hermes's son. The Syrian's looking for you. He's at the far end with the engineers, drunk, and he's asked three times for the boy who counts, and I've told him three times you were with Caesar, and he's said each time *then Caesar can spare him for a pit*."

Apollodorus was at the far end, under the scaffold where the apse would be, with lime on his arms still because he had come from the cut to the feast without washing, and six engineers, and a great deal of wine, and he saw the boy with the tablet and the stick and shouted, in the Greek, over the Senate, "There. The weigher. Come here. Sit. No, stand; you'll be standing in the morning." He put an arm along the engineers' end of the table as if it were a pier. "Tomorrow. The footing. I'm setting the first course of the column's base at the second hour, because the emperor wants it begun the day after and not a day later, and I want it measured first by a man who'll give me a figure and not a story, and my own men are all drunk and will be, and Blaesus can't hear a plumb-line." He looked at the boy with the Damascus face. "You'll be there. At the far end, by the shoulder, with the rod. Before the gangs. I've told the Guard. I've told Lucius. I've told the Augusta's woman, because she asked me what I was doing at the pit in the morning and I'm a Syrian and I don't lie to women with combs." The arm came off the table. "And I've told the dwarf."

Celer stood.

"Which dwarf, sir?"

"Tertulla's. The Augusta's niece's. The one on the west terrace who counts. He came to the hut on the Kalends with a message from nobody, which is how messages come on that hill, and said: *the Syrian was told "one day" and the day's after the triumph; which morning?* And I said the morning after, the second hour, I'm setting a course and I'll want the boy to measure it first. And he said *then I'll bring the measure*, and went." The Syrian drank. "I don't know what that was. I'm an engineer. But I've been on the hill long enough to know that when a dwarf says *measure* he doesn't mean a rod, and I thought the man who's going to hold my rod at the second hour should know there's a dwarf coming to the pit with something that isn't one." He turned back to the engineers. "Second hour. The rod's in the hut. Blaesus will give it you; he sleeps there; shout."

He went. At the basilica's end, under the arch where the Syrian's scaffold was, the Spaniard caught him up, alone, in the Greek cloak, and walked beside him three paces in the dark and said, in the Greek, to the ground, "The library. Tomorrow. I'll be in it from the first hour. I've decided. Plotina's decided not to know a thing and Lucius has decided not to know a thing and I've decided to be in a room with four scrolls and the door shut, so that I'll not be able to say, later, that I was anywhere else." Three more paces. "I asked her in April to see the cut with me. She said *what does it cost* and I told her, and the cost was you, and she said *not before the triumph*. The triumph's over. I'll not be at the cut. I'd like that known by the one man on this hill who'd not tell anyone." And was gone, back toward the high table, and the sky with no roof.

* * *

Chrestê had the lamp lit and the slate put away and her door open and a bundle on the table, and she was sitting beside it with her hands in her lap and the long face arranged, and he stood in the door of the three rooms with the day in him and looked at the bundle and understood it before she spoke, because he had learned, at last, to read her, on purpose, and she had let him.

"I've taken rooms," she said, in the Gulf Greek. "Over the fuller's, two streets down. Mine. On the two thousand's interest, which the banker says is enough for a freedwoman with a slate who'll do accounts for the Aventine's shopkeepers, who can none of them add. I go in the morning. Before light. Before you go to the cut." She did not look at the bundle. "You'll want to know why."

"Yes."

"Because I'll not be the woman at the top of the stair when you come back from it. Whichever way it goes." She said it as a manifest. "If she's there, and you stand side by side and look at a hole, and come back up this stair with your face doing the thing it did on the step when I sat down, I'll not be here to see it and be kind about it and keep your slate, because I'm free and I've decided what I'll do with it and that's not it. And if she's not there — if you stand at the far end eight months instead of seven and come back up this stair with your father's face on — I'll not be here for that either, because you'd come to my door, Gaius, you would, and I'd open it, because I liked it more than I planned, and we'd both know whose door you'd wanted, and I've been that woman for a merchant in Puteoli for four years and I'll not be her for a kind man on the Aventine for the rest of my life." The long face did not change. "So I've taken rooms. I'll keep your slate from there; the shopkeepers' and yours; I'll come on the Kalends and do it on this table and go. And you'll free yourself of the thing you've been not-doing with me since the magistrate, which is sparing me, and I'll free myself of the thing I've been not-doing with you, which is waiting, and we'll both have the receipt, and look at it now and then, and not spend it."

He stood in the door. The two boxes were on the shelf under the Lares with the stick across them, and the room was the room he had bought a woman to keep, and the woman had kept it, and was going.

"I'd like you to stay," he said. He said it because it was true and because his father had said it to a woman from Nola and it had been the best thing his father had done. "And I'll not blame you."

"I know. You said it at the foot of a stair and I sat down. I'll not sit down twice; a woman gets one." She stood. She picked up the bundle. She came to the door, where he was, and stopped, close, with the long face a hand from his, and did not arrange it, and let him see the thing underneath, which had moved on a step and was not going back, and which was, he saw now with the lamp behind her, not fear, not the pen-look, not the sum, but a woman of twenty-three who had told the truth to the man who bought her and had been told it back and was leaving before it cost either of them more than they had. "Gaius. Two things, and then I'm on the stair. The first. You're kind. I said at the start I'd heard it and I'd work with it and I have, and it's true, and it's not the thing about you. The thing about you is that you read a bill aloud to a girl at eleven to show her what you could see, and you've been looking for someone to show it to ever since, and you showed it to Sura and Sura kept it in a box, and you showed it to a Syrian and he put it on a board, and you've never once shown it to the one person who was in the room when you first did, because you were told to do nothing and you're good at it. Stop being good at it. For one morning. That's the first."

"And the second."

"The second's the dwarf's. He sent it down the hill by the wine-shop on purpose, the steward's boy says, the way I sent the altar up, because he and I count the same and we've never met and we've decided between us, through four wine-shops, that the two of you need telling things by people who don't count on the hill." She shifted the bundle. "He said: *tell the shipowner's boy that the girl's mother said "and?" and the girl's worked out this month that it was a question. Tell him she's going to ask it. Tell him to have something after it this time, because he said once that he'd nothing, and burned the sheet, and she's never seen the sheet and knows it was burned, because the Corinthian told the wine-shop that too, because the Corinthian tells the wine-shop everything he'd never say, and the Corinthian's leaving, and this is the last.*" She stepped past him onto the stair. "That's all. Go to the cut. Take the stick; it's the only thing you own she's never seen and you should have one. Don't take the boxes. They're an altar and you don't carry an altar to a hole in the ground; you leave it where the gods can see it and go and find out what's next, which is a thing your emperor said, and he's the only man alive who's done it the morning after and lived."

She went down. He stood at the top of the stair over the wine-shop and watched her go, with the bundle, in the dark, in the drunk city, two streets to a fuller's, and did not try not to read her, and read her, and what was there was a woman who had been sold with the furniture and had just freed herself of the last thing she was owned by, which was him; and he found that he was glad, in a way that was not the feeling at the foot of a stair and was its other half, and that he would miss her at the slate on every day but the Kalends for the rest of his life, and that both were a figure, and that his father would have said *both's a figure, learn to carry it*, and that he could.

He went up the stair, and at the turn of it, where she had stopped the day he freed her, a man was sitting on the step in the dark with a wineskin, and looked up, and had the Seventh's belt-plate on and one arm.

"That's a vitis," the man said, in the Picene Latin, not drunk enough. "In an eques's belt. I saw it on the road today behind the gold and I thought, that's wrong, and then I thought, no, I know that stick, I've had it across my back, that's Crescens's, from the second cohort, the one who went down at the second wall with a hand-stone and no sound." He looked at the tall man on the stair. "You're the one he carried over the Rhine. In Domitian's year. The shipowner's boy. He talked about it, once, drunk, on the far bank; he said *I carried a freedman's son across the Rhine on a boat and he was sick in it and I told him to be ashamed later and he's the only man I ever told that to who did it*."

Celer stood on the stair.

"The optio gave it me. After the wall."

"Aye. He'd have said to. He said a lot of things about you to us that he'd never have said to you, and I'll not say them now; I've one arm and a skin and I've walked behind the eagles today for the last time and I'm sitting on a stranger's stair because it was the first one I came to." The man drank. "I'll say the one. He said: *that boy counts everything and one day he'll count something that matters and he'll not know it till it's counted, and I'd like to be there, and I'll not be.*" He got up, slowly, with the arm. "That's all. I saw the stick. I wanted the man who wears it to know a man from the cohort saw it and knew it. Go up. You've a face on you like a man who's got somewhere to be in the morning."

He did not sleep. He fed the Lares from Nola with the last of her bread. He put the stick in his belt and left the boxes under the gods, as instructed, and at the end of the third hour of the night, when the city was as quiet as it was going to be, he went down the stair past a door that was open on an empty room, and down the Aventine, and along the river, and through the Forum Boarium where the flowers from the morning were being swept into the cattle-dung, and under the Palatine's flank past the box on its posts with the hangings down and no Guard at its foot now, and did not look up at it, and out along the Sacred Way through the sand that was churned to the colour of the cut, and past the Curia, where the Guard knew him now and did not stop him, and up to the edge, and along it, in the dark, to the far end, by the shoulder, where the pit was, and nobody.

He stood. He did what he had done since November. The light would come over the Esquiline behind him in two hours and touch the sliced shoulder first and come down it. He did not expect a litter. He had not expected one for seven months and he had been told by a dying man where the far end was and by a Corinthian to stop being good at nothing and by a dwarf through four wine-shops that a question was coming, and he stood in the dark at the end of a hill being taken away a basket at a time with a dead centurion's stick in his belt, and waited, which was not the same as doing nothing, and which he had not known, until that morning, was a different thing.
