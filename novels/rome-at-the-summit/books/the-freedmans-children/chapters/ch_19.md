# What the Column Means

She told Marcia on the Ides of July, in the room, with the comb, and it took the length of a hair.

She had thought about how. She had thought about it for a month, in the corridor nobody used and the litter and the dark of the room off the lady's with the ivory on the sill where she could see the gold on the break; and what she had decided was that it should be done the way her mother would have done it, which was all at once and without ornament, as a slate was read to a Rhodian who had drunk eleven. Not a confession. A column. She was being asked for a figure and she would give a figure and the figure would be true and what it cost was not on the sheet, and she would not put it there, because the Augusta had said *nothing's free* and she had understood that to mean, among other things, that you did not make the person you were paying watch you count the coins.

So on the Ides, when the room emptied and Marcia did not say *not you* but only looked at her from the door with the round face and the comb, she went through the door, and the Augusta was on the couch with the cloth, and Marcia stood at the shoulder, and Fausta stood by the wall with the box of pins, and began, in Latin, in the eggs voice, to the comb.

"The grain-office pays the carriers by weight at the quay, domina. Pondere, non mensura — by the pound, not the measure. The man who weighs is the prefect's and the man who provides the scale is the port's and in Puteoli the port's man for twenty years was a freedman of the house of Granius who's dead now and whose son has the place. Hermes of Puteoli has three hulls in the Alexandria run. His manifests state the cargo in modii from Egypt and in pounds at the quay, and the pounds are right. The pounds are always right. The prefect's man weighs what's on the pan. What's on the pan is wheat and a sail." She did not look up. "A wet sail. The carriers run with a spare mainsail stowed over the forward hold, and in the night before they're weighed it's gone over the side on a line and come up and been stowed again, and the forward hold is weighed with the sail across the hatch-boards because that's how it was stowed and the prefect's man's not a sailor. A wet sail is four hundred pounds. Three hulls, four hundred pounds a hull, nine voyages a year in a good year, ten years. And that's the bounty, because a hull that's over weight is a hull that's early and full and nobody docks a hull that's over. The Ostia carriers don't do it because the Ostia port's man is Sura's cousin's freedman and has been since Nerva. The Puteoli carriers all do it; Hermes isn't the only one; he's the only one who wrote it down." She let a breath go. "The fourth line of the second column of a sail-maker's bill in his strong-room, eleven years ago. *Pondere non mensura*, in his hand, in the margin, with the figure for a spare mainsail of the heavy canvas, and a second figure under it that's what the mainsail weighs wet, in his hand, because he's a man who writes the sum down. He reads everything twice and writes it once and it's never wrong and he's never burned a sheet in his life. The bill's in the second room off the strong-room in a pine box with the other sail-makers' bills from the year the *Isis* was bought and fitted, which was the year the old emperor adopted the Spaniard. The box has his seal on the lid and the lid's been off; I saw it."

The comb had stopped. She did not look at it.

"That's the column, domina. That's what it means. The short weight in the Subura bread for ten years is a wet sail in the forward hold of three hulls in Puteoli and a dead port-man's son on a scale, and it's in Hermes's own hand in a pine box, and if the prefect's people go to the box they'll find it, and if they go to the port-man's son first they'll find nothing, because he'll have had a boy in the strong-room before them. Hermes has boys everywhere. It's what he's for."

She stopped. The room was very quiet; the city went about its noise under the window.

Under the cloth, the Augusta said: "The name of the box."

"Pine, Augusta. Sail-makers. The *Isis* year. His seal."

"Marcia."

"I have it, Augusta."

"Then it's done." The cloth did not move. "Girl. The Spaniard's wife's stool. Sit."

She sat. She put the box of pins on her knees. She had not wept and would not. She had given the figure all at once without ornament as her mother would have, and it had taken the length of a hair, and now it was in Marcia's round head and would be on a sheet by evening and in Sura's office by the Kalends and on a stool, read, by whoever was on the stool, and she had done it, and there was a thing in her that was not the shutter and was not the floor and that she had not had a name for since a shutter would not bar, and she decided, on Sabina's stool, that she would call it the price, and keep it, and look at it when she needed to know what a thing cost.

"You told it like a manifest," the Augusta said. "Hull, weight, voyage, year. No *I*. Tertulla told me you'd do that and I said no, there'd be an *I* in it somewhere, she's eighteen; and there wasn't. So I was wrong, which happens once a year, and I'll mark the day." A pause. "What did you leave out?"

"Nothing, Augusta."

"Yes you did. You left out that the boy read it to you. You left out that you said *all right*. You left out the pine box's lid being off because you were in that room when it was, and the only reason a shipowner's strong-room has a freedwoman's daughter in it is that the shipowner's son put her there." The voice under the cloth was level, metrical, the voice for Horace. "I'm not asking you to put it in. It's not the figure. I'm telling you I know it's missing so that you'll not think you got it past me, because the day you think that is the day you start leaving things out that *are* the figure, and then I've no use for you and you're in Laurentum. Do you understand?"

"Yes, Augusta."

"Good. Then here's the other half, which Tertulla doesn't know, because I've not told her, because she's got the cough and I'll not spend her on it." The cloth came off. The Augusta sat up, and the plain face that showed what it decided to showed, now, something Fausta had seen once on it and had decided was nearly tiredness, and was. "Sura's back in October with the emperor and the gold. He's got the cough too, worse than Tertulla's; Hadrian writes me so, in the one sentence he spends on Lucius. The sheet goes to his office and he'll set it aside for the boy, because that's what he does. And the prefect's people will go to Puteoli with the sheet and the box, and Hermes of Puteoli will be fined. Not ruined; fined. He'll pay it, and it'll be most of what he has that isn't in his son's name, and he'll know in a day who read the column. He has boys everywhere." She looked at the girl on the stool. "You've not killed a man. You've made a man's figure wrong for the first time in his life, in front of his son. I've done it. It's worse."

"Yes, Augusta."

"*Yes, Augusta.*" The Augusta lay back. "You've got the voice for it already. I had it at twenty." The cloth went over the eyes. "Marcia. The sheet. Tonight. And have the girl taken home the back way; Tertulla's son has a boy at the foot of the hill who counts her in and out and I'd rather he counted her late today, and wondered."

* * *

The lady's cough took her to Laurentum in August, and Fausta did not go.

That was the lady's doing, and the Augusta's, and she understood both and was permitted to argue with neither. The lady went to the sea because the doctor from Laurentum said the air, and because she was sixty-four and had buried a husband and a daughter and had decided, Fausta thought, watching her decide it, that if she was to do the thing the cough was for she would do it where she could see water. And the girl stayed on the Caelian because the Augusta's hair was the Kalends and the Ides and the Augusta did not go to Laurentum in August, the Augusta went nowhere, the Augusta was a woman on a couch with a cloth on a hill and the hill did not move; and so the girl who did the hair did not move either, and the lady said so, in the atrium, with the litter waiting and the cough in her chest like a thing with a grip, and the pale eyes on her girl.

"You'll stay. Phaedrus has the house. Momus has you. The son's at the stable wing and the stable wing's the stable wing; he's not set foot in my side of this house uninvited in thirty-one years and he'll not start because I've gone to breathe." The thin hand came out of the litter and did not touch her face; it touched the ivory in her hair, at the side, with one finger, on the gold. "You'll do the Augusta's hair and you'll write to me on the Nones with what the room said and you'll write it in the Latin I taught you and not the eggs, and when I'm back we'll talk about Puteoli, because the Augusta's told me you've paid, and I'd like to hear from you what, since she'll only tell me the figure."

"Yes, domina."

"And child." The pale eyes held. "I've told Phaedrus a thing and I'll tell you so you know he's been told. If the son comes to my side of the house while I'm gone, for any reason, Phaedrus is to send to the Augusta the same hour. Not to me. To *her*. The son knows this; I told him at dinner, in front of the dwarf, so he'd know the dwarf knew. It's the only thing I can do from Laurentum and I've done it." The hand went back into the litter. "It'll hold. He's patient. Patient men don't break a thing they've waited for. That's what patience *is*."

The litter went. Fausta stood in the atrium with the pools and the ancestors looking down and Momus on his stool by the door, and the dwarf said, low, when the sound of the bearers had gone down the hill, "She's wrong, you know. About patience. It's not that they don't break a thing. It's that they wait till breaking it's cheaper than waiting. I've known three patient men and they all had a number, and the number moved."

"What was his number?"

"Her cough," said Momus. "I told you. And it's gone to Laurentum, and the doctor looks at it the way doctors look, and he knows that, because he was at dinner too, and so he's doing his sum again, girl, and I'd not be alone in a corridor till it's done."

* * *

She was not alone in a corridor. She was alone in the room off the lady's, in September, in the night, on the Nones, with the letter to Laurentum written and sealed on the sill beside the ivory, and the door barred, because she barred it, because she had barred every door she slept behind since a shutter that would not, and the bar was oak and the brackets were iron and the lady's house was a consul's and built by people who had meant it.

He did not come through the door.

She would understand, afterwards — not that night; that night she understood nothing, she was a thing in a room — that there was a second door. That the room off the lady's had been, once, before it was a girl's, a dressing-room, and that a dressing-room in a consul's house had a slaves' door, narrow, in the panelling behind the chest where the lady's winter things were kept, that gave on a passage that ran behind the lady's rooms to the slaves' court, and that had been papered over, and was known to the people who had been born in the house, and not to the people who had been in it twelve years, or three. Phaedrus did not know; he had twelve years and a key to every door he had been shown. Momus knew, because Momus had forty years and a stool at the end of the corridor it opened onto, and had thought it bricked. The lady knew and had forgotten, because a door you have not used since your husband died is a door that is not there. And the son knew, because he had been born in this house and had been a small patient boy in it, finding doors.

She woke because the lamp had moved. She kept a lamp lit; the lady had said so, the first week, *you keep a lamp lit, child, that's the first thing*, and she had, every night for three years, low, on the sill by the letter and the ivory, and it had moved, the light had moved, because a door she did not know was a door had opened in the panelling behind the chest and the air had come through and the flame had leaned; and she was awake, all at once, the way she had been awake at eleven in a lodging-house with the Rhodian on the stair, and he was in the room.

She would keep it. She kept everything. But she would keep it the way she had kept the egg-woman's mother's hands on her mother's body at the end, which was by looking at the lamp, at the ivory, at the sealed letter on the sill with the lady's name on it in the Latin the lady had taught her, and not at the thing itself; and so what she kept was the lamp, and the ivory, and the letter, and the sound of her own breath, which was the eggs voice and did not stop being it; and his weight, which was the heaviness that had gone to the shoulders; and his patience, which was the worst of it, which was that he was not quick and not rough and not drunk and did not speak, and did it as he did everything, as a man did a thing he had put where it would be seen and waited for; and that she did not scream, because the lady had told her at dinner in front of the dwarf that the son knew the dwarf knew, and so she understood, at the start, with the lamp leaning, that a scream was a thing he had already counted and that whatever he had counted it against was heavier; and that she did the sum, in the eggs voice, under his weight, with her eyes on the ivory, and the sum was *live*, and she lived; and that it was not long, and that it was not brief; and that when it was done he stood up in the leaning light and arranged his tunic as a man did who was going back to the stable wing by a passage, and looked at her, with his mother's narrow patient face, and said the only thing he said, which was, "Now it's known. It was always going to be known. I've only moved the day," and went out by the door in the panelling and pulled it to behind him, and the lamp straightened.

She lay in the straightened light. The letter to Laurentum was on the sill with the lady's name on it. The ivory was beside it with the gold on the break. She looked at them for a time she did not count, which was the first thing in eight years she had not counted, and then she got up, and unbarred the oak door that had held, and went out into the corridor nobody used, and down it, in her shift, with her hair down, to the end, where a stool was, and a dwarf on it who did not sleep, and who looked up at her face in the corridor's bad light and got down off the stool without a word and went past her, fast, on the short legs, toward the slaves' court and the steward's room, and she stood in the corridor with her back to the wall and her feet under her and waited, as she had waited for a Rhodian's eleven to come right, and it came right, because Phaedrus came, with a lamp, and a face like water with a stone dropped in it, and looked at her, and said, in the Greek, very quietly, "The panelling door."

"Yes."

"I never knew it was there. Twelve years, and I never knew. He did." The Alexandrian's face went blank the way a face did when a steward who had not declared anything in twelve years understood that he had just been made to. "I'm sending to the hill. Now. As she said. Not to Laurentum; to *her*. Momus is at the gate; he'll go himself; nobody stops a dwarf at the Palatine postern at night, they think he's a gift." He held the lamp up and looked at her the way the Augusta had, to see what was load-bearing. "Can you stand until the morning?"

"I'm standing."

"So you are." He lowered the lamp. "Then stand in my room. It has one door and I'll be in front of it. And in the morning Marcia will come, or someone Marcia sends, and you'll say it as a manifest, the way you said the sail, because that's the only way it'll be heard on that hill, and if you say it any other way it'll be a girl's story and girls' stories go to Laurentum." He turned. "Come. I'll not touch you. I'd not have anyone touch you tonight. But come."

She went. She stood in the steward's room with its one door and the Alexandrian in front of it with his back to her on a stool and the lamp between them until the light came grey through the shutter, and she did not sit, and she did not weep, and she did not sleep, and somewhere toward the end of it she found that she had put her hand to her hair, where the ivory would have been, and that it was not there, and that she had left it on the sill, and that she would go back for it, in the morning, and wear it, because it was hers now in a way it had not been, and because the Augusta had said *keep wearing it*, and because a thing he had put in her hand to be seen was now a thing she would be seen in, and the seeing would do the work, and he had taught her that.

* * *

Marcia came herself.

She came at the second hour with two of the Augusta's Germans, who stood in the atrium under the ancestors and did not look at anything, and she came into the steward's room with the round brown face arranged as it was in the room on the hill and sat down on Phaedrus's stool without being asked and folded her hands and said, in the Nemausus Latin, "Say it."

She said it. As a manifest. The hour, the lamp, the panelling, the chest, the passage behind the lady's rooms, the weight, the words he had said. No *I* that could be left out. It took the length of a hair.

Marcia listened. When it was done she sat for a moment with her hands folded and then she said, "The Augusta had Momus at the postern at the third hour of the night and had me out of bed at the fourth and I've been with her since, and I'll tell you what she said, all of it, because she told me to, and because you've earned the whole and not the figure." The round face did not change. "She said: *he's moved the day.* She said: *he's done his sum and the sum was that his mother's dying and the house comes to him and the girl with it, and that when it does, it's known, and that a thing that's going to be known can be made known sooner at the cost of one night's risk, and the risk was me.* She said: *he's wagered that I'll not move a hair-girl against a consul's son for a thing done in his own mother's house where the law gives him more than it gives her, and that by the time Tertulla's dead and the question's whose the girl is, the answer will already be in every mouth on the hill, and I'll find it easier to let it lie.*" Marcia unfolded her hands. "She said: *he's not a fool. That's a good sum. It's the one I'd have made in his place.*"

Fausta stood by the wall. The price was in her, where she kept it, and she looked at it.

"And then?"

"And then she said: *but he's made it against the wrong woman, because I'm not a hill, I'm a headache, and a headache doesn't do what's easier, it does what's* quiet. And she said this, and I'm to say it to you exactly: *tell the girl that I'll not move a hair against him. Tell her that. Tell her that the law's his and the house is his mother's and that nothing that happened in that room happened, on the hill, because if it happened then I have to do something about it and the thing I'd have to do is loud. Tell her I'm sorry; I've said it once in thirty years and I'm saying it now, through you, so it's not in my mouth. And then tell her that there's a procuratorship in Mauretania Tingitana, the lesser one, the one that counts the corn-ships at Tingis, that's been vacant since the spring because nobody with a consul's name will take it, and that the emperor's secretary for appointments owes me a thing from the year of the adoption, and that a man who's never been north of Ariminum could be in Tingis by the Kalends of November if his name went in a letter this week, and that Tertulla's son's name is a consul's name, and would do.*" Marcia stood up. "She said: *that's not justice. Justice is loud. That's paper. Ask the girl if she wants paper. If she says yes, she writes the letter herself, to me, in her own hand, asking for it, and I keep the letter, and she knows I keep it, and that's the price, and it's the last thing I'll ever give her for free, because after this she's not a hair-girl, she's a woman who's had a man sent to Africa, and women like that pay for everything.*"

The room was quiet. Phaedrus, by the door, had not moved. Outside, in the atrium, the Germans stood under the ancestors.

Fausta said, "He'll know who."

"Yes. He'll know. He'll count corn-ships in Tingis for five years knowing. When Tertulla goes you come to the hill, to Sabina; she's asked twice and been refused and won't be a third time." Marcia went to the door. "He's a patient man. In five years he'll come back and you'll be twenty-three and whatever the Augusta's made you by then, and he'll have to do his sum again. I'd not like to be him." She stopped at the door and looked back, and the round brown face, for the first time in Fausta's knowledge of it, did something that was not arranged. "I'm not sorry. Sorry costs and buys nothing. But I'll tell you this, girl. When I was fourteen in Nemausus there was a man in my mistress's house with a patient face, and there was no panelling door, and no dwarf, and no hill to send to. There was a river. I'll say no more about it than that. When you write your letter asking for Tingis I'll put it in the box with the only other letter like it I've ever kept, which is mine, to her, in the year of the adoption." She opened the door. "Write it today. The secretary for appointments dines with the Augusta on the Ides and I'll want it in her hand by then. Ink's in the steward's desk. Phaedrus will bring you the sheet. Then go and get your pin. It's on the sill. You'll want it for the Kalends."

She wrote it. In the Latin the lady had taught her, not the eggs, to Marcia, freedwoman of the Augusta, from Fausta, daughter of Hilara, freedwoman of Lucius Laecanius Bassus: that she understood the procuratorship of the corn-ships at Tingis was vacant, and that she begged the Augusta's freedwoman to recall to the Augusta that the lady Cornelia Tertulla's son was a man of a consul's name and unemployed, and that the lady Cornelia was ill at Laurentum and might be eased by knowing her son had a post. Four lines. No *I* that could be left out. She sealed it with the Dolphin, the brass fish, which she had carried against her skin for four years and had not used for anything but a lease, and gave it to Phaedrus, and went down the corridor nobody used to the room off the lady's, and in by the oak door, and the lamp was out, and the letter to Laurentum was on the sill, and the ivory was beside it.

She put it in her hair. At the side. Where the lamp would catch the gold on the break.

Then she sat down on the bed, for the first time, and looked at the panelling behind the chest where a door was that was not a door, and did the sum, slowly, out loud, to the empty room, because there was no one to hear it and she wanted to hear what it sounded like: "A man to Africa, for a letter. That's one. That's the first I've done by paper. Momus says a patient man has a number and the number moves. Mine's five years. I'll count them." And then, after a while, in her mother's voice, which she had not used since a mole: "And?"

There was nothing after it. She had known there would not be. She sat with it anyway, in the room with two doors, until Phaedrus knocked on the one that barred and said through it, in the Greek like water, that the sheet had gone to the hill with Momus, and that the son had left for the stable wing's country place at Tibur an hour ago with two mules and no word, and that the lady's letter was still on the sill and would she like it sent, or would she write it again.

"Again," she said. "She'll want to know what the room said. I'll tell her. It said Tingis."

* * *

On the Kalends of October she did the Augusta's hair, and the Augusta said nothing about it, which was the thing.

She had known she would not. She had been told, through Marcia, in words meant to be exact, that nothing had happened on the hill because if it had the hill would have had to be loud; and she went up it in the litter alone, with Momus walking beside the bearers because Phaedrus would not have her go up it without a face from the house, and through the gate and along the long wall into the room with one window and eleven women, and the benches looked at her as they had looked for three years, which was not at all, and that was the first thing she kept from that morning: that it had held. Whatever the son had counted on, the thing being in every mouth on the hill by the Kalends, it was not. The mouths were shut. A headache had shut them. She did not know how and would learn, over years, that it was not done by any one thing but by a woman on a couch having been for thirty years the person nobody wanted to be the one to tell.

She went through the door. The Augusta was on the couch with the cloth off and the book, and Matidia was on her stool with hers, and Sabina was not there, because it was the Kalends and the Kalends were her mother's; and Marcia stood at the shoulder with the comb and the round face arranged, and said, "The old way," as she said, and Fausta put down the box and began.

The Augusta read. She turned a page. Her hair was fine and grey and had been plain, once, on a coast built on ash, and Fausta's hands did what the coast woman had taught them in a steward's room three years ago, which was nothing, done perfectly, and the room was quiet, and the city went about its noise under the window, and the ivory at the side of her own head caught the light from the one window on the gold at the break; and when it was done, and the last pin was in, the Augusta put the book down and turned her head, once, to the left and the right, as she did, and said to the ceiling: "That's it. That's my mother's."

And then, without looking at anyone: "The secretary for appointments dined on the Ides. He tells me the lesser procuratorship at Tingis is filled, by a man of consular family who's been wanting a post, and that the man sails from Puteoli on the first fair wind. Tertulla will be pleased. She's always wanted him to do something." A page turned, though the book was closed; it was Matidia's. "Marcia. Have we anything else?"

"No, Augusta."

"Then the room may go."

That was all. Fausta picked up the box and went to the door, and at the door, as she had once before, she stopped, because there was a thing to be said and it was not true, and she had decided on a mole never to pretend about what was true, only about what it cost; and she turned, with the box, and said, "Augusta. Thank you."

The plain face that showed what it decided to looked at her from the couch for a long moment, and decided.

"No," said the Augusta. "That's the wrong word. You've said the right one once and I'll not have you unlearn it. Try again."

Fausta stood with the box. Matidia had looked up from the book, which she had not done in three years. Marcia's fingers had stopped on the comb.

"Augusta," she said. "What does it cost?"

"Better." The Augusta lay back and reached for the cloth. "It costs this. When Tertulla dies — and she will, the doctor writes me so, in the voice doctors use when they've stopped lying — you'll come here, to Sabina, and you'll be hers, the way you've been Tertulla's, and Sabina will love you, because she's lonely and clever and you don't perform, and she'll make you the one true thing in a house that's going to be the emperor's one day, and I'll let her, because I need one, and I'd rather it was you than a Greek girl who dies of a fever." The cloth went over the eyes. "And then one day a man I've chosen will look at you, the way he looks at everything that's interesting and a little short of what it might have been, and you'll have to decide what you are. That's the cost. It's not now. It's in five years, or ten. I've bought you from a patient man and I've put you in a room with a worse one and I've done it with my eyes open, and so, girl, have you, and I'd not have it otherwise. Go home. Write to Tertulla. Tell her the room said she was always wanting him to do something."

She went down the long wall behind the comb. In the litter at the foot of the hill, Momus climbed in without being asked and sat opposite her with his short legs out and looked at her face, and said nothing for three streets, and then said: "He sailed this morning. The first fair wind. I had it from the stable wing's boy, who's drunk because nobody told him he'd have to go too." The dwarf looked out at the Caelian going by. "Five years. You said you'd count them."

"I've started."

"Good. Count them out loud, now and then, where I can hear. I've counted a lot of things in that house and I'd like, once, to count one that comes out."
