# The Lease

The lady sent for her in November, and she went to Laurentum, and the lady was dying by the sea in a room with a window, as she had decided.

It was a small house. That was the first thing Fausta kept of it: that the consul's widow who had a house on the Caelian with twelve girls and an Alexandrian and a dwarf and a corridor nobody used had, by the sea, four rooms and a terrace and a doctor and one woman, and had had them for thirty years, and had gone there every August of those years to be, for a month, a woman with four rooms. The terrace looked at water. Not the Gulf; the open sea, grey in November, with nothing on it, no Alexandrian hulls, no mole, no *Dolphin* going out to burn; the sea that was simply the sea and had no manifest. The lady lay on a couch on the terrace in the cold with four blankets and the cough and the pale eyes and looked at it, and when the girl came out onto the terrace with the dust of the road on her she did not turn her head, but said, in the old Latin, "You've come. Good. Sit where I can see you without turning; the doctor says turning's a thing I've done enough of."

She had gone down the Caelian's stair for the last time the morning before with nothing in her hands, because Phaedrus had sent the chest ahead, and at the foot of the stair, by the hatch to the bakehouse, Zaidê had been standing with her arms folded and her plait over her shoulder, as if she had been there a while.

"You'll not come back."

"No."

"Good." The flat face. "I'll have the parapet, then. On the roof. In August. It's the coolest place and nobody wants it because of the drop." She did not unfold her arms. "The stoker's been sold. To a bath on the Esquiline. They don't tell you; you go to the furnace and there's a Thracian. So." A shrug, of the kind the house taught, that moved nothing. "I'm to be the one who pours for the son's wife when he has one. Phaedrus says. He says I've the face for it; nobody looks at it."

"They don't," said Fausta. "That's a thing you can use."

"I know what it is. You're not the only one in this house who learned where to stand." Zaidê looked at her, once, the whole length, from the ivory in the hair to the hem, the way she had looked at her in the steward's room with the basin, and said, "Go on, then. Up your hill. I'll not say I'll think of you. I'll think of the figs." And she unfolded her arms and took the jug from the sill beside her, which had been there the whole time, full to the brim, and carried it away across the court without a drop going over, which was a thing she had learned to do, Fausta understood, watching the plait go, in the same month, four years ago, on the same stairs, for the same reason.

Fausta sat. The sea went about its business below.

"The Augusta told me the figure." The lady's voice was a thread of what it had been, and the thread had the whole of the voice in it, as the egg-woman's mother's hands had had the whole of a lifetime of doing a thing. "Puteoli. The sail. I'd like to hear what it cost. She wouldn't tell me and Marcia only tells me what the Augusta's said she may, and I'm dying, child, and I've a right to the one thing on that hill nobody will give me, which is the price of something."

So she told her. On the terrace, in the cold, in the Latin the lady had taught her and not the eggs, with the sea below: the year of the tongue, and Sabina on her stool, and the pin, and the fountain, and the month, and the Ides of July with the comb, and what she had left out and why, and what the Augusta had said about it, and the paper from the prefect's archive under the hearth-stone with the other one. She did not tell her the panelling door. The lady knew, because Phaedrus had written on the Nones, as instructed, to *her* and not to Laurentum, and the Augusta had told the lady what she decided to; and the lady did not ask, and Fausta did not say, and it sat between them on the terrace like a third blanket.

When she was done the lady was quiet for a long time, looking at the sea.

"Tingis," she said at last. "I'd not have thought of Tingis. I'd have thought of Sardinia; it's nearer and worse. But Tingis is a port and he'll have to count things, and he's never counted anything in his life but days." The pale eyes did not move from the water. "I wrote to him. Did you know? The week after. Not about — not about anything. I wrote and said I was glad he'd a post and that his father would have been and that he was to write to me from Africa on the Kalends, and he's written twice, and the letters are about corn-ships and the heat. That's how I know. A patient man who's been sent somewhere writes about the weather." She coughed, and it took a while, and the one woman came and went. "I'll not see him again. The house goes to him; the law's the law. You're not in it when it does. You go up the hill the day I'm burned and you don't look back at the Caelian. I'd have had that door bricked up if I'd remembered it was there. I didn't. That's mine."

"Domina —"

"No. Let me. I've a thing to say and not much breath." The thin hand came out of the blankets and lay on the couch's edge, palm up, open, as it had never been in three years; and Fausta looked at it and understood that she was being asked to take it, and did, and it was cold and light and had nothing in it, and the lady's eyes stayed on the sea. "I took you for a price. You know that. I'd not have you think I was kind. I wasn't. I was a widow with a son I didn't like and a house I couldn't fill and I took a freedwoman's child off a coast because she'd a nose like a consul and a face like a shutter and I thought I'd see what she'd do." The hand closed, a little, on hers. "I've not been so entertained in thirty years. I'll not pretend it was anything higher than that. You'd know."

The sea went on. The hand in hers was very light.

"There's one more thing. The lease."

Fausta did not move.

"The Dolphin. Phaedrus has had a letter, from the young master's steward on the coast — the one who asked your birth-year. He's in Rome. He's come up with the young master, who's in the Senate now and finds he has to be seen in it, and he's written to my steward asking, very politely, whether the lady Cornelia's household includes a girl from Puteoli who holds a lease on a lodging-house by the harbour, and whether the girl might be willing to discuss its sale, since the young master is settling his father's affairs and the harbour's being rebuilt and the lodging-house is in the way." The pale eyes came off the sea, finally, and found her. "He's not come for the lease. You know that. A steward who wants a lease sends a boy. He's come to see you. He's come to put himself in a room with you and watch your face while he tells you a thing, because that's what he does; he did it to your mother for ten years and he did it to you at a funeral and he'll do it again, and I'd have Phaedrus send him away, except that I'll be dead by the time he comes and the house will be my son's and Phaedrus will be my son's steward and will have to let him in." The hand tightened. "So hear me. See him on the hill. Not the Caelian. Have Marcia give him a room in the Augusta's outer court with a German at the door and see him there, in your pin, with your feet under you, and let him say his thing, and sell him the lease, because it's ash and you know it and holding ash is a boy's move; and when he's gone, go and do Sabina's hair, and never think of Puteoli again as a place you could go back to. You can't. Your mother knew that in the shed on the mole. It's why she sent you; it's the whole of what she sent you with. *Don't come back. Go up.*"

She died on the Ides, in the night, with the window open and the sea doing nothing below it, and the one woman woke the girl at the first hour and said *it's done*, and Fausta went out onto the terrace in the cold and stood where she could see the water without turning and said the thing that was said, in the old Latin, badly, because she had only ever heard it said once and that had been by a grave in Puteoli in the eggs voice; and then she went in and did the lady's hair, the old way, plain, for the burning, because the one woman could not and someone had to and it was, she understood with the comb in her hand, the last price in a house that had been nothing but prices, and the only one she had set herself.

* * *

Sabina's rooms were on the north side of the hill, where the Augusta's were not, and had three windows, and were cold.

She came up the day of the burning, as the lady had said, with Momus walking beside the litter and the ivory in her hair and the box of pins and the Strabo and the letters and the two papers and the brass fish, which was all she had come from the coast with and all she took from the Caelian, and she did not look back at it, as instructed, and found that she did not want to; and at the top of the hill Marcia met her at the postern and took her not along the long wall to the room with one window but the other way, north, through courts she had not seen, past Germans and gardeners and a fountain that worked, to a door, and through it, to a woman of twenty-three sitting by a window with her hands in her lap and no book, who looked round with the beautiful disappointed face and said, in the low quick voice, "Oh. You. Good. She said she'd send you and I thought she'd send a Greek. Come here. Let me look at the pin." And looked at it, close, with the dry tenant intelligence, and said, "Island work. Mended. From the man she's sent to Africa. And you're wearing it. You're wearing it *into my rooms*." And sat back. "Yes. All right. You'll do. Marcia, go away; she's mine now, you've said so, and I'll not have you standing there counting her."

Marcia went. The door shut. Sabina looked at the girl in the middle of the cold room with her box and her feet under her and said, "Sit down. Not the stool; the couch. There. I'll say this once and then we'll never mention it, because I've been in this house twenty-three years and I know what's mentioned and what's not." She folded her hands. "I know what the Augusta did for you and what she did it for. I know what she'll want you for in five years, because I know who she's chosen and I know what he looks at. I'm his wife. I've been his wife since I was seventeen and I've watched him look at things that are interesting and a little short of what they might be, and I'm telling you now, the first day, so you'll have had it from me and not from Marcia with a pin: when he looks at you, and he will, I'll not hate you for it. I'll have expected it. I'll have *arranged* it, if I'm honest, by keeping you in my rooms where he can't help seeing. Because the one thing I've learned on this hill is that the only person a man like that can't own is the one his wife's already got." The beautiful face did a thing that was nearly a smile and had no warmth in it and a great deal of something else. "So. You're mine. I'll be kind to you because I've no one else to be kind to and because you don't perform, and I'll use you, because that's what the hill is, and you'll know I'm doing both, because you're not a fool, and we'll get on. Do my hair. Not the old way; I'm not an old woman from a coast built on ash. The new way. The way the legate's wife had it last Ides that made my great-aunt put the cloth on. I want him to come in from his forum and see it and not know for a moment who I am."

She did Sabina's hair. The new way. And when the Spaniard came in that evening, in the Greek cloak with the dust of the cut on it and the grey eyes, and stood in the door of the cold room and looked at his wife by the window, he did not know her for a moment, and Fausta saw him not know her, from the corner by the chest where she stood with the box, and then saw him know, and saw the look go on past the wife to the corner, and find a tall girl with a nose and a pin, and stop, for the length of time it took a flame to lean; and then go on, to the window, to the wife, with the thing on the face that found everything interesting and a little short; and he said, in the Greek, "Vibia. You've done something to your hair. It suits you. Who's the girl?"

"Plotina's," said Sabina, not turning from the window. "Mine now. From Puteoli. She knows what a thing weighs; your Syrian says so." And then, in the low quick voice, to the glass: "You've seen her before. On a rock, Apollodorus says, you went to see a boy count gold, and she's the girl who put the boy's father on a sheet. Don't pretend. I hate it when you pretend; it's the one thing you're bad at."

The Spaniard stood in the door. The grey eyes went to the corner again and this time did not stop, but rested, and the face did nothing, and then it loosened, and he said, in the Greek, to the corner, "Puteoli. The sail. Yes. I read it. I've read everything on that hill; it's my only vice." A pause. "Lucius has the boy. He's doing the figure for the forum. It's true, which is more than the Senate's used to." And then, to the window: "I'll dine with Plotina. She's asked. Don't wait." And he went, and the door shut, and Sabina laughed, once, short, dry, at the glass.

"There," she said. "Now he knows where you are. That's a stone laid. My great-aunt lays them in front of Syrians; I lay them in front of him. We're a family of masons." She turned from the window. "Sit with me. I'll not sleep. Tell me about the coast. Not the sail; I know the sail. Tell me about a lodging-house with a book and a girl who kept it. I've never kept anything in my life and I'd like to hear what it's like."

So she told her. Not the whole of it; she had learned on the Caelian that the whole of a thing was a price and you did not pay it on the first night. But she told her the slate, and the Rhodian's eleven, and the stairs, and the egg-woman's mother with her hands in a basin, and the shed on the mole with the sea in the glass, and Sabina sat by the window with her hands in her lap and the beautiful face turned to the dark and listened in a way nobody on the hill listened, which was without waiting to speak; and when Fausta stopped, at the shed, because the shed was where she stopped, the Spaniard's wife was quiet for a while and then said, to the glass, "A book. A real one. With a Rhodian in it who drinks and a figure that comes out right or doesn't. I've a husband who writes four sheets about a hill-fort and a great-aunt who's performed a headache for twenty years and a mother who's turned the same page of the same book every Kalends since I was born so she'd have somewhere to look, and I've never in my life been in a room where a thing was simply *counted*." She turned. "Teach me."

"Domina?"

"The slate. The book. How it's kept. Teach me. I'll not need it; I'm the emperor's niece's daughter and the next emperor's wife, if my great-aunt has her way, and she will, and I'll never settle a Rhodian's bill in my life. But I'd like to know how it's done. I'd like one thing in my head that's true because it adds, and not because somebody on this hill decided it would be." The dry tenant intelligence looked out of the beautiful face at her with something in it that was nearly need and had been trained, for twenty-three years, never to show as that. "You'll do my hair and you'll sit with me when I can't sleep, which is most nights, and in the mornings before the room you'll teach me a lodging-house book. That's the price of these rooms. It's a better one than you got on the Caelian and you know it."

"Yes, domina."

"And don't call me that when the door's shut. I've heard it from Greeks for twenty-three years and I hate it. Vibia. Or nothing. Say nothing, mostly; it's what I liked about you. And yes, I know the shipowner's widow's a Vibia too; Marcia told me; it's the commonest name in Italy and I've never once been let use it."

Marcia found her in the morning, in the court with the fountain that worked, going to the Augusta's room with the box, and fell in beside her along the wall with the comb, as if the Caelian had never been.

"You told her the coast."

"Some of it."

"Yes. Some of it. She'll have the rest in a month; she's patient when she wants a thing and she's never wanted anything she could have." The round face was arranged. "I've a thing to tell you, since you're on the hill now and not under it, and it's this. When you came, three years ago, I did a sum. I always do. I counted five things in that room that weren't yours and that somebody would use if you didn't: the paper, the steward's sheet, the boy, the widow, the son. I said to myself, that one's got five doors in her and no key to any, and the first one that gets a key is the one that'll own her, and it had better be the Augusta, because the rest are worse." Marcia did not look at her. "The paper's yours. The Augusta filed it; it's done. The steward's sheet's dead; he came up the hill to tell you so himself, Momus says, which I'd not have believed of a steward. The widow's burned. The son's in Tingis counting corn-ships, and that's a door with your own hand on it, and you know what it cost and so do I. That's four." The comb went into the sleeve. "One left."

"The boy."

"The boy. On Sura's staff and the Spaniard's eye and his father's roll, and standing by a stone this morning on your coast beside a man who's just had a stroke in a strong-room, Momus says, with the fine's receipt in front of him; and you've put the father on a sheet and left the son off it, and the whole hill knows you did and nobody knows why, including, I think, you." Marcia stopped at the Augusta's door. "I'll tell you what the Augusta said about that. She said: *the girl's kept one door shut on purpose. Good. A woman with no doors is a woman nobody comes to. But she'd better know which it is, because one day I'll need to go through it, and I'd rather she opened it than I had it broken.*" The round face turned to her, finally. "That's the sum. You've four of five. The fifth's yours or it's nobody's and I'd not wait ten years to find out which, because the Augusta's fifty-six and Sura's coughing and the Spaniard's thirty-one and the emperor's fifty-three and in this house the numbers move. Now go in. She's got the cloth on. It's a receiving day."

* * *

Thallus came in March, and she saw him on the hill, in a room in the Augusta's outer court with a German at the door, as instructed by a dead woman, and he was old.

That was what she had not counted on. He had been fifty-five in a lodging-house common room in the eggs voice saying *she'll be useful*, and sixty-two in a room with a stool and a slate saying *your birth-year*, and he was sixty-four now and it had gone to his hands and the skin of his face, which had been a steward's face, hard and reasonable, and was a map now, with the reasonableness still in it like a road through country that had been fought over. He came in behind the German in a good cloak that was the young master's giving and stood in the middle of the room and looked at her, in her pin, on a stool, with her feet under her and Momus on a stool by the wall because Sabina had said *take the dwarf; a steward from the coast will not know what to do with a dwarf and I'd like to hear what he does*, and his face went still the way a steward's did when it had carried a figure for eight years and was about to set it down.

"Fausta."

"Thallus."

Not *domine*. He noticed. He had always noticed everything; it was what he was for. He looked at the German's back in the door and at the dwarf and at the room, which was the Augusta's and said so in every surface, and he sat down, because she had not asked him to and because he was sixty-four and the road up the hill was long.

"You'll know why I've come."

"The lease."

"The lease." He took a sheet from the cloak and put it on the table between them. "The harbour's being rebuilt. The Syrian's man's drawn it; the mole goes out another two hundred feet and the lodging-houses on the front come down for a customs-hall, and the young master, who's settling his father's affairs and finds that his father's affairs were a villa he doesn't own and a dock he's leased to a man who's just paid the emperor four hundred and eighty thousand for a sail, would like to sell the ground under the Dolphin to the port before the port takes it. He can't while your lease stands. Your mother was clever. Twenty years, in her daughter's name, witnessed, sealed. Eight left." He pushed the sheet an inch. "He'll give you five thousand for the eight. It's generous. It's ash."

"It's generous," she agreed. "It's ash."

"Then sign."

"In a moment." She did not look at the sheet. "You didn't come up the Palatine for five thousand, Thallus. You'd have sent a boy. You've come to put yourself in a room with me and tell me a thing and watch my face, because you've done it since I was ten and you can't stop, and I'd rather you did it now, with the German there, than carried it back down the hill unsaid and sent it to me in a letter I'd have to burn." She folded her hands. "Say it."

He looked at her for a long time with the map of a face.

"You've grown into the nose," he said. "I thought you would. Your mother had the mouth and the voice and you had his nose and his way of sitting, and I watched you in that common room at ten and thought, that one's going to be a problem for somebody, and I've spent twelve years trying to make sure it wasn't me." He turned the sheet with one finger. "Two things. I'll say them both and you can do what you like with them; I'm an old man with a key to a house that isn't mine, as Hermes says, and I'd like to put them down."

"Go on."

"Hermes's boy wrote to his stepmother in the spring of the year Bassus died, about the egg-woman's husband, and the stepmother paid, and told Hermes, and Hermes let it stand. You knew that; you asked for it. What you'll not know is what Hermes said, in his strong-room, in July of the year before, with the boy on a stool, about how you'd come up the hill. He said *on her back*. In so many words. And the boy got up off the stool and struck him. Open hand. Across the face. In his father's strong-room, with the Lares watching. I had it from the man I've kept in Hermes's kitchen since Nerva, who had it from the boy who was outside the door." The steward's hands lay still on the table. "Hermes took the sentence back. He's never taken a figure back in his life and he took that one, and ordered the boy to write you nothing for a year, and the boy didn't, except the egg-man, which was a thing you'd asked and not a letter. That's the first thing. I tell you because I think you've been doing a sum for two years with a figure missing and I'd like, once, to have given you a figure instead of taken one."

She sat. The price was in her and she looked at it and it had moved, and she did not let her face do anything, because the shutter was hers and had been since a lodging-house, and Momus, by the wall, did not move either.

"The second."

"The second's smaller. Hermes has had his stroke." Thallus said it flat, a steward reporting a roof. "The Nones. In the strong-room, at the table, with the fine's receipt in front of him and the *Isis*'s bill of sale, doing the sum of what was left. The left side. He can't speak. He can write, with the right hand, badly, and what he writes is figures. The boy's been sent for; Sura's let him go; he'll be in Puteoli by now, standing by a stone with an *F* on it beside a man who can't say his name." The old hands moved, finally, and folded. "I've no stake in it. The villa paper's in his strong-room and the young master owes it and if Hermes dies the paper comes to the boy and the boy's on Sura's staff and the Spaniard's, and the young master's a senator who finds he has to be seen, and I'm sixty-four. I've done my sum. It comes out: sell the girl the truth, cheap, before someone sells her a story dear, and go back to the coast and count what's left of a house that was a consul's and is a man in the Senate who sits on a stool in a freedman's strong-room because the freedman's son has a ring." He looked at her. "Sign the lease. Take the five thousand. The Dolphin's ash; your mother's in the ground by the road; the egg-woman's husband's paid till he dies and after that it doesn't matter, because the paper's in the prefect's archive under the Augusta's seal, which I also know, because I've a man in that office too, and have had since before you were born, and I'll tell you now that I've never once used him for anything but to know." He stood. He was slow about it. "I asked you your birth-year at a funeral because I needed a thing on a sheet and I've never picked it up and I never will, and I'd like you to have that from me and not from the fact that nothing's happened. Nothing's happened because I decided. I'm the only one on that coast who ever did, and I'd like somebody on this hill to know it before I'm dead."

She signed the lease. In the Latin a dead woman had taught her. *Fausta, daughter of Hilara.* She sealed it with the brass fish, and gave him the fish's print and kept the fish, and he took the sheet and the five thousand went to a banker's on the Sacred Way in her name, which was the first money she had ever had that was hers and not in a hem, and he went to the door, where the German was, and at the door he turned, as people did at doors on that hill, and looked at the dwarf by the wall.

"You're Tertulla's," he said. "The one who went to the postern."

"I'm hers now," said Momus. "The one who counts."

"Then count this, for me, since you're there and I'll not be. Twelve years. From a common room to the Augusta's outer court with a German at the door. On her feet. I'd have said it couldn't be done and I've been in the house of Laecanius forty years and seen three generations try it from above and fail." The map of a face did nothing. "Tell her, after I've gone, that her mother said *and?* to me once, when I'd finished laying out the terms of the Dolphin's rent in the year the girl was born, and I didn't understand it then, and I do now. It means: *that's your figure; where's mine.*" And he went out past the German and down the hill, and did not come up it again in her lifetime, and she heard of his death four years later from a letter and found that she could not remember what she had felt in the room, only that she had signed, and that the fish had taken the wax, and that Momus had said, when the door shut, "Well. There's a man who's just told you the truth because it cost him nothing, and you'll not get many of those, so I'd keep it."

"I'm keeping it."

"Which part?"

"The strong-room." She stood up. Her feet were under her. "He struck his father for me. Two years ago. With the Lares watching. And then did nothing for two years because he was told to, and he's good at it." She picked up the box of pins. "That's a figure I didn't have. I'll put it in the column and see what it does to the sum."

"And the stroke?"

"That's not mine," she said. "That's his. He'll be standing by the stone by now. He can count that himself." And she went back through the courts past the fountain that worked to the cold rooms on the north side where a woman of twenty-three was waiting by a window with no book to be told what a steward from the coast did with a dwarf, and told her, and Sabina laughed, once, short, and said, "He *talked* to it. Oh, that's good. Nobody on this hill talks to Momus; they think he's furniture. Your coast is better than my hill, Puteoli. Do my hair. The new way. He's coming in from the cut and I want him to not know me again."
