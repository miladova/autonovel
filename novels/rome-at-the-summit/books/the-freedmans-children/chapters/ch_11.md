# The House on the Caelian

Her mother was awake, and knew her, and that was the goddess's joke, if it was one: that the last day was a clear one.

She sat on the edge of the bed in the window room with the mole outside going out into the morning and the Alexandrians' things in the corner where they had left them, because she had not moved the Alexandrians, she had moved her mother, in March, into the best room, and had told Thallus it was so the woman could see the sea and had not told him that it was so the woman could be in the room where the steward had been, with the window, as its owner, at the end. Hilara was propped on the Alexandrians' cushions. The hard thing in her side had made the rest of her thin and the face was all cheekbone now, the Thracian ones or the Syrian ones or simply hers, and the eyes were very large in it and perfectly clear.

"The Alexandrian's come," she said.

"Phaedrus. He's at the harbour. He'll wait for the tide."

"He's a steward. They always wait. It's what they're for." Her mother's hand moved on the blanket and Fausta took it. It was hot and dry and weighed nothing. "You'll go."

"Yes."

"Good. I'd have been angry if you'd made me say it." The eyes went to the window. "The Greek woman's mother will do the rest. I've paid her. There's a thing in the box under the hearth-stone for the goddess — don't look like that, it's two asses and a bit of honey-cake, she's not expensive — and the lease and the seal and the copy of the letter I wrote the widow, and under them a thing I want you to take and not open till you're on the water." A breath. "It's not money. There's no money. The quarters went to Thallus and the rest went to the old woman and I'm not sorry. It's a paper."

"Mama —"

"It's not *that* paper. That paper's in a box in Rome behind a lock and it'll be read out when he's dead, or burned the night before, and either way it's no use to me now and it never was. It's another one." Her mother's mouth moved; it was the not-quite-smile that Fausta had, that she had seen in the bronze and had thought was hers. "I'll tell you now, because I've the breath this morning and I'll not have it tonight. It's a thing I did the year you were born. I went to the magistrate at the basilica, the one who registers births, and I had him write you down. *Fausta, daughter of Hilara, freedwoman of Lucius Laecanius Bassus, born free in the house by the harbour on the fourth day before the Ides of May.* With witnesses. The Rhodian's father was one; he's dead. The egg-woman's husband was the other; he's not. And the magistrate sealed it, and I paid him what I'd been given in the bag for the purpose, half of it, and I've kept the copy under the hearth-stone for fourteen years and never once taken it out, because the day you take a thing like that out is the day someone asks why you thought you'd need it." The hand tightened, very slightly. "You'll need it. Not this year. One day someone in a room will say *the Puteoli woman's girl — was she born before or after?*, and they'll mean before or after I was free, and if it was before, I was a slave when I had you, and you're a slave's child, and a slave's child born in slavery is a slave, whatever the mother was after, and there are men who've made a living finding that out about freedmen's children twenty years on. I was free. I was free on the ship, by his word, the day he put me on it, in front of his steward. I wasn't free by paper till the lease. The paper under the hearth-stone says you were born to a freedwoman. It doesn't say when the freedwoman got free. It says what the magistrate was paid to say. It's the only lie I've ever put in writing and I'd do it again and you're to carry it and never once show it to anyone until the day you have to, and on that day show it as if it were the truth, because it is, in every way that matters except one."

Fausta sat with her mother's hand in hers and the mole in the window and the lie in her, and said, after a while: "Does Thallus know?"

"Thallus knows there's a paper. He doesn't know what it says. He's never asked; he's never needed to; he's had the house and the stairs and the quarters and a woman who did what she was told, and a steward who has all that doesn't go looking for a paper that might make him have to do something about it." Hilara's eyes came back from the window. "But he'll go looking one day. Not for me. For you. When you're something, in Rome, and he's old, and wants a thing from you, and needs a hook. He'll remember there was a paper. That's when you'll need to have it where he can't put a hand on it." A breath. "Keep it in you. Not in a box. In you. Learn it by heart and then burn the copy if you must; a thing you've by heart they can't take out of a box."

"I'll not burn it."

"No. You're your mother's. You'll keep the paper and learn it too, both, and sleep with it against your skin like that Greek book you think I don't know about." The not-quite-smile. "Go on. Go down and do the slate once more so the Greek woman sees how it's kept, and then come up and sit with me till the tide, and don't talk; I've said it all and I'm tired and I'd like to look at the sea with my daughter in the room and nobody owing anybody anything. That's all I want. It's the first time I've wanted it and been able to have it. Let me."

She went down and did the slate. She came up and sat. They did not talk. The sun went across the window and the mole and the water, and the Alexandrians' things stood in the corner, and the stair creaked once, in the place it creaked, under nobody, the way old stairs do. Her mother's eyes closed and opened and closed and the breathing was the February breathing, and then the Greek woman was in the door saying *the Alexandrian's sent up from the harbour, the tide's making*, and Fausta stood up, and bent, and put her mouth against her mother's forehead, which she had never done, and said, "Yes. And?" very low, into the hair, and Hilara's eyes opened and found her and the not-quite-smile came, the last one, and the hand on the blanket moved, toward the door, *go*, and she went.

She did not look back from the door. She had decided. She went down the stair, and out through the common room past the slate on its nail, and into the yard, where the goddess was in her niche with the saucer up high and the Greek woman's flower, and she took the two asses and the honey-cake out of the box under the hearth-stone and put them in the saucer, and the lease and the seal and the copy of the letter and the other thing in her gown with the Strabo. She stood in front of the pink ghost of a face for the time it took to breathe twice, and said nothing at all, and went out by the yard door into the lane with the whole of it watching, and down to the harbour, where a steward was waiting at the foot of a gangway in a grey cloak with the patience of a man who had been paid to have it.

Antiope was not there. Antiope did not say goodbye; it was bad luck in the trade. But at the harbour steps, where the mole met the land and the customs-house had its brazier going, there was a woman in a shawl sitting on the bollard with her back to the gangway, looking at the sea, and as Fausta went past her the woman said, without turning round, in the price-of-things voice: "A rich man who's not cruel. Ask her for me, when you get there. She's more likely to listen to you; you've the nose for it." And then: "Go on, duck. Don't make me look."

She went up the gangway. She did not make her look.

* * *

Phaedrus lied to her once on the voyage, as he had said he would, and she caught it, and he was pleased.

The sea was not the sea she knew. She had known it from a mole and a stair and a window, a thing that came to the town and went away from it, and she had never once been on it, and the first morning, when the coaster cleared the mole with the whole of the Gulf opening to starboard and the hull began to do the thing a hull did, which was move in every direction a floor was not supposed to, she stood at the stern-rail with both hands on the wood and understood that the floor had gone, actually gone, and that there would not be one again for two days. She did not vomit. She had decided not to, on the mole, and it was a thing that could be decided, she found, if you kept your eyes on the one place that did not move, which was the line where the sky came down, and did not once look at the deck. The crew went by her bare-footed on the wet planks as if the planks were still. The smell was pitch and bilge and wet rope and the Gulf's own smell, which from a window had been clean and from the deck was not; and the sail above her made a noise when the wind came into it like a door slammed in a big house, every time, so that she flinched the first ten and had stopped by the twentieth. Off Misenum the swell came round the point from the open sea and the coaster put her shoulder down into it and the water came over the bow in a sheet and ran aft along the deck to her feet, cold, and went out through the ports, and the steersman beside her laughed at something, not at her, and leaned on the oar, and she looked at his forearms, which were doing a thing she could not have done, and thought: that is a skill, and I have none that would keep me alive out here for an hour.

The ship was the lady's own, a Tyrrhenian coaster with a cabin aft and a crew of eleven who had carried the lady's wine and the lady's furniture and the lady's girls up and down this coast for twenty years and were not curious about a fourteen-year-old from Puteoli in a gown that would not let down, and Phaedrus sat with her in the stern under an awning for the two days it took to raise Ostia, and talked. He talked the way Nicias had, as a man talks who has decided to teach and does not propose to be thanked for it, and he talked about the house.

"The lady is fifty-eight. Her husband was Tertullus, who was consul under Domitian and Nerva's friend before it was safe and after, and died in the year of the Guard, in his bed, which was the only clever thing he ever did without her telling him to. She has been a widow six years. She will not marry again; she has said so, to the emperor, who asked, because the emperor likes to see women of her kind settled, and she said, *Caesar, I have been settled, and I did not care for it*, and he laughed, and nobody else did." The Alexandrian looked at the coast going by. "She goes to the Palatine twice a month. Not to the emperor; to the empress. Plotina receives perhaps a dozen women of Rome in her own rooms, and the lady is one, and has been since the entry, when she went up on the second day with a basket of figs from her Laurentine garden and said, *you'll want someone who knew the old court and isn't sorry it's gone*, and was kept. That is the whole of the lady's power and it is a great deal. There are consuls who would give a province to be in that room and they are not, and she is, with figs."

"And the son?"

Phaedrus was quiet for a moment.

"Cornelius Priscus is twenty-four," he said. "He is the lady's one failure and she knows it and it is not spoken of. He was to have been quaestor last year and was not, for reasons that cost the lady money to have forgotten. He lives in the house. He has his own wing, over the stables, and his own people, and he dines with his mother on the Kalends and the Ides and is charming on those days, because he can be, he has her gift for it and none of her use for it. The rest of the time he is in the city, or in his wing, with whatever he has brought back from the city." The steward's voice did not change. "He does not come into the lady's side of the house. That is the arrangement. It has held for three years. The girls know it. You will know it. If you see him in the lady's peristyle, which you will not, you will have seen a thing that is not supposed to happen, and you will tell me, and not the lady, because the lady has decided not to know certain things about her son and it is my work to see that she need not."

Fausta looked at the coast. She thought about *like a man who's just realised he's allowed*. She said, "And the dwarf?"

"Momus. He's hers; he was her father's. He is sixty and the cleverest man in the house and he is treated as furniture by everyone but her, and it suits him, because furniture is not asked where it was." Phaedrus almost smiled. "Be kind to him. Not pitying. Kind. He will know the difference and so will she; it is one of the ways she tests."

"And the eleven."

"Twelve, now. She bought one in the winter, a Syrian, to annoy the others." He turned and looked at her, finally, with the Alexandrian's eyes that had been forty years in a house like that and had learned to see without appearing to. "I will tell you the truth about the girls, because nobody told me and I lost two years finding it out. They are not your rivals. They think they are. They will treat you as one, and you will let them, because a rival is a thing they know how to hate, and a thing they know how to hate is a thing they stop looking at. Your rival is not in the dormitory. Your rival is the lady's own idea of what you are for, which she has not yet formed, and which she will form from what you do in the first month. Do nothing in the first month. Pour. Read when told. Say *domina* and whatever's true. Let her form it. She forms slowly and she does not unform; once she has decided what you are, you will be it until one of you dies." He looked back at the sea. "That is the one piece of advice I have. The rest is furniture."

They were off the Circeian cape, with the mountain standing up out of the water on the left hand, when he lied.

"The lady," he said, "had your mother's letter the day the Rhodian put it in the hand. She read it, and said *the girl who watched Sura*, and put it in her box, and decided that night. The year and a half was the master. Bassus would not release you. He wrote three times that the girl was needed on the coast, that the lodging-house could not spare her, that the mother was ill and the daughter her only support. The lady let him say it. Then in March she wrote to him that she had heard the mother was dying and that she, the lady, would be glad to take the daughter into her house as a kindness to his freedwoman's memory, and that she would mention his generosity to the empress. He wrote back the same day."

Fausta turned her head and looked at the steward, who was looking at the cape.

"No," she said.

"No?"

"She didn't decide that night. She decided in the autumn, when Thallus wrote to her that the Dolphin was making what it made when the mother was well and the girl was doing it from a slate. Thallus told me the widow would write. In May. He said he'd seen you keep the Rhodian's seal and that Alexandrians don't keep what they mean to throw away. He didn't say it like a man guessing. He said it like a man who'd been asked." She watched his face. "And Bassus didn't write three times that I was needed on the coast. Bassus has never once thought about whether I was needed anywhere. Thallus wrote, in Bassus's name, because Thallus wanted the quarters from the slate for one more year before he lost the girl who kept it. And the lady let him have the year because a girl who could keep a dying woman's house from a slate for a year was a girl worth having, and one who couldn't wasn't, and the year was the test." She looked back at the cape. "That's the one. That's your lie. You made it the master's doing to see if I'd believe a steward would do that for a master instead of for himself."

Phaedrus did not say anything for a while. The ship went on. Then he said, in the Greek like water, "Thallus wrote in October. He said: *the girl has the house in her hand and does not go up the stairs, and I have not asked her to, and I will not, and you may tell the lady that if she wants her she had better be quick, because I am a steward and not a saint and the quarter at the Saturnalia is a long way off.*" He folded his hands. "The lady read it to me. She said: *a steward who tells me he is not a saint is a steward who has thought about it. Write to him that I'll take her in the spring and that he's to keep thinking.*" He looked at her. "You've done well. Most of them get it on the second day, if at all, and they get it because I let the lie be clumsy. I didn't, with you. I was curious."

"Why?"

"Because the lady said *the girl who watched Sura*," said Phaedrus, "and the lady has said that about nobody else in nine years, and I wanted to know what she'd seen." He stood up, under the awning, and looked forward, to where the coast was flattening toward the river-mouth and the haze that was the city's. "Ostia by the evening. The Caelian by tomorrow noon. Sleep if you can. You'll not, the first month."

* * *

She did not sleep the first month. She poured.

The house of Cornelia Tertulla stood on the Caelian two streets from Bassus's, in the same street of blind walls and single doors, and inside it went back and back the way they all did and was, in every particular that could be measured, the same: the atrium and the pool and the masks, the tablinum, the peristyle with its garden, the wings, the slaves' court, the kitchens, the hundred and sixty people. And it was not the same at all. She felt the difference the first hour and could not have said what it was until the third week, when she stood behind the lady's chair at the noon receiving with the jug and saw a senator — a consular, a man with a province behind him — come into the atrium and stop at the edge of the pool and wait, with his hands folded in front of him like a steward's, until the lady looked up from the letter she was reading and let him be seen; and she understood that the house of Laecanius was a house that faced the Palatine, and this one was a house that the Palatine, on two days a month, faced back.

The lady did not speak to her for nine days. On the tenth, at the noon receiving, with the atrium full and a Greek in it — a sophist from Smyrna with a voice like a lyre and a letter from the governor of Asia, who was holding forth to the lady's left on the subject of Homer's geography and getting the bay wrong — the lady reached back without turning for the cup, and Fausta filled it. The Greek said, in a Greek so beautiful it was almost unkind, *and of course Strabo tells us the mole at Dicaearchia is founded on sand, which is why the harbour has never been what the Alexandrians claim*, and the word *sand* went into her and found the place where the Strabo was and the thing she had read three times under the stairs the night a slave gave it her, and she said, in Greek, before she had decided to, quietly, to the jug: "Ash. It's founded on the ash the mountain threw. That's why it stands."

The atrium did not stop. A jug-girl had spoken; that was a thing that happened in houses, and was not heard, by anyone whose business it was not to hear. But the sophist heard, because it was his language and his error. He turned on the couch and looked at the girl behind the lady's chair with his lyre-voice stopped in his throat and an expression on his face that Fausta had seen once before, on a Spaniard, looking down a dinner-table to see who had given a figure; and the lady did not turn, and did not stop reading her letter, and said in the dry low voice that went exactly as far as she sent it: "She's right, Polemon. I've stood on it. Go on about Homer; you're better on things nobody can check."

And the sophist went on about Homer, and the receiving went on, and nothing had happened; and at noon, in the small room off the peristyle, with the door shut and Momus the dwarf on his stool in the corner being furniture, the lady Cornelia sat in her chair with her four-years-old hair and looked at the girl standing in front of her for a long time and said:

"Phaedrus told you not to let me find out you could read."

"Yes, domina."

"And not to speak. And not to do anything in the first month. He says that to all of them; it's his one virtue, he's consistent." The lady's hand moved on the arm of the chair. "You've been nine days in my house and you have poured and said nothing and looked at nobody, exactly as told, and I had begun to think the girl who watched Sura had been left on a quay in Puteoli. And then a Greek got a mole wrong and you corrected him in his own tongue in front of thirty people without raising your voice." The shoulders moved, the width of a stylus. "Well. Now I know what you are. You're a girl who can be silent for nine days and can't bear a false figure on the tenth. That's useful and it's dangerous and I shall have to decide which it's more of, and I'll take my time, because the last one I decided that about is over the stables with his own people." A pause. "Where did you read Strabo?"

"A slave gave it me, domina. A tutor. He'd stolen it from his master's library in the year Titus died."

"Bassus's Greek. Nicias. Yes; he had a good hand. He wrote to me once, you know, years ago, about a child at the back of his room. I didn't answer. I don't answer slaves; it unsettles them." The lady looked at her a moment longer. "You'll keep pouring. You'll keep silent. But on the days I go up to the Palatine — the Kalends and the Ides — you'll come, in the litter, behind, with the other two, and you'll stand in the empress's outer room with the jug and look at nobody, and when we come back you'll tell me who looked at whom, as you did in my cousin's house, except that this time if you're wrong it won't be about a senator's wife's brother; it'll be about the woman who made this emperor. She notices who notices, and she's kinder than Sura and much, much less forgiving." She reached for the cup that was not there and Fausta put it in her hand. "Go and tell Phaedrus he was right about you and I'm displeased with him for it. And Fausta." It was the first time the lady had said her name. "The house of Laecanius had a letter this morning, from the wife of my cousin's freedman — the one from Nola, with the lip. It was for you. Phaedrus has it. I've read it. Everyone will have read it by the time it reaches you; that's a house. I'll tell you what's in it so you can have your face arranged before you open it, which is a kindness I was not shown, and it's this: your mother died on the fourth day after you sailed, in the window room, with the Rhodian's old midwife by her and the sea in the glass, and the midwife says she asked for nothing and said one thing at the end, which the woman from Nola has written down because she thought you'd want it, and it was *and?*" The lady looked at the garden on the wall. "I don't know what it means. The Nola woman didn't either. I expect you do."

Fausta stood in the small room with her face doing nothing. She felt it do nothing; she felt it from the inside, the shutter gone to, the thing her mother had wanted and had practised her in on a wall at dusk with nobody listening; and she thought that it was the second time in her life she had had a death told her as a sentence by a woman who had not turned round. That the first had been a Syrian in a doorway and *Nerva is dead*, and that she had got the shutters then, because that was the order of things, and she would get them now.

"Yes, domina," she said. "I know what it means."

"Then you'd better go and read it in private and come back for the second hour with the jug, because Zaidê is going to spill something on the Smyrna Greek before he leaves, I can see her planning it, and I want to watch her do it with someone behind me who'll not laugh." The lady did not look round. "Momus. Go with her. Sit in the door. Don't speak. She's not to be alone in a corridor in this house with a letter in her hand and that nose; there are people in it who'll want to see what her face does, and I'd rather it did it for you than them."

The dwarf got off his stool. He was a small square man with grey hair and a face like a walnut, and he came to the door and held it for her, which no one had ever done, and as she went past him he said, very low, in a voice that had been sixty years learning to go no further than it was meant to: "I was in the room the day your mother was bought. In the old master's house, in the year Vespasian died. I was holding the lamp. She was four and she didn't cry. I thought: that one will do." He shut the door behind them. "I was right. Come on. I know a corridor nobody uses."

* * *

She read the letter in the corridor nobody used, with Momus on the floor at the end of it with his back to the wall and his eyes on the other door, and it was what the lady had said, in Vibia's even hand. It had at the bottom, after *and?*, a line the lady had not mentioned, which was: *The steward came the day after and took the lease and the slate and put the Greek woman in, and stood in the yard a while before he went, in front of the shrine, and I am told he put something in the saucer, and I am told it was a coin, and I do not know what it adds to. You will.*

She folded the letter along Vibia's folds. She put it in her gown with the Strabo and the two letters and the lease and the seal and the other thing, the paper under the hearth-stone, which she had opened on the water, as told, off the Circeian cape, while Phaedrus looked at the coast, and had read once, and had by heart, and had not burned. She sat in the corridor nobody used with all of it against her skin and her face doing nothing and her hands perfectly steady in her lap, and the dwarf at the end of the corridor did not look at her, and did not speak, and after a while she said to the wall opposite, in the voice her mother used for the Rhodian's eggs:

"He put a coin in the saucer."

"Who?"

"Thallus. The steward. At the shrine in the yard. After he'd taken the lease." She looked at the wall. "I told you once — I told someone once — that he wasn't paying for the news. He was paying so you'd remember he'd paid. That's a denarius' worth of owing." Her hands did not move. "She's dead and he's put her goddess in his debt. That's the man. That's the whole of the man."

Momus said nothing for a time. Then he said, from the floor, in the voice that went no further than it was meant to: "Or he's an old slave who held a key for thirty years and stood in a yard and didn't know what else to do with his hands. I've been that. It looks the same from a lane." He did not turn his head. "You'll find that out, in this house. That it looks the same. That's the hard part, girl. Not the ones who are cruel. The ones you can't tell."

She sat with that. The corridor was quiet. Somewhere beyond the other door the house went on being a house, a hundred and sixty people and a dwarf and a lady who faced the Palatine back, and over the stables a man with his own people who did not come into the peristyle, and she had been in it ten days, and had corrected a Greek, and had been told what she was, and had been given a corridor; and she thought about a girl of four who did not cry, and a lamp, and *that one will do*. She found that there was one thing in her that was not steady, and it was not her hands, and she let it be not steady for the time it took to breathe twice, in a corridor nobody used, with furniture at the door; and then she got the shutters.

"Second hour," she said. "The jug. The Syrian's going to spill something."

"She is. She's been planning it since the figs." Momus got up off the floor, which took him a moment. "Walk behind me. Nobody looks at what's behind a dwarf. It's the one use I've found for it, and it's taken sixty years, and I'll give it you for nothing, because the lamp was heavy that day and your mother didn't cry, and I've never forgotten either."

* * *

Zaidê spilled it. A half-cup of the Chian down the Smyrna Greek's left sleeve as he rose to go, with a face of such flat Syrian stupidity that he could not even be angry at her, only at the house; and the lady watched it over her letter and did not move her mouth, and Fausta, behind the chair, did not laugh, and that night in the dormitory over the bakehouse Zaidê came and stood over her pallet in the dark.

She was a year older than Fausta and a head shorter, with a wide flat face and black hair she wore in a plait as thick as a rope and hands that were always wet, from the jugs; she had been bought at six in Antioch and had been in the house nine years and had poured at the lady's left for the last two, and before the girl from Puteoli came she had been the one who went up the hill with the jug on the Kalends and the Ides. She did not sit. She stood with the plait over her shoulder and said, in a Latin that had come to her late and had never settled, "You spoke."

Fausta said nothing. Phaedrus had said not to, and she had done it once that day already.

"Nine days you were quiet. I thought, good, a quiet one, a nose and nothing behind it. And then you spoke, in Greek, in the atrium, to a *guest*, and she heard you, and now you'll go up the hill with the jug and I'll stay and pour for the women." The wet hands did not move. "I'm not clever. I know that. I've been in this house nine years and I've learned the one thing, which is where to stand, and you came off a boat and learned it in nine days. So." She bent, and Fausta felt the breath, which smelled of the kitchen's garlic. "I'll not touch you. I'm not a fool; they'd sell me. But everything you pour, I've carried, and everything you carry, I've washed, and you'll want to think about that before you drink from the house's cup. That's all I came to say."

She went back to her own pallet, at the far end, by the door, which was the best place because it was the coolest, and which she had had for two years because she had been the one who went up the hill.

In the morning, Fausta poured from a jug Zaidê had filled, and drank from the house's cup, in front of her, at the common table, without looking at it first, and set it down; and Zaidê watched her do it with the flat face and then went on eating, and nothing was said. But for a month after that the jug Fausta carried was always filled to the brim so it slopped on the stairs, and the cloth she was given to wipe the lady's cup had always been used first on something, and the pallet she slept on was moved, twice, in the night, by nobody, from the wall to the draught by the hatch; and she did not go to Phaedrus, because Phaedrus had told her what a girl did who went to a steward in the first month, and she did not go to Momus, because the dwarf was the lady's and this was not. She carried the brimming jug without spilling it, which took a month of her wrists to learn, and found that by the end of the month she could carry a full cup across the atrium on a tray through thirty people without a ring of wet on the tray, which no one else in the house could do, and which the lady noticed, and said nothing about, and sent her up the hill with it.

She saw the son on the Kalends of June, at his mother's table, and he saw her see him, and that was the mistake.

He came in from the stable wing at the lamp-lighting in a dinner-tunic the colour of good wine with his hair oiled and his mother's gift for a room all over him like scent, and he kissed the lady's hand and said something that made her shoulders move the width of a stylus, which Fausta had not seen anyone but the Smyrna Greek's error do, and he took the couch at her right and was, for two hours, the most charming man Fausta had ever watched. It was a thing to see. He had Rufus's trick of being gracious to people as though he had decided to, and none of Rufus's tiredness in it; he remembered the names of the lady's girls, all twelve, and the Syrian's, who had been in the house five months, and asked Momus a question about the old master's library as though the dwarf were a man who might know, and listened to the answer. He talked to his mother about the war — Sura's staff had written; the king's granaries were being found one by one by someone who knew the roads; the emperor would have the mountains by autumn — and about the Smyrna Greek, whom he had met at the baths and thought a fraud, and about a horse. He drank very little. He looked at no one longer than was civil.

She stood behind the lady's chair with the jug and watched his hands, as she had watched Rufus's, and they were beautiful hands, long, with a ring on the left that had been his father's, and they did nothing, all evening, that a hand should not; and she thought, in the second hour, that Phaedrus had been wrong, or that the lady's one failure was a failure of some kind she had not been shown, and she began — she would remember this — to let the watching go slack, the way you let a rope go when the ship has stopped pulling.

And then the lady said, to the table, "The Puteoli girl corrected Polemon on Strabo. In front of the Asian's letter. I've not enjoyed anything so much since your father's funeral," and the son laughed, and turned his head, for the first time, to look at the girl behind his mother's chair who had done it; and she saw it.

It was exactly as Antiope had said. It was like watching a lamp lit. The face did not change — the charm was still on it, the smile, the mother's gift — but behind the face, in the eyes, which were brown and had been pleasant all evening and were pleasant now, something came on, the way the wick caught, and it was not desire, or not only; she had seen desire on the stairs every morning of her life and knew its sum. It was the other thing. It was a man looking at a thing in his mother's house and realising, pleasantly, with interest, that it was *in his mother's house*, and that his mother's house was the one place in the world where everything, in the end, was his, and that there was no one in the room who could say otherwise, and that the thing had a nose.

She saw it a breath before he did. That was the only protection there was, Antiope had said; seeing it a breath before he does. And she had it, the breath, she had the shutter half to —

— and he saw her see it. She watched him see. She watched the pleasant brown eyes register that the jug-girl behind his mother's chair had read him, in a breath, as nobody in that house had read him in three years, because nobody in that house looked at him, by arrangement; and she watched the lamp, which had been lit with interest, go up, with something else, that she had no name for yet and would learn the name of in a corridor four years on, and that was, she thought afterwards, when it was too late to think anything else, simply this: he had never once been *seen* being what he was, and he had liked it.

He turned back to his mother. He said something about the horse. The dinner went on, and ended, and he kissed the lady's hand and went back to the stable wing with his own people, and did not look at the girl again, not once, not even the civil length; and the lady, in the small room afterwards, with Momus on his stool, said, "Well? Who looked at whom?" and Fausta said who had looked at whom, all of it, the Syrian at the son and the son at nobody and the lady at her own hands, and was right, and did not say the other thing, because the lady had decided not to know certain things about her son and it was Phaedrus's work to see that she need not; and she went and found Phaedrus, in the slaves' court, in the dark, and said it to him instead, all of it, the lamp and the breath and the seeing, in the voice her mother used for eggs.

Phaedrus listened. When it was done he was quiet for a long time, and then he said, in the Greek like water, very low: "You've been in this house a month and you've done the one thing I told you not to do, which is to make him look at you, and you did it by being good at the thing the lady keeps you for, which is the one way I couldn't have warned you against." He looked at the dark. "Three years the arrangement's held. He doesn't come into her side. He's never had a reason to. Now there's a girl on her side who's seen him, and he knows it, and he'll want to know what else she sees." A breath. "I'll not tell the lady. She'd send you back to the coast to keep the arrangement, and there's nothing on the coast now, and she'd lose the girl who watched Sura, and she'd not forgive either of us. So. You'll be on the Palatine side of every room he's in. You'll sleep in the dormitory with the twelve and never in a corridor. You'll be behind Momus. And on the Kalends and the Ides you'll pour with your eyes on the jug and let him think the breath was luck." He turned to go, and stopped. "It wasn't luck, was it."

"No. A woman told me what to look for. On a stair. She'd had it from a man at the villa who was sorry after."

"Then she did you the one kindness there is," Phaedrus said, "and I'll do you the other, which is this. A man like that is slow. He'll want it to be his idea and he'll take years having it. You've years. Use them to become something the lady can't do without, and then when it comes — and it'll come; it always comes, with a house like this; the arrangement's a thing the lady made so she needn't watch, not a thing that stops anything — when it comes, you'll be a thing she has to choose between, and not a jug." He went into the dark. "Pour. Say nothing. You've till he's decided. It's more than most get."
