# The Outer Room

The empress's outer room was forty feet long and had one window and eleven women in it, and Fausta stood against the wall by the door with the jug for three hours on the Kalends of May and learned that none of the eleven was owned by the person she appeared to be owned by.

That was Marcia's sentence. She did not have it yet; she would have it in October, from a woman with a comb. But she had the thing itself by the end of the first hour, the way she had had the warehouse boards before Celer told her what they were for: she stood against the wall, and looked at the jug, and saw, at the edges of looking, the room arrange itself.

The lady Cornelia sat on a bench against the long wall with four other women of her kind, consulars' widows and wives, each with two girls behind her, and the five of them talked to each other in the dry carrying voices of women who had been in rooms like this under four emperors, and did not look at the door at the far end, which was the empress's, and had not opened. Across from them, on the other bench, sat three younger women who had not been in rooms like this under anyone, who had come up with the Spaniards — the wife of a legate, the sister of a man on Sura's staff, a Narbonese cousin of the empress's with a face like a pleasant horse — and they were quieter, and looked at the door, and their girls behind them looked at the lady's girls, and the lady's girls looked at the ceiling. In the corner by the window, alone, on a stool, with a book, sat a woman of perhaps thirty-five with a plain face and plain hair and a plain gown of very good wool, who was not talking to anyone and whom nobody was talking to, and who turned a page every so often with a hand that had a ring on it; and Fausta had been in the room a quarter of an hour before she understood that every woman on both benches knew exactly where that woman was at every moment, the way the men on the stairs at the Dolphin had always known exactly where her mother was. That this was the empress's niece, Matidia, the emperor's sister's daughter, who had a daughter of her own married to the Spaniard with the accent and who held, by the arithmetic of the room, the whole of the future in a book.

And between the benches, moving, with a comb, was the freedwoman.

She was the only person in the room who moved. She went from woman to woman with a small ivory comb in her hand and a box at her belt and did their hair — not did it; touched it, a strand here, a pin there, the work of a breath, the kind of attention a woman gave another woman's hair when the hair was a reason to stand close and say a sentence. She was perhaps forty. She had been pretty and had decided, Fausta thought, at some point, to stop, in the way the lady had decided to keep her hair four years out of fashion; she had a round brown unremarkable face and quick hands and a way of being at a woman's shoulder before the woman had thought to want her. She was called Marcia, and she was the empress's, and had been since Nemausus, and when she came to the lady Cornelia's bench and put two fingers to a pin at the back of the lady's head, the lady said, without turning, in the voice that went exactly as far as it was sent, "Marcia. Is she well?" and the freedwoman said, "She slept, domina. She asked after your Laurentine figs," and the lady said, "She shall have them," and the fingers went away; and that, Fausta understood, had been the whole of the morning's business, and the three hours were what it cost to be in the room when it was done.

The door at the end did not open. The empress did not come out. At the third hour the freedwoman went through the door and came back and said to the room, "The Augusta thanks you and is engaged," and the room stood, all of it, and bowed to the door, and went; and in the litter going down the hill the lady said, without preamble, "Well?"

"The Narbonese one is frightened of the legate's wife, domina. She sat where she couldn't be seen by her and looked at her the whole time. The legate's wife knows and likes it. The one from Sura's staff's family doesn't look at anyone; she's counting the room for her brother. Matidia turned nine pages in three hours and read none of them; she turned one every time the legate's wife laughed. And Marcia —"

"Yes."

"Marcia did everyone's hair except Matidia's and the Narbonese cousin's. The cousin because she's family and doesn't need it. Matidia because —" Fausta stopped.

"Go on."

"Because Matidia's hair's already done, domina. By someone else. It's done the way the empress's is in the painting in your atrium, the old way, plain. Somebody in that house does Matidia's hair the way the empress has hers, and it's not Marcia, and Marcia doesn't touch it, and I think the room knows that and I don't know what it means."

The lady was quiet for a street. Then she said, "It means that the empress has no children and her husband's niece has a daughter married to a Spaniard, and that in that house there are two women who are going to decide who sits in the chair after this man dies, and one of them does her own hair." The litter went on. "You saw that in three hours against a wall. It took me a year. I had to be told by Momus, who had it from a man who carried the empress's bath-water." The shoulders moved. "You'll go on the Ides. And every Kalends and Ides after. And you'll stand where you stood and you'll bring me the room, as you've brought it today, and in return I'll tell you a thing now that you'll want to know, and it's this: Marcia looked at you. Twice. Once when you came in and once when I asked after the figs. She doesn't look at jug-girls. She's been forty years not looking at them; it's how she got from a hairdresser's stool in Nemausus to the only woman in Rome who goes through that door without knocking." A pause. "I don't know what she saw. I expect it was the nose. Everyone on that hill knew my cousin when he was handsome."

* * *

"Sit," the lady said, in June, in the small room, which she had never said; and Fausta sat, on the stool by the window, and understood that the price was coming, and that it was not what she had expected, and that it was worse.

She had expected to be asked for the room. She had been asked for the room, and had given it, Kalends and Ides, the long wall and the short wall and the stool by the window and the comb, and the lady had taken it and said nothing, which was praise. What she had not expected was to be called into the small room off the peristyle on an ordinary afternoon with Momus on his stool and Phaedrus by the door, and to be handed a letter, folded, sealed with a seal she knew because she had grown up a street below it, and to be told: "Read that. Not aloud. Then tell me what it's for."

It was Hermes's seal. The letter was to the lady, in Hermes's clerk's Latin, and it was a letter of thanks, elaborately, for the lady's kindness to the daughter of his patron's freedwoman, and an assurance that the house of Laecanius Hermes would be glad to be of any service to the lady's house in the matter of oil, or wine, or carriage, at the lady's convenience; and folded inside it, on a separate sheet, in a different hand, a square hand, was a list, which was a copy of a manifest, the *Isis*'s, for the spring carry, with the figures to the modius.

She read it twice. She looked up.

"It's for you to know he can read me, domina," she said. "The manifest's in Celer's hand. From before the war; it's the old carry, the Nerva rate. Hermes has sent you a thing his son wrote so that you'll know that his son is on Sura's staff and that his son's hand is a thing you can recognise. He's telling you the house has a man in Sura's tent. He's offering to be of service and showing you the kind of service he means." She put the letter down. "And he's sent it through me. Not to you. He's written *the daughter of my patron's freedwoman* so that when you want a thing from Sura's office you'll think of the girl behind your chair and the boy behind Sura's as a pair, and come to the pair through the one you own."

The lady looked at her for a long moment.

"Yes," she said. "That's what it's for. He's a clever man, your Hermes. He's never once been in a room with me and he's read me down a dinner-table across sixty couches and found the one thing I'd want from a Puteoli shipowner, which isn't oil." She reached for the cup and Fausta put it in her hand. "So. Here's the price, and I'll say it plainly, because you're the one girl in this house who can bear plainness and I'm tired of the other eleven. I'll want things from Sura's office. Not now; this year, next. Small things. Whether a letter's been read. Whether a name's on a list. The kind of thing a boy on a stool by a lamp sees and doesn't write down. And when I want them, you'll write to him — through the Nola woman, who's shown she can carry a packet past a husband — and ask, as a girl asks a boy she grew up with in a street, and he'll answer or he won't, and either way I'll know something." She drank. "That's what you're for, in the end. Not the room. The room I could get from Momus's bath-water man. You're for the hand on the other side of the city that's the same hand as yours."

Fausta stood very still.

"And if I won't, domina?"

"Then you'll pour, and go up the hill on the Kalends, and stand against the wall, and be a girl who watched Sura, and in five years you'll be one of the twelve and hate the Syrian, and that's not nothing; most of them would cut you for it." The lady's face did not change. "But you'll not be anything else. Because the only thing you've got that the twelve haven't is a street in Puteoli, and I've just told you what it's worth, and a girl who won't spend the one thing she's got is a girl who'll be spent instead. Your mother knew that. She spent seven years of it and got a lease. You've a hand on Sura's staff. Spend it better."

The room was quiet. Momus, on his stool, was furniture. Phaedrus, by the door, was looking at the painted garden.

She thought of the mole. She thought of *I'll not spend it. Not here. Not for nothing.* She thought of a boy at the foot of the third couch who had looked at her because a woman had told him to, in front of the man who had made the emperor, and whose face had done nothing, perfectly, and under the nothing she had seen — she had seen it, she had been looking at that face since a yard and a cake — the thing that was not a sum, the figure he could not find, and she had said at the door *you didn't need to send it*, and had meant *I know what you did in January and I know you'll never tell me and I've worked out the true part and it's that you've started to be the boat*, and he had said *yes*. And now a widow was asking her to make him a thing she wrote to for lists.

"I'll write," she said. "When you want a thing. I'll ask. He'll answer or he won't." She heard her own voice, level. "But I'll tell him it's for you, domina. In the letter. Not as a girl who grew up in a street. As your girl, asking for your house. He'll know anyway. He's never once in his life not known what a letter was for. And if I pretend it's the street, he'll know that too, and he'll answer the pretending, and you'll get a tally-board. If I tell him it's you, he'll answer *you*, and you'll get a thing that's true, and he'll decide what it costs him, and that's his to decide, not mine." She took a breath. "That's the price I'll pay. The street's not for sale. The hand is."

The lady Cornelia looked at her across the small room with the four-years-old hair and the dry face, and something in the face moved that Fausta had not seen move in a year, and could not name, and would, much later, in another room, decide had been the nearest the woman ever came to respect.

"Well," the lady said. "Well. The widow's girl has terms." She set the cup down. "Yes. All right. Tell him it's me. God knows he'll have a better opinion of us both for it." And then, as Fausta turned to go: "You've just done the thing your mother never learned. You've charged me for the water and looked at me while you did it. Don't do it again this year. Once is character. Twice is a habit, and I've a son with habits."

* * *

"You. The jug. Come here and hold this." It was October, the Ides, the outer room, and the woman with the comb had not looked up, and was holding out a hairpin between two fingers toward the wall where Fausta stood.

She had not expected it. She had stood against the wall by the door for ten months of Kalends and Ides and had been looked at twice and had brought the lady the room and had not once been spoken to by anyone in it, which was correct; a jug-girl was not spoken to. But on the Ides of October the door at the end opened and did not close again — that was the first thing; the room went still — and the freedwoman came out of it with the comb and did not go to the benches. She came down the long wall to the door, where the jug-girls stood, three of them, the lady's two and the Narbonese cousin's one, and she stopped in front of the lady's second girl, who was Fausta, and lifted two fingers to a pin at the side of her head that was not loose, and said, in a low Nemausus Latin with the south in it, to the pin:

"The Augusta has a headache. She asks the lady Cornelia to go in, alone, for the length of a cup of water, and she asks that the girl who brings the water be the one who corrected the Smyrna Greek. She has heard about the mole. She was raised on a river and has opinions about what things stand on." The fingers moved the pin that was not loose. "You'll go in behind the lady. You'll pour one cup. You'll stand where I put you and look at the floor, and the Augusta will not speak to you and you will not exist, and afterwards the lady will ask you what you saw and you will tell her, as you do, everything, because the Augusta wishes the lady to know that she knows what the lady's girl is for and does not mind." The pin was done. "And then, next Kalends, you will stand where you stand, and I will come to you with the comb, and you will tell *me* what the lady said when you told her. Not everything. One thing. The thing that's true of more than forty." The round brown face did not change. "That's the arrangement. I don't ask whether you accept it. Nobody in this house is owned by the person they appear to be owned by, girl, and the sooner you stop appearing, the sooner you can start being."

She went in behind the lady. She poured one cup. She stood where she was put, which was by the wall, three paces from a couch on which lay a woman of fifty in a plain gown with a cloth over her eyes and her hair done the old way, plain, by her own hand. She looked at the floor, and did not exist, and heard the lady say *Augusta* and the woman on the couch say *Tertulla, sit, don't fuss, I've had this head since Nemausus and it's never once killed me*, and heard, for the length of a cup of water, two women talk about figs and the Senate and a Spaniard's accent and which of three men would be made consul and why, in the voices of women who had been in rooms like this together for four years and had stopped, somewhere in the second, pretending to each other; and she did not look up. She had been on a roof. She had seen the straight back and the covered head and the eyes on the road and had thought *that one is the one to watch*, and now she was three paces from her with a jug. She did not look up, because she had been told not to and because she had learned, in a corridor nobody used, that the ones you could see were the ones who could see you, and that this woman could see through a cloth.

But at the end, when the cup was empty and the lady was rising, the woman on the couch said, to the ceiling, not moving the cloth: "The girl."

"Augusta."

"Ash, or sand?"

"Ash, Augusta. The mountain's. It's why it stands."

"Yes. The Rhône's the same. Everyone thinks it's mud and it's stone underneath, from the old fires. Nemausus is built on it." The cloth did not move. "Tertulla, she's got Bassus's nose. Everyone will have told you. I'm telling you I don't care and neither should she. Half this hill has somebody's nose. It's the other thing that matters." A pause. "What's your name, girl?"

"Fausta, Augusta."

"*Lucky.*" The woman under the cloth made a sound that might, in a different woman, have been a laugh. "Your mother had a sense of humour. Or a hope. Go on. Marcia will say when I want water again."

* * *

She told the lady everything, in the litter, as she did. She told Marcia one thing, on the next Kalends, with the comb at her ear, and it was that the lady had said *she'll want the figs on the Ides and she'll want to be told the Spaniard's been made praetor before Sura tells her, and I can do the first*; and Marcia's fingers moved the pin and the round face did nothing and the freedwoman said, "Good. That's true of more than forty," and went back up the long wall; and on the Ides the lady had the figs and the empress had the news before Sura, from somewhere, and nobody said anything, and it was done, and she was in it.

She was in it. She lay in the dormitory over the bakehouse with the twelve breathing round her and the Strabo and the letters and the paper under the hearth-stone against her skin and she thought about it, in the dark, with the house too big to hear. She was the lady's, all the way through; she had said so, at the door, to a boy, and had meant it. And she was the freedwoman's, one thing a Kalends, and the freedwoman was the empress's, and the empress was the city's, and so on down, Nicias had said, and up. The only question that mattered about any of them was *by whom*; and she had been in Rome eighteen months and had three owners and a nose, and a widow's son over the stables who was slow and would want it to be his idea, and a hand on the other side of the city that was the same hand as hers and that she had agreed to write to for lists.

In August the house slept on the roofs.

Everyone did; the dormitory over the bakehouse was an oven and the lady's own rooms were not much better, and the twelve went up the ladder at the end of the slaves' court at dusk with their pallets and lay on the tiles under a sky with no colour in it, and the whole of the Caelian did the same, so that at night the hill was a thing of breathing, roof after roof, and a man talking in his sleep three houses away could be heard as if he were in the room. Fausta had the place by the parapet because she had come last to the roof the first night and it was the place nobody wanted, since the parapet was waist-high and a girl who rolled in her sleep was in the street. Zaidê had the place beside it because it was the second-worst, and because she had come second-last, having stayed below to wash the lady's cups, which was still her work, and would be.

They did not speak for a week. They lay a hand's breadth apart in the heat with the plait and the nose turned away from each other and the house breathing round them.

On the eighth night Zaidê said, to the sky, "There's figs."

Fausta said nothing.

"In the Greek's garden. The sophist's. Three houses down. They hang over his wall into the alley and his porter sleeps, and I've a boy who gets them." She did not turn her head. "You can have two. Not for anything. I've too many and they'll go."

Fausta put out her hand, in the dark, and a fig was put in it, warm from being under someone's shift, split a little on one side the way they split when they were ready, and she ate it, lying on her back on a roof on the Caelian. It was the first thing she had eaten in sixteen months that had not been given her by the house, and it tasted of the alley and of someone else's garden and of the sea-wall at Puteoli where the figs had been Antiope's.

"Who's the boy?"

"The one who stokes the baths. Syrian. From Emesa; it's not Antioch but it's near enough that we can insult each other properly." Zaidê's voice in the dark was not the voice she used in the court. It was lower and had a thing in it that was almost a laugh. "He's got arms like a mule's legs and nothing in his head and he thinks I'm beautiful. I'm not. I've a face like a dish. But he thinks it, and he's not clever enough to be lying, so." The plait moved on the tiles. "You've nobody."

"No."

"There's a boy you write to."

Fausta lay still. There was no sense in asking how; the house knew what it knew.

"Phaedrus's boy carries the sheets down the hill. He can't read. He can see a girl's face when she gives him a sheet, though; we all can." A fig was eaten, in the dark, with the sound figs made. "I don't care. I'm telling you I know so you'll know I've not told. The lady knows; she knows everything; but the others don't, the twelve, and they'd not have it from me." A pause. "Not for you. Because I'd want the same."

The house breathed. Three roofs away a child cried and was hushed. Down in the street a watchman went by with his lantern, and the light came up the wall and over the parapet and lay a moment on the two of them, the wide flat face and the nose, side by side on the tiles, and went on.

"I'll tell you one thing more and then I'll sleep," Zaidê said. "I don't go on the garden side after the fourth hour when the lady's at the baths. Not the peristyle; not the corridor by it. I go round by the kitchens. It's longer." She turned over, with her back to the nose and the parapet. "That's not for you either. It's what I do. You can do what you like."

Fausta lay a long time looking up at the sky with no colour in it and the fig's taste in her mouth, and then she turned over too, with her back to the plait, so that they lay back to back a hand's breadth apart on a roof like two people who had agreed on something, and had not.

She had not written yet. The lady had not asked. But she had the Rhodian's papyrus, the last sheet of it, under the pallet, and she had begun, in October, after the cloth and the ash, a thing that was not a letter and that she did not intend to send and that began, in the square letters Nicias had taught her in a room over a warehouse: *What I saw. The outer room, which is forty feet long and has one window and eleven women in it and none of them owned by the one she appears to be. A woman under a cloth who asked me ash or sand and did not care about the nose. A comb.* And then, under that, after a space, because it was true and she had decided on a mole that she would not pretend about what was true, only about what it cost: *I have agreed to ask you for things. For the widow. I will tell you when it is her. When it is not her, I will not write at all, because I have nothing to say to you that is not a list, and you have nothing to say to me that is not a count, and we were both right on the mole and both wrong, and it is both. Feed the gods. Vibia says they like to see a face.*

She did not send it. She put it with the others. And in November, when the lady wanted her first thing from Sura's office — whether a letter from Bassus to the emperor about a priesthood had been read or set aside — she wrote a different sheet, four lines, without ornament, beginning *The lady Cornelia Tertulla asks*, and gave it to Phaedrus for the Nola woman, and it went; and nine days later a sheet came back by the same road, in the square hand, that said: *Set aside. Not read. Sura's note in the margin says: "Bassus wants a priesthood for his debts. Tell him the gods aren't lending." He has not been told. The lady may tell him or not. — C.* And under that, in a smaller hand, as if added after the lamp had been trimmed: *I fed them. They liked it. Nothing else to say; you said so, and you were right, and it is both.*

She read it twice and gave the first part to the lady and kept the rest, which was hers, and was a list, and was not.

* * *

The son came onto his mother's side of the house on the last day of November, in the afternoon, when the lady was at the baths with four of the twelve and the peristyle was empty, and Fausta was in it, and Momus was not.

She had been sent for a book. That was all; the lady wanted the Strabo — the lady's own, the house copy, which had never been opened — brought to the baths so that she could put the Smyrna Greek's error to a friend there with the page in front of her, and Phaedrus had sent the Puteoli girl because the Puteoli girl knew where in the fifth book the mole was, and the library was on the garden side, off the peristyle, and the peristyle was between the library and the door, and the dwarf was at the baths with the lady, holding the strigils, being furniture. It was eighteen months. She had been behind Momus for eighteen months, and on the Palatine side of every room, and had slept in the dormitory and never in a corridor, and the arrangement had held, as Phaedrus had said, because a man like that was slow and wanted it to be his idea; and she had let the rope go slack, a fraction, as you did, as she had at the dinner on the Kalends of June, and had come out of the library with the roll under her arm into the colonnade. He was standing at the far end of it by the fountain in a plain tunic with his hands behind his back looking at the water as though he had been there some time.

He had. She saw that. He had been there since the lady's litter went; he had known when it would go, and who went with it, and who was left, and what the lady had sent for and who would be sent for it, because he was his mother's son and had her gift and had used it, this once, for the one thing in her house she had arranged not to see. He had taken eighteen months to make it his idea. He had made it, now. She could see it on him from forty feet, the way you could see a ship's draught from how she sat.

She stopped. She did not go back into the library, because a girl who went back into a room was a girl who could be followed into it, and the library had one door. She did not go forward. She stood in the colonnade with the Strabo under her arm and her face doing nothing and her hands steady, and he turned from the fountain and looked at her down the length of the peristyle and smiled, the charming one, his mother's, and said in a pleasant carrying voice: "The Puteoli girl. With a book. You're always with a book, I'm told. Come here; I want to ask you about the mole."

She thought — very fast, in the way she had learned on a stair with a slate — about what she had. She had a roll. She had a peristyle with four doors and a man between her and three of them. She had the paper under the hearth-stone against her skin, which was no use. She had eighteen months of Kalends and Ides, and a comb, and a woman under a cloth who had asked her ash or sand; and she had the lady's lesson from the small room, *two people who don't look at each other are the only two people everyone can see*, and she had, under that, the other thing, from the same room, the thing she had charged the lady for: *the street's not for sale. The hand is.*

She walked toward him. Not fast. The way a girl walked in a house she was owned in, which was as if the walk were an errand. She stopped at the distance that was civil, with the fountain between them, and looked at him — the proper look, at no cost, the Spaniard look — and said in the eggs voice:

"Domine. The lady wants the Strabo at the baths. I'm to take it. Phaedrus is waiting at the door with the boy." This last was a lie and she said it as the magistrate had been paid to say a thing, as if it were the truth, because it was, in every way that mattered except one.

"Phaedrus can wait. He's good at it." The smile did not change. He came round the fountain. "I've a question about the mole. The real one. Not the sand. I'm told you were born under it."

"Behind it, domine. In a house by the harbour." She did not step back. A girl who stepped back was a girl who could be stepped toward. "The Augusta asked me the same, on the Ides. Ash or sand. She says the Rhône's the same; stone under mud, from the old fires. She says Nemausus is built on it." She let it sit, the name, the way you let a jar sit on a scale until the beam stopped. "Her freedwoman has asked for me on the Kalends. To do the Augusta's hair the old way, since I know how it's done on the coast. The lady's agreed. I'm to go up with the comb."

It was not true. Not yet. It was a thing Marcia had said with two fingers at a pin — *you might learn it; she likes it done by someone who was raised where it's done* — and she had put it where she kept things and had not taken it out, and she took it out now, in the colonnade, and laid it between them on the paving like the paper under the hearth-stone, and watched him read it.

He read it. She watched the lamp behind the pleasant eyes, which had been lit with interest and had gone up, on the Kalends of June, with the other thing; she watched it meet the word *Augusta* and the word *comb*, and she watched him do the sum, which was his mother's sum, which was that a jug-girl on his mother's side of the house was his mother's and his mother's was in the end his. That a girl who went through the door at the end of the outer room with a comb was *not*; she was a thing that stood three paces from a woman who could ask the emperor for a man's head with a cloth over her eyes and have it brought in by the Ides. She watched him understand that the thing in his mother's house with the nose had, in eighteen months, while he was making it his idea, made herself somebody else's; and she watched, under the charm, which did not move, the lamp do what a lamp did when a hand was put over it.

It did not go out. That was the thing she would carry. It went down, and stayed lit, and the light that came round the edges of the hand was not interest any more, and not the other thing either, the being-seen; it was the thing a man had when he had been told the market disagreed, and had believed it, and had decided to be patient about it.

"The Augusta's hair," he said pleasantly. "Well. We're all going up in the world." He stepped aside from the fountain, and made her a small gesture toward the door, the gracious one, Rufus's. "Don't keep Phaedrus. He's old."

She went past him at the civil distance with the Strabo under her arm and did not hurry and did not look back, and at the door Phaedrus was not waiting, because she had lied, and she went out into the street with the roll and walked to the baths alone through the Caelian in the November light with her face doing nothing, and gave the lady the Strabo at the fifth book. The lady looked at her once and said nothing, and that night in the slaves' court, in the dark, she told Phaedrus, all of it, the fountain and the comb and the hand over the lamp, flat, as a manifest; and Phaedrus listened and was quiet a long time and then said:

"You told him you were the Augusta's."

"I told him Marcia had asked for me. She has. Not in those words."

"Then you'd better be, by the Kalends. Because he'll ask. Not his mother; he knows better than to ask her anything. He'll ask the Palatine, through whoever he drinks with, whether the widow Tertulla's Puteoli girl does the empress's hair, and if the answer's no, you've lied to him about the one person in Rome it's not safe to lie about, and he'll have you." The Alexandrian's voice was very low. "And if the answer's yes, you've made yourself the one thing in his mother's house he can't put a hand on without the hand being seen from the hill. Which is the cleverest thing you could have done and the most dangerous, because a man like that doesn't forgive being made to be patient, and he's got years, and so have you, and now you're both spending them on each other."

"I know."

"Yes. You know. You always know; it's why the lady keeps you and it's why I'm afraid for you, and I've not been afraid for a girl in this house in nine years, and I don't like it." He turned to go in, and stopped. "Learn the hair. Properly. I'll find you someone from the coast in the kitchens who did it for her mother. Learn it by the Kalends, and go up with the comb, and make the lie true, because that's the only kind of lie that holds in a house like this, and your mother knew it, and so did the magistrate she paid."

She learned the hair. She went up on the Kalends with the comb. Marcia's fingers moved a pin that was not loose and the round face did nothing and the freedwoman said, "He asked. Through a man at the baths who drinks with a man of ours. I said yes. I'd have said yes anyway; you've the hands for it, and she likes the coast. But I said it a day before you'd touched her head, girl, and I'll want to know one day what that cost you, and you'll tell me, and it'll be true of more than forty." And she went through the door at the end with the comb behind the freedwoman, and stood three paces from a woman with a cloth over her eyes, and did her hair the old way, plain, as it was done on a coast built on ash, and the woman said, under the cloth, "That's it. That's how my mother had it. Tertulla's girl has found the one thing on this hill nobody else can do and it's a hairpin," and made the sound that might have been a laugh; and Fausta did not look up, and did not exist, and was, from that morning, hers.
