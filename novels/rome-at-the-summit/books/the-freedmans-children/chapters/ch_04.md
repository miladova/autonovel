# The Books

The grain fleet came in on the ninth day before the Kalends of June, two hundred and eleven sail, and his father made him count them.

He stood on the roof of the warehouse with a tablet and a stylus and the whole of the emporium below him roaring, and he counted. It was not easy. They came round the point of Misenum in no order, the big Alexandrian hulls with their artemon sails bellied out ahead of them like a second ship, and the smaller carriers tucked in among them, and the harbour boats going out to meet them in a swarm, and the town on every roof and the mole black with people and the magistrates on the harbour steps in their purple-bordered best with the priests of Isis behind them and the trumpets; and from the warehouse roof, with the sun on the water and the whole bay full of canvas, they looked less like ships than like a thing that had happened to the sea. He counted anyway. He counted by masts, because masts did not move, and he made a mark for every ten and a cross for every fifty, and when the last of them was inside the mole and the roar had settled into the long rolling noise of a town that would be drunk by nightfall, he had two hundred and eleven, and he went down the ladder and gave the tablet to his father.

His father looked at it. "The harbour-master will say two hundred and nine."

"I counted twice."

"I know you did. The harbour-master will say two hundred and nine because two of them are ours and they came in the night before last and anchored off the springs, and he's been paid not to have seen them." His father put the tablet in his belt. "You're right. Remember that you're right. And remember that being right about a thing the harbour-master has been paid to be wrong about is worth exactly nothing until you know who paid him." He turned to go down to the quay, and stopped, and looked back at his son with an expression Celer could not read. "I paid him. In case you were going to ask. Now come and watch them unload the *Isis*."

* * *

The summer went like that: a lesson in everything, from a man who gave lessons the way other men breathed, without appearing to notice he was doing it, and who had decided, that year, that the time for his son to learn the business by watching was over and the time to learn it by doing had come.

So Celer did. He went down to the warehouse at the first hour and stayed until the lamps. He learned the measures, Italian and Baetican and Alexandrian and the one the Rhodians used that was none of these. He learned the tallies, which were kept on wooden boards hung in the aisles and which he was made to reconcile every evening against the wax, and against the papyrus in the strong-room that was the record of record, and he learned that the three never quite agreed and that his father could look at the discrepancy and say to within an amphora who had been careless and who had been thieving and which of the two was cheaper to tolerate. He learned the names of the masters of the three hulls and what each of them drank and what each of them was owed and what each of them owed back, and he learned the shape of the contract that was the whole of the house's fortune: so many thousand modii of Egyptian wheat carried from Alexandria to Puteoli each season on behalf of the prefecture of the grain-supply in Rome, at a rate per modius fixed under Nerva, with a bounty for early delivery and a penalty for short, and the whole of it countersigned by a freedman of the prefect's office named Felix whose name his father never said without a small movement of the lips, as though tasting something.

"Felix," his father said, in the strong-room, with the lamp between them and the contract unrolled on the table, "is the kind of man who has a price and lets you know it. That's a good kind. It's the ones who don't that cost you. Felix is a freedman of Nerva's household, and Nerva is dead, and the new man will bring his own freedmen down from Germany, or he won't, and either way there'll be a season when Felix doesn't know whether he's Felix any more, and in that season he'll be expensive. I've put money by for it." He touched the papyrus. "Read me the penalty clause."

Celer read it. His Greek was bad, Nicias said, but his Latin was his father's, and the contract was in the Latin of the grain-office, which was a language of its own, with no verbs to speak of and every noun hedged about with others. He read it twice before he understood it, and then he said, "It's by weight. Not by measure. The penalty's by weight."

"Yes."

"But the tallies are by measure."

"Yes."

"So if the grain's wet —"

"If the grain's wet," his father said, "it weighs more, and the prefecture gets its weight and we get our bounty, and the baker in the Subura gets a loaf that's a tenth water, and nobody counts that, because the baker's a baker. And if the grain's dry it weighs less, and the prefecture docks us, and we go to Felix. And Felix, who is paid by the prefecture to weigh the grain, weighs it on a day when it's been under a wet sail." He rolled the contract. "You're going to ask me if that's honest. Don't. It's the measure. It's the Baetican jar again; it's smaller, and everyone knows it's smaller, and the price is set knowing. The man who'd lose by it is a man in Rome who's never seen a ship and never will. I'm not going to the lamp for him."

Celer did not ask if it was honest. He had learned, since the warehouse and the four hundred and twenty, not to ask that, or rather to ask it somewhere else, in a place behind his face where the answer was always the same and never made any difference. He said instead: "Who else knows it's by weight?"

His father looked at him for a long moment across the lamp.

"Felix," he said. "The prefect, if he's ever read it, which he hasn't. Me. You. And the girl from the Dolphin, if she's read this table as often as I think she has when my back's turned, because she reads everything, that one, and remembers it, and I've never yet caught her doing it, which is the only reason I let it go on." He put the contract in its box and locked it. "I'm not angry. I'd rather she read my papers than Thallus's. Only remember, when you're grown and have papers of your own, that the people who can read them are the people who can hurt you, and that there are only ever two things to do about that. You can stop them reading, which you can't; or you can make it cost them more to tell than to keep. That's the whole of business. Everything else is jars."

* * *

She was in the tutor's room that afternoon, with Nicias asleep and a book in her lap she was not reading, and she looked up when he came in and said, "You've been in the strong-room. You've got lamp-smoke in your hair."

He sat on the step. He did not say *my father knows you read his papers*. He had thought about it the whole way up the hill and had decided that it was his father's to say, and that his father would not say it, and that this meant something, though he did not yet know what. He said instead, "The contract's by weight."

"I know."

"How long have you known?"

"Since the winter. I read it when he was out and you were counting jars." She said it without any shame at all, as she said everything. "It's in the fourth line of the second column. *Pondere, non mensura.* He's had it there for two years and he reads it every month and he's never once had the words cut out, because the day he does, Felix knows he's noticed." She turned a page she had not read. "Does he know I know?"

"Yes."

She was quiet. He watched her put it where she kept things; he had begun, that summer, to be able to see her do it, a small settling of the face, like a bird closing its wings. "And he's not angry."

"He says he'd rather you read his than Thallus's."

"That's not the same as not angry."

"No," Celer agreed. It was the thing about her, that she would not let a sentence by that was doing two jobs. "It's not."

They sat. Nicias snored once, softly, and turned over on his pallet with the book sliding off his face, and did not wake. Through the half-shutter the sound of the town came up, the long drunk roar of the fleet's second day, and the smell of fish frying in a hundred streets, and under it, faint, the sulphur.

"Show me the tallies," she said.

He looked at her.

"The boards. You said they never agree with the wax. Show me how you tell who's thieving and who's careless. I want to see if I can do it." She closed the book. "Not the papers in the box. I won't go in the box again, you can tell him. The boards. They're hung in the aisles for anyone to see; a boy with a broom sees them. That's not reading his papers. That's looking at his wall."

He thought about it. He thought about his father's face across the lamp, and *the only reason I let it go on*, and the thing he had not been able to read in it; and he thought about the girl in front of him who had read the contract in the winter and had not told him until he found out, which was either a betrayal or the opposite, and he could not, at eleven, make up his mind which, and suspected — he was beginning to suspect this about a great many things — that it was both.

"Tomorrow," he said. "First hour. Before he's down. Come in by the quay door; the men know you."

"They know me as Hilara's girl."

"That'll do. Hilara's girl comes for fish-sauce. Bring a jar."

She smiled, then, which she did not often do, and never for long; it went across her face like light off water and was gone. "I always bring a jar," she said.

* * *

So in the summer of the Spaniard's first year, while Rome waited for its emperor and the senate made speeches about liberty and the Guard sat in its camp and sulked, the son of Hermes and the daughter of Hilara stood in the aisles of a warehouse on the emporium of Puteoli at the first hour of the morning with a fish-sauce jar between them and read the walls.

He showed her the boards. He showed her how each aisle had its own, and each board its own hand, because each aisle had its own man, and how you learned the hands before you learned anything else: Pyrrhus in the first aisle made his fives like a gallows and never got a sum wrong and was, in consequence, never watched; Syrus in the third made beautiful figures and lost a jar a week and was watched always and never caught, because the jar was never a jar, it was a sixth of one, poured off the top of six. He showed her how to run a finger down a column and feel for the place where the hand changed — not the figures, the pressure, the way a man leaned on the stylus when he was writing what he had counted and the way he did not when he was writing what he wished he had. He showed her the wax, which was the aisle-men's boards copied each evening by the clerk, and the places where the clerk's copying improved on the boards without meaning to, because the clerk was a man who liked round numbers. And he showed her the thing his father had shown him, which was that the discrepancy between the board and the wax and the papyrus was not a problem to be solved but a fact to be read, like a face, and that a house whose three records agreed was a house where somebody had gone to the trouble of making them.

She was faster than he was. He had known she would be. She had the memory, the thing Nicias had said about her in the doorkeeper's hearing years ago, and once she had seen a column she had it, and could set it beside another column from the day before and see where the two did not meet without going back to look. He did not have that. What he had, and found out that summer he had, by watching her not have it, was the other thing: he could see what the column was *for*. She would say, "Syrus is a sixth short on the Baetican again," and he would say, "Yes, and he's a sixth over on the Italian, because he's selling the Baetican oil in Italian jars to the sausage-woman's brother and splitting it," and she would look at him and say, "How do you know that?" and he would not be able to tell her, except that he knew Syrus and he knew the sausage-woman's brother and he knew what a sixth of a jar was worth and to whom, and it had fitted together behind his face while she was still reading.

"You see the system," she said once, on the quay, with the jar full and the sun just over the warehouse roof. "I see the page. That's the difference."

"Nicias says you've got the better memory."

"Nicias is right. And he says you've got no Greek style and he's right about that too. It doesn't mean what you think it means." She hefted the jar. "A page is only a page. A system's a thing you can live in."

He thought about that, going back up the aisle. He thought about it so long that Pyrrhus asked him if he was ill.

* * *

He took her into the strong-room once that summer, at the end of it, with the lid off, and read her a bill.

It was not against his father's word; his father had said *I'd rather she read mine than Thallus's* and had meant it as a figure, and Celer had decided, at eleven, that a figure a man said was a figure he could be held to. So on an evening in September with the fleet gone and the town quiet and his father at the Augustales' dinner, he lit the lamp in the strong-room and took down from the shelf a pine box with his father's seal on the lid, and broke nothing, because the seal was on a thread that lifted, and set the lid beside it, and she stood in the door and did not come in.

"You said you'd not go in the box again."

"I'm not. You are. I'm reading it to you. That's different, and he'd say so." He unrolled a sheet. "You've read the contract. *Pondere, non mensura.* You know what it says. You don't know what it's *for*. I'll show you what it's for, and then you'll know the thing he knows and I know and Thallus doesn't, and there'll be three of us, and he'd rather that than two of them."

She came in. She stood across the table from him with the lamp between and her hands at her sides and the shutter to, and he read it to her, with his eyes on the sheet and not on her, because that was how he read: a sail-maker's bill, from the year the *Isis* was bought, for a mainsail of the heavy Egyptian canvas, so many pounds, so much the pound, and in the margin, in his father's small square hand, a second figure beside the first, and beside that a word.

"*Dry*," he read. "That's the first. That's what the sail weighs rolled on the quay. And this one, under it, he's written himself — it's not the sail-maker's, see, it's his ink — that's the sail *wet*. He had it weighed both ways the day it came. Four hundred pounds between them." He looked at the margin. "A spare mainsail's stowed over the forward hold. If it's been over the side in the night on a line and come up and been stowed again across the hatch-boards, and the forward hold's weighed with it on, by a man who's not a sailor, the hold's four hundred pounds over. And a hull that's over is a hull that's early and full, and nobody docks a hull that's over. They pay her the bounty." He set the sheet down. "That's what *by weight* is for. That's the whole of it. He's written the sum in the margin of a sail-maker's bill because he's a man who writes the sum down, and he's never burned a sheet in his life, and the bill's in this box, and now you know."

She did not say anything for a long moment. She looked at the margin, and at the two figures, and at the word, and he saw her put it where she kept things, the small settling, the bird closing its wings; and then she looked up, at him, across the lamp, which she did not often do when he was reading, and held it, so that he looked up from the sheet and found her already looking, and could not read her, and would remember that he could not.

"All right," she said.

That was all. *All right.* He put the lid back on and the seal back on its thread and the box on its shelf, and she went out by the quay door, and he stood in the strong-room with the lamp and understood that he had just shown her the one thing in his father's house that could be used against his father, because he had wanted her to see what he could see, and that she had said *all right* and looked at him, and that he did not know what either of those meant, and that he had done it anyway, and that this was going to be true, he suspected, of a great many things.

* * *

Nicias caught him at it in July — not the warehouse; the thinking.

"You've a face on you like a man doing long division in a temple," the tutor said. "Stop it. Sit. We're doing Demosthenes, and you'll hate it, and that's the point." He had the roll open on his knees, and the cup beside him, and the shutters half-closed, and the girl was not there; she had the fleet's accounts to help with at the Dolphin, where the masters lodged in the season and ran up bills that Hilara had begun, that summer, to let her daughter keep. "*On the Crown.* He's defending his whole life in front of a jury that's already decided. Read me the opening."

Celer read it. His Greek was what it was: correct, and flat, every word in its place and none of them alive. Nicias listened with his eyes shut, and when it was done he said, "Yes. Well. You've just killed a man who's been dead four hundred years and I didn't think it could be done twice." He opened his eyes. "Do you know why you've no style?"

"Because I don't care about it."

"No. Because you don't believe anyone is listening. You read as if the words were a tally-board — a thing that's true whether anyone looks at it or not. Demosthenes didn't believe that. He believed that nothing was true until he'd made five hundred Athenians feel it in their stomachs, and he was right, and it's the only thing worth knowing about Greek." Nicias reached for the cup. "Your father has the same fault, and it has made him rich, so I don't say it's a fault in a shipowner. But you're not going to be a shipowner. You're going to Rome on a senator's staff, and in Rome nothing is true until someone has said it well in the right room, and a man who can only count will spend his life counting for men who can talk."

"The girl talks."

"The girl listens, which is rarer, and she'll talk when she needs to, because she's listened to how it's done. You've never listened to anything in your life but a number." He drank. "I'm not scolding. I'm telling you where the gap is so you'll know what you're stepping over when you fall in it."

Celer looked at the roll. He said, after a while, "You write to Rufus about me."

"I write to Rufus about everything. He pays me to — not in coin; he lets me keep the books. It's the same thing." Nicias did not look away. "I wrote to him in the spring that you had a head for figures and no Greek style and that you would be useful to him in exactly the way your father intends and in no other, unless someone took the trouble. He wrote back asking whether I thought it worth the trouble. I said I didn't know yet."

"And the girl? Do you write about her?"

The tutor was quiet for a moment. Then he said, "I wrote once. In the winter. I said there was a child at the back of my room who had a memory like a strong-room and no one had thought to lock it. He wrote back and asked whose child. I told him. He didn't write again about it." He set the cup down. "I shouldn't have said that to you. I've said it. Put it where you put things, and read me the next paragraph, and this time pretend that someone in the room wants you to lose."

Celer read the next paragraph. He pretended. It was no better, but Nicias said it was, slightly, and let him go; and he went down through the house with the roll's words still in his mouth and the other words under them, *whose child*, and *he didn't write again*, and he thought that a man who did not write again about a thing was a man who had put it somewhere, and that he would like, very much, to know where.

* * *

His father forbade him the Dolphin in August.

It was not a scene. His father did not make scenes; a scene was a thing a man did when he had failed to arrange matters so that it was unnecessary. It was done at breakfast, in the ordinary voice, with the tablets open and the stepmother at the loom in the next room where she could hear and was meant to.

"The steward goes back to Rome with the family on the Kalends of September," his father said. "Until then, and after, you'll keep away from Hilara's house. Not the girl; the girl may come here, by the kitchen, as she does. The house." He turned a leaf of the tablet. "You've been seen going in. Twice in July. By the quay door, in the evening, when you'd told your mother you were at the baths."

Celer said nothing. There was nothing to say that was not either a lie or an admission, and he had learned that silence, with his father, was taken as the second and forgiven more readily than the first.

"I don't ask what you did there. I know what you did there; you sat in the yard with the girl and the other one and talked, because you're eleven and there's nothing else you'd know how to do. I'm not a fool and I wasn't born a Roman. But I'm telling you to stop, and I'm telling you why, which I don't have to. It's this." His father put the stylus down. "Thallus goes to that house. The master's son goes to that house, or sends for what's in it. Half the villa goes to that house in the season. It's a lodging-house with a woman upstairs; that's what it is, and that's what it's been since before you were born, and there's no shame in it for Hilara, who does what she can with what she was given, and none for the girl. But there's shame in it for *you*. Not now. In ten years. When you're in Rome on Rufus's staff and some clerk who's jealous of you finds out that Hermes's boy used to be at the Puteoli whorehouse every evening of his twelfth summer, and tells it at dinner, and makes it something it wasn't." He picked up the stylus again. "I've spent thirty years making sure there's nothing anyone can say about this house that's true. I'm not going to have you hand them something. Keep away."

"She's not —"

"I know what she's not. I said so. I'm not talking about what she is. I'm talking about what can be said." His father looked at him then, and there was something in the look that was almost — Celer would think about this for years and never settle it — almost an apology, or the place where an apology would have been in a different man. "You think I'm hard. I'm not hard. I'm *careful*. You'll be glad of it. Now go down to the warehouse; the *Isis* is loading for the return and I want the manifest in your hand, not the clerk's."

He went down to the warehouse. He held the manifest. He did not go to the Dolphin for eleven days, and on the twelfth he went, and that was the first time in his life he disobeyed his father in a thing that mattered, and he did it, as he would do most such things afterwards, without deciding to.

* * *

He went in by the yard, over the wall from the alley behind, because the quay door was watched now — he had seen Daphnis's son loitering at the corner on the ninth day and had understood what that meant — and dropped down among the cats by the shrine with the pink ghost in it, and stood still, and listened.

The house was quiet. It was the dead hour after noon, when the lodgers were out or asleep and the women were in the kitchen or upstairs with the shutters closed against the heat, and the yard had the stillness of a place that has been used hard and is resting. Antiope was not there; Antiope was at the villa, had been since the spring, and came down only on the goddess's days with silver in her hem and a look in her face that he had seen and not understood and had, lately, begun to. The shrine's saucer had milk in it. Someone had put a flower by it, a single red one, and the cats had not touched that.

The girl came out of the kitchen with a basin and saw him and stopped.

She did not say *you're not supposed to be here*. She looked at him over the basin, and he watched her read him — the wall-dust on his knees, the sweat, the way he was standing — and put it away, and say, in the ordinary voice: "You've got nine days before the steward goes. If you're going to be stupid, be stupid in the house. The alley's watched from the baker's roof."

"Daphnis's boy."

"And one of the steward's. Since the Ides. He sits with the baker's girl and watches the yard wall. He's not very good at it." She set the basin down. "Come in. My mother's asleep. She sleeps now, in the afternoons; she didn't use to."

He went in. He had been in the common room many times, and the kitchen, and the yard; he had never been further, and she did not take him further now, but she took him to the foot of the stairs, where the light came down from the window on the landing, and sat on the third step, and he sat on the first, and they looked at each other with the whole of the quiet house around them like held breath.

"He's forbidden you."

"Yes."

"Because of what can be said."

"How did you —"

"It's what he'd say. It's what Thallus would say. It's what anyone would say who'd thought about it, and your father's never not thought about anything." She put her chin on her knees. "He's right."

"I know he's right."

"And you came anyway."

"Yes."

She was quiet. The light moved on the stairs. Somewhere above them a shutter ticked in the heat, and a lodger turned over, and the whole house settled a fraction on its timbers the way a ship did at anchor.

"Why?" she said.

He did not know. That was the truth and he knew it was the truth and he knew she would know it if he said anything else, so he sat on the bottom step and looked at her and tried to find it, the way you tried to find a sum that had gone wrong somewhere in the middle; and what he found, when he went looking, was not an answer but a number, and the number was eleven days, and under it another, and the other was *two years, three*, and under that, very small, so that he could barely read it, the thing his father had said at breakfast about the twelfth summer and the clerk at dinner and *makes it something it wasn't*; and he understood, sitting there, that his father had been describing a thing that had not happened yet in order to prevent it, and that he, Celer, had climbed a wall in the heat of the afternoon to find out whether it was true.

"I don't know," he said.

She looked at him a long moment. Then she nodded, slowly, as if he had said something exact.

"All right," she said. "That'll do. It's better than a reason." And she got up off the step and went into the kitchen and came back with bread and a piece of the hard cheese the Rhodians liked, and gave him half, and sat down again on the third step, and said with her mouth full, "Tell me about the *Isis*. Is it true she's got a bronze eye on the bow that the master touches before he sails?"

So he told her about the *Isis*. And at the end of it he said, because it had been in him since the strong-room and had to come out somewhere, "He said you're the only one besides Felix and him who knows it's by weight. He said the people who can read your papers are the people who can hurt you, and there are only two things to do about it, and one of them's to make it cost them more to tell than to keep."

She stopped chewing.

"And which has he done with me?"

"I don't know. I don't think he's decided." He looked at the bread in his hand. "I don't think he knows what it would cost you to tell. I don't think anyone does. I think that's why he lets it go on."

"And you?" she said. "Do you know?"

He thought about it honestly, because she had asked honestly, and because the house was quiet and it was the dead hour and there was nobody to perform for. "No," he said. "I know what it'd cost him. I know what it'd cost me. I don't know what it'd cost you, because I don't know what you'd get. I don't know what you want." He looked up. "What do you want?"

It was the first time he had asked. He saw it land. He saw her face do the thing it did — the bird closing its wings — and then, unexpectedly, open again, so that for the length of a breath he was looking at a girl of ten on a stair with bread in her hand and nothing in front of her face at all.

"I want the house to stand," she said. "That's what I put in the Ship. I didn't say which house. I thought she'd know." She put the last of the bread in her mouth. "That's what you've got that I haven't. You know which house. Your father's told you every day of your life. It's on a stone on the Capua road with a ship under it." She stood up. "Go on. Over the wall, not the quay. The baker's girl goes in at the ninth hour and he goes with her, and he won't be back on the roof for a quarter. Count a hundred after you hear the baker's door."

He counted a hundred. He went over the wall. Nobody saw him, or if they did, nobody said; and he walked back up to the house above the macellum through the dead white heat with the taste of Rhodian cheese in his mouth and her question in him, *do you know*, and his own, *what do you want*, and the thing she had said about the stone, which was true, and which he had never once in his life thought of as a thing that could be envied.

His stepmother was at the loom. She looked at the wall-dust on his knees. She said, "Your father's at the warehouse till dark," and went back to the thread, and that was all; and he understood that he had been given something, and did not know whether it was a kindness or a debt, and put it with the others.

* * *

On the Kalends of September the family went back to Rome, and the steward with them, and the villa was shut, and the slipped tile stayed where it was; and on the second day of the month a letter came down from Rufus to his father, by the ordinary post, which Celer was allowed to read because it concerned him.

It was short. The young master thanked Hermes for the summer's hospitality, by which he meant, Celer's father said drily, the loan. He reported that the emperor was still in the north and had gone from the Rhine to the Danube to review the Pannonian legions, as Hermes had predicted, and that Rome did not expect him before the next summer at the earliest. He mentioned that the Dacian king had sent an embassy to the emperor's camp and that nobody knew what had been said, which was, in Rufus's phrase, *the kind of silence that is usually expensive for somebody.* And he closed by saying that he looked forward to seeing Hermes's boy in Rome when the emperor came, as his father had so kindly proposed, since the young man would want to see the city before he was asked to be useful in it.

"*When the emperor comes,*" his father said, and folded the letter. "Next summer, then. A year." He looked at his son across the table. "You'll go up with me for the entry. You'll see the thing. You'll see him."

"Yes, Father."

"And you'll stop going over the Dolphin's wall." It was said in exactly the same voice. "The baker's girl is Vibia's laundress's niece. I've known since the first time. I let it go because the steward was here and a boy who's forbidden a thing and does it anyway is a boy who'll be hard to break later, and I'd rather you were hard. But the steward's gone and so's the reason." He put the letter in the box. "Nine days. I counted too. It's a thing you'll find, in this house, that there's never only one of us counting."

Celer said nothing. He went down to the warehouse. He held the manifest. And in the strong-room, with the lamp, when the clerk had gone, he unrolled the contract and read the fourth line of the second column, *pondere, non mensura*, and he thought about the girl on the third step saying *a system's a thing you can live in*, and he thought that she was wrong about one thing, which was that he knew which house.

He had thought he did. He had been told every day of his life. But he sat in the strong-room of his father's warehouse with his father's contract under his hand and he found, when he looked, that the house he wanted to stand was not on the Capua road, and was not above the macellum, and was not anywhere he could have put his finger on a map; it was a stair with light coming down it, and two people on the steps, and a quiet that was not his father's quiet or Thallus's or anyone's; and he did not have a word for it, and he did not look for one, because a boy who has been raised to count knows better than anyone that there are things which, once you have put a figure to them, can be taken out of a box and put in a fire.
