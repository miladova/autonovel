# The Measure

She had not slept, and at the end of the third hour of the night she stopped pretending that lying on a floor was a thing she was doing for a reason, and got up, and did her own hair.

Not the old way; not the new. The way she had done it at ten on a lodging-house stair with no glass, which was back, and tied, and out of the way of a slate; and then she stood with the ivory in her hand in the one lamp and looked at it, the small carved face with the plain hair and the gold on the break, and did the sum, and put it in. At the side. Where the light would catch it. Because a patient man had put it in her hand to be seen and she had made it hers by being seen in it, and because it was the one thing she owned that had been a price and had come out, and because — she was honest; she had decided on a terrace — he had never seen it, and she would like him to see the one thing on her that the hill had given and she had kept, so that he would know what she had been wearing while she was not turning round.

The Strabo, the fish, the prefect's copy under the Augusta's seal, and his letters stayed in the chest under the book. She had decided that too. You did not carry a receipt to a hole in the ground. You left it where it could be found and went to find out what was next, which was a thing her mother had asked with her last breath and she had taken four years to hear.

Sabina was awake. Sabina was at the window in the shift with her hair still wrong from the night and the beautiful face turned to the dark where the city was being quiet at last, and said without turning, in the low quick voice, "You've done your own. Back and tied. Like a girl with a slate." A pause. "Good. He's never seen it any other way, has he. Not the old way or the new; those are ours. That's yours." She turned. The dry tenant intelligence looked at the girl in the door with the pin and the plain hair and the face that did nothing, and did a thing Fausta had not seen it do, which was soften, briefly, as a thing softened that had been held hard a long time and was tired.

"He told me a thing. In the door, at midnight, before the library. He doesn't tell me things; he tells me where not to look. But he stood there with the sky still on him from the Syrian's roofless feast and said: *the emperor asked Hermes's son what was next, at the high table, in front of the Senate, and the boy said Sura's office and mine and the forum and the roll, and then he said there was a thing that wasn't Caesar's or Sura's or mine and that he'd not say it at a table because the tables on this hill are for figures. And my uncle said "good, there's always one," and told him to go home and find it, and said Plotina had written him the same in the eleventh letter from Cologne, at the end, in a line nobody's seen.*" Sabina's hands were still in her lap. "And then my husband said — to the glass, not to me; he'd caught sight of himself — *I've sat at that table seven years and never once said a thing at it that wasn't a figure, and a freedman's son with a stick has done it on the day of the triumph and been told "good."* And he went to the library and shut the door." The beautiful face did the nearly-smile, and for once there was something in it besides the nothing, and it was not kind to anyone, least of all its owner. "He was jealous, Puteoli. Not of you. Of *him*. Of a boy who had a thing left over and said so at a table. I've been married to that man since I was seventeen and I've never seen him want a thing he couldn't buy or read or outwait, and last night he wanted to be a shipowner's son for the length of a sentence, and couldn't, and went and read Sappho about it. I'd like that in your book. It's the only thing I've ever had over him and I'll not have it again."

"Yes, Vibia."

"Don't. I'm getting fond of it." She turned back to the window. "Go on. Momus is at the postern; he's been there since the second hour of the night, Marcia says, sitting on the step like a parcel. My husband's in the library with the door shut; he went at midnight; he told the Germans he was not to be disturbed by anyone for any reason until the room, and the Germans have never once in seven years heard him say *anyone*, and they've decided it means me, and it doesn't." The nearly-smile, with nothing in it and everything. "He's decided not to know. So has she. So has Lucius, on his couch, if he's alive. Three of the four people on this hill who've known everything for thirty years have decided, on the same morning, not to know where a hair-girl goes before the room, and the fourth is my mother, and she's asleep, because she's the only one of them who's never needed to know anything to be in the room." She turned back to the window. "That's what you've done to this hill, Puteoli. In four years. You've made the people who count stop counting for a morning. I'd not have believed it. Go and see your hole. Come back and do my hair; I'll want it done the new way and I'll want to be told nothing, and I'll know anyway, because I'll see your back."

Marcia was on the covered stair.

Not at the postern; on the stair itself, halfway, in the dark, where the Germans could not see her from either end, with no comb, in a cloak, and she stood up off the step as the girl came down and did not say anything for a moment, and the round brown face in the no-light was not arranged, and Fausta understood that the freedwoman from Nemausus had been sitting on a stair in the dark since some hour of the night in order to be met by accident.

"She's asleep." Marcia said it first, as the one true thing, because that was what she gave. "The Augusta. Since the fourth hour. She said the arithmetic and lay down and slept, which she's not done on the night of a thing in thirty years; I've sat by her for all of them and I know. I've left her. The Germans have the door. She'll wake for the room and put the cloth on and not ask me where you were, and I'll not tell her, and that'll be the first morning in forty years I've had a thing she hasn't." The round face did a thing. "I'd like to know what it's like too. It seems to be going round."

"Marcia."

"I've a thing. Then go." The freedwoman took it out of the cloak. A sheet, folded, soft at the folds, with a seal on it that was a brass fish. "Yours. The letter. Tingis. In your hand, to me, asking. I said I'd put it in the box with mine and I did, and they've sat there a year, the two of them, and last night after the box she said — not to me; to the ceiling, with the cloth off, which is how she says the things that are to me — she said: *Marcia, the girl's letter. Give it back. I've one sentence of hers in that box and I'd like to see if it stays true without the other.*" She held it out. "The sentence she's keeping is *the only one you'll never own*. She's giving you back the one that's the price of a man in Africa. I don't know what that means. I've worked for her forty years and I've never once not known what a thing she did meant and I don't know this, and I've decided that's what she wants, and I've decided to let her have it."

Fausta took it. It weighed nothing. The fish had taken the wax and the wax had held a year in a box on the hill with another letter like it from Nemausus, and she stood on the covered stair in the dark with it in her hand and did not open it, because she knew what it said, and because a thing you had written to buy a man's absence was not a thing you read on your way to another man's presence.

"And yours?"

"Mine stays." Marcia pulled the cloak round. "I'm fifty-four. I picked a woman with a headache and I've never been back to Nemausus and the letter's the receipt and I'll keep it. You're nineteen and you've been given yours back by the only woman on this hill who never gives a thing back, on the morning she's decided not to know a thing, and I'd not stand on a stair with it, girl. I'd go." She sat back down on the step, in the dark, halfway, where neither end could see. "I'll be here when you come up. I'll not ask. I've decided. It's catching."

She went down. At the postern, the step, the dwarf.

She put the letter in her gown, against the skin, where a paper had been for five years and was not now; and it sat there, light, folded, with the fish on it, the whole way down the hill, and she did not think about it, and knew it was there, and that was, she thought, going down the Clivus with a dwarf beside her, exactly the sum: a thing you had paid, given back to you, that you had decided to carry anyway, and not spend.

* * *

Momus was on the step at the postern with his legs out, and got up when she came, and looked at the hair.

"Back and tied."

"Yes."

"And the pin."

"Yes."

"Good. Both. That's the whole column." He fell in beside her, on the short legs, as she went out through the postern past the German who did not look at her because she was the Augusta's hair and the dwarf was a gift; and they went down the covered stair, and out onto the Clivus, and down. The city was the colour the city was at the fourth hour of the night after a triumph, which was no colour, and the noise had gone out of it at last and left the thing under the noise, which was a hundred thousand people asleep where they had fallen and the sweepers, and the smell of wine and flowers and cattle and the sea-coal the bakers were lighting, and the dust, which had settled on everything in the night as it had not since the Syrian started cutting, because for one day there had been no baskets.

"I'll tell you a thing on the way down," said Momus, "since it's a long way for me and a short one for you and I'd rather talk than be carried. I went to the Syrian's hut on the Kalends. I'd not told you. I asked him which morning, and he said this one, the second hour, he's setting a course and he wants the shipowner's boy there first with the rod to measure it, because the Syrian's a man who measures a thing before he builds it, which is more than most on that hill. And I said I'd bring the measure." The dwarf looked at the road going down. "I didn't mean a rod. He knew I didn't. He's a Syrian; they don't do the thing where they let a man find out. He'll have told the boy at the feast, drunk, with lime on his arms. So the boy knows a dwarf's coming to the pit with something, and he'll have done the sum, because he does, and he'll have come out at *her*, because there's nothing else it could be, and he'll be at the far end anyway, since the third hour, Chrestê says, because he's been going there since November and he's decided, for once, to do nothing in the right place."

"You've spoken to Chrestê."

"Never in my life. We've spoken through four wine-shops and a steward's boy for two months and we've never been in a room and we count the same, and she's gone, tonight, to rooms over a fuller's, because she's free and she'd not be the woman at the top of his stair whichever way it goes, and she sent me that by the baker's wife at the second hour so I'd know before I walked you down." He went on down. "I'd like to meet her. I'll not. People like us don't meet; we count from the ends of corridors. But I wanted you to have it. She's not on the stair. There's nobody on the stair. There's a man at the end of a cut with a rod he's not been given yet and a stick he has."

They came down past the foot of the Caelian, where the road to the Sacred Way went under it, and she looked up, once, because the house was there, at the top, on its own ground, with the atrium and the pools and the ancestors and the corridor nobody used and a room with two doors; and it was dark, and it was a senator's who was in Tingis counting corn-ships, and Phaedrus was its steward and had thirteen years in it and had declared one thing in all of them, at the second hour of a morning, with his back to a door; and she felt, looking up, the price, where she kept it, and looked at it, and it was there, and it had not moved, and she had not expected it to, and she looked away from the house to the road and found that she could, which was the figure.

"That's the Caelian," said Momus, not looking up. "I know. I sat at the end of it forty years. It's a house. I've decided it's a house." He walked. "Phaedrus sends to me on the Kalends. He says the fountain's stopped again and nobody's started it and he's decided not to, and that he's bricked up a door in a dressing-room, himself, at night, with his own hands, which he's never used for anything but a key, and told the master in Africa it was damp. That's all. He wanted you to have it and he'd not write it to the hill."

They came down through the Velabrum. The Forum Boarium, with the flowers in the dung. The Palatine's flank, and the box on its posts with the hangings down and the timber grey in the no-colour light, and she looked up at it once, because she had stood in it nine hours and not looked at the road, and it was a box, on posts, empty, and she felt nothing, which she kept, and they went on under it onto the Sacred Way.

"Momus."

"Girl."

"You said you were in a cage. Two years. And then Tertulla's father's, and then hers, and forty years at the end of a corridor." The sand was churned to the colour of the cut and their feet went in it. "You've never said what you'd have been. If there'd been no cage."

The dwarf walked a while.

"Tall," he said. "That's all. Not clever; I'd have been clever anyway, it's in the head and the cage doesn't reach it. Tall, and a baker's boy in Praeneste, and dead of a fever at thirty, and nobody on this hill would ever have heard the name, and I'd have never once stood at the end of a corridor and watched a lodging-house girl walk up it with a false paper in her gown and her feet under her, and I'd not have had the one thing I've had, which is to count something that came out." He looked up. "That's not a thing I'd say on the hill. The hill wants a dwarf to be sorry so it can be kind. I'm not sorry. I'm shaped. There's a difference and the Augusta knows it, which is why she's never once in her life been kind to me, and I've loved her for it, in the way you love a woman who sees what you are and doesn't flinch and doesn't make a thing of not flinching." He walked. "You're shaped too. By a stair and a shutter and a panelling door and a sail and a man in Africa. You'll not be sorry either. You'll be what you are. And so's he, by a boat and a tent and a rock and a strong-room, and the two of you are going to stand at the end of a hole in the ground shaped like that and look at the same thing, and I'm going to sit on the Syrian's rod-chest and count, and nobody on the hill's going to know, because they've decided not to, and that's the only morning like it there'll ever be, girl, so walk a bit slower; I'd like it to last."

She stopped at the Rostra.

Not for a reason. The dwarf went on three paces and stopped too, and did not ask, and she stood in the churned sand of the Sacred Way at the foot of the old speakers' platform with the beaks of ships on it that had been taken from somebody's enemies four hundred years ago and were bronze now and green, and the Forum round her empty in the grey, and the Capitol above with its gold gone to lead in the no-light, and she did the sum. The whole of it. In order, the way she kept things, because a woman at a loom had told a boy that the gods liked to see a face, and the boy had told nobody, and Chrestê had told the wine-shop, and the dwarf had told her, and she had decided that if he could do it at the end of a cut in the dark she could do it at the foot of the Rostra in the grey, and that she would do it standing, because she had done everything standing.

A common room. A steward saying *she'll be useful*. A strong-room with the lid off and a boy reading a bill aloud with his eyes on the bill and not on her, so that she had looked at him instead, and had kept it. A mole in March, in the dark, and a kiss with his eyes open, and her hand flat on his chest and the arm held to its length, hers, because it was the only thing on the mole that was. A shutter that would not bar. A Rhodian on a stair, and the sum, *live*, and living. A dying woman and a slate and a basin and a word. A shed with the sea in a glass. A road up a hill with a false paper in a hem and a Thracian's wife's voice, and a widow's house with a corridor nobody used and a fountain that stopped, and a boy at a dinner she did not look at so that he would know exactly how far up the room she was. A room with one window and eleven women and a comb. A year of her tongue. A pin with gold on a break. A panelling door. A letter in her own hand asking for Tingis. A sail, told to a comb, with no *I*, and a boy left off the sheet on purpose, and her not knowing, on leaf three, whether that had been the cruellest thing or the kindest. A terrace by a sea with no manifest. A floor with a slate on it and a woman who had thrown her children in a basin saying *it comes out*. A letter to a dying man in a hand only his son could read. A receipt burned over a brazier for Germans' hands. A book with nothing in it. A box with forty boards going by and her lips still on the twenty-second. A woman in an emperor's chair saying *I don't know what it costs*.

It did not come out. She had known it would not. There was a thing left over, and it was the size of a girl on a mole saying *not for nothing* with her arm out straight and a boy saying *all right* and meaning it, and she had been carrying it in the place where she kept the Rhodian's eleven for six years and had called it the arm, and the price, and the fifth door, and leaf three, and it had been, all along, the same thing a dying woman had meant with one word, which was *next*.

"Girl," said Momus, three paces on, not turning.

"I'm counting."

"I know. I can see it from here; you've gone still the way he does. Chrestê says he did it at the end of the cut yesterday in the dark and said a thing out loud to the hole." The dwarf looked at the Capitol. "You'll not say yours out loud. You're your mother's; you say one word and go. But I'd like you to know I've counted the two of you doing the same sum at the two ends of the same road a day apart, and that in forty years at the end of a corridor I've never once seen that, and that I've decided it's the thing I'll remember when I'm dying, and not the cage."

She stood a moment longer at the foot of the Rostra. The Forum was empty. The sweepers were somewhere behind. Above, on the Palatine, a window was lit on the south side where the old library was, and a man was in it with the door shut and four scrolls, deciding not to know, and she looked at the window, once, and looked away, and the price was where she kept it and had not moved, and she found that she could.

"And?" she said. Out loud. Her mother's. To the Forum. Not telling.

Nothing answered, because it was a question, and the answer was at the end of a cut with a stick in his belt and no rod yet, and she had an hour.

"Walk me," she said to the dwarf.

"I'm walking," said Momus. "You're the one stopped."

* * *

The Curia. The Guard, two of them, at the corner where the Sacred Way went past into the Argiletum and the new road went right toward the cut, and they looked at a tall girl with her hair tied back and a pin and a dwarf and did not stop them, because the Syrian had said *a dwarf will come with the measure* and this was clearly the dwarf and whatever he had with him was clearly the measure; and the new road went up a little, and round, and the cut was there.

She had seen it from the west terrace for a year, every morning, as a square of red with the dust over it and a dark square in the red at the far end. She had stood at its edge once, for the length of a count, at the third hour of a morning the Augusta had chosen because it was the one hour he was not at it, and had said a thing to a Syrian to be carried. She had not seen it like this, with the light coming and nobody in it. From its edge it was not a square. It was a hill gone. A hundred paces across and more than that long and a hundred and twenty feet down to the floor the Syrian had made, with the Quirinal's shoulder standing sheer on the far side where it had been sliced, as raw as a thing cut yesterday though it had been cut a year, because tufa did not heal; and the floor of it bare and swept, because the gangs had not come yesterday and would come today, with the lines of the basilica's footings laid out in string and pegs, and the libraries, and the market's curve going up the Quirinal's side in brick already to its second storey; and at the far end, at the foot of the sheer shoulder, where a column was going to stand a hill's height so that nobody would forget, the pit. Square. Deep. Full of the first grey of the light, which had come over the Esquiline behind her while she walked and was coming down the shoulder now and had not yet reached the bottom of anything.

And at the pit's edge, on the near side, with his back to her, standing, a man.

He was not holding a rod. He had not been given one yet; the hut was dark and Blaesus was asleep in it. He had his hands at his sides and a stick in his belt and the plain tunic and the stripe, and he was tall, and had been since fourteen, and he was standing the way he had sat on a mole at fourteen with the whole of the sea in front of him, which was still, with his weight on both feet, looking at a thing to see what it weighed. He did not turn round. He could not have heard them; the sand on the new road was deep and the dwarf walked quietly and she had been taught by a consul's widow to make no sound on a floor. He did not turn round because he was on a mole, and nobody on a mole turned round, and because a Corinthian had told the wine-shop he had stood at the far end since November and never once seen a litter, and he was not looking for a litter. He was looking at the hole.

Momus stopped at the edge. There was a chest there, the Syrian's, with the rods in it and a plumb-line coiled on the lid, and he climbed onto it, with the short legs, and sat, and put his legs out, and did not look at her.

"There," he said, in the walnut voice, very low. "That's the far end. I've walked you. The Syrian's at the second hour with a course to set and it's the first now, and the light's half down the shoulder. You've an hour. It's more than most get; the Augusta said so about a month once and she was right." He looked at the pit. "I'll count. I'll not hear. There's a difference and I've lived in it forty years."

She stood at the edge of the cut with the hill gone in front of her and the light coming down the sliced shoulder and the man at the far end with his back to her and his hands at his sides, and did the sum, and it came out, and there was a thing left over, and she had known what it was since a mole and had been holding it at the length of her arm for six years because the arm was the only thing on the mole that had been hers, and she looked at it, and her feet were under her, and the floor was there, and it was not paper.

She walked along the edge. Not fast; the way a woman walked who had been taught to make no sound on a floor and had decided, this once, to make some. The sand went under her feet. The light came down the shoulder. The pit filled. And he heard her — she saw him hear her, the way she had seen a face in a lamp hear a stair; the shoulders did a thing and did not turn — and he did not turn round, because he was on a mole, and she came the length of the cut's edge with the ivory in her hair and her hair tied back like a girl with a slate, and stopped, beside him, a pace to his left, where she had sat at twelve with her hand flat on his chest, and did not put her hand on him, and looked at the hole.

They stood side by side and looked at the same thing. The light reached the bottom of the pit.

"It's a hundred and twenty feet," he said, after a while, in the house's shorthand, to the hole, in the voice he had used at eleven in a strong-room to read a sail-maker's bill aloud to show a girl what he could see. "He's going to put a column there exactly that high so nobody forgets there was a hill."

"I know," she said, in the same hand, in the eggs voice, to the hole. "I've watched it from a terrace for a year. I didn't know it was that deep. From up there it's a square."

"Most things are."

"Yes."

Behind them, on the Syrian's chest, a dwarf with his legs out counted, and did not hear, and the dust that had settled in the night on everything in Rome lay on the cut's floor undisturbed, because the gangs had not come yet, and would.
