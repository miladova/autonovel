# The Slate

The first letter came in June by the hand of a woman who had never in her life set foot in the lower town, and the whole of the lane came to its doors to watch her do it.

Vibia Procula walked. That was the thing they would say about it afterwards, at the baker's and the baths: that Hermes's wife, the freeborn one, the one from Nola with the long lip, had come down from the house above the macellum on her own two feet with a single slave behind her and had walked the length of the Dolphin's lane in the ninth hour with her head covered and her eyes in front of her, and had stopped at the door, and knocked, and waited to be let in like anyone. Nobody could remember a freeborn woman of the Augustales' houses knocking at the Dolphin. Nobody could remember one knowing where it was.

Fausta let her in. She had been at the slate and had the stylus still in her hand and she stood in the doorway with it and looked at the woman on the step, and the woman looked back with the pale eyes and said, "You're the girl. I've seen you go through my atrium with an oil-jar for six years and never once looked at your face. I'm looking at it now. Yes. Well." And held out a folded packet with a seal that had been broken and pressed shut again. "This came to my husband inside his own letter from the north, with a note asking him to see it reached you. He read it, as he reads everything, and put it in the box and said nothing. I took it out of the box. He knows I took it. He hasn't said anything about that either. He's not saying anything this year; it's how he's frightened." She put the packet in Fausta's hand. "There'll be others. I'll bring them when I can. I'll not send a slave; a slave can be asked. Is your mother receiving?"

"She's upstairs. She can't do the stairs."

"Then I'll go up." And she did; she went up the Dolphin's stair, which the stair had not had done to it by a woman of her kind in its existence, and the stair creaked in the place it creaked, and Fausta stood in the common room with the packet in her hand and heard, overhead, the door of the room with the window, and the stepmother's even farm voice saying *Hilara. I'm Vibia. We've not spoken. I've come to sit for an hour so the girl can read her letter in peace, and because I've a thing or two to say about the men in both our houses that I'd rather say to someone who already knows them*; and then the door closed.

She read it in the yard, by the shrine.

It was in his square hand on a sheet of army papyrus, the coarse kind, written small to get it all on. It did not begin with her name. It began: *What I saw.* And then it was a list.

*The Danube, from the bank at Viminacium, in April. It is not a river. It is a thing the land does. Half a mile across and brown and moving faster than a horse walks, and on the far side nothing, trees, and above the trees the mountains where they are, and the men stand on the bank and look at it and do not talk. The bridge is boats. Forty boats, lashed, with planks across, and it moves under you, the whole length of it, like the mole in a swell, and the horses will not go on it until they are blindfolded and then they go, and the men go after the horses with their eyes open, which I think is the wrong way round.*

*The emperor, once, at a distance of three hundred paces, on foot, with his head bare, walking the line of the bridge-boats before the first cohort crossed, and stopping at every boat, and putting his hand on it. I asked the quartermaster why. He said: because the men will cross a bridge the emperor has touched. I said: and if it breaks? He said: then it broke under the emperor's hand and not theirs, and that is a different kind of drowning. He is a man called Lucilius who owes my father something from thirty years ago. I gave him the letter. He read it and looked at me and said, your father has a long memory, and gave me a tent to myself, which a tribune's secretary does not get. I have not asked what the thing was.*

*Rufus, on a horse, in a cuirass that cost what the dowry cost, being brave at the men, who like him. I did not expect that. They like him. He talks to them the way he talked to the mule-brothers at the villa, as if they were people he had decided to be gracious to, and it turns out that in a camp that is rare enough to be mistaken for love.*

*The country beyond the river, which is called Banat by the people who were there before, and is flat and then is not, and has farms on it that are empty, with the fires still warm, and dogs that follow the column for a day and then go back. I saw a child's shoe in a doorway. I saw a loom with the cloth half-done. I thought of my mother. Not the dead one. The one at the loom.*

*A centurion of the Seventh called Crescens, a Spaniard, forty, with a nose that has been broken and a voice like a cart on gravel, who came to my tent on the second night across the river and said: you are the boy who found the depot. I said yes. He said: good; the depot is short, as you said, and the legate is going to ask whose name goes on the tablet, and I want it to be the grain-office's and not mine, and you are going to help me make that so. And I did. And he gave me a knife, afterwards, an ordinary one, a soldier's, and said: you will not need it, you are the kind that stays in the tent, but a man who has not got one is a man people notice. I have it in my belt. My father would say it was a denarius. It is heavier.*

*The mountains, on the fifth day, when the trees stopped. I do not have anything to say about the mountains. Nicias would have had Homer for them. I have only that they are there, and that the king is in them somewhere with everything he owns, and that we are going up.*

*That is what I saw. You said you would work out the true part. I have not put the true part in. I do not know what it is yet. I will send the next when there is something.*

*Feed the goddess. — C.*

She read it three times, as she read everything. Then she folded it along his folds and put it inside her gown against her skin, where the Strabo had been until she had found a better place for the Strabo, and she sat on the step by the shrine with her hands between her knees and did not cry, because she had decided on the mole that she would not, and she was a girl who, once a thing was decided, could carry it.

*I thought of my mother. The one at the loom.* She turned that over. She thought that it was the first thing he had ever written down that was not a number, and that he had put it in a list to make it one, and that this was exactly like him, and that it was the true part, and that she would not tell him so.

Upstairs, the door opened, and Vibia Procula came down the Dolphin's stair with her head covered and her face doing what it always did, and stopped at the foot of it, and said to the girl in the yard without raising her voice: "Your mother says the Greek woman's mother is to be sent for on the Kalends, not the Ides, and that she'll not argue about it. I agree with her. And I've said to her what I came to say, and she's said what she had to say back, and we understand each other, and that's enough for two women who've been in the same street for thirteen years and never once been in the same room." She drew her veil forward. "The next letter I'll bring in the evening. The lane talks less after dark."

* * *

Antiope came down from the villa in April with nothing in her hem and something in her belly, and the Greek woman's mother was sent for a month early, and not for Hilara.

She told Fausta on the stair, the first night, with her knees up and her cloak still on, in the voice she used for the price of things. It was one of the winter men. The villa was shut from the Kalends of September to the Ides of April and Thallus left four slaves and a freedman to keep it, and the freedman was a Gaul called Mettius who had the keys and the wine-cellar and nothing else to do for seven months, and he had sent down to the Dolphin in January for company and Hilara had been too ill to go and too ill to say no, and so Antiope had gone up, and had stayed, because it was warm and the villa had food and the Dolphin that winter had had neither in any quantity. "And he was careful," she said. "He was careful every time but one. There's always one. You can be careful for seven years and it's the one that counts." She looked at her hands. "Three months. I felt it at the Ides. I thought: well, there it is, duck, the goddess has a sense of humour after all."

"Does he know?"

"Mettius? He knows. He said he'd speak to Thallus about a place in the villa kitchens when it came and I said I'd rather it didn't come, and he looked relieved and gave me four denarii, which is the going rate for relief." She pulled the cloak tighter. "Don't. I can see you doing the sum. Four denarii is what the Greek woman's mother charges and he knew it to the as; that's what *careful* means, in a man."

The Greek woman's mother came on the Kalends of May. She was a small woman, very old, from Rhodes, with a bag, and she had delivered half the lower town and undelivered a good part of the other half, and she looked at Antiope on the bed in the back room and at the girl standing in the door and said in Greek, "You. Water. Hot, and a lot of it. And cloths, and the strongest wine in the house, and then you'll stay, because I've only two hands and she'll want one to hold and I'll want the other for the work."

So she stayed. She would not afterwards describe it, to anyone, and did not need to, because it had gone into her in the way the warehouse boards had gone in and the Rhodian's bill and the dog's face in the stair, as a thing she had now and could not put down. She would remember the wine, which Antiope drank off in three swallows and asked for more of and was refused. She would remember the old woman's hands, which were brown and spotted and perfectly steady and did not hurry. She would remember the smell of the herb that was burned in the brazier, which was bitter and green and which she would smell again in a palace twenty years on and have to leave the room. She would remember Antiope's hand in hers, which broke two of her fingers' worth of grip and left marks that lasted a week, and Antiope's face, which did not scream, because the lane would hear, but went white and then grey and then a colour she had no name for and made a sound through its teeth like the mud at the burning fields. She would remember the thing itself, in the basin, which she looked at because she had decided to look at everything, and which was nothing, a clot, a handful, a thing you could have thrown to the cats, and which the old woman wrapped in a cloth with great care and took away with her and did not say where. And she would remember the old woman at the door afterwards, with the bag, taking the four denarii and counting them and putting them away and looking up at the girl with eyes like black stones and saying: "You didn't look away. Good. Most do, the first time. You'll want to know how it's done, one day, for yourself or someone, and you've seen it now, and that's the start." And then, lower: "Your mother's the other thing. The hard thing in the side. I'll come for that too, when it's time, and it'll not be this; this is a thing you can do something about. That's a thing you can only sit with. You've a year, maybe. Feed her what she'll eat. Don't let the steward tire her."

She fed her mother what she would eat. She did not let the steward tire her, because Thallus, when he came on the Ides of May and found the woman upstairs grey and the girl in the common room with the slate and Antiope on the stair with her knees up and a look on her like a shutter, sat on the bench with his back to the wall and his hands folded and did not go up. He took the quarter. He took the accounts. He looked at the slate for a long time.

"You've improved your sevens," he said.

"Yes, sir."

"And you've charged the Rhodian for the water."

"Yes, sir. Sixty-eight. He paid it. He said he'd wondered when someone would."

Thallus turned the cup a quarter-turn on the table. He looked at her over it with the pricing look, and she looked back, at no cost, the way the widow had said, and he saw her do it, and she saw him see, and the look went on a moment longer than either of them had planned.

"Your mother," he said, "is not going to recover."

"No, sir."

"You know what the lease says."

"It says the house is hers for life and after that the master's. It says the master will provide for her at his death. It doesn't say what happens if she dies first." She had read it; her mother had got it out of the box under the hearth-stone in March and made her read it aloud twice and then read it again herself with her lips moving. "It doesn't say anything about me."

"No. It doesn't." He drank. "So you understand the position. When she goes, the house reverts. I can put anyone in it. I can put Antiope in it, if she'd take it, and she might; she's thirteen years older than you and she knows the trade. I can put the Greek woman in it. I can shut it and sell the lease to the chandler, who's asked. And you're a freeborn girl of thirteen with a slate and a nose, and no paper, and nowhere." He set the cup down. "I'm not saying this to frighten you. I've never in my life frightened anyone on purpose; it's a waste. I'm saying it because you can read and I'd rather deal with someone who can read. The widow has your mother's letter. I know that; I know what's in the bronze hand before the lady does. The widow hasn't answered. She may. She may not. She's a woman who takes a year to decide whether she wants an egg. And in the meantime there's a quarter due at midsummer and a woman upstairs who can't earn it and a girl downstairs who could."

The common room was very quiet. Antiope, on the stair, had not moved.

Fausta looked at the steward. She looked at the small folded hands and the bald head shining under the lamp and the face that had been set, a long time ago, to show nothing, and she thought of the fishing boat, and the hand on the tunic, and *in every way that matters except one*, and she thought of Antiope saying *like a man who's just realised he's allowed*, and she looked for it in Thallus's face, the lamp being lit, and it was not there. It was not there. He was not looking at her the way the men on the stairs did. He was looking at her the way he looked at the slate.

"You want the quarter," she said.

"I want the quarter."

"Then you'll have it. From the slate. Not from the stairs." She said it in the voice her mother used for the Rhodian's eggs. "I've raised the water and the wine and I've put the Alexandrians in the window room at a denarius and they pay it, because it's the only room in the lower town with a window on the mole and they're homesick. I've let the back room to the Greek woman's cousin by the month, which is less a night and more a quarter. I've stopped the Rhodian's six asses. I've got the Dolphin making what it made when she was well, and I've done it with a slate and a stair I don't go up, and if you'll look at the figures instead of at me you'll see it." She pushed the slate across the table. "The quarter's there. It'll be there at midsummer. And when she goes, if the widow hasn't written, you can put the Greek woman in and I'll go wherever the Greek woman wants a girl who can keep a slate, and you'll have lost nothing, because you never had anything to lose, because I'm not in the lease." She took a breath. "And?"

He did not move. For a moment nothing in his face moved. Then — she would remember this for a long time, and tell nobody, and when at last she did tell someone it was in a palace, to a woman with a comb in her hand, who laughed — the corner of the steward's mouth went up, perhaps the width of a stylus, and came down again, and he reached out and turned the slate round and looked at the figures, all of them, from the top, with his finger going down the column feeling for the place where the hand changed; and he did not find it, because there was not one, and he sat back.

"Sixty-eight," he said. "You charged him the water and lost him the custom of his island."

"I charged him the water and he laughed and said the Dolphin had grown up. He's bringing two more in July. He said so."

"Did he." Thallus folded his hands again. "Then you've found the thing your mother never found, which is that a man will pay for what he's charged if you look at him while you charge it." He stood. "The quarter at midsummer. From the slate. I'll not go up; tell her I asked after her, and that the lease stands while she does, and that I've no plans for the house that I've made yet, which is true, and which she'll know how to weigh." At the door he stopped, with his hand on the bar, and said without turning round: "The widow will write. I've seen the Rhodian's seal in her steward's hand and I've seen that steward keep things. He kept yours. He's an Alexandrian; they don't keep what they mean to throw away. It'll be the spring. They never do anything in the winter." And then, in exactly the same voice: "The boy's letters come through Hermes's house. I know that too. I've read none of them; it's not my house. But Hermes has read all of them, and the second one's been in his box a month, and his wife hasn't yet found the key. You might tell her where he keeps it. It's behind the Lares. It's always behind the Lares, with that sort; they think the gods are a lock."

And he went. And Antiope, on the stair, let out her breath, and said, "Well. Duck. You've either saved us or sold us, and I'll be damned if I can tell which," and Fausta sat down on the bench where he had sat, with her back to the wall and her eyes on the door, and found that her hands were perfectly steady, and that this frightened her more than anything the steward had said.

* * *

The second letter came in July, in the evening, by the same hand, which had found the key.

*What I saw.*

*The pass at Tapae, in the last days of September, which is where Domitian's army was beaten and where the emperor had decided, I think before he left Rome, that this one would not be. I was not in it. I was in the tent with the tablets, two miles back, as my father said I would be, and I heard it, which is not the same, and is not nothing. It is a sound like the fleet-day, if the fleet-day went on for six hours and nobody was glad.*

*The wounded coming back past the tent. I did not know a man could be opened like that and still walk. One of them stopped and asked me for water and I gave it him and he drank it and said thank you, boy, in a Spanish Latin like Crescens's, and sat down against the tent-rope, and was dead when the orderlies came, with the cup in his hand. I took the cup back. I do not know why I am telling you that. It is a cup.*

*The emperor, afterwards, with the staff, in the rain, tearing his cloak into strips for the surgeons because the linen had run out. That is a thing that happened and that every man in the army knows happened and that I am told he has done before, at the Rhine, and that it was no less true for having been done before. Crescens says: the men would cross a bridge he had touched, and now they will die on a hill he has torn his cloak for, and that is what an emperor is, boy, it is a man who has worked out what cloth is worth. I think Crescens and Nicias would have liked each other. I think they would have agreed about everything and fought.*

*Rufus, who was in it, on the left, and whose horse was killed under him, and who came back on foot with his sword out and his face white and said to me in the tent, I did nothing, I stood there, they came at us and I stood there and the men went past me and did it and I stood there with this thing in my hand, and then he sat down and I gave him wine and he drank it and said, write to my father that the Seventh held the left, which is true, and that the tribune was in the line, which is also true, and that is all, and I wrote it. He is asleep as I write this. He has been asleep nine hours. The men say he held the left. They are not lying. He was there. I have decided that this is what holding means.*

*The prisoners, which there are a great many of, and which are put in a pen below the camp, and which I was sent to count, because somebody has to and the quartermaster is a man who uses what he has. I counted them. There are four hundred and six. I did not look at their faces because you do not look at the faces of a thing you are counting; my father taught me that in a warehouse and I did not know until this week what he meant. One of them spoke to me in Greek. He had been a merchant at Sarmizegetusa and traded down the river to the Black Sea and he said, boy, you are a Greek, you have the look, tell them I can be useful, I know the roads. I told Crescens. Crescens said: everyone knows the roads. I have not told the quartermaster. I do not know why. I have the number. The number is the thing I was sent for.*

*The king's men, dead, on the slope, in the morning, which I went up to see because the tent was two miles back and I had decided that I would look at everything. They wear a kind of cap, the nobles, a felt cap, and the rest do not, and you can tell the dead nobles from the dead rest by the caps, which is a thing I did not expect to be able to do and now can. They have a weapon like a scythe. The surgeons hate it. I saw what it does; I will not write what it does. I looked. I did not look away. You would have been proud of me, or Nicias would, or the old woman you wrote about; I do not know who I am being looked at by, up here. I only know I am.*

*That is what I saw. It is more than the last and I do not know what it adds to. You will. — C.*

*The goddess?*

She wrote back. She had not, to the first. She had not known what to put, and had put it off, and had told herself it was the post and the house and her mother; and when the second came she sat down in the yard with the Rhodian's good papyrus and the stylus and wrote, that night, by the shrine, with the lamp:

*What I saw.*

*My mother, in the window room, in June, when Vibia came up the stair and sat with her. I was in the yard. I did not hear what they said. I heard my mother laugh, once, the real one. She has not done it since.*

*Antiope, on the bed in the back room, on the Kalends of May, and the Greek woman's mother's hands. I will not write what they did. I looked. I did not look away. The old woman said that was the start of something. I think it was the start of knowing what a body is for, which is not what the men on the stairs think, and not what the goddess thinks either; it is a thing that can be opened, like your soldier, and go on walking, and ask for water.*

*Thallus, in the common room, on the Ides of May, not going up the stairs. I gave him the quarter from the slate and I said the thing I practised at the burning fields and his mouth moved. The width of a stylus. I have decided that this is what winning means, in this house. It is not much. It is not nothing.*

*The second letter, which was a month in Hermes's box before Vibia found the key, which is behind the Lares, which Thallus told me, which means Thallus has been in your father's house and knows where your father keeps the gods. I do not know what that adds to either. You will.*

*The goddess is fed. The cats have stopped taking it; I put the saucer higher. The flower by it is a thing the Greek woman does; she will not say for whom.*

*You asked who you are being looked at by. I can tell you. It is not me; I am two months behind you and reading the paid number off a sheet. It is you. You have started to be the boat. You said so, in the pen, with the four hundred and six: you did not look at the faces. That is the boat. You will have to decide whether to be it. Nicias was, for fifty-five years, and it was the only thing he had, and it was not enough, and he knew it, and he gave me a book.*

*Count the king's men by their caps if you must. But count the merchant too. He knows the roads. — F.*

She gave it to Vibia to put in Hermes's packet north. Vibia read it, standing in the yard, with her head covered, all of it, as she read everything, and folded it, and said, "You've told him to count a Dacian. His father would say that was the first sentimental thing you've ever done." And then, putting it in her gown: "His father's wrong about a great many things. He's never once been wrong about a number and it's made him think the two are the same. Feed your mother. I'll bring the next."

* * *

There was no next.

Not that winter. The war went up into the mountains and the snow came down out of them and the Danube froze, which it did, Hermes said, in a hard year, so that a man could walk across it, and in January the Dacian king and his Sarmatian horsemen did walk across it, in the dark, into Moesia, where the depots were, and burned what they found; and the news came down the Capua road in February as a list of towns, and one of them was Viminacium, and Hermes went into the strong-room and did not come out for three days, and Vibia did not come to the Dolphin, and Fausta did the slate.

She did the slate. She did the Alexandrians in the window room and the Greek woman's cousin in the back and the Rhodian's two friends, who had come in July as promised and stayed into the autumn and paid the water without being asked. She did the quarter at midsummer and the quarter at the Saturnalia and gave them to Thallus from the slate, and he took them, and did not go up, and did not say *and the rest*, and said each time at the door, with his hand on the bar, *the widow will write; it's the winter*, as if he were reminding himself of a tide. She did her mother. She did the broth and the cloths and the pot and the stair, forty times a day, up and down, until she could have done it blind and sometimes did, in the dark, when the lamp had gone out and her mother called. She did the Greek woman's mother, who came on the Ides of each month with her bag and her black-stone eyes and felt the hard thing in the side and said *not yet*, and then, in February, said nothing, and took no money, and at the door said: *the spring. If the widow writes, go. Don't wait to be let. She'll not know you've gone; by then she'll not know much. I'll see to the rest; I've done it for half this lane and I'll do it for her. She was good to my daughter when the Syrian wouldn't pay.*

And she did not cry, because she had decided; and she did not go to the mole, because she had decided that too; and she did not read the two letters again, because she had them, every word, in the place where she kept things, and reading them would only have been looking at a thing she already owned.

But she went to the yard, on the last night of March, when the house was quiet and her mother was asleep with the breathing that had changed in February and had not changed back, and she stood in front of the goddess with the pink ghost of a face and the saucer she had moved higher and the Greek woman's flower, and she said — aloud; it was the first time she had ever spoken to her aloud — "The house is standing. You did that, or I did, or the slate did. I don't know who to thank and I don't think it matters to you. But she's going, and he's on a river that froze, and the widow hasn't written, and I've got one quarter left in me and then I don't know. So. If you were listening. If you've a sense of humour, the way Antiope says. This would be the time."

The goddess said nothing. She never did; that was how you knew it was her and not a man. But in the morning the Rhodian came up from the harbour at the first hour with his cap in his hand, which he never took off, and stood in the common room, and said: "There's a ship in from Ostia. Private. A lady's. The master says he's carrying a steward, an Alexandrian with a shaved head, and the steward asked on the quay for the lodging-house at the sign of the Dolphin, and when the quay told him, he said *Puteoli?* like a man who'd been saying the word to himself for a year." The Rhodian turned his cap round in his hands. "He's coming up the lane now. I thought you'd want your hair up."

She put her hair up. She put the Strabo in her gown and the two letters inside the Strabo and the slate on its nail, and she went to the door, and drew the bar, and opened it; and Phaedrus was standing in the Dolphin's lane in a grey travelling-cloak with the sulphur-smoke coming down the hill behind him and the whole lane at its doors, and he looked at her — the hair, the gown that would not let down any further, the face doing nothing — and said in the Greek that sounded like water:

"She's decided she wants the egg. It took her a year and a half; it usually does. Can you be ready by the evening tide, or do you need to bury someone first?"

"I need to sit with someone," Fausta said. "Till the evening. Then I can go."

Phaedrus looked at her a moment longer. Then he nodded, once, the way the steward had on the harbour steps four years ago, ticking a thing on a list he had already made, and said: "Then sit. I'll wait at the harbour. I've waited a year and a half; I can manage till the tide," and turned, and went back down the lane with the whole of it watching, and she closed the door, and went up the stair, which creaked in the place it creaked, to the room with the window on the mole.
