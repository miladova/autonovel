# The Low Chair

He asked her to see the cut on the Ides of May, in the library, with the Sappho open between them, and she said what she had been told to say, and he laughed, and it was not the laugh she had been told to expect.

"Apollodorus wants to show you the column," he said, to the scroll. "Not the column; the hole where the column goes. He's dug the footing. It's a hundred and twenty feet of hill measured out in a pit and he's as pleased with it as if it were a child. He says Plotina told him *one day you'll want to show her*, and he's decided the day's tomorrow, because the triumph's in three weeks and after that the whole city will have seen it and he'd like one person to have seen it who'll know what it weighs." The grey eyes did not come up. "I'll walk you down. In the morning, before the gangs. No litter. The Augusta's said yes, which you'll have guessed, because she says yes to things that put you where she can watch."

"Hadrian." The door was shut; the doors were labelled. "What does it cost?"

He looked up. And he laughed — but it was not the short dry surprised laugh of a man who had been told the figure and liked it; it was lower, and went on longer, and had in it a thing she had not heard in him and kept, because she kept everything, and turned over afterwards on the west terrace with Momus on the parapet and could not name until the dwarf named it for her, which was *relief*.

"Plotina told you to say that."

"Yes."

"She told me she had. She tells me what she's told you so I'll not undo it; I said. We're a family that labels its doors." He put the stylus down. "And you've said it, and you've said it in the voice, and I've laughed, as she said I would, and now I'm to not ask again for a while, because a man like me wants the one thing in the room that's not been priced." He leaned back. "She's right. She's always right; it's the most tiresome thing about her and the reason my uncle's emperor. But she's right about the man she's made, and she's not quite right about the man, because she's never once in thirty years asked him what a thing costs *him*, and nobody has, and you've just done it, and that's why I laughed." The face did the thing, and then did not; it simply looked, as it had once across four scrolls. "It costs this. If you walk down to the cut with me in the morning, alone, before the gangs, the hill will have it by noon, and Sabina will have arranged it and will say so, and Plotina will have permitted it and will say nothing, and the wine-shops on the Aventine will have a word for it by evening, and a man who's making boards for the carts with his figure on them a hand high will hear the word from his freedwoman over a slate, because she hears everything, and he'll do nothing, because he's good at it, and it'll go in a box." He held her eyes. "That's what it costs. Not you; him. I'm telling you because you asked, and because I've read the sheet you left him off, and I'll not have you walk down to the cut with me not knowing what the walk's for. It's not for the column. It's for the *seeing*. I had that from Tertulla's son's pin; Sabina told me the whole of it. I'm a man who learns."

She sat in the low chair with her hands in her lap and her feet under her and the cases going up into the grey light and did the sum, and it came out where she had known it would, and she said it.

"Then no. Not before the triumph."

"After?"

"After the triumph the whole city will have seen it. You said. Then it's a hole in the ground and a Syrian and a girl who knows what a thing weighs, and the hill's got nothing to say about it, and nobody hears a word over a slate." She did not look away. "I'll see the column when it's a column. Not when it's a thing to be seen seeing."

The Spaniard looked at her for a long time. Then he picked up the stylus.

"Lucius said a thing about you two on the rock," he said, to the scroll. "I've told you half. Here's the rest. *The one thing neither of them learned is how to be had — and the one thing neither of them will ever learn is how to be had by each other, because they were raised on the same mole, and a mole's a thing you stand on side by side and look out from, and nobody on a mole ever turns round.*" He turned a column. "He's dying, and says true things because they're cheaper. Go and do Sabina's hair. Tell her I asked and you said no and she'll be pleased with you and furious with me, which is the arrangement, and which she arranged."

* * *

Sabina was not pleased.

She was on the floor with the slate and she looked up when the girl came in and read the face that did nothing and said, "He asked. You said no. Good." And then, with the slate, not looking: "What did he say when you said no?"

"That Sura said we were raised on a mole, domina. The boy and I. And that nobody on a mole turns round."

The slate stopped. Sabina sat with it on her knees and the beautiful disappointed face turned to the window, and said, after a while, in the low quick voice: "That's the cleverest thing Lucius has ever said and he said it about you and not about me, and I've been his friend's wife for seven years and he's never once said a clever thing about me, because there isn't one; I'm a stool by a window." She put the slate down. "I'll tell you what I am, Puteoli, since you've told me what you are. I'm the price of him. My great-aunt said so, in that room, to your face; Marcia told me; I made her. *I made her the price of him.* That's what I've been since I was seventeen. A thing he had to take to have the other thing, and knows it, and I know he knows it, and we've been very civil about it for seven years in three cold rooms." She looked at her hands. "And now there's a girl in my rooms who does my hair the new way and teaches me a book and says *what does it cost* to him in a library and he laughs, and the laugh's a thing I've never had out of him, and I arranged it, and I'd arrange it again, and I'd like — once — to hate you for it, and I can't, because you came in and told me the mole." The dry tenant intelligence came up out of the beautiful face and looked at her. "Sit down. Here. On the floor. I'm going to say a thing I've not said to anyone and you're going to keep it in your book and not in your mouth."

Fausta sat.

"I'll never have a child. I've seen to it. There are women on the Subura who know how and I've been to one, twice, since the marriage, and I'll go again if I have to, and Plotina knows, because she knows everything, and has said nothing, because a child of his would be a thing she'd have to plan round and she'd rather not. So there it is. I'm the price of him and I've made sure the price has no change." Sabina's hands lay still on the slate. "I'm telling you because in a year, or five, when he looks at you the way he looks at a thing he wants, and he will, and Plotina says you'll say *what does it cost* and he'll not ask again, she's wrong about the second part. He'll ask again. He's patient; he had it from her. And when he does, and you're deciding, I'd like you to have one true figure in the column that nobody else on this hill will give you, which is: *his wife will not hate you. His wife has nothing to lose that she hasn't already thrown in a Subura basin.* Put it in the book. It's the only gift I've got that's worth more than the boards it's bound in."

Fausta sat on the cold floor. The slate was between them with a Rhodian's week on it that did not come out.

"Vibia," she said. It was the first time.

"Don't. I'll start to like it." But the beautiful face did a thing, and it was not the nearly-smile; it was the other thing, the one the Augusta's face did that was nearly tiredness, and Fausta understood, sitting on the floor, that it ran in the family like the long bones. "Make me a house. No. Not a house. Tell me the mole. The two of you on it. Counting ships. I'd like to hear what it's like to stand beside someone and look out and not turn round. I've never done it. I've spent seven years turned round."

So she told her the mole. Not for the book; for the floor. And Sabina listened without waiting to speak, and when it was done — at the hull going to ash, at the arm held to its length — the Spaniard's wife said nothing for a long time and then said, to the glass: "*Not for nothing.* He said that. At thirteen. On a mole, to a girl with a slate." A breath. "My husband has never once in his life said a thing to me that wasn't for something. Go and do my hair. The new way. He's coming in from the cut with the Syrian's hole in his head and I want him to not know me, and then to know me, and then to look past me at the corner, and I want to watch him do it, because it's the only time in seven years I've seen him want a thing he couldn't have, and I'd like to see it again before the triumph, when the whole city will be watching him want a thing he's got."

* * *

Matidia spoke to her on the Ides, which she had not done in four years, and it was in the Augusta's room, after, with the cloth on and the benches gone, and the Spaniard's mother-in-law closed her book.

She was forty-four. She had been widowed young and had not remarried, which on that hill was a decision and not a misfortune, and she had the long bones she had given her daughter and a face that had been handsome and had chosen, at some point, to be plain instead, as a woman on a coast built on ash had chosen her hair. She closed the book on her finger, to keep the place, and looked at the girl by the wall with the box of pins, and said, in a Latin that had no province in it at all: "You've taught my daughter a lodging-house book."

"Yes, domina."

"She showed me. On the floor. She made me sit on the floor, which I've not done since I was six, and she did a Rhodian's week and a sail-maker's canvas and it came out, and she looked at me the way she looked at me when she was four and had built a thing out of blocks that stood up." The page under the finger did not move. "I've never seen her look at anything that way since she was four. I've given her everything there is on this hill and none of it made her look like that, and a freedwoman's child from Puteoli has done it with a slate and a man who drinks." Matidia did not smile. She was not a woman who did; it was the other thing she had given her daughter. "I'm not thanking you. The Augusta says you've the wrong word for it anyway. I'm telling you I've seen it, and that I've been turning the same page of the same book in this room every Kalends for twenty years so that I'd have somewhere to look that wasn't my daughter's face, and that on the Ides I looked at it, and it had a thing in it I'd given up on."

Under the cloth, the Augusta said: "Matidia. You're performing."

"I'm not. You know I'm not; it's why you let me in the room." Matidia did not look at the couch. "She told you about the basin."

A silence under the cloth.

"Yes. She told me about the basin. She told me because she was told by a dwarf that I'd keep it in a book and not a mouth, and she was right, and I'm telling *you* I know she told you, so that you'll not think it's a thing you have over me." The long handsome plain face held the girl's. "I knew. I've known since the first time. I'm her mother. I went to the Subura after her, both times, and paid the woman again, more, to be careful, and never said. The Augusta knows I know; it's the one thing between us we've never spoken of in a room, because it's the one thing on this hill that's not a move." Matidia opened the book. "That's all. You've one true figure from Sabina. I've given you the figure under it. Put them both in your book and never say either in this room, and when my daughter's the emperor's wife and has nothing and knows it, be in her rooms, because I'll be dead, and the Augusta will be dead, and the Spaniard will be in Athens or Antioch or wherever a man like that goes to not be in a room with his wife, and she'll need one person on the hill who sat on the floor with her and made a thing come out."

"Yes, domina."

"Matidia. When the door's shut. We label them." She found her page. "Go. She's counting."

The benches had a new face on them on the Kalends of June, and it was the one face on the hill that Fausta had never expected and should have, and it looked at her, and knew her, and said nothing, and that was the first thing she kept of it.

Laecania Paulla. Bassus's daughter, who had married a Spaniard from Italica the year a girl of five had carried a slate up to the house with columns and been told to say nothing; who had been nineteen then, and plain, and kind in the way daughters of consuls were kind to freedwomen's children, and had gone away to Baetica with a man who had land and olives, and had never, in Fausta's knowledge, been back. She was thirty-four now, on the fourth bench from the door, in a good gown that was five years behind the hill, with a face that was her father's without the weight and her mother's without the sweetness; and she had come to the Augusta's room because her husband was in Rome for the triumph, because every Spaniard with land was, because the emperor was a Spaniard and the Spaniard's nephew was going to be, and a woman from Italica with a consul's name went to the Augusta's room on the first Kalends she could to be counted; and she sat on the fourth bench and looked along the long wall at the tall girl at the Augusta's shoulder with a comb and an ivory pin, and her face did the thing a face did when it had been given a figure it had not known was in the column.

She did not speak. The room did not permit it. But when the hair was done and the door opened and Marcia said *the Augusta thanks you and is engaged*, and the benches stood, Paulla did not go with them; she stood by the fourth bench with her hands folded and waited until the room had emptied to the two of them and the Germans at the door, and said, in the Latin of Baetica now, which had the coast under it still: "Hilara's girl."

"Yes, domina."

"My father's freedwoman's child. From the lodging-house. You carried a slate up to the house when you were five and stood in the atrium and my father said *that's Hilara's* and you didn't look at the floor." Paulla's hands did not move. "My brother wrote me you'd gone to the Caelian. He wrote that Tertulla had taken you for a price. He didn't write what the price was and I didn't ask; I'd a house in Italica and olives and three sons and I thought: well, Hilara's girl's gone up, good, Hilara was the only one in that house who ever told me a true thing about my hair." She looked at the pin. "And now you're the Augusta's, and doing her hair the way her mother had it, and the whole of Baetica knows it, because the whole of Baetica's in Rome this month and talks of nothing else, and I've sat on a bench for an hour watching you not look at the floor and thought: *that's my father's house. That's the last of it. A freedwoman's child at the Augusta's shoulder with a pin.*" The plain face did a thing. "My brother's in the Senate and sits on a stool in a freedman's strong-room. My father's tomb's by the road with his debts on it. The Falernian land's my husband's and the olives are good. And the house of Laecanius is a girl with a comb on the Palatine and a boy with a ring behind a cart of gold, and neither of them's ours, and both of them have the name, and I came to this room to be counted and I've been, and I'll go back to Italica and tell my sons that the house went up after all; it just went up without us."

She turned to go. At the door, where people turned, she turned.

"Fausta. He asked about you. My father. At the end; my brother wrote it. He couldn't speak much, by then, and what he said was *Hilara's girl, is she placed*, and my brother said yes, the Caelian, and he said *good*, and then he said a thing my brother didn't understand and wrote to me because he thought I might. He said: *the nose was always going to go somewhere.*" Paulla's face did nothing. "I didn't understand it either. I think you do. I'd rather not know."

She went. Fausta stood by the wall with the box, and under the cloth, on the couch, the Augusta said, to the ceiling, in the voice for metre: "That was the consul's daughter."

"Yes, Augusta."

"And she's just told you, in my room, with the Germans listening, what the consul said about your nose. Which I've known since the day you walked in with Tertulla, and Tertulla knew before that, and Marcia worked out in a morning, and the whole hill has decided not to know, because it's the kind of thing a hill decides." The cloth did not move. "It changes nothing. You were born free, with witnesses; it's in the prefect's archive under my seal. But you'll put it in your book, because it's a figure, and you'll never say it in this room, because in this room it would be a *thing*, and I've enough things. Go and do Sabina's hair. Tell her the consul's daughter was on the fourth bench. She'll want to know what Baetica's wearing."

* * *

Momus had the baker's wife's message on the Kalends, and brought it to the terrace, and it was from a woman neither of them had met.

"The Corinthian. Chrestê. Laecania Chrestê, as she is now; she signs it, the baker's wife says, on the shopkeepers' slates she's begun doing for a fee, which is how a freedwoman two streets from a wine-shop talks to a dwarf on the Palatine: by way of a loaf." He climbed onto the parapet. "She sends: *tell the girl with the shutter that I settled Granius's factor's slate with her once a month for a year when she was fourteen and she took the lower figure every time and looked at me, and I've never forgotten it, and I've told him so. Tell her the boxes are under the gods and I said "altar" to the wine-shop on purpose. Tell her he goes to the far end of the cut every morning from the first hour to the second and has since November. Tell her I'm free, at a wage, in my own room with a door he's not been through, and that I liked it more than I planned and I'm telling her that because she'd rather have the whole figure than the clean one. And tell her I'd not be the woman on the stair, whichever way it goes, and that I've not decided yet what that means for me but I've decided it for her.*" The dwarf looked at the cut. "That's all. She's a Corinthian who kept a merchant's books. She counts the way we do. I'd have liked to meet her and I'll not; people at the ends of corridors don't; we send loaves."

Fausta stood with her hands on the parapet.

"What do I send back?"

"Nothing. You don't send a Corinthian a figure; she's got them all. You send her a thing she hasn't." He thought. "Send: *the egg-woman's husband was paid by his hand and I never asked him for anything else, and that's the only thing I ever asked him for myself, and she can have that, because she's told me the only thing she had.*" He looked at the girl. "That's not a figure. That's a receipt. She'll know the difference. She keeps them."

She sent it. By the baker's wife, by the wine-shop, by the steward's boy, by a loaf; and it came back, four days later, by the same road, one line, in a merchant's Greek on the edge of a slate the baker's wife had been asked to show and not give: *received, and kept, and he'll never know you sent it, because I've decided that too. — L.C.*

* * *

Marcia told her about the box on the day before the Nones, at the shoulder, with the comb, in the Nemausus Latin — an extra morning, not a Kalends or an Ides, because the hair was to be seen by a hundred thousand people the next day and the Augusta wanted it tried — and it was not owed, and Marcia gave it anyway, and Fausta's hands did not stop.

"Philon's given Hermes's son a box. The archivist; Sura's father's man; the one with the cough. Out of the third room, where nothing comes out. The steward's boy had it from Philon's boy, who carried it down the Aventine and says it was light and the young master carried it like it wasn't." The fingers moved a pin. "It's your letters. A year of them. The lady Cornelia's requests with your lines at the foot. Sura had them kept separate; he said the lines were worth more than the asking. Philon gave the box to the boy because Philon's dying too and wanted to give one thing away before the Spaniard's lists, and he said the boy would know what it weighed." The comb went into the box. "It's on a shelf on the Aventine under the Lares from Nola, beside his father's, the freedwoman says. She says it's an altar. She says it to the wine-shop, which is how I have it, and she says it on purpose, because she's a Corinthian who kept a merchant's books and knows that a thing said in a wine-shop goes up the hill by evening, and she wanted it to."

Under the cloth, the Augusta said nothing. Matidia's page did not turn.

"That's all," said Marcia. "The fifth door. He's put your hand on a shelf under his gods. I've told you. I'd not wait."

Fausta put the last pin in. The Augusta's hair was fine and grey and plain, her mother's, and done; and the woman on the couch turned her head, once, to the left and the right, and said to the ceiling, "That's it," and then, after a moment, in the voice that was not for metre and that Fausta had heard perhaps four times: "Girl. The triumph's tomorrow. You'll be in the box with Sabina, on her left, standing, with the pin. He'll be in the procession behind the gold on foot with a centurion's stick, which the emperor's allowed because he was told why and said *let him*. I said I'd be watching your faces. I will. And I'll tell you now what I'll be looking for, because I'd rather you knew than guessed, and because you've asked me the price of everything I've given you and I've told you, and this is the price of the box." The cloth did not move. "I'll be looking to see whether either of you turns round."

She went down the long wall behind the comb. Across the courts. Past the fountain that worked. And not to the north rooms; to the west terrace, where nobody went, where the city was, and the cut, and the dust standing over it; and Momus was there already, on the parapet, with his legs out, because he had been told by Sabina that the girl would come, and had come first, and did not say so.

She stood with her hands on the parapet. Below, in the saddle, the Syrian's pit was a dark square in the red, where a column would go that was a hill's height, so nobody would forget.

"He's put the letters under his gods," she said.

"I heard. The whole hill's heard; the Corinthian saw to it." Momus looked at the cut. "She's clever, that one. She's told the hill a thing about him he'd never tell, so that he'll not have to, and so that you'll know it without his having said it, which is the only way a man like that can say anything. She's done you a kindness and him a worse one and she knows it and she'd do it again. I'd like to meet her. I've a feeling we'd count the same."

"What do I do?"

"Nothing, before tomorrow. The Augusta's said what she's looking for and she's never once said what she's looking for in forty years, Marcia says, and that means she wants it seen, which means she wants it *not done*. If either of you turns round in front of a hundred thousand people and the Senate and her, it's a thing on the hill, and a thing on the hill is hers. So you'll stand in the box on Sabina's left with the pin and look at the gold and he'll walk behind it with the stick and look at the boards, and neither of you will turn round, and she'll have seen that, and it'll go in her box with the other two sentences." The walnut face turned up. "And then, girl, after. After the Nones. When the city's seen the column's hole and the gold's in the treasury and the Spaniard's in his library and Sura's on his couch at the window and the dust's settled on the cut. Then there's a Syrian who's been told *one day*, and a hole in the ground that's a hill's height, and nobody on the hill with anything to say about a girl who goes to look at it, and a man who's made the figure for it, in a hut, with a deaf clerk, and goes there every morning before the gangs to stand at the far end by the shoulder, the Corinthian says, and look, and has done since November, and has never once seen a litter, and goes anyway." He looked back at the cut. "That's not mine to arrange. I'm a dwarf on a parapet. But I count people in and out and I've counted that one in at the far end of the cut every morning for seven months through a wine-shop's gossip, and I'd not have you go to your book tonight without the figure."

She stood a long time. The dust hung. Somewhere under it, a man was checking a board.

"Momus."

"Girl."

"My mother said *and?* And then she died. And my whole life since I've thought it meant: *that's your figure; where's mine.* Thallus said so. I believed him; he'd known her longer." She did not look away from the cut. "It doesn't. I've been doing a lodging-house book on a floor with a woman who's thrown her children in a basin and I've been saying to her *it comes out, there's nothing left over*, and I heard myself, this morning, and I thought: that's not what she meant. My mother never once in her life had a book come out. She had a thing left over every month and the thing was me, and she carried it, and she sent it up a hill, and she said *and?* to the egg-woman's mother with her last breath because she wanted to know what was *next*. Not what was owed. What was next." Her hands were on the parapet. "She was asking. Not telling. She'd done her sum and there was a thing left over and she wanted to know what it would do."

Momus was quiet on the parapet.

"Then that's the first line in your book," he said at last. "Not the mole. That. Go and write it. Sabina's counting, but she'll let you have the hour; she told me so; she said *the girl's got a face on her like a slate that's about to come out and I'd rather it did it in her book than in my rooms*."

She went in. The north rooms were empty; Sabina had gone to the Augusta's to be shown what she would wear in the box, and to dislike it. The book was where she kept it, in the chest, under the Strabo, with the letters and the brass fish and the prefect's copy under the Augusta's seal; and she took it out and sat on the cold floor with it on her knees and the ink from Sabina's table and the first leaf blank, as it had been for three weeks because she had not been able to decide; and she wrote, in the Latin a dead woman had taught her, in the small hard hand that had kept a lodging-house book at ten, the first line, which was not the mole:

*My mother said "and?" and died. I have been nine years working out what she meant. I think she was asking.*

And under it, after a while, because she was her mother's and there was a thing to be said and it was true:

*He has put my letters on a shelf under his gods. His freedwoman told the wine-shop on purpose so that I would know without his saying. The Augusta will be watching on the Nones to see if either of us turns round. We will not. We were raised on a mole. But after the Nones there is a hole in the ground a hill's height and a Syrian who has been told "one day," and a man who goes to the far end of it every morning before the gangs and has never seen a litter. I am writing this down so that I will not be able to say, later, that I did not know the figure.*

She closed the book. The boards were oak and heavy and it had been a gift and not a price. She put it under the Strabo and went to do Sabina's hair, because the Spaniard's wife would be back from the Augusta's with the look she had when she had been dressed by someone else, and would want to not be known for a moment, and then to be, and then to be looked past; and that was the hill; and Fausta had decided on a terrace by a sea that she would stop pretending about what was true, only about what it cost, and the cost was a man walking behind gold on the Nones with a stick, and her not turning round, and she could pay it; she had paid worse; she had the receipts.
