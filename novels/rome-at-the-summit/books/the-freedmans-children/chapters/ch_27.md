# The Box

She did two heads before light, and the second was the Augusta's, and the Augusta spoke while she did it, which she did not do.

The north rooms first. Sabina at the glass in the dark with one lamp, in the shift, with the long bones showing and the beautiful disappointed face already arranged for a day it had decided to dislike, saying, "The new way. No. The *newest*. The thing the legate's wife had on the Ides that nobody's dared copy. I want to sit beside my great-aunt in my great-uncle's box with my hair done like a woman from the provinces who's trying too hard, and I want her to see it and say nothing, because today she can't put the cloth on." And then, as the comb went in: "He came in last night. Late. From the Campus. He stood in the door and looked at me in the glass and said *tomorrow, don't look at the box; Plotina's watching for a thing and it's not us*, and went to bed. In seven years he's never once told me where not to look. I've decided to be pleased." The hands went where they went. Sabina's hair was heavy and dark and took a thing well, and Fausta did the thing, the newest way, with the pins the Spaniard's wife had chosen and the Augusta would dislike, and when it was done Sabina turned her head to the left and the right in the glass and said, in the low quick voice, "There. I look like someone who wants something. Good. Go and do hers. She'll have been awake since the third hour; she's awake every triumph, Matidia says, she sits up and does the sum of the thing again to see if it still comes out."

The long wall was dark. The benches were empty; there was no receiving on the day of a triumph, there was a woman in a room with one window getting dressed for a chair. Marcia let her in with the comb and did not say *the old way*, because it was understood, and the Augusta sat on the couch's edge in the gown that had been chosen by the emperor's freedmen and was purple and heavy and that she wore, Fausta saw, exactly as a woman wore a thing that was a uniform and not a choice; and the fine grey hair was down, and the plain face in the one lamp had been awake since the third hour and showed it, which it did not do in daylight.

"The old way," the Augusta said, to the wall. "My mother's." The hands began. The Augusta was quiet for a time and then said, in the voice that was not for metre, "Lucius will ride. The Greek's forbidden it and he's forbidden the Greek. He'll not fall; he's never done a thing badly that he was watched for. Then he'll go home to his couch and die by the autumn." A breath. "He sent for the boy last night. The third room. Marcia had it from Philon's boy. And at the first hour this morning the boy was at the far end of the cut, before the carts, before anything, standing. The Guard at the Curia let him by; he had the stripe and a centurion's stick and they took him for the Syrian's." The hair was half done. "On the morning *of*. Not after."

Fausta's hands did not stop. The pins went in.

"You know why I'm saying this."

"So that I'll know it before the box, Augusta. Not after."

"Yes. Because after the box you'll have not turned round, and you'll have paid, and I'd not have you pay for a thing without the whole of the figure, because that's what a hill does to a girl and I decided four years ago that I'd rather do it to you myself, on purpose, with the figure in your hand, than have it done to you by Tertulla's son's pin." The plain face in the lamp did nothing. "That's the last thing I'll give you before the triumph. He stood at the far end of the cut this morning in the dark and said something out loud to the hole. The Guard didn't hear it; it was in a hand they couldn't read. Momus says you'll know what it was without hearing it and I've decided to believe a dwarf, which is new."

The last pin went in. The Augusta turned her head once to the left and the right and said, "That's it. That's my mother's," and stood, in the purple, heavy, a woman in a uniform, and Marcia came with the veil, and the Augusta said, from under it, as the comb went into the box: "Girl. In the box, you'll stand at Sabina's left shoulder. You'll be able to see the road and the Capitol and the cut's mouth and the twenty-second cart when it comes, because the Syrian's loaded the forty-first bar on it and the boy walks behind the board he's answerable for. I've arranged that. I arrange everything. It's what the headache's for." The veil settled. "Don't look. I'll be watching. And I'll tell you tonight what I saw, because I've never once lied to you about a figure, and I'm not going to begin on the day my sum comes out."

* * *

The box was timber and hangings and stood out over the Sacred Way on posts at the turn below the Palatine where the road came out of the cut, and from it, if you were standing, you could see the whole of the city that mattered, which was the part that was looking back.

She stood. Sabina sat, on the left of the Augusta, who sat on the left of the empty chair that was the emperor's and would be empty all day because the emperor was in the road; and Matidia sat on the Augusta's right with no book, for once, with her hands in her lap and her long face turned to the Capitol; and behind Sabina's left shoulder, a pace back, where a woman stood who was somebody's and not furniture, Fausta stood, with the ivory in her hair at the side where the June light would catch the gold on the break, and the box of pins left in the north rooms because today there was nothing to pin; the hair was done, both heads, the old way and the new, and would hold till dark.

It was the fourth hour. The procession had been forming since before light on the Campus and had come in through the Triumphal Gate at the second, and the sound of it had been coming up the Sacred Way toward the box for an hour the way the sea came up a beach: not a thing you heard arrive but a thing you found you had been hearing. The crowd was the sound. A hundred thousand, the Augusta had said, and she had not been exaggerating, which she never did; they were on the stands and the steps and the roofs and the Capitol's slope and in the windows of every house with a window on the road and on the scaffolding of the Syrian's basilica where the Guard had tried to stop them and given up, and they were making a noise that was not cheering yet, because there was nothing yet to cheer, and was the noise of that many people waiting, which she had never heard and would not forget.

"You've not looked at the road," said Sabina, not turning her head, in the low quick voice that the sound ate at a pace.

"No, domina."

"Good. Don't. Not till the gold. Then look at the gold and nothing else, and when the boards come, read them, because you'll be the only person in this box who can, and I'll want to know afterwards if a nine was wrong." The beautiful face was turned to the Capitol, with the hair done the new way under a veil the Augusta had chosen and Sabina disliked. "She's watching you already. Don't look at her either. Look at the Capitol. It's gold and it's been there four hundred years and it's the one thing on this road today that nobody's going to have to pay for."

She looked at the Capitol. Beside her, a pace forward and to the right, the Augusta sat with no cloth, because today was not a receiving day, today was the one day in twenty years when Pompeia Plotina was simply a woman in a chair at her husband's triumph, and the plain face showed what it decided to, and what it had decided to show was nothing, perfectly, and Fausta, who had done that face's hair for four years, could see from behind and to the left the one thing it could not decide, which was that the eyes were moving.

* * *

The Senate came first. That was the order; it had been the order since there were triumphs, the magistrates and the Senate walking ahead of the thing they had voted so that the city could see who had voted it; and they came up the Sacred Way out of the Forum in their hundreds in the white and the stripe, slowly, with the lictors, and the crowd did not cheer them and did not jeer them, it let them pass, which was what the crowd did with the Senate; and she did not look, and then did, once, because Sabina had said *the Capitol* and the Senate was between her and the Capitol, and saw, in the third rank, a man with a consul's nose and none of his weight, walking with his hands in his toga, looking at nothing, who was Rufus, who had sat on a stool in a strong-room, and who had, Marcia said, moved a motion on a hull and had it carried because it was the week of the Rhine; and she looked away from him to the Capitol and found that she had felt nothing, which she kept, because it was a figure.

Then the spoils. The carts began, and the sound changed, because this was what the sound had been waiting for: the first cart came out of the Forum under the Capitol's slope piled with the arms, the Dacian curved swords and the long shields and the dragon-standards with their cloth bodies that the wind had filled on the far bank and that hung now limp from their poles in the June air, and the crowd made a noise that was the first noise of the day and it went along the road ahead of the cart like a wave going up a mole, and she felt it through the timber under her feet. More carts. The pictures — the great painted boards on frames, each one a thing from the war, the bridge, the city on its mountain burning, a man in a gully with a knife at his own throat, each one carried past the box and tilted toward it so that the women in it could see what the emperor had done; and Matidia looked at every one, and the Augusta looked at none, and Sabina said, to the Capitol, "That's the bridge. Apollodorus painted it himself; he'd not let anyone else. Twenty piers. He said a boy counted them." And then the prisoners, in chains, in their hundreds, the Dacian nobles with the caps and the long hair walking as men walked who had been told what the road ended in and had decided to walk it as if it did not; and she did not look at them long, because she had been eleven in a lodging-house with the Rhodian on the stair and knew what a face looked like that was doing the sum *live*, and these faces had done it and the sum had not come out.

And then the gold.

It came round the turn out of the Forum and the sound stopped. That was what she kept: that a hundred thousand people stopped, for the length of a breath, the way the mole had stood up for one ship in February, because the first cart came into the light below the Capitol and it was not a cart, it was a thing on wheels that the light could not get past. Bar on bar on bar, stacked, lashed, the lumps and ingots and flattened cups and cut arm-rings of a kingdom's twenty years, gone to blocks in a king's furnace so they would pack and sunk under a river and dug up by a Syrian and weighed by a boy on a rock, and they were not polished and did not need to be; they were gold, in the sun, in the June, in a quantity that the eye did not have a figure for; and beside the cart, carried on a pole by a Guardsman, a board, pine, whitewashed, a man's height, with black letters a hand high that said what the cart weighed.

She read it. She was the only one in the box who could, and she read it, and it was right, and the second cart came behind it with its board and she read that, and the third; and the sound came back, all at once, as it had gone, and it was not a cheer, it was a roar, the roar of a city that had been told a figure and had not believed it and was being shown; and it went up the road and over the box and up the Palatine behind and she felt the timber move under her feet; and she stood at Sabina's shoulder and read the boards, one after another, as the carts came, and did not look at the road behind the carts, because she had been told what the Augusta was looking for and because she had decided, on a parapet, with a dwarf, that she could pay it.

Forty carts. She read forty boards. On the twenty-second she stopped reading for the length of a flame leaning, because the board was right and the figure on it was one she knew — *twenty-two pounds, four ounces, bar, forty-first* had been one line on a tablet on a rock, Marcia had said, with the Spaniard crouching to read it upside down, and the cart that carried the forty-first bar was the twenty-second cart because that was how the Syrian had loaded them, and behind the twenty-second cart, on foot, a pace back from its tailboard, where the man walked who was answerable for what was on the board, in the narrow stripe and the plain tunic with his lips moving, with a centurion's vine-stick in his belt and his hands at his sides and his face turned to the board and not to the box, a tall man with a nose, walking.

She did not turn round. She was on a mole. She read the twenty-third board.

Beside her, a pace forward and to the right, the Augusta's eyes had stopped moving.

"There," said Sabina, very low, to the Capitol, with the roar eating it. "That's him. With the stick. Reading. He's not looked up once since the Forum; Hadrian's man on the roof says so, I had him put there." The beautiful face did not move. "You've not turned round. He's not turned round. She's seen it. It's done. You can look at the gold now like everyone else, Puteoli; you've paid."

She looked at the gold. It went by. Forty carts. And behind the last of them, on a horse, in the purple-bordered toga of a man who had been consul twice, upright, with a face gone to bone and a cloth in his left hand that he did not raise, Lucius Licinius Sura, riding with a cough down the Sacred Way behind the man he had made in front of the Senate, which was where he had done everything else; and the crowd knew him, and the roar changed shape for him, and he did not look at the box either, and she understood, watching the knife-face go by below her, that he had told the boy where the far end of the cut was, because that was the kind of thing a dying man did with his last useful breath, and that the boy had gone there anyway, this morning, before the carts, because he was good at doing nothing and had decided, once, to do it in the wrong place on purpose.

And then the emperor. The four white horses, and the chariot, and the man in it with the painted face and the laurel and the slave behind him holding the crown over his head and saying the thing the slave said, *remember you are a man*, which nobody could hear and everybody knew; and the roar went past being a sound and became a thing you were inside; and the Augusta, in her chair, with no cloth, looked at her husband going by below her in a chariot behind a king's gold with the Senate ahead and the legions behind and the whole of the city that mattered on its feet, and her face showed what it had decided to, which was nothing, and the eyes did a thing that Fausta saw from behind and to the left and had never seen them do and did not have a word for, and decided, that night, in the book, was *arithmetic*: a woman looking at a sum she had written in eleven letters from Cologne, come out.

Behind the chariot, on a horse, in a legate's cloak, with the grey eyes on the road and the face doing nothing, the man she had chosen. He did not look at the box either. He had been told what the Augusta was looking for and he was a man who learned.

* * *

The legions came last, and she had not expected the legions to be the thing, and they were.

Not all of them; four, with their eagles uncased now and the standards and the horns, in cohorts, in step, in the sound that forty thousand men made walking on sand that had had a king's gold go over it, which was not a sound so much as a thing the ground did; and the crowd, which had roared for the gold and gone quiet for the emperor and roared again, did neither for the legions; it *stood*, the whole of it, the stands and the roofs and the Capitol's slope, as a town stood for one ship, and let them pass. The First Minervia, which was the Spaniard's, and he turned his horse at the head of it and rode back along its first cohort for the length of the box and did not look up. The Fourth Flavia. The Thirteenth. And the Seventh Claudia, which had wintered on the far bank both winters and gone up the ladders at the second wall, and which came up the Sacred Way with a gap in its second cohort where a century should have been and was not, because the Seventh did not fill a century that had lost its centurion at a wall until it was home, and it was not home; and at the back of the Seventh, behind the last cohort, walking, not in step, because they could not, the ones who had come back with less than they went with: the one-armed, the men with a stick instead of a leg, the man with half a face who walked with his good side to the box; and the crowd stood for them as it had not stood for the gold.

"Look at that," said Sabina, to the Capitol, in the low quick voice, and for once the voice had a thing in it that was not dry. "My great-uncle's put them at the back on purpose. He said at dinner, *the Senate's seen the figure; now they'll see what it cost, and they'll see it last, so it's what they go home with.* Plotina said it was a story and he said *yes, Pompeia, a man's allowed one.*" A pause. "There's a gap in the Seventh. Second cohort. That's a century with no centurion. Hadrian says the boy with the stick was at that wall when it happened and was sick in the lee of the first wall after and wrote to his father that night *it was quick*, and Lucius told Hadrian, and Hadrian told me because he tells me what'll hurt." The beautiful face did not move. "I'm telling you because you've read forty boards and you'll want the figure for the stick, and that's it. A hand-stone. A man who carried him over the Rhine. It's in your column now. Don't look at the gap."

She did not look at the gap. She looked at the Capitol. The Seventh went by with its century missing and its broken men at the back, and the crowd stood, and the sound the ground made went up the Sacred Way and over the box and up the Palatine behind and she felt it in the timber under her feet, and under that, where she kept the things that were not figures, she felt a thing she had not felt since a shed on a mole with the sea in a glass, which was that a sum could be right and still be the wrong thing to have written, and that the people who knew that were walking at the back of a legion not in step, and that one of them had been carried over a river by a man whose stick was in a boy's belt behind the twenty-second cart, and that none of it would be on a board.

* * *

It was the ninth hour before the last cohort had gone up to the Capitol and the box could be left, and the Augusta did not leave it.

The others went. Matidia first, with a look at her daughter and no word, up the covered stair to the Palatine's postern. Sabina, standing, shaking out the gown she disliked, with the veil in her hand, saying to the girl behind her shoulder, in the low quick voice, "Come up. No. Stay. She wants you; I can see it in her back. I'll do my own hair tonight; I've watched you long enough to get it wrong on purpose." And gone, with the Germans. And then the box was the Augusta in her chair and the girl standing, and the road below with the sand churned and the crowd going home over it in its hundred thousands making the noise a city made after, which was lower, and tired, and had wine in it, and would go on till morning.

"Sit," said the Augusta. "Sabina's chair. You've stood nine hours. I had it from a Guardsman that you read forty boards and never once looked past the tailboard of the twenty-second, and that he never once looked up from it, and that Lucius rode past with his eyes on his horse's ears, and that Hadrian looked at the road. Four people on the Sacred Way today who didn't look at my box, and three of them were my doing, and the fourth was Lucius, who's never done what I wanted in his life and has been right every time." She did not look round. "Sit."

Fausta sat. The ivory was in her hair. Her feet were under her and the floor was timber over posts over a road where a king's gold had gone by.

"I said I'd be watching your faces. I was. I'll tell you what I saw, since you'll want the figure and I've never once in four years lied to you about one." The plain face looked at the road. "I saw a girl read a board. Forty of them. With her lips still and her eyes moving and the face she keeps, and on the twenty-second the eyes stopped for the length of a breath and went on, and that was all, and if I'd not been watching for it I'd have thought she was checking a nine. And I saw a man with a stick walk behind a cart reading the same board with his lips moving, and on the twenty-second — my Guardsman says, and he's a man who sees — the lips stopped, for the length of a breath, and went on." The Augusta was quiet. The city went home below. "Neither of you turned round. Neither of you *looked*. And I sat here in my husband's chair at my husband's triumph with the thing I wrote in Cologne going by on four white horses and I found I wasn't watching it; I was watching two children from a coast not look at each other across a hundred thousand people with their lips stopped, and I thought: I've paid a sail and a man in Africa for this, and it's the best thing I've bought in thirty years, and I don't know what it is."

"Augusta."

"Yes."

"What does it cost?"

The Augusta turned her head, finally, which she did not do, and looked at the girl in Sabina's chair, and the plain face showed the thing that was nearly tiredness, and then the other thing, and then — Fausta kept it; she would write it that night under the arithmetic — something she had never seen on it in four years and had been told by Marcia did not exist, which was uncertainty.

"I don't know," said Pompeia Plotina. "That's the first time I've said it since Nemausus. I know what I've paid and I know what I've got and I can't do the sum. There's a thing in it that's not a figure." She turned back to the road. "I wrote eleven letters from Cologne. He read them. We've been very civil for thirty years across a table and never once stood on anything together and looked out." The face showed nothing. "It's not a receiving day. I'd like, once, to have said a true thing to someone who'll put it in a book and not a mouth."

The city went home. The sand on the Sacred Way was churned to the colour of the cut.

"Go up," said the Augusta. "Not to Sabina; she's doing her own hair wrong on purpose and wants to be found at it. To your book. Write the day. Write the twenty-second board. Write what I said; I'll not say it again and I'd like it kept somewhere." She lay back in the emperor's wife's chair with no cloth. "And girl. Tomorrow. The city will be drunk and the Senate will be voting games and Hadrian will be in his library and Lucius will be on his couch, if he's alive, and I'll have the cloth on, because it'll be a receiving day again and I've a headache to perform. And there's a Syrian with a hole in the ground who was told *one day*, and the day's not mine any more; I said yes to it in April and the triumph's over. Where you go in the morning before the room is a thing I've decided not to know. It's the first thing in thirty years I've decided not to know. I'd like to see what it feels like."

She went up. The covered stair to the postern, the courts, the fountain that worked, the north rooms, where Sabina was at the glass with her hair half down and a pin in her teeth and said round it, "Don't. I'm getting it wrong. Go and write. Momus is on the terrace; he's been there since the gold; he says he counted forty carts and one stick and he'd like it known he was right about the stick."

She went to the terrace. The dwarf was on the parapet with his legs out and the city below going home in the long June dusk, and the cut beyond the Forum a dark square in the red, and he did not look round, and said, "Well?"

"I didn't turn round."

"I saw. I saw from here; you've a back like a shutter. He didn't either. His freedwoman will have it from the wine-shop by now and be pleased and not say so." Momus looked at the cut. "And the Augusta?"

"She said she doesn't know what it costs."

The dwarf was quiet for a long moment.

"Then that's the first true thing anyone's said on this hill in forty years," he said, "and she said it to you, in her husband's box, on the day of his triumph, and you'll put it in your book, and nobody will ever read it, and that's exactly right." He turned his face up. "Tomorrow. Before the room. I'll walk you. Nobody stops a girl with a dwarf at the Syrian's cut; they think she's come to see the hole and I'm the measure."

* * *

She wrote it that night, on the cold floor, with Sabina asleep at last in the next room with her hair wrong, and the lamp, and the book open on her knees at the third leaf, in the small hard hand.

*The Nones of June. The triumph. I stood nine hours at Sabina's left shoulder and read forty boards and did not turn round. He walked behind the twenty-second with Crescens's stick in his belt and did not look up. The Augusta watched both and said afterwards, in the emperor's box, in the emperor's chair's shadow, that she does not know what it costs. I am writing that down because she will never say it again and because it is the first time I have heard her say a thing she had not decided to.*

*Her eyes did a thing when the chariot went by. I have called it arithmetic. I think it is what my mother's face did when a month came out, which it did twice in my memory.*

*He stood at the far end of the cut this morning before light and said something to the hole in a hand only I can read. I was not there. I know what it was. I have known since a mole. I said it at twelve with my arm out straight and he said "all right" and I thought "all right" was his price for it, and it was not; it was the thing left over, and I have been six years carrying it in the place where I keep the Rhodian's eleven, and tonight the Augusta said she has decided not to know where I go in the morning, and Momus says he will walk me, and I have decided.*

*I will go and look at a hole in the ground a hill's height, with a dwarf, because a Syrian was told "one day" and the day is not hers any more. If he is there, we will stand side by side and look at the same thing, which is what Sura says a mole is. If he is not, I will have seen the column's footing, and the Syrian will have been pleased, and I will come back and do two heads.*

*I do not know what it costs. That makes two of us on this hill tonight, and the other one is the Augusta, and I would not have believed, on a lodging-house stair at ten, that I would ever write that sentence.*

*And? — I am asking. Not telling. I have worked that out. It took four years and a woman who threw her children in a basin saying "it comes out" on a floor. My mother was asking what was next. So am I.*

She closed the book. The boards were oak. She put it under the Strabo with the letters and the fish and the prefect's copy under the Augusta's seal, and lay down on the floor by the chest in her gown with the ivory still in her hair, because she would not sleep and there was no reason to be comfortable not sleeping, and listened to the city be drunk below, and counted, because she counted everything, the hours till the first.
