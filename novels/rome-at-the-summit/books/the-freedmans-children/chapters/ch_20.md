# Four Ways

Sura set the sheet aside on the third morning back, and Celer read it, and it was shorter than the sail.

That was what he noticed first, because he noticed first what a thing weighed. It was one sheet. It was in a clerk's hand, the prefect's office's, headed and dated and numbered as a thing was that had been through four hands before it reached a fifth; and the body of it was eleven lines, and the eleven lines were the manifest — hull, weight, voyage, year, *pondere non mensura*, the spare mainsail of the heavy canvas, the forward hold, the port-man's son, the pine box, the *Isis* year, his father's seal, the lid off — and under the eleven lines was a line in another hand, the Palatine's, that said *from information laid before the Augusta by Fausta, daughter of Hilara, freedwoman of L. Laecanius Bassus, of the household of Cornelia Tertulla*, and under that, in Sura's, in the Spanish, small: *Read it. Then come and tell me one thing.*

He read it four ways.

He read it first as his father would have, which was as a figure, and the figure was right. Every line. He had been eleven in the strong-room and he had read it aloud to her and she had said *all right* and she had kept it eight years, the way she kept everything, in the place where she kept the Rhodian's eleven and the sound a slate made, and it had come out of her, when it came, exactly as it had gone in, with no *I*. He knew that. He could hear it. He could hear the eggs voice saying *the pounds are always right* to a comb, in a room he had never seen, on a hill, and he could hear that it had taken the length of a hair and had not shaken, because it would not have; she had told a dying woman's slate to an Alexandrian in the same voice. The figure was right. That was the first way and it took no time at all.

He read it second as Sura would have, which was as a move, and the move was good. A girl in a widow's house with a paper under a hearth-stone and a steward on the coast asking her birth-year in front of a praetor's man, and a woman on a couch who had the hill to give and did not give things free; and the girl had been asked for a figure and had held it a year, which was the long game, and had given it when the price was right, which was the only game; and what she had bought with it he did not know and could guess, because he knew what a girl with a false paper needed, and it was a true one, and the only true one was the hill's. She had sold the street for the hill. The Spaniard had said *the only thing a woman in a room with no door could have done* and the Spaniard was right and Sura would say the same and he said it himself, on the stool, in the Spanish, under his breath: *good move*. That was the second way.

He read it third as the Spaniard had told him he would, which was as a boy raised on a figure, and he did not want to and did it anyway, because a man who did not read the way he would be tempted to read was a man who did not know where his own hole was. And the third way was this: that she had known whose it was. Not the house's; *his*. He had read it to her. Not his father; he had. In a strong-room, at eleven, with the lid off the pine box because he had taken it off to show her, because he had wanted her to see what he could see, and she had looked at him while he read it and said *all right*; and eight years later on a hill she had given a comb the fourth line of the second column and the figure for the wet mainsail *in his hand*, and the one thing she had left out, the one thing the eleven lines did not have, was *the boy read it to me*. She had left him out. She had put his father's name and his father's seal and his father's box and his father's margin on a sheet for a prefect, and had not put the boy, and he sat with that on the stool and felt it go through him cold and clear and recognised it, because he had felt it on a sheep-rock reading a Spaniard's four pages: it was the feeling of being left out of a column on purpose, and he did not know, and could not from the sheet, whether it had been done to spare him or because he had not been worth the line.

That was the third way, and it was the hole, and he knew it was the hole and sat in it anyway for the time it took the clerks in the outer room to go through one box.

And then he read it the fourth way, which was the one he had not been taught, and he read it because on a sheep-rock a man with lime on his boots had said *I think a man's made by what he does on the next page*, and because a woman over a wine-shop had said *I'd rather keep house for a kind man who's honest about what he bought*, and because a woman at a loom had said *the gods like to see a face*, and none of these was a figure; and the fourth way was: *what did it cost her?*

He did not know. The sheet did not say. The sheet said *from information laid before the Augusta* and it did not say *for a year of her tongue and a thing she'd rather have kept*; the Spaniard had said that, on a rock, and the Spaniard had had it from the Augusta in one sentence, and he had not. He had eleven lines and a name. And he sat on the stool and understood, the way he had understood on a mole that she was holding him off at the length of her arm and that the arm was the thing and not the holding, that the sheet had been written to be read by his father and by Sura and by the prefect and by the eleven in the Spaniard's box, and that it had not been written to be read by him, and that this was either the cruellest thing she had ever done or the kindest, and that he could not tell which from the paper, and that he would have to ask her, and that his father had said *do nothing*, and that he was good at it.

He got up and went in to Sura.

* * *

"One thing," said Sura, from the couch, where he lay now in the mornings with the cough, which had got worse on the road home and worse again in the Roman damp; and the knife-face was thinner and the eyes were the same.

"She left me out, sir."

Sura looked at him for a moment.

"Yes," he said. "She did. I noticed. I thought you'd lead with the figure; it's right, by the way, the prefect's people were at the pine box on the Ides and found the margin, your father's seal, the two sums, wet and dry, the whole thing in his own hand, and I've the inventory on the table there if you want to see your father's handwriting copied by a clerk, which is a thing I'd not wish on anyone." He coughed, into a cloth, and looked at the cloth, and put it away. "But you led with that. Good. It's the right thing. It's the thing that's not on the sheet and it's the only thing on the sheet that matters to you, and a man who goes to the thing that matters to him first and the figure second is a man who'll be some use when he's forty, if he lives, which I'll not." He put the cloth down. "She left you out. Why?"

"I don't know, sir."

"No. You don't. And you'll not from here. You'd have to ask her and you can't, because your father said *do nothing* and because I've told you the Caelian's barred and because the Augusta's got her now and nobody asks the Augusta's people anything." Sura lay back. "So you'll not know. You'll carry it. It's the first thing you'll carry that you can't put in a box and count, and I'm glad to have been in the room when you picked it up, because I've carried one for thirty years and I'd like to have seen what it looked like from outside, once." The eyes closed. "I'll tell you one thing about it, since you've told me one. A sheet with your father's name on it and not yours is a sheet that doesn't touch the roll. Your name's on the roll at four hundred and six thousand in vines and the vines are yours and the sail was his. If she'd put *the boy read it to me*, there's a man in the prefect's office who'd have asked whether the boy knew, and when, and whether the vines were bought with the sail, and whether a man who knew and said nothing for eight years was a man who should have a ring. She didn't put it. So nobody asked. So you've a ring." The eyes stayed closed. "That's one way to read it. There are others. I don't know which is true and neither do you and I'd not trust anyone who told you they did, including her. Go and draft the thing for the prefect."

"Sir?"

"The finding. The figure. Somebody has to write what the emperor's office recommends be done about a shipowner in Puteoli who's been weighing wet sails for ten years, and it's this office, and in this office it's the man with the best hand and the clearest head for a figure, and that's you, and I'll not spare you it because it's your father, because if I spared you it you'd be a man who'd been spared, and that's no use to me or to the Spaniard or to anyone." Sura opened his eyes. "Write it straight. The figure is the figure: ten years, three hulls, a sail, the bounty on the short weight, doubled, as the law says, less the credit for the *Pelican*'s Rhine run, which I'll have put in because it's true and because the emperor stood up in an inn for it. Don't soften it. Don't sharpen it. Write it the way she said it, as a manifest, with no *I*. And when you've written it, sign it, with your name and the ring, because the prefect will want to know who in this office did the sum, and when he knows, he'll know what kind of office this is, and what kind of man I've got on the stool, and that's worth more to you than your father's fine, which he can pay, and will, and will never forgive, and that's between you and him and not between you and me."

He wrote it. It took the afternoon. It was two sheets, in the square hand, and it was the figure, and he did not soften it and did not sharpen it and wrote the credit for the *Pelican* in as Sura had said, and at the end of it he wrote the sum, which was four hundred and eighty thousand sesterces, which was a little more than the vines and a little less than the villa and was, he knew, almost exactly what his father had in the strong-room that was not in his son's name; and he signed it, *G. Laecanius Celer, eques, of the office of L. Licinius Sura*, and pressed the ring into the wax, and the ring weighed less than a denarius and the wax took it, and he sat for a while looking at his own name under his father's figure and thought that she had left him out of her sheet and that he had just put himself on the next page, in his own hand, and that the Spaniard had said that was where a man was made, and that he did not feel made. He felt like a boy who had read a bill aloud in a strong-room to show a girl what he could see, and had been seen.

* * *

Chrestê knew before he told her. Everyone did; it was Rome.

He came down the hill in the dark to the three rooms over the wine-shop with the thing in him that he had not put in a box and she had the lamp lit and the slate on the table and the long face not arranged, and she said, in the Gulf Greek, before he had the door shut, "The Puteoli girl. The sail. It's in the wine-shop; the steward's boy had it from Sura's kitchen. Your father's fined and she's the one." She looked at him. "Sit down. You've the face your father had when the *Pelican* was late."

He sat. She put bread in front of him and he did not eat it and she sat down across the table with the slate between them and did not pick it up.

"I wrote the finding," he said. "The figure. Sura made me. I signed it."

"Of course he did. He's a Spaniard; they think a thing's not done unless it's cost you." She was quiet a moment. "How much?"

"Four hundred and eighty."

"Then he'll pay it and hate her and keep the villa paper and the hulls and the vines in your name, and in five years he'll be richer than he was, because men like your father always are, and he'll still hate her, because the money's not the thing, the thing's that he was *read*." Chrestê looked at the lamp. "I kept a merchant's books four years. I know what a man's face does when someone's been in his column. It's not the money. It's that he thought the column was his."

"She left me out of it."

"I know. The steward's boy said. *Hermes's hand, Hermes's seal, Hermes's box, and not a word about the son who was in the room.*" She looked back at him. "And you've been up there since the second hour reading that four ways, and you've come down the hill with the fourth one, which is *why*, and you're going to ask me, because I'm the only person you've got who knew her, and I'll tell you now that I knew her over a slate once a month for a year when she was fourteen and that's not knowing a woman, that's knowing her arithmetic." She reached across and turned the ring on his finger, once, where it had been made smaller and was not loose. "But I'll tell you what her arithmetic was. It was: *take the lower figure and look at the man so he knows you know he was told to pass the higher.* That's what she did with Granius's factor's slate. She'd not take the higher figure even when it was mine to lose and not hers. She took the lower one and looked at me. Every month." Chrestê let go of the ring. "If that's the girl, then she left you out because you were the higher figure and she took the lower, and looked at the Augusta while she did it, so the Augusta would know she'd seen the higher one was there. That's not sparing you. It's not forgetting you. It's a girl keeping a book straight. You were a thing she could have put in and didn't, and she wanted the other woman to know she could have." A pause. "Which is the cruellest thing or the kindest. I can't tell you. I don't think she can."

He sat with it. The lamp went down.

"Come to bed," said Chrestê, after a while. "You'll not sleep and I'll not either and I'd rather not sleep beside you than across a table from you with your father's face on."

He went. And in the inner room with the lamp she did a thing she had not done in the eighteen months he had owned her, which was that she did not wait for him; she took the tunic off over her head herself, standing, in the light, with the long brown body and the scar on the hip, and looked at him with the long face not arranged, and said, "I'm not her. I'm telling you so you'll not have to tell yourself. I'm a woman you bought in the Subura who's kept your slate honest and your rooms clean and has been on her back for you when you came to the door and has liked it more than she'd planned to, which is a thing I'd not have told you a year ago and am telling you now because you've got a face on you tonight that wants someone to say a true thing to it. So. I'm not her. Now come here and be nineteen for an hour and leave the sheet on the hill."

He came. He would remember it, as he remembered all of it, by what he saw; and what he saw, this time, was her, and not the Bithynian girl, and not the chandler's stair, and not the eggs voice saying *that's done*; he saw a woman of twenty-two who had been sold with the furniture and who put her hands on him now not where they went but where she wanted them, which was different, and he felt the difference, and it undid something in him that the sheet had tied; and he took her with the lamp going down, slowly, with his mouth on the scar on the hip and her hand in his hair holding him there, hard, so that he understood it was not a thing she was giving but a thing she was taking, and that she had decided to take it, tonight, from him, on purpose, because he had come down the hill with his father's face and she had wanted to see if she could take it off; and she could; and she did; and when she came it was with a sound he had not heard from her, low, surprised, as if she had not planned that either, and her nails in his back, and her eyes open on his, in the light, with the long face unarranged entirely, so that he saw, for the length of it, the thing underneath that was not fear and was not the pen-look and was a woman in a bed who had, for once, not done the sum first; and then he followed her, because he could not have done anything else, with his face in her neck and the Gulf Greek in his ear saying his name, which she had never used, *Gaius*, not *domine*, not *Celer*, the praenomen, the one nobody had said since his mother; and afterward she lay with her arm over her eyes and breathed and said, to the ceiling, after a long time, "Well. That's the first time I've done that and not been working. I'd not planned it. Don't make anything of it. It's a thing that happened in a room with a lamp and it's not on anyone's sheet."

"No," he said.

"Good." She moved her arm and looked at him. "I'll keep house for you till you free me, which you will, because you're your father's son and he freed his, and I'll not ask when. And I'll say this once. The girl on the hill has done a thing to your father that you'd never have done, and she's done it to you by leaving you out of it, and you've written the figure with your own name under it, and you'll go to Puteoli, because he'll send for you, and you'll stand in his strong-room and he'll say what he said before about her and you'll not strike him this time, because you're nineteen and not seventeen and because he'll be right about the figure and wrong about the woman and you'll have learned by now that a man can be both." She turned over. "Go to sleep. The forum's being cut out of the hill and the Syrian wants you and Sura's dying and the Spaniard's waiting and your father's fined and the girl's on the Palatine and you've a woman in your bed who's just called you by your name. That's a lot for one night in three rooms over a wine-shop. Count it in the morning."

* * *

Sura lent him to the Syrian for a day in November, and the Syrian showed him the hill.

It was the saddle between the Capitol and the Quirinal, where the old Forum ran out of room and the ground went up, and for a hundred years emperors had looked at it and said *there* and done nothing, because it was a hill, and a hill was a hill; and the Syrian had looked at it and said *it's a hundred and twenty feet of tufa and clay and I've a king's gold and ten thousand prisoners and a man who can count*, and the emperor had said yes. They had been at it since the summer. Celer stood with Apollodorus on the edge of what had been the saddle and was now a cut, a raw red wound in the city a hundred paces across with the Quirinal's shoulder standing above it sheer where it had been sliced, and the gangs going down into it in lines with baskets and coming up in lines with baskets, and the carts, and the dust, which was everywhere, which was in the bread on the Aventine by evening and in the Augusta's room on the Palatine, they said, where the one window had to be shut.

"A hundred and twenty feet," said the Syrian, with lime on his arms, in the Greek. "I'll put a column there, when it's done, exactly that high, so nobody forgets there was a hill. Lucius says it's vanity. Lucius is right. It's also the only way to make a man believe a thing's been moved, which is to leave the measure standing." He pointed with a whole arm. "Basilica there. Libraries there and there, Greek and Latin, facing, so the scholars can glare at each other across the column. The temple he'll not let me put in because he says a man doesn't build his own temple, which is true until he's dead, and then his nephew does it. And the market, up the side of the Quirinal, in a curve, in brick, six storeys, because the shopkeepers I've moved off the saddle have to go somewhere and I'd rather they went up than out." He dropped the arm. "You see what it is."

"It's a road, sir. Like the bridge. You've put a road through a hill so the border can't be rolled up."

"Ha." The Syrian looked at him sideways. "Lucius said you'd say that. He said you say everything's a road. He's not wrong and neither are you. It's a road from the old Forum to the Campus and from the Republic to whatever he thinks he's making, and he's made me cut a hill in half to lay it, and in twenty years nobody will remember the hill, only the column. That's what a forum is. It's a thing that makes you forget what it cost." He began to walk along the edge of the cut, and Celer walked with him. "I asked for you for the figure. The gold's paying for it and the Senate's asked three times how much is left and Lucius sends them a story and they want a figure and I want a man who'll give them one that's true, because a true figure's the only thing that shuts a senator. You'll do it in a week. I'll give you a table in the hut and a clerk who can't read, which is the kind I prefer." He stopped. "And I asked for another reason, which Lucius doesn't know, and I'll tell you because I'm a Syrian and we don't do the thing you Romans do where you let a man find out."

Celer waited.

"The Augusta walks here. Not often; she doesn't walk anywhere often. But the emperor's asked her to see it, because he can't, and she comes on a morning in a litter with the curtains shut and gets out at the far end, there, by the shoulder, where nobody is, and stands, and looks at it, and goes. Twice since the summer. And the second time she had a girl with her who does her hair, Lucius says, a tall one with a nose, who stood a pace behind and looked at the cut the way you're looking at it now, which is the way I look at it, which is to say counting; and the Augusta said to me, as she got back into the litter, *this one's from Puteoli; she knows what a thing weighs; one day you'll want to show her the column*, and got in, and went." The Syrian looked at the boy with the lime drying on his arms. "I don't know what that was. I'm an engineer. But I've been on the hill long enough to know that when that woman says *one day* in front of a man she's never spoken to, she's laying a stone, and I thought the man who's going to count my forum should know there's a stone laid in it with a girl's name on, and that the woman who laid it did so in front of me on purpose so that I'd tell him." He turned back to the cut. "Come to the hut. The clerk's called Blaesus and he's deaf as well, which is why I keep him."

He went to the hut. He did the figure, in a week, at a table, with a deaf clerk, and it was true, and it shut the Senate; and every morning of the week he came up the Aventine in the dark and along the Forum and up to the edge of the cut, early, before the gangs, and stood at the far end by the shoulder where nobody was, and looked at the hill being taken away a basket at a time, and did not see a litter, and had not expected to, and stood there anyway, for the time it took the light to come, because a woman on a couch had said *one day* in front of a Syrian, and because he was his father's son and knew what it was to be told a thing had been laid for you and to go and stand by it before it was cut.

* * *

His father's letter came on the Kalends of December, and it was one sheet, and it was the shortest thing Hermes of Puteoli had ever written him, and he read it standing, in the outer room, with Chrestê at the table with the slate not looking.

*The prefect's people were at the box on the Ides of November. They had the line. They had the year. They had the sail wet and dry in my hand and they had a sheet from Rome that told them which box, and the sheet had a name under it and the name was hers. I've seen it. I have a boy in the prefect's office in Puteoli and have had since Nerva. Four hundred and eighty thousand, less the Pelican. I'll pay it. It's most of what I've got that's not yours. I'll not touch yours. I'll not touch the villa paper. I'll pay it from the strong-room and from the Isis, which I'll sell to the Alexandrian who's wanted her, and I'll have two hulls and a tomb and a paper on a headland and a son on the roll, which is more than I walked off a ship with, and I'll say that to the gods in the morning with the bread and mean it.*

*You wrote the finding. The boy in the prefect's office says the sheet from Sura's has your hand and your ring. I'd have done the same. I'd have made you do it, in Sura's place. I don't hold it. A man who puts his name on a true figure is a man, and that's what I built the stone for, and if the figure's mine, then it's mine, and I've had ten years of water in the Subura bread out of it and I knew what I was doing and so did every carrier in this port and I'm the one who wrote it down because I'm the one who writes things down. That's the and. There's nothing after it.*

*But hear me, and I'll say it once, and I've thought about how to say it since the Ides and I've decided to say it the way I said it two years ago, because it was true then and you struck me for the sentence and not the truth, and I'll take the sentence back and keep the truth. She's gone up that hill on what she had, and what she had was my strong-room, and she's sold it, and the price was the hill, and she's got it. I don't blame her. I'd have done it. I did do it; I sold a patron's dock to a corn-office for forty years and built columns on it. She's mine, that one, more than she's her mother's; she's a freedman's child who found a thing in a box and knew what it weighed. I'd have been proud of her, in another house.*

*But she's not in another house. She's on the Palatine with my column in her mouth and my son's name left off it, and I know why she left it off, and so do you, and it's not kindness. It's that she's keeping you. For later. A woman on that hill with a boy on the roll whose name she could have put on a prefect's sheet and didn't is a woman with a paper on him, the way I've a paper on Rufus's villa. She's not spent you. She's holding you. She learned it from me, in my strong-room, with the lid off, and I'd have told her she'd learned it well if she'd stayed on the coast long enough to be told anything.*

*Come to Puteoli when Sura can spare you. Not for the fine. For the stone. I said I'd stand by it with you and I'd like to do it before the spring. I'm fifty-five and my hand's worse and your stepmother says I've stopped smiling since the Ides, and she's right, and I'd like to stand by the stone with my son before I've forgotten how. — Your father.*

He read it twice. He folded it. He put it in the box with the stick and the Lares and the forty-one days of a king's gold, and closed the lid, and stood with his hand on it.

"Well?" said Chrestê, to the slate.

"He says she's holding a paper on me."

"Is she?"

"I don't know." He took his hand off the lid. "He says come to Puteoli for the stone."

"Then go. Sura can spare you; Sura can't spare anyone and spares everyone, it's the cough. Go and stand by your father's stone and let him say what he says, and don't strike him, and come back." She looked up from the slate, finally, with the long face arranged again, as it was in the day, and the thing under it that he could read now and did. "And Gaius. Before you go. There's a thing in the wine-shop that I'd rather you had from me than from the steward's boy on the road. Tertulla's son. The one on the Caelian. He's gone to Africa — Tingis, the corn-ships, the post nobody takes. The Augusta's secretary did it, on the Ides of September, in a week, which is not how posts are given, and the wine-shop says there was a night in Tertulla's house while Tertulla was at Laurentum, and a dwarf at the Palatine postern at the third hour, and the Augusta's Germans in the atrium at the second hour of the morning, and the girl doing the Augusta's hair on the Kalends with a pin in it as if nothing." She held his eyes. "That's all the wine-shop has. It's a wine-shop. But I kept a merchant's books four years and I know what a column looks like with a line missing from the middle of it, and so do you, and I'd not have you hear it on the Appian road from a boy who thinks it's a story about a girl who's had a man sent to Africa. It's not that story. You know what story it is. You've read it four ways; read it a fifth."

He stood in the outer room with his hand on the box.

"Yes," he said, after a while. "I know what story it is."

"Then go to Puteoli," said Chrestê, "and stand by the stone, and when your father says she went up the hill on what she had, you'll know what else she paid, and you'll not strike him, and you'll not tell him either, because it's not yours to tell, and because he'd put it in a column, and it's the one thing on that hill that shouldn't be."
