# The Burning Fields

They walked the coast road at dusk because her mother would not walk it in the dark any more, and it was on the coast road, with the bay going from blue to pewter on their left and the villas lighting their lamps behind them one terrace at a time, that Hilara told her about the seven years.

She had not asked. She had decided a long time ago, on the stairs, that she would not ask, that the counters could be laid side by side for as long as it took and she would not be the one to push them into a shape. But her mother had been slow all day, slow in the kitchen court at the villa where the coast women did the washing-up for sixty, slow on the stair with the dishes, slow now on the road, with a hand that went to her side when she thought the girl was not looking; and when they were past the last of the villas and the road ran empty between the hill and the water, she stopped, and sat down on the low wall where the road turned, and said, "Sit. I want to say a thing while I've the breath, and I'd rather say it here than in the house, where the walls have been listening for thirteen years."

Fausta sat.

"You saw him today. In the atrium. With his hand up."

"Yes."

"You've seen him every April of your life and you've never once asked me, and I've watched you not ask, and I've thought, she's her mother's, that one, she'll wait till the paper's in front of her. Well. There isn't a paper for this. There's only me." Hilara looked at the water. "I was bought at four. I told you that. Delos, in a lot, with a woman who said she was my mother and wasn't, and died on the ship. I was in his father's house in Rome till I was twelve and then in his, because his father died and the household was split and I went with the young master's share. I did hair. I was good at hair. And when I was fifteen he came into the room where the women slept, one night, with a lamp, the way they do, and looked at the row of us, and stopped at me." She said it as she might have said *the Rhodian wants eggs*. "That's all that was. There's no more to it than that. He looked along a row and stopped. He could have stopped anywhere."

Fausta said nothing. The bay went on going grey.

"Seven years. Antiope's told you that; she tells you everything, she thinks it's a kindness. It's true. Seven years, in Rome and here, and I was the one, in that time, and I'll say this for him, he wasn't cruel. He wasn't anything. He was a man who'd stopped at me and kept stopping because it was easier than looking along the row again. He gave me things. He gave me a room. And when I was twenty-two and he'd been married three years and his wife had worked out where the room was, he put me on a ship for Puteoli with a lease on a house by the harbour and a sum in a bag and a letter to his steward saying I was to be free at his death and have the house. He said, *you'll want for nothing, Hilara*, and he was right, I've wanted for nothing, I've had the Dolphin and the Rhodians and the goddess in the yard and I've never once in thirteen years wanted for a single thing that could be bought." She stopped. Her hand had gone to her side again and she let it stay. "And I was three months gone when he put me on the ship, and he knew it, and that was why the ship."

The road was quiet. Somewhere up the hill a dog barked and stopped.

"I'm not telling you he's your father," her mother said. "I'm telling you I was his for seven years and I was three months gone when I left and you were born in the house by the harbour six months after, and you've got his nose, God help you, and the widow saw it across a dining-room in the lamplight in one look, and so did Thallus the day you were born, and so would anyone who'd ever stood in that atrium and looked up instead of down. I'm telling you because you're twelve and you'll bleed this year and when you do the widow will write, and you'll go to Rome, and somebody there will say it to you in a corridor to see what your face does. I want your face to do nothing. I want you to have had it from me, on a wall, in the dusk, with nobody listening, so that when they say it you can look at them the way you looked at Thallus today and think, *yes, and?*" She turned, finally, and looked at her daughter. "That's the seven years. It's not a story. I've made it sound like one because I've had thirteen years to shape it, but it wasn't one at the time. It was a man with a lamp."

Fausta looked at her mother's face in the last of the light. She looked at the broad cheekbones and the deep eyes that the Rhodians said were Thracian and the Alexandrians said were Syrian, and she thought about the bronze in the room under the stairs and the face in it that she had decided at six was simply hers, and she did the sum she had been not doing for two years, and it came out. She had known it would. She had known the answer since the fishing boat, since the hand on the tunic and *seven years, he must have liked her*, and she had left the sum undone the way you left a door shut, not because you did not know what was in the room but because you did.

"Yes," she said. "And?"

Her mother looked at her for a long moment. Then she laughed — the real laugh, the one from the morning of the Baetican jars — and it turned into a cough, and she bent over it with her hand on her side until it passed, and straightened, and wiped her eyes, and said: "That's my girl. That's it exactly. Now help me up; the Rhodian's in and he'll want his eggs, and I'll not have him saying the Dolphin's gone soft because the woman sat on a wall."

* * *

There was a scarf round Antiope's throat when she came in by the yard, in August, a good one, Tarentine wool, blue, and it had been tied by someone who did not usually wear a scarf, because the knot was at the front, and it did not cover the mark.

She came in the evening, by the yard, and sat on the bottom stair in her travelling-cloak with her knees up, as she had always sat, and Fausta, who had been at the hearth, looked at her once and went and barred the door and came back and sat on the floor in front of her, and did not say anything, because she had learned from her mother that there were things you waited for.

"Well," Antiope said eventually. "He's rich."

"Rufus."

"Rufus." She pulled the scarf off and dropped it on the step. The mark was a hand. It was yellow at the edges now and blue in the middle and you could see, if you looked, where the thumb had been. "He asked for me particular. That's true. He's a man who likes to have a thing that's his for the season, and he's careful with it, mostly, and he pays. He paid. I've more in this hem than I made in three winters." She touched the mark, lightly, with two fingers, the way you touched a bruise to see if it was still yours. "He's not cruel. I want to say that, because I asked the goddess for it and I'm not going to say she didn't deliver. He's not. He's a man who's been told what he's worth and has started to think the market's wrong, and when he's had wine he needs to find out, and the nearest thing to hand is whatever's in the room. It was me in the room. He'd his hand here and the rest of him in me and his eyes shut, and he was saying a figure. Not my name; a figure, over and over, the way your Celer does with jars, and when he'd finished he looked at his hand like it was somebody else's and gave me a gold piece and went and washed. It would have been a slave if it hadn't been. It was a slave twice, in June, when I had the flux. He was sorry after. He's always sorry after; it's one of the things he's good at." She looked at Fausta. "Don't do that with your face. I'm telling you because you're going to Rome, not because I want you to hold my hand. You're going to that widow's house and the widow's got a son, and all these houses have sons, and sons are all the same thing in different tunics. I want you to know what the thing looks like before it's in front of you."

"What does it look like?"

Antiope thought about it. "Like a man who's just realised he's allowed," she said. "That's all. That's the whole of it. You'll see it come into the face. It's like watching a lamp lit. And once you've seen it you can't not see it, and that's the only protection there is, duck, seeing it a breath before he does." She picked up the scarf and wound it back round her throat with practised fingers. "Your mother knows. Don't tell her I told you. She'd want to have done it herself and she's not got the breath for it this summer."

"What's wrong with her?"

"I don't know. Nobody knows what's wrong with women; there's no money in it." Antiope stood. "She's tired and she's thirty-four and she's been upstairs with Thallus twice a month since the spring, and that'd tire a mule. She'll mend when he's gone to Rome. They always mend in the autumn." She looked down at the girl on the floor. "You've not bled yet."

"No."

"You will. You've the look. And when you do, tell her, and then tell nobody, and when the widow writes, go. Don't wait for me to say goodbye; I'll be at the villa till the Kalends and I don't say goodbye, it's bad luck in the trade." And she bent and kissed the top of Fausta's head, quickly, as though it were a thing she had decided against and done anyway, and went up the stairs with her hem clinking, and the stair creaked in the place it creaked, and the house settled.

* * *

There was blood on the pallet under the stairs when she woke, on the sixth day of September, a brown mark the size of her palm on the sacking, and she lay looking at it for a long time in the grey light with the dog's face above her.

She did not feel anything. That was the thing she would remember: that she had expected to feel something — she had been told she would, by Antiope, by the Greek woman, by the lady Cornelia's eleven girls in the dormitory over the bakehouse in Rome, who had talked of nothing else for ten nights — and there was nothing, only the dark stain on the linen and a dull pulling low in her belly that she had taken, half-asleep, for hunger. She thought: *that's done, then.* She thought: *now the widow will write.* She thought, because she was her mother's daughter and had been told it on a wall, *now a man can be put in a room with me and think about it first*. She found that this thought, which should have frightened her, did not; it went into her and settled, cold and useful, beside the other things she kept, and she got up, and folded the linen, and went to find her mother.

Hilara was at the hearth. She looked at the folded linen and at the girl's face and did not say anything for a moment, and then she put down the ladle and came and took her daughter's face between her two hands, which she had not done since Fausta was small enough to be lifted, and looked at it — the nose, the eyes, all of it — as if she were reading a paper she had been waiting a long time to see.

"Well," she said. "There's the house, then. That's what it's made of now." And she let go. "Antiope's told you what to do with the linen. Do it. And then come back and we'll write to the widow before Thallus can; I want it in my hand that went to her, not in his."

So they wrote. Her mother dictated and Fausta wrote, in the square letters, on a sheet of the good papyrus that the Rhodian had left in payment once and that had been in the box under the hearth-stone for four years waiting for something worth it. The letter said that the freedwoman Hilara sent her duty to the lady Cornelia Tertulla and begged to inform her that the girl she had been kind enough to notice at the master's house last autumn was now of an age to be of use in a lady's household, and that if the lady's kindness still held, the girl was at her disposal, at the lady's convenience, and the master's. It did not say *please*. It did not say anything about the Dolphin, or the steward, or the summer, or the hand on the side. It was eleven lines, and when it was done her mother read it over twice and said, "Good. Now put at the bottom, in your own hand, so she sees the hand: *The girl can read, and knows not to.*"

Fausta put it. Her mother sealed it with the Dolphin's seal, which was a dolphin, and which Bassus had given her with the lease, and which she used perhaps once a year.

"It'll go by the Rhodian," Hilara said. "He's sailing for Ostia on the ninth. He'll put it in at the widow's door himself; he owes me six asses and he knows it." She looked at the sealed letter on the table between them. "She'll not answer till the spring. They never do anything in the winter, the great; they go in and shut the door and come out in April like the rest of us. But she'll answer. She saw your nose."

"Mama."

"What."

"You said *there's the house*. When you looked at me."

Her mother was quiet. Then she picked up the letter and put it in the breast of her gown, and said, not looking at the girl: "I said it. I meant it. The Dolphin was never the house; the Dolphin's a lease and a man with a key. You're the house. You've been the house since I was three months gone on a ship and worked out that the one thing he'd given me that he couldn't take back through a steward was the thing I was carrying. That's what a child is, to a woman like me. It's the paper you can't burn." She turned back to the hearth. "Now go and do the linen, and then go up to Nicias; you've missed a week, and the boy goes back to Rome in a month, and after that you'll have the room to yourself."

* * *

She did not have the room to herself. Nicias was recalled to the villa in the third week of September, to write the Spaniard's letters, and the last lesson was the last lesson. He knew it and she knew it and Celer, who was at the warehouse counting the return cargo and had been told to be there, did not, and so it was the two of them, in the room with the half-shutter and the cup, with the roll of Demosthenes that nobody had got to the end of lying between them on the step like something that had died.

He did not teach. He sat on the edge of his pallet with his thin hands hanging between his knees and looked at her, and she sat on the step where the boy sat, and after a while he said, "You've bled."

She did not ask how he knew. He was one of the six.

"Good. That's the end of me, then. A tutor's a thing for children and you've stopped being one, officially. The widow will have her own Greeks who'll teach you to say *Homer* with the right vowels and despise you for having learned it in a room over a warehouse." He reached under the pallet and brought out a roll, a small one, old, with the ends frayed and the wooden boss cracked and mended with a bit of wire. "Take this. Don't open it here."

She took it. It was heavy for its size, the way old papyrus was.

"It's Strabo. The fifth book. It's got the bay in it — this town, the villas, the lake, the burning fields, all of it, as a Greek saw it eighty years ago when it was the greatest port in the world and Rome was a place you sent the grain. He got some of it wrong. He got the mole wrong; he says it's built on sand and it's built on the ash that the mountain threw, which is why it stands. Read it, and find where he's wrong, and then you'll know something about this town that nobody in Rome does, and that's a thing to carry into a widow's house: one true thing about a place they think they own." He sat back. "I stole it from the master's library in Rome in the year Titus died. He's never missed it. He's never read it. That's what a library's for, in a house like that; it's a tomb with better light."

"Nicias —"

"Don't. I've been a slave fifty-five years and I've no use for being thanked; it embarrasses the both of us and changes nothing." He picked up the cup, found it empty, set it down. "You asked me once, the first week, what the second thing was. About Rome. The boy brought you the first — the games and the wheat — and said I'd gone back to sleep before the second. You remember."

"Yes."

"I hadn't gone back to sleep. I'd thought better of it. You were ten." He looked at the shutter. "The second thing is this. Everyone in Rome belongs to someone. The slave belongs to the master and the freedman belongs to the patron and the client belongs to the great man and the great man belongs to the greater and the greatest belongs to the emperor, and the emperor —" He stopped. "The emperor belongs to the city. That's the one they don't tell you. He's the most owned man in the world. He can't eat, or marry, or die, or walk down a street, without it being a thing the city has decided about him. You saw him walk. You told me. On foot, embracing old men. Did you think he wanted to?"

"No. I thought he'd been told what the city wanted."

"Yes. And so he gave it. Because he's owned, and he knows it, and a man who knows it can be reasonable about it. The dangerous ones are the ones who think they own the city. Domitian thought that. It killed him." Nicias turned the empty cup a quarter-turn, the way Thallus did, and she wondered if he knew he had the habit from the same house. "So. Second thing. When you're in that house on the Caelian and they're all looking at each other to see who's watching whom, as you said — you had that right, by the way, at eleven, in a slaves' dormitory; I was proud of that for a week — remember that every one of them is owned, from the widow to the dwarf, and that the only question that matters about any of them is *by whom*. Find that out and you've found the whole of them. It's the same as the tally-boards. It's the same as the hand on the stylus. Who's leaning, and on what." He stood up, slowly, with a hand on the wall, the way her mother stood up now. "That's the lesson. Go on; the boy will be back from the hulls and I'll not have him see me give a girl a book I didn't give him. He's had enough of being second to you in this room; it's done him good, but there's a limit."

She stood with the roll against her chest. She looked at the thin indoor face and the hanging hands and the cup, and she thought that she would not see him again — she was right; he died at the villa two winters later of a chest, and Bassus, told of it at dinner, said *the Greek? pity, he had a good hand* — and she did not know how to leave a room with a man she would not see again and had not been taught, so she did the thing she had been taught, which was to look at him, at no cost, the way you looked at a Spaniard, and say the true thing.

"You could see them," she said. "And they couldn't see you. You said. You were the boat."

"Yes."

"I saw you."

He was still for a moment. Then the dry face did something she had not seen it do in six years, which was nothing, exactly, but a different nothing; and he said, in Greek, very quietly, the vowels right: "Yes. You did. It's been — go on. Go on, girl. The hulls."

She went. She went down through the house with the roll under her gown, past the loom where the stepmother looked up and looked at the shape under the cloth and looked down again, and out by the kitchen door and down the hill with the smoke coming off the fields behind her, and she did not open it until she was under the stairs with the curtain drawn and the dog's face above her; and then she did. It was the bay, as a Greek had seen it when it was the greatest port in the world, and he had got the mole wrong, and she found the place, and read it three times, and lay with it against her face in the dark the way other girls, she supposed, lay with other things.

* * *

Celer took her to the burning fields on the last day of September, because he was going north to Rome on the Kalends and had, he said, never been, and could not go to the Danube having lived his whole life a mile from the mouth of the underworld and not looked in.

It was not the Danube yet. It was Rome again, for the winter, with Rufus, and then — nobody knew. The whole coast had been saying *the war* for two years and the war had not come, and had not come, and in September the news came down the road that the new consul, a lawyer called Plinius who had a villa on the Laurentine shore and more money than anyone could account for, had stood up in the senate and made a speech to the emperor that went on for three hours and said, among a great many other things, that the emperor was not a man who sought war but that if war sought him the Danube would learn what it had forgotten under Domitian; and Hermes, reading the summary that Rufus had sent down, had said *that's the war, then, that's the speech they make before they've decided to tell you*, and had gone to the strong-room and stayed there till the lamps.

So they went up the hill. It was an hour's walk from the town, up through the olive terraces and over the ridge behind the amphitheatre and down into the bowl where nothing grew; and she had been, once, with Antiope, years ago, and had not forgotten it and had not wanted to go back, and went, because he had asked, and because he was going.

The ground was warm. That was the first thing, always; you came over the lip of the bowl and the earth under your feet went from the ordinary autumn cool of the hill to something else, a heat that came up through your sandals like the floor of a bath-house, and the air thickened, and the smell that lay in the streets of the town every morning until noon was here not a smell but a presence, yellow, that got into your mouth. The bowl was white. Not white like marble; white like bone, like something that had been alive and had been in the fire; and across it, here and there, the earth cracked and steamed, and in the cracks the yellow crusted thick as candle-wax, and in the middle, where the guides took the Romans who came up from Baiae to be frightened for a fee, there was the mouth.

They stood at the edge of it. It was a pit the size of the Dolphin's yard, grey mud, and the mud moved — slowly, as if something underneath it were breathing — and burst, and the burst was a smell and a sound like a pot on the boil and a gout of steam that went up and hung; and the Greeks had said it was Hephaestus's forge. The Romans said it was a door, and the guides said whatever you were paying for, and she, looking into it, thought that it was none of those things: it was simply the ground not pretending.

"Nicias says Strabo says the whole bay's like this underneath," Celer said. "That there's fire under all of it, under the town and the villas and the fleet, and this is only where it comes up. He says the hot springs at Baiae are the same fire with the lid on."

"Then the great are lying in it."

"Yes. For their health." He crouched and put his hand flat on the white ground, and took it away, and looked at his palm. "My father says Strabo's a Greek and Greeks say that sort of thing. He says the ground's the ground and the fire's the fire and a man who thinks about what's under his warehouse will never fill it."

"And you?"

He stood up. "I think the tile's still off the roof at the villa," he said, "and the dowry's in coin, and the Spaniard's uncle put his seal to a thing about me that he thought was about olives, and Rufus owes more than the house is worth and I'm the only one who knows it and I'm thirteen." He said it looking at the mouth. "I think everything's like this underneath. I think my father knows it and has decided not to think about it, and that's what being my father is. I don't know yet if I can do that. I'd like to. It'd be easier."

The mud breathed and burst. The steam went up.

"My mother told me about Bassus," she said.

He did not turn. He had known; she had seen him know on the stair in June, had seen him not say it, had put that beside the rest. "On the road?"

"On the wall where it turns. In June. She said I'd got his nose and the widow saw it in one look and so would anyone who'd ever stood in that atrium and looked up instead of down." She heard her own voice saying it, level, into the yellow air, and thought that her mother would have been pleased. "She said someone in Rome would say it in a corridor to see what my face did, and she wanted it to do nothing. So I'm saying it to you first. In a place where it can go straight down." She nodded at the mouth. "Say it. Say what they'll say. I want to practise."

He turned then. He looked at her for a long moment with the steam going up between them and the whole white bowl around them and the smell in both their mouths. She saw him decide — she could see him do it now, the small settling behind the face that was the sum coming out — and he said, in a voice that was not his, that was a corridor voice, light and curious and pleased with itself: "They say you're Bassus's get. They say the old man's freedwoman down on the coast threw a bastard and he put her in a whorehouse to keep it quiet. Is it true? You've his nose."

Her face did nothing. She felt it do nothing; she felt it from the inside, like a shutter going to. She looked at him the way she had looked at Thallus in the atrium, the way the lady had said, steadily and at no cost, and she said, "Yes. And?"

And he laughed. It was not a thing he did often and it came out of him sideways, surprised, and he put his hand over his mouth as if to catch it and failed, and stood in the burning fields with his shoulders shaking. She stood and watched him and felt the shutter come open again, slowly, the way the ground did, and found that she was laughing too, which she had not expected and did not entirely understand, and that it hurt, low down, where the pulling had been since the sixth of September, and that she did not mind.

"That's it," he said, when he could. "That's exactly it. God. You'll do. You'll have them in the Tiber."

"The widow said her girls would have *me* in the Tiber."

"The widow's never heard you say *and*." He wiped his eyes. "Do it to Thallus. Next time he asks you a sum. Give him the sum and then look at him like that and say *and?* and see what his face does. I'd give the denarius to see it."

"You've still got it?"

"I've still got it. I'm not spending it till I know what it bought." He looked down at the mouth again, and the laughter went out of his face the way it had come, sideways, and left it older. "I'm going on the Kalends. Rufus wants me in Rome before the senate sits; there's a thing about the Danube supply that he's been given to look at and he can't read a manifest. Then — I don't know. If it's this year, it's this year. If it's next, it's next. Either way I'll not be at the Dolphin again before it, because my father's forbidden it and this time he's right, and there's no tutor's room to be at, now Nicias is gone up to write the Spaniard's letters. So this is —" He stopped.

"This is the mouth of the underworld," she said, "and you've looked in it. That's what you came for."

"Yes."

"Then let's go down. The ground's hot and the Rhodian's in and you've got a hull to count." She turned from the pit. And then, because they were in a place where things could go straight down and because she had practised one thing that afternoon and might as well practise another, she said over her shoulder, not looking at him: "Write. From the Danube. Not what's true. What you saw. I'll work out the true part."

He was quiet behind her for the length of three steps on the hot white ground. Then he said, "All right. What I saw." And then, lower, in his own voice, not the corridor one: "I saw you laugh. I'll put that first. I've not seen it since the cake."

She did not turn round. She walked up out of the bowl with her face doing nothing and the yellow air in her mouth and the heat coming up through her sandals, and when she got to the lip where the ordinary hill began and the ground went cool she stopped, and waited for him, and when he came up beside her she took his hand — the first time; she had never, in six years, done it; they had sat on steps and keels and the ends of moles and had never once touched on purpose — and held it, hard, for the length of a breath, and let go, and said, "There. That's the true part. Now you've got it. Don't write it down," and went on down the hill ahead of him toward the town and the smoke and the Dolphin and the Rhodian's eggs, and he came after, and neither of them said anything more about it, then or in the letters, or for a very long time.

* * *

He went on the Kalends. She did not see him go; she was at the hearth, because the Rhodian wanted eggs, and because her mother was slow that morning, and because a girl who went down to the Capua road to watch a boy on a cart was a girl who would be talked about in a town where six people did not know by nightfall what the other thirty-nine thousand had done. But she heard the cart. She heard the mules' bells go by at the end of the lane, and the jars talking under the sail. She stood with the ladle in her hand and counted, and got to a hundred and ten before the sound went under the sound of the harbour, and then she put the ladle down and went out into the yard, where the goddess was in her niche with the pink ghost of a face and a saucer the cats had been at, and she stood in front of her for a while and did not ask for anything.

Her mother came out and found her there.

"He's gone?"

"Yes."

"Good. One of you's out of it, then." Hilara put her hand on the wall, which she did now, in the yard, where there was nothing to hold. "The Rhodian says the widow's door had a new knocker on it, bronze, in the shape of a hand. He says he put the letter in the hand. He says her steward came out and looked at the seal and looked at him and said *Puteoli?* like a man who'd been expecting the word." She looked at the goddess. "So that's two of you out of it, by the spring. And then it's me and the Dolphin and the man with the key, the way it was before either of you, and I'll tell you a thing, Fausta, since we're in the yard: I find I don't mind. I've had thirteen years of being a house, and I'm tired, and I'd like to be a woman at a hearth for a while with nobody's paper in me. Let the widow have you. Let the Danube have him. I'll keep the goddess fed." She pushed off from the wall. "Eggs. The man's waiting."

And Fausta went in and did the eggs, and the season went on, and the fleet went home, and the smoke came down the hill every morning and lay in the streets till noon, the same as it always had, the same as it would when all of them were ash; and on the far side of the world, on a river she could not picture, a soldier who had been told what the city wanted was looking across the water at a kingdom and deciding, in a room, that it did not matter. She did not know this, and it did not yet know her, and the ground under all of them went on not pretending.
