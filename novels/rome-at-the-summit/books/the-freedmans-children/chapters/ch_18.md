# By Weight

Crescens died on the eleventh day, at the second wall, in the morning, and it took him the length of time it took to say so.

Celer saw it because he was there, which he should not have been, and was because Sura had sent him up with a sheet for the Seventh's legate, a thing about water, the king's conduits having been found and cut and the staff wanting to know by noon how many days the city had in its cisterns, and the legate had been at the second wall where the engines were, and Celer had gone up to the second wall, on foot, with the sheet, between the mantlets, with the stones coming down from the king's towers at the long slow intervals that meant they were saving them, and had found the legate, and given him the sheet, and turned, and seen a man he knew at the foot of a ladder.

He had not seen him in four years. Four years, a war, a tent-camp, a bridge; the Seventh had wintered on the far bank both winters and the staff had wintered in Rome, and the house's post went through Philon and not through the Seventh's lines, and the last he had had of Crescens was a line in his father's hand in the first war's second year, *the centurion asks after you; I've sent him oil*. And here he was, at the foot of a ladder, in the morning, under the second wall of the king's city, with the transverse crest on and the vine-stick in his belt and the Seventh's cohort round him, forty-seven now, grey in the beard, the ugliness and the kindness both gone to leather; and he looked round, as a man did who had been in walls for twenty-six years and felt a thing behind him, and saw a tall freedman's son in a staff tunic with a ring on his hand, and the leather face did a thing Celer had seen once before, on a quay, when a Seventh man had come off the *Pelican* and said *wine*.

"Well, look at that," said Crescens. "The nose. Lucius Sura's boy, they say. With a ring." He did not come over; he was a centurion at the foot of a ladder and a ladder was a thing you did not leave. He raised the vine-stick, a little, by way of greeting, and the voice came across the ten feet of trampled ground and the noise exactly as it had come across a moonlit beach at Castra Vetera, *the Rhine. Be ashamed later, it's cheaper*. "Your father's made it, then. Good. He'd the right of it from the start; I'd not have given you a sword if you'd begged. Get down off this wall, boy, you've no helmet. Go and count something."

"Sir —"

"That's *not* a thing I'm saying twice." And he turned back to the ladder, and put his hand on it, and looked up its length at the wall and the king's men on it, and said to the men round him in the flat Picene Latin a thing Celer did not hear, and the men laughed; and the stone came down.

It was not a big one. That was what he would remember, with the rest, in the order he kept things: it was not one of the great ones from the towers that came at the long slow intervals, it was a hand-stone, a thing a man on a wall threw, the size of a loaf, and it came off the parapet above the ladder without a sound in all that sound and struck Crescens on the side of the head below the crest where the helmet's cheek-piece did not reach, and the centurion of the Seventh who had been at Vetera in Domitian's day and had carried a freedman's son across the Rhine on a boat and sat on a crate on a quay and said *there was never a right moment, that's the trick of them*, went down, as a sack went down, all at once, with the vine-stick still in his belt and his hand still on the ladder, so that the hand dragged on the rung as he fell and left skin on it. He did not make a sound. He lay at the foot of the ladder on his side with the leather face turned up and one eye open, and the eye was looking at nothing, and the blood came out of the ear on the struck side slowly, as if it too had been told there was no right moment, and then faster; and the men round him did what men round a fallen centurion did, which was to look at him for the time it took to see, and then at the optio, and the optio said the thing the optio said, and the cohort went up the ladder anyway, over him, because that was what the ladder was for; and the legate of the Seventh, who had Sura's sheet in his hand and had seen it all, looked at the staff boy with no helmet standing ten feet from a dead man with his mouth open, and said, not unkindly, in the voice of a man who had seen a thousand of these and would see a thousand more, "You knew him?"

"Yes, sir. He — yes."

"Then you'll want to tell someone. Tell them it was quick. It's the only thing anyone wants to know and it's even true." The legate looked at the sheet. "Four days in the cisterns, tell Sura. Five if they stop washing. Now get off my wall."

He got off the wall. He went down between the mantlets with the stones coming at the long slow intervals and his hands at his sides and the noise all round him and he did not run, and he did not weep, and he did not have Homer, and at the bottom, in the lee of the first wall, where the engineers were, he stopped and was sick into the trampled ground, briefly, the way a man was who had not eaten, and wiped his mouth, and went on to the staff tent and told Sura four days, five if they stopped washing, and Sura looked at him and said, "What else?" and he said, "A centurion of the Seventh. Crescens. He took me across the Rhine. A hand-stone, at the second wall," and Sura said, "Was it quick?" and he said, "Yes, sir," and Sura said, "Then write to whoever it is and tell them so, tonight, before you've thought about it. A thing like that's better written by a man who's still stupid with it. Tomorrow you'll want to make it mean something and it didn't."

He wrote to his father that night, by the lamp, the four lines, *Crescens. The second wall. A hand-stone. It was quick*, and under them, because he was still stupid with it: *he said get down off this wall, boy, you've no helmet, go and count something. I did.* And he sealed it and gave it to the post and fed the Lares from Nola and did not sleep, and in the morning the king's city had four days of water and the staff knew it and so, by noon, from the towers, did the king.

They burned the Seventh's dead on the third evening, below the first wall, where the ground was flat enough, on a pyre of the king's own timber from the breached palisade, and he went down to it, because Sura said *go*, in the voice that was not a kindness, and stood at the back of the cohort with his hands at his sides while the optio who had said the thing said the other thing, the one that was said at pyres, in the flat Picene Latin, badly, because the optio was a man who could hold a ladder and not a man who could speak; and the fire took, and the cohort stood, and the smoke went up the mountain toward the city that had four days of water, and the men on the king's towers watched it go up and did not throw anything, because there were things even a man with four days of water did not do.

Afterward the optio found him. He was a short man with a broken nose and the look that optios had, of a man who had been doing a centurion's work for a year without the pay and would now have the pay and would rather not; and he had a thing in his hand wrapped in a bit of cloak, and he held it out, and said, "You're the shipowner's son. Hermes's. He talked about you. Not much; he didn't talk much; but when the oil came from Puteoli he'd say *that's the boy's father* and pass it round, and we'd know." He unwrapped the cloak. "This is yours, he said. If it came to it. He said it two winters ago, drunk, on the far bank, when the Pannonian horse had been through us and we'd lost nine; he said *if it comes to it the stick goes to Hermes's boy, he'll know what it's for, and if he doesn't his father will*. So."

It was the vine-stick. The centurion's, the vitis, the length of a forearm, a thing cut from a vine on a Picene hill and seasoned and carried for twenty-six years and used, on men's backs, for the things men's backs were used on, and worn smooth at the grip by one hand until it had the shape of the hand in it; and the optio put it in his and he stood with it below the first wall of the king's city with the smoke going up and found that it weighed, like the ring, less than he had thought a thing like that would weigh, and that this was going to be true, he understood, of every thing he was ever handed that had been described to him first.

"I don't know what it's for," he said.

"No. He said you wouldn't." The optio wrapped the cloak back round his own hands, which were empty now and did not seem to know it. "He said: *he'll put it in a box and count it. That's what it's for. Somebody ought to.*" And the broken nose went back to the cohort, and the cohort went back up the hill to the ladders, because the city had three days of water now and the ladders did not hold themselves.

He put it in the box. He did not count it; there was one of it. He wrote on the inside of the lid, in the shorthand, *Crescens. Vetera. The Rhine. The Pelican. The second wall*, which was the inventory, and closed the lid, and went back to the stool, and that night when he fed the Lares from Nola he set the stick beside them on the camp-table in the tent, because a woman at a loom had said that the gods liked to see a face and he had decided, on a beach, at fourteen, that a man was his face, and this was the nearest to one that Crescens had left.

* * *

The head came in on the sixth day after, in a bag, and he counted that too.

He would have said, if anyone had asked, that he had not expected it to be a thing he saw. He was on a stool in a tent with a box; the war happened outside and came in as sheets. But the king had broken out of the burning city with his guard on the fourth night, west, into the mountains, the way the sum had said he would, because a man with nothing to bargain with bargained with distance; and the emperor had sent the cavalry after him, as the sum had said; and the cavalry were auxiliaries, Pannonians and Moesians under Roman decurions, and one of the decurions, a Moesian from Philippi called Tiberius Claudius Maximus who had been a trooper in the Seventh when Crescens was a young centurion and had been promoted out of it for being the kind of man who did not stop, had run the king to earth in a gully at the end of a ridge with the horses blown and the guard dead or gone, and had come up on him with four troopers as the king sat against a rock with his own curved knife in his hand; and the king had looked at the Moesian and the Moesian at the king, and the king had put the knife into his own throat, under the beard, and drawn it across, as a man did who had been told there was no right moment and had decided to make one; and the Moesian had got off his horse and gone to him and taken the knife out of his hand and the head off his shoulders, because that was the thing you brought back, and had ridden four days to the staff camp below the city with it in a saddlebag and had come into the emperor's tent at noon with the bag in his hand and the whole of the staff standing, and had put it on the table in front of the emperor, and said, in the Latin of Philippi, "Decebalus, lord. He did it himself. I was too late for anything but this."

Celer was at the back, against the tent-wall, with the box.

He saw it. The emperor stood up, which he did not do for legates, and looked at the thing on the table, and it was a man's head, with the beard and the long hair and the cap the Dacian nobles wore still on it, somehow, the Moesian having been careful, and the eyes half open and the mouth a little open and the throat under the beard cut in a way that was not neat and was the king's own doing; and it was a face, as Apollonios's had been a face, and it had been an enemy of Rome for twenty years and had taken Fuscus's eagle and made terms and broken them and built a city on a mountain with conduits and cisterns and had looked at the Moesian in a gully and done the sum. The emperor looked at it for a long time. The staff did not move. And then the emperor said, in the slow Spanish Latin that had made Hermes of Puteoli stand up in an inn, to the Moesian, "You were too late for nothing. You were in time. That's the whole of soldiering; a man who's in time," and took off his own ring, the signet, not the gold, and put it on the Moesian's hand, and said, "Decurion no longer. You'll have your rank by the Kalends and a sheet to say why. Sura; see it's written so a man can read it on a stone in Philippi." And then to the tent: "Have it carried round the camp. Every legion. Let them see it. Then send it to Rome, to the steps, where the Senate can see it, since they've been asking what the terms are." And he sat down again, and the sheet in front of him was the one about the conduits, and he went back to it.

Sura wrote the Moesian's citation that evening, and Celer sat on the stool beside him and told him one thing, which was the name of the Seventh's centurion who had been on the Rhine when the Moesian had been a trooper there, and Sura wrote it in, *formerly of the Seventh Claudia under the centurion Crescens*, and looked at him sideways and said, "That's for the stone in Philippi. Not for you. I'll not have you think I've done you a kindness," and Celer said, "No, sir," and understood that the man had, and that the understanding was the thing that must not be shown, and did not show it; and Sura, who could read anything, read that, and nodded once, and went back to the sheet.

* * *

Before the treasure there was the list, and the list was people, and he wrote it.

The city had burned and the king was a head in a bag and what was left on the mountain and in the valleys below it was a kingdom with nobody in charge of it, and the emperor wanted it counted. Not the gold; that came after. The people. How many, where, of what kind, able to walk, able to work, able to be sold, able to be settled, able to be sent to the Syrian's cut in Rome to carry baskets. Sura gave it him on a sheet in the Spanish, *the inventory of the kingdom; begin*, and he began, on a stool in a tent on the far bank with the Seventh's clerks bringing him tallies from the valleys and the auxiliaries' decurions bringing him tallies from the hill-forts and the slave-dealers who had followed the army up from Moesia like birds behind a plough bringing him their own tallies, which were higher, because a dealer counted what he hoped.

Fifty thousand. That was what it came to, by the end of September, on his sheets, in columns — men, women, children under ten, children over, the old, the hurt, the nobles separately because nobles were a different price and a different problem — and he wrote it the way he had written a granary and a sail, in the square hand, with no story, and the figure was the figure, and it was fifty thousand people, and he sat on the stool with the sheet and understood that he had just done the thing his father had said a man in Rome did who was a man, which was to write a number that other men would be moved by, and that the men being moved were in the columns and could not read them.

Sura read it. He read it the way he read everything, fast, with his lips still, and put it down, and said, "That's a province. That's what a province is; it's a sheet like that with a governor's name at the top. You've written the first one of this reign that wasn't a story. Hadrian will read it tonight and write four pages about it in good Greek for the box." He coughed, which was new that autumn, and looked at the boy on the stool. "You've a face. Say it."

"They're people, sir."

"Yes. Fifty thousand of them. And in ten years half will be dead and a quarter will be farming in Moesia under Roman names and a tenth will have carried the Syrian's hill away in baskets and a few hundred will be the household slaves of men who sat in the Senate today and voted the games, and one or two will be free, and rich, and will build tombs by a road with their names cut a hand taller than their patrons', and will sit in strong-rooms with the lid off teaching their sons what a thing weighs." Nothing in the thin face moved. "Your father was on a sheet like that once. Delos, probably, or Puteoli's own market; I don't know; he's never said and you've never asked. He was a figure in a column and a man wrote him and a man bought him and he's spent forty years making sure his son would be the one with the stylus and not the one in the column. He's done it. You're holding it. Fifty thousand people in your hand on a stool and you're the one who wrote them." A breath. "Don't put it down. That's the only thing I've got to teach you about that sheet. Men who put it down are the ones who stop seeing it's people, and men who can't pick it up are no use to anyone. Hold it. It's heavy. It's meant to be."

He held it. He gave it to the courier for the Senate with the rest. And that night, with the Lares fed and Crescens's stick beside them, he did not write to his father about it, because there were things his father had never said and he had decided, on a stool, with fifty thousand people in his hand, that he would not be the one to ask.

* * *

The treasure was under the river.

It came out in August, from a man called Bicilis, who had been the king's friend, in the way that men who had been a king's friend in a burning city were anyone's friend by the time they reached the staff tent; and what he had, for his life and a farm on the Moesian bank, was the river. The Sargetia ran under the city's mountain in a gorge, and the king, in the winter after the first war's terms, when he had known what the terms meant and what he would do about them, had taken a cohort of his own prisoners and diverted it — a dam above, a channel cut round, the bed laid bare for two hundred paces — and dug the bed, and put into the digging everything the kingdom had that could not be spent on a war he had already lost, and filled the digging, and broken the dam, and let the river back, and killed the prisoners. Bicilis had held the torch. He drew it, on a sheet, in the staff tent, with a shaking hand: the bend, the dam-site, the paces from a rock shaped like a sheep.

Apollodorus did it in nine days with four cohorts and the Seventh's engineers. Celer watched him do it; the staff went up to the gorge, because the emperor went, because this was the thing the war had cost and the emperor wanted to see it come up out of the ground with his own eyes; and the Syrian stood on the sheep-rock with lime on his arms and shouted, and the dam went up and the channel went round and the river, which had run in its bed under the king's mountain since before there were kings, moved over, sullenly, in a brown slide, and left the bed bare and steaming in the August sun, two hundred paces of wet stones and silt and the bones, already, of the men who had dug it the first time, lying where they had been thrown in before the water came back; and the cohorts went down into it with mattocks.

It came up on the third day of digging. It came up as the sum had said it would, not as a thing but as a column: first a stone slab, then under it a chamber lined with oak, then in the chamber the jars, and in the jars, when the first was broached and tipped, in the bed of a river in front of the emperor of Rome and his staff and a Syrian with lime on his arms, the gold. Not coin. The kingdom had not minted much. Bar and ingot and vessel, the plate of the king's house, the arm-rings of twenty years of nobles, the cups of the temples, the things taken from Fuscus's dead in Domitian's day with the eagles still on them, all of it gone to lumps and bars in the king's furnaces that last winter so it would pack, and packed, and sunk, in jars, under a river, by a man who had known he would lose and had wanted, at the end, to have the last word in the only language the people he was losing to understood.

The emperor looked at it. He said, "Sura. Weigh it. All of it. Every bar. I'll have a figure for the Senate that's a figure and not a story." And Sura turned on the sheep-rock and looked at the boy with the box and said, "Celer. You've a stool. Get it."

So he weighed it. He sat on a stool on the sheep-rock above the bed of the Sargetia for eleven days with a scale the engineers had built out of a beam and a quartermaster's weights, and a tablet, and two clerks from the Seventh, and the cohorts brought it up out of the river in baskets and set it on the pan and he wrote the figure, and the clerk read it back, and the other clerk marked the bar with a punch, and it went into the wagon, and the next came. Bar. Ingot. Cup, flattened. Arm-ring, cut. A thing that had been a breastplate. A thing that had been an eagle, once, and had been melted and had not quite taken and still had, in the lump, the line of a wing. He weighed them all, and wrote them, and did not look at the line of the wing longer than at anything else, because he was on a stool and that was what he was for, and he was good at it; and on the fourth day, with the tablet in his lap and the pan going down under a bar the size of his forearm, he understood what he was doing, and stopped, with the stylus up.

He was doing it by weight.

Not by measure. Not by jar or basket or bar; by the pan. *Pondere, non mensura.* The fourth line of the second column of a sail-maker's bill in his father's strong-room when he was eleven, read aloud to a girl who had said *all right* and looked at him while she said it, the thing his father had taught him was a figure and that a woman on the Caelian had, by now — he did not know this; he knew it; he had been told by nobody and knew it as he had known Apollonios would be in the room before he was — had decided what it was worth. He sat on the sheep-rock with a king's gold on the pan and a river going by in a channel a Syrian had cut and knew, with the clarity that came after the sum and never before it, that somewhere behind him, in Rome, on a hill, the house with the columns that weren't marble was being read, line by line, by someone who had been eleven in the strong-room, and that the line being read was this one, and that he was sitting on a rock doing it right, in front of an emperor, for a figure that would be a figure and not a story, while his father had done it wrong for ten years for a Subura baker's loaf that was a tenth water.

"Celer." Sura, from the wagon. "You've stopped."

"Sir." He wrote the figure. The clerk read it back. The punch came down. "Twenty-two pounds, four ounces. Bar. Forty-first."

"Good." Sura came up the rock with the knife-face and stood behind him and looked at the tablet over his shoulder, as he had looked at a granary sheet in a tent four years ago, and said, very quietly, in the Spanish, so the clerks would not have it: "You did the sum. I saw you do it. It went across your face like a sail going over. What was it?"

"My father, sir."

"Yes. I thought it might be. The prefect's people have been in his books since the autumn and the Augusta's had the figures from Puteoli since the spring and there's a sheet on my table in Rome that I've not opened and that Philon tells me has a girl's name under it." The voice did not change. "I'll open it when we're back. I'll set it aside. You'll read it. I'll not spare you that and I'll not pretend I would; it's the only way you'll ever be any use to me, if you've read the worst sheet there is with your own name on the next page and gone on counting." The hand came down on his shoulder, once, as it had on the stool on the Aventine when he was fourteen and had said *he's going to be emperor*; a weight, and gone. "Now weigh the king's gold. All of it. By weight. It's the one honest thing that's happened on this river and I want a boy from Puteoli to have done it, because the Senate'll ask who did the figures and I'll tell them, and some of them will laugh, and the ones who don't will be the ones to watch."

He weighed the king's gold. Eleven days. The figure, when it was done and the last wagon had gone down the gorge toward the Syrian's bridge and the road and Rome, was a figure, and not a story, and he gave it to Sura on a sheet in the square hand and Sura gave it to the emperor and the emperor read it and said *that'll pay for the forum* and gave it to the Senate's courier; and the figure went down the road behind the head, and became, in Rome, within the year, a story anyway, because that was what Rome did with figures, and the story had no boy in it, and the ones who did not laugh asked, as Sura had said they would, and were told, and remembered.

* * *

The Spaniard came to the sheep-rock on the ninth day.

He came up the gorge on foot from the Minervia's lines with two of his own staff and the lime of the dam on his boots and stood below the rock in the August evening looking at the river in its channel and the bed laid bare and the baskets coming up, and then at the boy on the stool with the scale, and said in the Greek, to nobody, "He's done it by weight. Of course he has. Lucius told me he would and I didn't believe him; I thought you'd count the bars and be done. Nobody weighs. Weighing's for merchants." The grey eyes came up. "You're a merchant's son."

"A shipowner's, sir."

"Same god, different altar." The Spaniard climbed the rock. He was thirty, and a legate, and had taken four hill-forts and written sixteen sheets about them, and he stood on the sheep-rock beside the stool as if it were a couch on the Aventine, and looked at the tablet, and read it, upside down, fast, the way Sura read, and said, "Twenty-two pounds four ounces, forty-first bar. You've a hand like a surveyor's. Apollodorus wants you for the forum; he's told the emperor so, in front of me, which is how I know he means it. Lucius says no. Lucius says *until I'm dead*." He looked at the river. "Lucius has a cough this year. Have you noticed?"

Celer had noticed. He said nothing.

"No. You wouldn't say. That's why he keeps you." The Spaniard's face did the thing it did, the thing that was not quite a smile and was the look of a man who found everything interesting, and a little short of what it might have been. "I'll not ask you to leave him. I asked once and he told me his sum and he was right. I'll tell you a thing instead, since we're on a rock and Lucius isn't, and you can do what you like with it, which is what you'll do anyway." He crouched, so that the grey eyes were level with the stool. "The sheet on Lucius's table. The one with the girl's name under it. I've read it. Not the original; the Augusta's copy; she sends me what she thinks I'll want, which is most things. I know what it says and I know what your father did and I know, because the Augusta told me, in the one sentence she spends on such things, what the girl paid to say it, which was a year of her tongue and a thing she'd rather have kept." He let that sit. "Lucius will set it aside for you, because that's Lucius; he thinks a man's made by the worst sheet. I think a man's made by what he does on the next page. And I think, Hermes's son, that when you've read it four ways you'll pick the wrong one, because you're nineteen and you were raised on a figure, and I'd like you to know, before you do, that there's a man on this hill who's read it and read *her* and thinks she did the only thing a woman in a room with no door could have done, and would have done it himself, and has." He stood. "Weigh your gold. Apollodorus says the wing on the forty-first was the Fifth Alaudae's and I think he's right; it's the only eagle Fuscus lost that wasn't found."

He went down the rock. His staff fell in behind him. Celer sat with the stylus and the tablet and the forty-second bar on the pan and the Spaniard's back going away down the gorge in the evening with the lime on its boots, and did not weigh the bar for the length of time it took the river to move a hundred paces in its channel, and then did.

"Nineteen pounds, eleven ounces. Bar. Forty-second."

"Nineteen eleven, forty-two," said the clerk, and the punch came down.

And that night, in the tent, by the lamp, with the Lares fed and the figure for the day in the box and Sura coughing two tents away, he did the thing that Sura had told him not to do and that he had not done for Crescens and did now, which was to try to make it mean something; and he sat with the tablet on his knee and wrote, in the square hand, on a sheet he would not send, in the house's shorthand that only one other living person could read: *F. — I've weighed a king by the pan on a rock in a river. 41st bar had a wing on it. Crescens is dead; a hand-stone; it was quick; he said go and count something. You'll have told them by now. I know what. I know why. I've not read it and I know, because I'm on a stool and that's what I'm for and it's not for nothing. I'll read it four ways when I'm back and I'll pick one and the Spaniard says I'll pick wrong. Say "and?" I've nothing after it. — C.*

He read it once. He held it to the lamp. He watched it go, the way he had sat on the end of a mole in the dark with a girl's hand flat on his chest holding him at the length of her arm, and no further; and when it was gone he put the stylus down and sat with his hands open on his knees in the tent on the far bank of the Danube in the last year of a kingdom and listened to Lucius Licinius Sura cough, and counted the coughs, because he counted everything, and the number was higher than it had been in June.
