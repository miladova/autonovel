# The Tomb

His father took him to see the tomb on the morning after the news, because that was what his father did with news: he went and stood in front of the thing he was building and measured it against what had changed.

The tomb was on the Capua road, a quarter-mile out past the amphitheatre, where the land levelled and the monuments of the town began — a street of the dead running out into the country with the living hurrying along it between them. Hermes had bought the plot in the year Domitian died. He had told his son the price, which was a thing he did with every price, so that the boy would carry the figures of the family's rise in his head like a second set of bones. He had bought it from a decayed decurion's heirs who had wanted ready money, and it was a good plot, on the right-hand side going out of town, where the morning sun struck, between the monument of a sea-captain and the enclosure of a family of dyers who had been in Puteoli since before the Greeks left and had, in consequence, the air of regarding the whole road as their yard.

They walked out. His father did not keep a litter, on principle; he said a man who had been carried as a slave — not literally; he had been a clerk — had no business being carried as a free one, and that the day he could not walk to his own tomb he would have earned the right to be laid in it. Celer walked at his left and a step behind, which was the place, and the boy Daphnis's son came behind with the tablets. They went up through the town in the early light with the shops opening and the smoke coming down the hill, and people said *Hermes* to his father as they passed, and some said *Augustalis*, and he gave them each the precise degree of nod that their saying it had earned.

He, Celer, had learned the degrees by watching. There was the nod for a man who was Laecanius too, which was slight and lateral, a nod between equals who both knew they were not. There was the nod for a man who was free-born and poor, which was fuller, because that man had the one thing that could not be bought and it was polite to acknowledge it even in a man who had nothing else. There was the nod for the magistrates, which was not a nod but a lifting of the hand, as between men of business. And there was the nod he gave to the decurions' sons who sometimes came through the macellum in the mornings on their way to the baths, which was not visible at all, and which his father gave anyway, inside, where it cost nothing and could be collected on later.

"You saw the steward," his father said, when they were past the amphitheatre and the road had opened out.

"At the steps. He gave me a denarius."

"For what?"

"For telling him what the ship said."

"Which he knew."

"Yes."

"And you took it."

"Yes."

His father walked on a few paces. "Good," he said. "A man who refuses a coin from a steward is telling the steward he thinks he's above him, and the steward will remember that and so will the master when the steward tells him. A man who takes it is telling the steward he understands how the house works. You understood. That's worth more than the denarius. Keep the denarius too." He glanced sideways. "He say anything about the villa?"

"The Ides of March. He wants to be on the coast if the emperor comes south."

"*If.*" His father made the word into a small dry sound. "He won't come south. He's in Cologne with the legions and he'll go up the Danube first, to show the Pannonian army his face, because the Pannonian army is the one that could have made someone else. Then Rome. Autumn at the earliest. Next year more like. But Bassus will open the villa in March, because Bassus is a senator, and senators don't know how to wait. It's what's wrong with them." He said this as a man might remark on a defect in a breed of dog. "We'll be ready. I want the spring oil moved before the Ides and I want the big warehouse swept and whitened so that when the steward walks through it he sees a house that is doing well and has nothing to hide. Write that down."

Celer took the tablet from the boy behind and wrote it down.

* * *

The tomb was not finished. It had been not finished for four years, and would be, Celer sometimes thought, not finished for ever, because his father went on changing what he wanted of it as the family's prospects changed, and the family's prospects had done nothing but rise since the day the plot was bought.

It had begun as a simple thing, a brick enclosure with an altar and a space for the urns. Then the shipping had gone well — the Alexandrian contract, the three hulls, the warehouse — and the enclosure had got a facing of stone. Then his father had been made one of the Augustales, which was the thing he had wanted for ten years and had paid, Celer knew because he had been shown the figure, the price of a hull to get; and the Augustales had rights, among them the right to have the fasces and the magistrate's chair shown on your monument, and so a mason had been hired to cut a relief. The relief had grown, and now the front of the tomb was a thing you could not walk past without looking at. There was Hermes, in his toga, seated in the curule chair he was entitled to sit in on two days of the year. There were the two lictors who attended him on those days. There was a ship, below, under sail, and on the ship men at work, and if you knew the house you could see that the men had Greek faces and the ship had the lines of the *Isis*, which was the first of the three hulls and the one his father never named without touching something made of wood.

The inscription was not cut yet. His father had been composing it for four years and had got as far as the names and the offices. *C. LAECANIUS C. L. HERMES, AUGUSTALIS, SHIPOWNER OF THE ALEXANDRIAN TRADE.* The *L.* was there, because it had to be; a freedman who left it off his tomb was a freedman who could be prosecuted, and a man who had spent his life learning the law so as to stand exactly on the right side of it did not stumble at the end. But the mason had instructions. The *L.* was to be cut last, and smaller, and in a slightly different hand, so that it would look, to anyone who did not stop to count the letters, like the kind of abbreviation a stonecutter squeezes in when he has misjudged his line.

"And here," his father said, standing in front of the blank space under the ship, "is where you go."

He said it every time. It was not morbid; it was a kind of accounting.

"*C. LAECANIUS C. F. CELER.* Son of Gaius. *F*, not *L*. Nobody will ever cut an *L* after your name, not on this stone, not on any stone. That's what the whole of this is for." He gestured at the tomb, the relief, the lictors, the ship, the thirty years. "I'm a freedman. I'll be a freedman when I'm ash. You're the son of a Roman citizen, born in his house, in his marriage, to his wife —" he did not say *first* wife; he never said it — "and the law doesn't know the difference between you and the son of a consul. The law is the only thing in this world that doesn't know the difference. Men do. Men always will. So you have to make it so that men have to go through the law to get at you. You understand?"

"Yes, Father."

"You don't. Not yet. But you've a good memory and one day it'll come back to you, and that'll do." He put his hand briefly on the back of his son's neck. It was not a caress. It was the way he put his hand on a hull he was inspecting, to feel whether it was sound. "There's an emperor dead and a soldier in his place and Bassus is going to be scared for a year and spend money to show he isn't. Scared senators do two things. They borrow, and they look for men to stand in front of them. We'll lend. And when the time comes, you'll stand."

"When the time comes for what?"

But his father was already turning away, toward the road, and the boy with the tablets, and the warehouse that wanted whitening, and the morning that was getting on. "Rome," he said over his shoulder, as if it were a place one could simply walk to, as one walked to one's tomb. "When the time comes."

* * *

His stepmother was at her loom when they came in, which was where she always was at that hour and which, he had come to understand, was where she arranged to be seen to be. Vibia Procula was freeborn. It was the first thing anyone said about her and it was, very nearly, the last; she had been the daughter of a man with a small farm and a large name near Nola, a man whose family had had the citizenship since the Social War and nothing else since, and when his father had come looking for a second wife — a *mother for the boy*, he had said, though the boy had been nine and in no want of one — he had gone looking in exactly that stratum, among the free and the poor, and had paid for her what she was worth to him, which was a good deal.

She was thin. She had a long upper lip and pale eyes and a way of holding herself very straight at the loom as though someone might at any moment come in to draw her. She was pious in the old way, the way of the farm, and kept the household gods with a care that his father found restful and the slaves found exacting, and she had given his father, two years since, a second son, whom his father had named Lucius after no one, so that the boy would have a name with no history in it at all. Celer did not dislike her. He had tried, in the first year, and failed, because she had never once given him cause; she treated him with the exact, measured courtesy of a woman who has been brought into a house to perform a function and intends to perform it perfectly, and who understands that the elder son is part of the function.

"You went to the tomb," she said, not looking up from the loom.

"Yes, Mother."

"He's worried, then."

He was silent. She looked up. She had a trick of looking up that was almost like a question and not quite.

"He goes to the tomb when he's worried," she said. "He thinks I don't know. Men always think the thing they do most often is the thing nobody notices." The shuttle went across. "The new emperor is a Spaniard and a soldier and nobody knows him, and your father has three ships and a contract with the grain office that was signed under Nerva by a freedman who will not be at the grain office by summer. That's the worry. He won't say it to you because he doesn't want you to carry it. I'm saying it to you because you'll carry it anyway, and you may as well know what it weighs."

He had not known she knew about the contract. He had not known she knew about anything. He stood by the loom and felt, as he sometimes felt with Nicias, that he had been living in a house with more rooms than he had been shown.

"What should I do?" he said.

"Nothing. You're eleven." The shuttle went across. "But when he tells you to do something, do it quickly and do it well, and don't ask why in front of anyone. He'll tell you why when it's safe to. He always does." She looked up again. "And stay away from the lodging-house for a while."

Something in him went still, the way the harbour went still before the wind changed.

"Why?"

"Because Thallus will be in the town for a month, opening the villa, and Thallus goes to the lodging-house, and your father doesn't want Thallus seeing his son come out of it. " She said it very evenly, looking at the thread. "It's not about the girl. It's about the steward. Everything in this house is about the steward until the master comes, and then it's about the master. You'll learn."

* * *

The villa at Baiae opened on the Ides, as the steward had said it would, and on the morning after, the freedmen of the house of Laecanius went up to pay their respects.

They went up together, because that was the custom, in a body from the lower town — Hermes at the front in the toga he wore four times a year, with his son beside him, and behind them the oil-man and the chandler and the mule-brothers and the sausage-woman and the gravedigger and the rest, perhaps forty people, the men in whatever white they owned and the women in their best, walking the coast road in the early sun with the bay on their left hand going blue. It was five miles. Nobody rode. You did not ride to your patron's door; you arrived on your feet, warm and a little dusty, so that he could see you had made the effort.

Celer looked for the Dolphin's people and found them near the back, where they always were. Hilara in a dark-red gown he had not seen before, walking very straight, with her hair up under a scarf. The girl beside her in something blue that had been let down at the hem. Antiope was not there; Antiope was not Laecania, she belonged to nobody in particular, which was a thing he had thought of, when he was younger, as a kind of freedom, and had come to think of as the opposite. He caught the girl's eye once, over the shoulders of the mule-brothers, and she looked at him and then away, and he understood from the way she did it that she had been told, too, to keep her distance while the steward was on the coast, and that she had worked out why faster than he had.

The villa was built on the headland above the hot springs in the way of all the villas on that coast, which was to say that it climbed; it went up the slope in terraces, each with its colonnade and its view, so that from the sea the whole hill looked like a staircase for giants with white pillars at every step. Bassus's grandfather had built the lowest terrace. Bassus's father had built the middle. Bassus had built nothing, and had let the roof of the upper bath-house go, and it was this, more than anything his father said, that told Celer where the family stood; because he had walked in through the great door with his father and looked up the terraces and seen, between the second and the third, a stretch of tile that had slipped and not been replaced. He knew what a slipped tile cost to fix, because his father had told him.

They were received in the atrium. It was a very large atrium, with a pool in the floor that had real fish in it and a skylight above through which the sun came down in a square, and around the walls the masks of the family's ancestors in their cupboards, wax faces, a dozen of them, going back to a consul under Nero whose name was on the base of the household shrine in letters as tall as a hand. The freedmen filed in and arranged themselves, the rich at the front, by instinct, as water finds its level. Hermes stood where the square of sun would strike him when the master came. Celer stood at his shoulder and looked at the masks.

He had seen them before. He saw them every year. But he looked at them that morning with the tomb in his head, and his father's blank stone, and he thought: that is what a name is. It is a wax face in a cupboard that your great-grandson takes out at funerals. And he thought: we have one face. It is my father's and it is not finished and it is on the Capua road with a ship under it. And he thought, with the cold clarity that came to him sometimes and that he had not yet learned to be afraid of: *the fish in that pool cost more to keep than the Dolphin makes in a month, and the tile on the third terrace has been down since last summer.*

Then the master came in and he stopped thinking.

* * *

Lucius Laecanius Bassus was sixty that year. He had been praetor under Vespasian and consul — a suffect consul, for two months, in a year nobody remembered — under Domitian, and he had held, since, a succession of the offices that are given to men who are owed something and cannot be given anything that matters: a curatorship of roads, a priesthood of the deified Titus, a seat on a commission about the Tiber banks. He had been in Rome for the killing of Domitian and had not been in the room; he had been in Rome for the mutiny of the Guard and had kept his doors shut; and he had, in sixteen months of Nerva, borrowed, as far as Celer's father could learn, about eight hundred thousand sesterces against the Baian estate at a rate that assumed the old man would live and the senate would rule. He was a tall man run to softness, with a fine head of white hair that he was known to be proud of and a face that had been handsome and was now only large. He had a way of standing with one hand on his hip under the fold of the toga and the other raised, slightly, as if about to bless or to beckon, and he had been standing that way, Celer thought, since before anyone in the room was born, and had never once been told it looked like anything.

He came in through the tablinum with his steward a pace behind and his son a pace behind that, and he stopped in the square of sun, and he said, "My people."

And they said, "Patron."

It went as it always went. He spoke. He said that an emperor had died and that the gods had given Rome another, and that he had every confidence in the senate's choice — *the senate's* — and that he had come down early this year because a man's place in uncertain times was among his own. He looked round the atrium as he said this as if to count them, and Celer saw his eye go over the rich at the front and slow, very slightly, at the dark-red gown at the back, and move on. Then he said that the steward would see them each in turn about the year's business, and that he hoped they would all dine, in the garden, at his expense, and that he was glad — here the raised hand came down, and rested, for a moment, on Hermes's shoulder — glad to see the house of Laecanius doing so well in his absence. The hand was on his father's shoulder for perhaps the time it took to breathe twice. His father did not move. But Celer, standing at his elbow, saw the colour come up the back of his father's neck above the toga, slowly, like a tide coming in over flat sand, and understood that he had just watched his father receive the one thing in the world he could not buy, and that it had cost the giver nothing, and that both of them knew it.

Then the master went out, and the steward took his place, and the real business of the morning began.

* * *

He met the son in the garden, over the dinner, which was bread and cold meats and a thin wine served at trestles under the fig trees on the second terrace, with the sea below and the slipped tile, if you looked, above.

Laecanius Rufus was twenty-eight. He had been quaestor two years since, which had cost his father a sum Celer's father had been unable to discover and had therefore estimated high, and he was now at the stage of a senatorial career where a man waited, and attached himself to people, and hoped. He had come down from Rome with the news and was going back with the spring, and in between he was doing what the sons of patrons did in the country, which was to walk about among his father's freedmen being gracious and finding it tiring. He was a well-made young man with his father's height and none of his softness, a boxer's shoulders under a plain tunic, a short beard in the new style that had come in with the Spanish soldier, and a face that Celer could not read and did not, for the moment, try to; he had only the sense, watching him move among the trestles, that here was a man who had been told what he was worth and had believed it, and was now discovering that the market disagreed.

Rufus found him by the wall where he had gone to look at the sea.

"You're Hermes's boy."

"Yes, sir."

"The one Nicias teaches. He writes to me about you. Did you know that? He writes to me about everything, the old fraud, he thinks I'm still eight." Rufus leaned on the wall beside him. He smelled of the baths and of a scent Celer could not name and later learned was Syrian and cost more an ounce than the Dolphin paid for oil in a month. "He says you've a head for figures and no Greek style at all."

"He says that to me too, sir."

"Does he? Good. He's right, probably. He usually is about the things that don't matter." Rufus looked at the sea. He had the look, Celer thought, of a man who had been looking at seas from villa terraces all his life and had stopped expecting them to do anything. "You know what's going to happen, I suppose. Your father will have told you."

Celer said nothing, because he did not know, and because saying nothing was what you did with the sons of patrons until you did.

"Trajan will come to Rome," Rufus said. "Next year. He'll settle the Guard and give the people a show and the senate a speech about liberty, and then he'll do what all of them do, which is look round for a war. There's one waiting. The Dacians have been taking Domitian's money for ten years and laughing at us across the Danube, and a soldier can't have that; he'll want to go up there and make it stop. And when he goes, every man in the senate under forty who wants to be anything will go with him, and I am under forty, and I want to be something." He said it without heat, as a man recites a sum. "I'll need a staff. A man on a staff needs people who can count, and write, and keep their mouths shut, and don't cost anything because they belong to him already. Your father has offered me you."

The sea went on doing what it did.

"When?" Celer said. His voice came out steady, which he noted with a kind of distant interest, as if it were someone else's.

"When it happens. Two years. Three. You'll be fourteen, fifteen. Old enough to hold a tablet." Rufus turned and looked at him properly for the first time, and there was something in the look that was not unkind and was not kind either; it was the look of a man pricing a horse he has been given and would not have chosen. "I'm telling you now because your father asked me to, and I'm telling you here because he didn't want it said in his house. He thinks you'll take it better from me. I think he's wrong, but it's his son." He straightened from the wall. "Think about Rome, boy. It's the only place anything happens. Everything else is just where people wait for news of it."

And he went back along the terrace toward his father's guests, and Celer stood by the wall with his hands on the warm stone and looked at the bay, and at the point beyond it, and at the town beyond that, white on its hill, with the mole going out into the water like a finger pointing east; and he found that he was looking for the Dolphin. That he could not pick it out from here, and that he had known he could not before he looked.

* * *

He told her on the shore below the town, three days later, in the evening, where the fishing boats were drawn up and the nets were spread on the stones and nobody from either house would think to look.

He had meant to tell it well. He had rehearsed it, walking down, the way he rehearsed Greek: *the patron's son is going to the war when it comes, and he'll need a staff, and my father has offered me, and I'll go.* He had meant to say it plainly, as a fact, so that she would see it was a fact and not a thing he had chosen or could un-choose. He had meant, if he was honest, to be a little grand about it.

She was sitting on the keel of an upturned boat with her feet in the shingle. She listened to the whole of it without moving. Then she said, "I know."

"You — how?"

"Antiope. A man from the villa was at the house on Tuesday. One of the Rome slaves, the ones who came down with the son. He told her in the morning. He said the young master had a freedman's boy for a secretary now and would be dragging him up to Rome to carry his tablets, and wasn't that a thing." She looked at him. "He said it before the son said it to you. So the whole villa knew first. And then the Dolphin. And then you."

He stood there in the shingle with his rehearsed sentence in his mouth, useless, and felt, for the first time in his life and not the last, the specific humiliation of discovering that a thing he had thought was his own had been passed from hand to hand before it reached him.

"Why didn't you say?"

"We weren't supposed to speak. The steward's in town." She said it without reproach, as a fact of weather. "Besides. It was yours to tell."

He sat down on the shingle beside the boat, because his legs had decided it for him. The stones were still warm from the day. Out on the bay the lamps of the fishing fleet were coming on one by one, and the smell of the sulphur was coming down off the hill the way it did in the evenings when the wind dropped.

"I'm not going to carry his tablets," he said. "I'm going to do his accounts. There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"Yes." He said it with more force than he meant. "His father's borrowed eight hundred thousand against the villa and the tile's off the roof. A man like that goes to war to get rich, and a man who goes to war to get rich needs someone to count it. That's not a slave's job. That's —" He stopped, because he did not have the word.

"That's your father's job," she said. "With a sword behind it."

He looked at her. She had the last of the light on the side of her face and she was not smiling and she was not sorry for him; she had simply said the thing, as she did, as Nicias did, and left it there for him to pick up. He picked it up. He turned it over. It was true.

"Yes," he said.

She was quiet a while. Then: "When?"

"When the war comes. Two years. Three."

"And you'll come back."

"Of course I'll come back. Everyone comes back from Rome. Antiope came back."

He said it lightly, and heard it land wrong even as it left him, and watched her hear it. She did not say anything. She looked at the fleet. And he understood — slowly, and then all at once, in the way he would come to understand most of the important things in his life, too late to do anything but watch — that he had just told the first lie of his life that mattered. Not *I'll come back*. He might. But *of course*, and *everyone*, and the lightness, which said that he had already begun to think of here as a place one came back to, and not as a place one was.

"I'll write," he said. It sounded, in his own ears, like something the Syrian might have said on the stairs.

"You'll be fourteen," she said. "You'll be in Rome with a senator's son. You'll be busy." And then, before he could answer, she slid down off the keel onto the shingle beside him, close enough that their shoulders touched, and said in a different voice, the one from the yard and the tutor's room and the mole: "Tell me about the villa. Did you see the fish? Antiope says there's a fish in the pool that's older than the master. She says it's got a ring in its lip that his grandfather put there."

So he told her about the fish, and the masks, and the slipped tile, and the hand on his father's shoulder and the colour coming up his father's neck, and she listened, and asked the right questions, and once laughed; and the lamps came on across the bay, and the stones cooled, and neither of them mentioned Rome again that evening, or the steward, or the two years, or three. He felt the lie sitting between them the whole time like a third person on the shingle, and he thought that she must feel it too, and he could not tell, from her face in the dusk, whether she had decided to forgive it or simply to put it where she kept things.

When he got home his stepmother was at the loom with a lamp, and she looked up, and looked at the shingle-dust on his tunic, and said nothing at all, and went back to the thread. And the next morning his father said, at breakfast, not looking up from the tablets: "Rufus spoke to you."

"Yes, Father."

"Good." And then, after a moment, as if it were the same subject, which it was: "The steward will be at the lodging-house tonight. The usual hour. Stay in."

He stayed in. He sat in the tutor's room with the lamp and the wax and did the Baetican ratios again, all of them, until they came out the same three times, and he did not think about the usual hour, or what it meant that his father knew it, or what it meant that the girl had known it before him and had not said that either. He thought about numbers. It was, he was discovering, a thing he could always do, and he put that knowledge away too, with the denarius, in the place where he was beginning to keep the things he owed.
