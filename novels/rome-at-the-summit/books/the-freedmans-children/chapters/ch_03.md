# The Ship of Isis

The day after the Nones, at the usual hour, her mother sent her to bed early, and she went, and did not sleep, and that was the first time she counted.

She had heard the steward come. She had heard him from the cupboard under the stairs with the curtain drawn: the door, and the particular pause of her mother's feet in the passage, and his voice, low, saying something that had her mother's name in it, and then the two sets of feet going up. She had heard the stair creak in the place where it creaked. She had heard the door of the room with the window, which was the best room, and which her mother had told the Rhodian in it at noon that he would be changing for the one at the back, with no reason given and the price not altered. And then she had heard nothing, which was what she had been listening for, and she had lain in the dark and counted it.

She did not know why she counted. It was not for anything. It was that the silence had a shape, and she wanted to know the size of it, so that if it came again she would recognise it. She got to four hundred and something and lost the thread and began again, and the second time she got to six hundred before the door, and the feet, and the stair, and his voice in the passage again, not low now, ordinary, the voice he had used on the harbour steps — *the Ides, then, as agreed* — and the outer door, and the bar going down.

Then her mother's feet, going not up but into the kitchen, and water being poured.

She lay with her eyes open and looked at the underside of the stairs. There was a knot in the fourth tread that she had looked at every night of her life and had decided, at six, was the face of a dog. It was still the face of a dog. She thought: *six hundred, and he said the Ides, and the Rhodian's in the back room, and the price wasn't altered.* She did not put these things together into anything. She was ten. But she put them side by side, carefully, the way you set out counters before a game, and she left them there, and in the morning she got up and did the shutters, and her mother was at the hearth with her hair done and her face ordinary, and said, "The Rhodian wants eggs. Go and see if the Greek woman has any," and she went.

* * *

The feast of the Ship came on the fifth day of March, and the whole town went down to the water for it, as it did every year, in its best, with flowers.

It was the goddess's feast, Isis of the Sea, who had opened the sailing-season since before there were Romans to sail in it, and in Puteoli, which had an Alexandrian for every third man on the emporium and a temple to her above the harbour with a roof of gilded bronze, it was the greatest day of the spring. The priests came down from the temple in white linen with their heads shaved, with the sistra going and the long trumpets and the image of the goddess on her litter under a canopy, and behind them the whole of the town's Egypt — the grain-factors and the ship-masters and the women in their fringed shawls and the children with painted faces — and behind them everyone else, because a goddess who opened the sea was nobody's property in a port. They went along the harbour street to the steps where the steward had waited for the courier, and there on the water was the Ship: a little boat, painted and gilded, with a sail of embroidered linen and no crew, filled to the gunwales with flowers and spices and the written prayers of everyone who could write or pay to have it done, and the high priest said the words and the ship was pushed out, and the wind took her, and she went out past the end of the mole into the open bay with the sun on her sail, and when she was small enough that you could cover her with your thumb the whole harbour cheered and the season was open.

She had put a prayer in. She had written it herself, on a scrap of papyrus the Greek woman had given her, in the square letters Nicias had taught her, and she had not shown it to anyone, and she had folded it small and pushed it down among the lilies when the priests' backs were turned. It said: *Let the house stand.* It did not say which house. She had thought about that for two days and decided the goddess would know.

Antiope had put one in too. Antiope had let her read it. It said: *A rich man who is not cruel,* and Antiope had laughed when she wrote it and said, "She's never managed it yet, but she's the only one I've asked who hasn't told me it was my own fault."

Celer had not come. She had looked for him, among the Augustales who walked in the procession behind the priests with their own little image of the Emperor's Genius on a pole, and she had found Hermes, in his toga, with the band of office across his chest, and behind him the stepmother very straight with the baby, and no boy. She had not expected him. The steward had been in the town for ten days and the boy's house had its rules and so, she supposed, did hers. But she had looked, and had seen herself look, and had put that beside the counters too.

* * *

So she went out to Baiae in the fishing boat with Antiope instead, in the afternoon, when the feast was done and the town was drunk, and that was when she saw the great.

The boat belonged to a man called Scorpus who fished the bay and was, in the loose way of the lower town, Antiope's friend; he took them because Antiope asked, and because he was going that way anyway to set his pots off the hot-spring shore, where the fish came for the warmth. He had a boy with him and the boy rowed and Scorpus sat in the stern with the tiller under his arm and said nothing, and Fausta sat in the bows with the water going by close enough to touch and the whole of the coast opening in front of her the way it never did from the land.

She had been to Baiae. She had walked the coast road behind her mother every April of her life to stand in the patron's atrium. But the coast road ran behind the villas, along the hill, and from it you saw gates and walls and the tops of cypresses, and the sea only in pieces. From the water you saw the front. You saw what the villas were for.

They were for being looked at from the water. She understood that within a quarter-mile. Every one of them had been built by a man standing in a boat, saying *there*, and then going ashore to make the hill agree with him; and the hill had agreed, over a hundred years and more, until the whole curve of the shore from the point to the springs was one long staircase of white columns and painted walls and terraces coming down to the sea, with fishponds cut in the rock at the foot of them and boat-houses with their mouths open and gardens on the roofs, and you could not, from the water, tell where one man's property stopped and the next began, because none of them had wanted you to.

And on the terraces, that afternoon, with the season open and the sun warm and the feast of the goddess going on across the bay, were the people the villas were for, and they were naked.

Not all of them. But enough. It was the thing about Baiae that Antiope had told her and that she had not, until that moment, quite believed: that the great, who in Rome went about in more cloth than a sail, came down to this coast in the spring and took it off. There was a terrace with a pool cut in it and three women in the pool and a fourth on the edge with her feet in the water and nothing on at all, laughing at something, with her hair down her back in a rope, and a slave standing behind her holding a cloth she had not asked for. There was a man on a couch under a vine, brown as a nut and bald, with his belly resting on his thighs and a book held up against the sun. There was a boat-house with its doors open and in the shadow of it a young man with his tunic round his waist and a girl on her knees in front of him, and the young man's face turned to the sea with an expression on it of complete blank patience, like a man waiting for a slow clerk. There were two boys of perhaps her own age racing along a parapet with nothing on and a dog after them. There was a litter being carried along the shore-path by six Nubians in matched tunics with a woman inside it whose arm hung over the side, and on the arm, from the wrist to the elbow, gold.

Nobody looked at the boat. Scorpus set his pots fifty feet from the woman on the pool's edge and she did not turn her head. The man with the book did not lower it. The young man in the boat-house did not change his expression, though the girl, once, looked up and out over the water and met Fausta's eye, and looked back down. They were a fishing boat. They were a fact of the water, like a gull. The great had built their houses to be looked at from the sea and then had forgotten, or had never needed to know, that there would be anything on the sea but their own reflection.

"That's him," Antiope said quietly. "There. Second terrace from the top, with the striped awning."

It was the patron's villa; she knew it by the slipped tile. Under the awning there were couches, and on the couches people, and one of them, large and white-haired and lying on his side with one hand raised as if to beckon a servant, was Bassus. He had a wine-cup. There was a girl pouring. He was, from this distance, exactly the man who had stood in his atrium and said *my people*, except that his toga was on a chair, and the girl who was pouring was Antiope's age, and he was looking at the girl, not the sea, and his hand had come down from its beckoning and rested, lightly, where the girl's tunic ended.

"That's the one from Nola," Antiope said. "The new one. He bought her in the autumn. She'll be at the Dolphin by Saturnalia, you watch; he never keeps them past the winter." She said it without any particular feeling, as Scorpus might have said the fish were running. "Your mother was his for seven years. Did you know that? Seven. That's the longest anyone remembers. He must have liked her."

Fausta watched the hand.

"He let her go," she said. It came out as a question and she had not meant it to.

"He let her *out*. There's a difference. He put her in the lodging-house with a lease and a name and a baby, and he put it in his will that she's free, and she's been free ever since in every way that matters except one." Antiope leaned back against the thwart and shut her eyes to the sun. "And the one that matters is the one Thallus comes about."

Scorpus, in the stern, cleared his throat. Not a warning; a comment. Antiope opened one eye at him and said, "She's ten, not deaf. Row on."

They rowed on. The villa went behind them. The woman with the gold on her arm was set down at a landing-stage and helped out by the Nubians and went up a flight of marble steps into a house with its whole front open to the sea, and the awning of the patron's terrace flapped once in a breath of wind off the land and was still; and Fausta sat in the bows with her hands between her knees and put it all side by side with the rest. The hand on the tunic. Seven years. *In every way that matters except one.* She felt the counters grow in number until they were almost a shape, and then she stopped, deliberately, the way you stop before the end of a sum you do not want to come out, and looked instead at the water going by, which was green, and clear, and had fish in it, and asked nothing of anyone.

* * *

Thallus came to the Dolphin again on the Ides, and this time she did not go to bed.

She had not planned it. She had meant to go up with the Rhodian's water and come down and draw the curtain and count, as before. But her mother was in the yard when the steward's knock came, filling the lamps, and Fausta was in the kitchen, and the knock came a second time before her mother could get the oil-jar down, and so it was Fausta who went to the door and drew the bar and let him in.

He stood in the passage with his hands folded and looked at her. He had a boy with him, a different boy, who stayed outside. The lamp over the door made the top of his bald head shine.

"The Dolphin's girl," he said.

"My mother's in the yard, sir. She's coming."

"I'm in no hurry." He went past her into the common room, where the two tables were, and the bench, and sat down on the bench with his back to the wall, which was where men sat who liked to see the door. "Bring me wine. Not the Rhodian's. Hers."

She brought it. He drank a little and set the cup down and looked at it, and then he looked at her again with the pricing look, and said, "You read, I'm told."

"Yes, sir."

"Who told you that you could?"

She did not understand the question, and then did. "Nicias, sir. In Hermes's house. He lets me sit."

"Nicias." Thallus turned the cup a quarter-turn on the table. "Nicias is my master's slave, lent to Hermes at a rent that Hermes is three months behind on, and if Nicias is teaching a lodging-house girl to read on my master's time I should like to know who is paying for it." He said this without the smallest heat, as one notes a discrepancy in a column. "Not that I object. A girl who can read is worth more than a girl who can't, in the right place. I only like to know where the money's going." He drank. "What do you read?"

"Whatever he has. He's got Homer. He's got a book about Egypt."

"And figures?"

"Yes, sir."

"Give me the sum of the Rhodian's bill. He's been here nine nights at four asses and he's had wine every night at two and the girl twice." He said *the girl* as though Antiope were an item on the slate, which, she realised, to him she was. "What does he owe?"

She did it in her head. Nine fours, thirty-six; nine twos, eighteen; and Antiope, who was eight an evening for a lodger — she knew this because she had heard her mother say it on the stairs — sixteen. "Seventy asses, sir. Four denarii and six."

"And what will he pay?"

She looked at him.

"He'll pay what he's charged," Thallus said patiently, "which will not be what he owes, because your mother will have rounded it to four denarii to keep his custom and will have forgotten, as she forgets every spring, to charge him for the water the girl carries up. So he'll pay sixty-four and your mother will say *seventy* to me when I ask, and the six in the middle is the kind of thing that a steward is paid to notice." He finished the wine. "Not that I'll mention it. It's not worth the mention. I'm telling you so that you understand what reading is for."

Her mother came in from the yard then with oil on her hands, and stopped in the door, and looked at the steward on the bench and the girl standing before him, and something went across her face that was there and gone before Fausta could name it.

"Thallus."

"Hilara." He stood. "I was examining your daughter in arithmetic. She has it. She'll be useful." He said it to Hilara and he kept his eyes on Fausta while he said it, and she felt the word *useful* go into her and lodge, the way a splinter goes in, too small to find and too deep to leave alone. "Shall we go up? I've the villa to get back to and the master wants his accounts by the lamp."

Her mother said, "Fausta. The Rhodian's water."

"The Rhodian," said Thallus, "can wait. He's not paying for it." And he went up the stairs, and after a moment her mother wiped her hands on her gown and went after him, and Fausta stood in the common room with the empty cup in her hand and listened to the stair creak in the place it creaked, and did not count. She found she did not need to. She knew the size of it now.

* * *

Her mother came down afterwards and found her still in the common room, on the bench where the steward had sat, with the cup in front of her and the slate.

She had not meant to be found. She had meant to be under the stairs with the curtain drawn. But the slate had been on its nail by the door and the stylus with it, and she had taken them down without quite deciding to and had sat where he had sat, with her back to the wall and her eyes on the door, and had written out the Rhodian's bill the way he would have written it — nine nights, the wine, the girl, the water nobody charged — and had been looking at the two numbers at the foot of it, the one that was owed and the one that would be paid, when the stair creaked and her mother was in the doorway in her shift with her hair down.

Hilara looked at the slate. She did not take it. She came and sat on the other side of the table, which she never did, since the other side of the table was where the lodgers sat and she had a rule about it, and she put her elbows on the board and her chin on her hands and looked at her daughter across the lamp.

"He asked you the Rhodian."

"Yes."

"And you gave him seventy."

"Yes."

"And then he told you I'd take sixty-four." Her mother's mouth moved; it was not a smile. "He does that. He asks a child a sum and then tells the child where the mother's cheating, so the child goes to bed knowing something about the mother that the mother didn't tell her. It costs him nothing and it's a hook in you for life. Don't take it to heart. I'd have told you myself in a year; he's only moved the day." She reached out and turned the slate round and looked at the figures. "Your sevens are too like your ones. He'll have noticed that too. Make the tail longer."

Fausta made the tail longer.

"I'll tell you what he didn't tell you," Hilara said, "since he's started it. I take sixty-four from the Rhodian because the Rhodian comes four times a year and brings two others with him and tells the whole of his island that the Dolphin's honest, and the six asses I don't charge him buys me that. Thallus knows this. Thallus doesn't care. Thallus wants the six asses on his slate as a thing I owe him, because a woman who owes him six asses can be made to owe him anything, and a woman who owes him nothing can't. That's the whole of it. That's the whole of the man. He's not clever. He's only never once in his life forgotten a number." She pushed the slate back across. "You're cleverer than him already. Don't let him see it. Give him his seventy and let him catch you in the six and look ashamed, and you'll have him thinking he's got you, and a man who thinks he's got you leaves you alone. That's a thing I know."

The lamp guttered and she trimmed it without looking.

"Mama." It was a word Fausta used perhaps twice a year. "What does he come for?"

Her mother was still for a moment with the trimming-pin in her fingers. Then she set it down.

"He comes for the quarter," she said. "I pay the lease on this house to the patron through the steward, and the steward comes for it, and I give it him. That's one thing. And he comes to see the accounts, because the patron likes to know what his people are making, and I show him. That's another." She looked at the lamp, not at the girl. "And he comes because there's a paper in a box in the patron's study in Rome, and in the paper it says that a woman called Hilara who was the patron's is to be free when he dies, and have this house, and a sum. And a paper like that can be taken out of a box and put in a fire by any man who holds the key to the room. Thallus holds the key to the room. That's the third thing." She was quiet. "You've worked out most of this already, I think. You've been counting on the stairs. I heard you the first night. You breathe different when you're counting."

Fausta looked at the table.

"Don't be ashamed of it. Count. It's what I'd do." Hilara reached across and put her hand, briefly, over the girl's hand on the slate, and took it away. "I'm not telling you this so you'll be frightened. I'm telling you so that when you hear men say your mother's a free woman you'll know what it costs to stay one, and so that when you're grown you'll know what to look for in a box. The paper's the thing. Not the man. Men come and go up the stairs; they always have; a lodging-house is a lodging-house. But the paper is the one thing in this world with my name on it that says I'm not a thing, and until the patron's dead and it's been read out in front of witnesses, it belongs to whoever's standing nearest the fire." She stood. "Now go to bed. And tomorrow you're to go to Nicias as usual, steward or no steward, by the kitchen door; I've thought about it and I'd rather you learned than hid. A girl who can read a paper is a girl who can tell when one's been changed."

"Thallus said he'd like to know who was paying for it."

"Did he." Her mother picked up the slate and hung it on its nail. "Then you can tell him, if he asks again, that I am. In the usual coin. At the usual hour." She said it flatly, to the wall. "He'll understand. He's never forgotten a number in his life."

And she went up, and Fausta went under the stairs and drew the curtain and lay a long time looking at the dog's face, and thought about the box, and the key, and the fire, and found that she had stopped being ten at some point in the evening and had not noticed when.

* * *

She went to the tutor's room the next day by the kitchen door with the oil-jar on her hip, as she had been told, having decided on the way up the hill that her mother was right and the rule about the steward was a rule about being seen, and that a girl with an oil-jar was not seen by anyone, and that if she was wrong about this she would find out.

She was not wrong. Daphnis looked at the jar and at her and let her through, and the stepmother was at the loom in the atrium and did not look up, and Nicias was in his room with the shutters half-closed against the morning and a cup beside the bed, awake for once, writing something on a tablet that he turned face-down when she came in. Celer was not there.

"He's at the warehouse," Nicias said. "With his father. There is a consignment of oil that his father wishes him to see counted, because the steward is coming to look at it tomorrow and his father wishes his son to know what a warehouse looks like when it has been prepared for a steward. It is an education in itself. Sit down." She sat. "You've missed a week."

"I had to."

"I know you had to. I didn't ask you why. I said you'd missed it." He picked up the cup. "You've been on the water, I hear. Out to the springs."

She stared at him.

"Scorpus's boy is my laundress's son," Nicias said. "This is a town of forty thousand souls and there are perhaps six who don't know by nightfall what the other thirty-nine thousand did in the afternoon, and I regret to say that I am not one of the six. Don't look like that. It's nothing. Everyone goes out to look at the villas once. It's how the villas are supposed to be looked at. The only people who think they're private are the people in them." He drank. "What did you see?"

She thought about the hand on the tunic. She thought about the young man in the boat-house with his face turned to the sea. She said, "They didn't look at the boat."

Nicias set the cup down.

"No," he said. "No, they didn't. Good. That's the lesson, and you've had it, and most people go their whole lives without it." He leaned forward with his elbows on his knees, and for a moment the dry indoor face was something else, something that she would remember later and come to think of as the nearest the man ever got to anger. "Listen to me. I am a slave. I have been a slave for fifty-three years and I shall die one, because the master has no reason to free me and a great many reasons not to, and I have taught his son and his daughter and his freedman's son and I have had, in all that time, exactly one thing that was mine, and it was this: I could see them, and they could not see me. Not *would* not. *Could* not. I was the boat. You understand? A man who is being looked at and does not know it is a man who can be read, as you read a page, and he will turn his pages for you, one after another, and never once think to ask what you are writing down." He sat back. "That is what reading is for. Not accounts. Thallus told you accounts, I imagine; it's what Thallus thinks it's for, and for Thallus he's right. But Thallus is a steward, and a steward is a man who has read one book very carefully. There are others."

She sat with her hands in her lap and felt something in her chest that she did not have a name for and that she would later, when she had read more books, call *being seen*; and it was so unlike the pricing look of the men on the stairs that for a moment she did not recognise it as a kind of looking at all.

"He said I'd be useful," she said.

"You will. That's not in your gift. What's in your gift is to whom." Nicias reached for the tablet he had turned over, and turned it back, and she saw it was a letter in Greek, half-written, with a name at the top she could not read upside-down. "Now. The boy will be back after noon and he'll want to tell you about the warehouse, and he'll tell it badly, because he's eleven and he thinks a warehouse is about oil. Let him. Then ask him how many jars the steward counted and how many his father said there were. It won't be the same number. It never is. And watch his face when he realises that his father lied to the steward in front of him and that he was meant to see it done." He bent to the letter. "That's his lesson. He'll have it today or next year, but he'll have it. Go and fill your jar; you're supposed to be fetching oil."

* * *

Celer came back after noon with warehouse-dust on him and told her about the warehouse. He told it badly. She let him. He told her about the stacks and the tallies and the whitewash and how the steward would walk the aisles tomorrow with his hands folded and look at everything and say nothing, and how his father had had the men move the Baetican jars to the back where the light was bad, and she said, "How many jars did your father say there were?" and he said, "Four hundred and twenty," and she said, "And how many did you count?" and he opened his mouth, and shut it, and she watched his face, as she had been told to, and saw it happen.

It was not a large thing. His eyes went to the side, to the wall, where there was nothing, and came back; and his mouth, which had been open to say a number, closed on it; and something behind his face that had been a boy's rearranged itself and was, for the space of a breath, not. Then it was a boy's again, and he said, "Four hundred and twenty," and looked at her steadily, and she understood that he had just chosen, in front of her, to carry his father's number instead of his own, and that he knew she had seen him choose it.

"All right," she said.

"It's the Baetican measure. They're smaller. If you count by the Italian —"

"I said all right."

He was quiet. Then he said, in a different voice: "You went out to Baiae. In Scorpus's boat."

"Everyone knows."

"I didn't. I had to hear it from Daphnis." He looked at the wax on his knee. "What was it like?"

She thought about how to tell him. She thought about the woman with her feet in the pool, and the boys racing on the parapet, and the gold from wrist to elbow, and the patron's hand. She thought about the boat-house, and about the girl's eyes coming up over the water and meeting hers and going down again, and about how she had felt, meeting them, a thing she had not felt before and had no name for yet, which was not pity, and was not fear, and had a heat in it she did not understand and did not like and had, since, lain awake with. She had seen a man's body before; she had grown up in a lodging-house. But she had not before seen one being *used*, in the open, in the sun, as a thing is used, and had not before felt herself, watching it, become for a moment a thing that could be used too, and known it, in her skin, the way you know cold.

She did not tell him any of that. She was ten. She said, "They didn't look at the boat."

And he, who was eleven, and had just chosen a number, and was looking at her across the tutor's room with warehouse-dust in his hair and something in his face that was new and that she would spend years learning the name of, said after a moment: "No. They wouldn't."

So they did ratios, because Nicias had gone out and left the tablets, and because it was the thing to do with hands. And when she went home at dusk with the oil-jar full, the house was quiet and her mother was at the hearth and the Rhodian had paid — sixty-four asses, exactly, she saw it on the slate — and Antiope was on the stairs with her knees up, and said as she passed, "Well? Was the goddess kind?"

"She didn't say."

"She never does, duck. That's how you know it's her and not a man." Antiope yawned. "Thallus says the villa's full. Senators' sons up from Rome for the season, because it's safer here than there until the Spaniard's settled. He says I'm to go up from next week. Good money. He says the young master's asked for me particular." She stretched, and the shift went off her shoulder, and she did not fix it. "A rich man who's not cruel. We'll see. Maybe she was listening."

And she went up, and Fausta stood at the foot of the stairs and looked at the knot in the fourth tread that was a dog's face, and counted, this time without meaning to, and got nowhere, and went in to the hearth, and her mother, without turning, said: "Did Hermes's boy tell you his number?"

"Four hundred and twenty."

Hilara nodded slowly, at the fire. "Then he's his father's," she said. "That's settled, then. That's one thing we know." And she handed her daughter the ladle, and the day was over, and the season was open, and the little gilded ship was somewhere out past the point with its freight of flowers and prayers, going wherever the wind had decided, with nobody aboard to say otherwise.
