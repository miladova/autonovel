# The Ring

The ring came on the Ides of March, from a clerk, in a room, and weighed less than the denarius.

That was what he noticed. He had expected — he did not know what he had expected; his father had been describing the thing for eighteen years and had never once described the moment, only the stone it would be cut on — and it was a room in the old palace below the Palatine with a clerk in it, a freedman of Capito's with ink on his cuff, who read his name off a sheet, *Gaius Laecanius Celer, son of Gaius, of the tribe Falerna, property qualification attested at four hundred and six thousand in land in the territory of Capua*, and looked up, and said, "That's you?" and he said yes, and the clerk made a mark and reached into a box and took out a plain gold ring of the kind the clerk had seen a thousand of and put it on the table between them and said, "There. You're on the roll. The stripe you buy yourself; the tailor's in the Argiletum, tell him Capito's office sent you and he'll not cheat you more than usual. Next."

He put it on. It was loose. He would have it made smaller, in the Argiletum, by the tailor's brother, who did rings. He walked out of the old palace into the Forum with it on his hand and the hand felt no different and he stood at the foot of the Palatine for a while looking up at it with the gold on his finger and found that he was thinking not of his father, or the stone, or the four hundred and six thousand in vines he had never seen, but of a chandler's stair in the dark and a girl saying *that's done, you'll find it's easier than counting*, and that the ring was the same: a thing that had been described to him his whole life as the whole of it, and that turned out to be a clerk, and a box, and *next*.

He wrote to his father. He wrote four lines, in the square hand, *the roll, the Ides, Capito's office, the ring*, and under them, because he had been told by a woman at a loom that the gods liked to see a face and because he had struck the man and the man had said *I think I've got it now*, he wrote a fifth: *I'll come to the stone when the war's done and stand by it with you. Not for the stone. For you. — C.* And he sealed it and gave it to Philon for the post and went to the Argiletum to be cheated.

* * *

He took rooms. An eques on Sura's staff did not sleep in a room with a window on a wall in a Spaniard's house; Sura's steward said so, and found them, on the Aventine below Sura's, three rooms over a wine-shop with a stair and a door of their own and a view, if you leaned, of the river. And an eques with rooms needed a household, the steward said, and Celer, who had never in his life owned anything but a denarius and a knife he had given away, went to the market in the Subura on a morning in April with Sura's steward's boy to show him where, and bought a woman.

He would not pretend, afterwards, that he had not known what he was doing. He had known since a chandler's stair. He stood on the dealer's platform's edge in the April sun with the ring on his hand and the boy at his elbow and looked at a row, and he was not his father's patron with a lamp in a women's room and he was exactly that, and he knew it, and he looked along the row anyway, because he needed a household and a household was a thing you bought, and he stopped.

She was Greek — Corinthian, the dealer said, which meant nothing; they were all Corinthian when the buyer had a Greek face — and twenty, and had been a merchant's in Puteoli, which was why he had stopped, and knew it, and did not let it stop him from stopping. She was tall and brown and had a long plain face that she had arranged to show nothing, and the arrangement was good but not thirty years good; he could see through it, as he could see through most things now, to a thing underneath that was not fear and was not the pen-look and was simply a woman on a platform doing the sum. Her name was Chrestê. It meant *useful*. The dealer said it as if it were a selling point and Celer thought of a steward in a common room saying *she'll be useful* about a girl of ten, and of the splinter it had been, and he said the price without bargaining, which the boy told him afterwards was a mistake and which he had done because he could not stand on the platform's edge any longer.

She was good. That was the thing he would have to say to himself, honestly, in the rooms over the wine-shop in the months after, when he looked at what he had done. She was good at all of it. She kept the three rooms as a merchant's woman kept a house, with the accounts on a slate — she could read; the dealer had not said so; she had not said so either, until he found the slate and she had looked at him with the long face arranged and said *you didn't ask* — and she bought at the market for less than the steward's boy did and she cooked, which he had not expected, and in the evenings she sat in the outer room with mending and did not speak unless he did, and when he did she answered, in a Greek with the Gulf in it, exactly as much as was wanted.

And on the ninth night he went into the inner room where she slept, because that was what the room was for, and what she was for, and what he had bought, and he knew all of that and went in anyway, because he was eighteen and had been in a tent for four years and a Spaniard's house for two and had not, since a chandler's stair at thirteen, been in a room with a woman where the woman's attention was on him; and she was awake, and sat up, and looked at him with the long face in the lamp, and did not arrange it, for once; she let him see the sum being done, which was *so, now*, and *well, he's young, and clean, and hasn't hit me*, and *better now than a year of waiting for it*; and then she arranged it, and said, in the Gulf Greek, "Yes. All right. Come here, then. I'd rather it was you than the steward's boy, who's been looking."

He went. He would remember it, as he remembered everything, by what he saw; and what he saw was that she was the one in the room who knew what was happening, again, as the Bithynian girl had been, and that she took his hands and put them where they went, as the Bithynian girl had, and that he was not thirteen now, and had learned a thing or two from nothing and nobody, and that it was not brief; and that her body under his hands was long and brown and warm and real and had a scar on the hip that he put his mouth to without deciding to and felt her go still and then not; and that when it was done she did not say *there, that's done*; she lay with her arm over her eyes in the lamp and breathed and said nothing for a long time, and then said, to the ceiling, "You're kind. I'd heard you were. The steward's boy said you paid without bargaining. I thought: that's a fool or a kind man, and either one's a thing I can work with." She moved her arm and looked at him. "Which?"

"I don't know."

"No. You wouldn't." She reached and touched the ring on his hand, turned it, once, on the finger, where it was still loose. "A kind man with a ring and a woman he bought in the Subura. That's Rome, that is. That's the whole city in one bed." And she turned over, and slept, and he lay beside her with the lamp going down and the river somewhere beyond the wall and thought, as he had thought at the foot of a stair at thirteen, that he had liked it — not the act, which had been long and strange and partly hers; the other thing. The being the one in the room who was not a task. And that she had just told him, to his face, in his own bed, with her hand on his ring, that she knew it, and would work with it, and that this was what *useful* meant, and that he had bought it for the price of a good mule and had not bargained because he could not bear to, and that not being able to bear a thing was not the same as not doing it, and never had been, and that his father had known that for thirty years and had built a tomb on it.

He was kind to her. He would say that too. He never raised a hand or a voice; he paid her a wage, which the steward's boy said was mad; he let her keep the slate and did not check it, which she noticed and said nothing about; and in the mornings, when he went up the hill to Sura's stool, she stood at the top of the stair over the wine-shop with the mending and watched him go with the long face arranged, and he could not read her, and did not try, because he had learned on a mole that there were faces he could not read and had decided that this was going to be one of them, on purpose, because the alternative was to read her, and know, and go in anyway, and he was not yet the man who could do that with his eyes open. He would be. He could see him from here. But not yet.

She told him about Puteoli on the last night before the staff went north, in the inner room, with the lamp, after, when she lay with her arm over her eyes and he had learned that this was when she talked, if she was going to.

"The merchant was called Aulus Granius. Wine. He had a house on the slope above the macellum, two streets from a freedman's with columns that weren't marble." Her voice was the Gulf Greek, level, a manifest. "I was his for four years. I did his accounts, because his wife couldn't and he couldn't and somebody had to, and I did the other thing, because that's what a girl in a merchant's house does when the wife's at Nola with her mother. He wasn't unkind. He was a man who'd stopped at me and kept stopping because it was easier. When he died the wife sold me in a lot with the furniture, which was fair; I'd been furniture, mostly." She moved her arm and looked at the ceiling. "I knew your father's house. Everyone on that slope did. The Augustalis with the tomb he kept changing. I never saw you; you'd gone north by then, they said, with the patron's son. But I knew the girl."

He did not move.

"From the lodging-house by the harbour. The Dolphin. Granius sent me down there once a month with the slate to settle what his factors had drunk, and there was a girl kept the book — thirteen, fourteen, with her mother dying up the stairs and a face on her like a shutter — and she'd go down my slate with her finger and find the place where Granius's factor had leaned on the stylus and say, *that's not what he drank, that's what he wished he had*, and take the lower figure, and look at me while she did it, so I'd know she knew I'd been told to pass the higher." The long face in the lamp was not arranged. "I liked her. I'd not have said so to her; you don't, between houses. But I thought: that one's not going up the stairs. That one's going somewhere else. Did she?"

"The Caelian," he said. "A widow's. She does the empress's hair."

Chrestê was quiet for a moment.

"Well," she said. "Then I was right, and it's the only time I've been right about anything on that coast." She turned on her side, toward him, with her hand under her cheek, and looked at him in a way she had not before, which was the way the girl had looked at her over a slate: so that he would know she knew. "You bought me because I'd been Granius's in Puteoli. The dealer said it and you stopped. I saw you stop. I thought: there's a man who's buying a street." She did not say it unkindly. She said it as a figure. "I'll work with that too. I've worked with worse. But I'd rather you knew I'd seen it, because a thing you know I've seen is a thing you'll not have to perform, and I'm tired, and I'd rather keep house for a kind man who's honest about what he bought than for a kind man who thinks he's kind."

He lay in the lamp with her hand under her cheek and her eyes on him and the river beyond the wall, and found that he had nothing to say that was not either a list or a lie, and he had been told by a girl with a slate not to do the thing where he decided what was true first.

"Yes," he said. "I bought a street. I didn't know I was doing it till you said. I'd have told you otherwise if you'd asked yesterday, and I'd have believed it." He looked at the ceiling. "I don't know what that makes me."

"It makes you your father's son with a ring," said Chrestê, "which is what you were when you walked onto the platform, and what you'll be when you come back from the war, if you do, and I'll keep the slate and the three rooms and not ask you what the street's for, because I know, and you know, and the only person who doesn't is the one with the shutter, and she's on the Caelian doing an empress's hair and will never in her life set foot over a wine-shop on the Aventine." She closed her eyes. "Go to sleep. You've a bridge to see. The steward's boy says it's a mile long and built by a Syrian who shouts. Count the piers for me; I've never seen a thing that size that wasn't a debt."

He went north in the morning. At the top of the stair over the wine-shop she stood with the mending and watched him go, as she did, and this time he did not try not to read her, and read her, and what was there was a woman who had been sold with the furniture and had told the truth to the man who bought her and was waiting to see what it cost; and he went down the stair with the ring on his hand and the sum undone and the knowledge, new and cold and exact, that he had two things now in Rome that he could not bear to look at and had bought anyway, and that one of them was over a wine-shop and the other was on the Caelian, and that the one over the wine-shop had just told him which was which.

* * *

The king broke the terms in June, as the sum had said, and the emperor went north, and the staff went with him, and Celer saw the bridge.

He had heard of it for a year. Everyone had; it was the thing the war had been doing while it waited, in the gorge below Drobeta where the river came out of the Iron Gates and ran, for a mile, straight and deep and fast between two banks of rock, and a Syrian from Damascus with a beard and a temper and the emperor's ear had been building across it, since the spring of the second year, a thing nobody had built across that river in the history of the world. The staff came down to it in July by the road the first war had cut along the gorge-wall, and the road came round a shoulder of rock, and there it was, and the staff stopped, all of it, the way the mole had stood up for one ship in February; and Celer, on a horse he had bought with the vines and did not yet sit well, looked at it and did not have anything, as he had not had anything for the mountains. Nicias would have had Homer. He had: twenty piers. He counted them. Twenty piers of stone in the river, each the size of the Dolphin, in a line from bank to bank, with the brown water piling up against their upstream faces and going white, and on the piers, arches, of timber, great curved ribs of oak lashed and pinned, and on the arches a road, wide enough for four men abreast, with rails, going across the river in the air for a mile; and at either end a gate-tower of stone with the eagle on it; and on the far side, on the Dacian bank, where the road came down, the fort, and the camp, and the army, already across, in its thousands, which had walked over the river, on a road, as if the river were not there.

"Well?" said Sura, beside him, on a better horse. "One thing."

"It's not for the war, sir."

"Go on."

"The army crossed on boats in the first war and it can cross on boats in this one; the river's the same and the boats are cheaper. It's not for crossing. It's for *after*. It's a road, and a road's a thing you pay on every mile of it, to someone, and a road into a kingdom with a gate-tower at either end is a tax-gate. He's not building a bridge. He's building the first mile of a province." He heard himself say it and heard, under it, a dead Greek by a brazier, *a road is a thing you pay on, boy*, and did not say that.

Sura was quiet for a moment, on the shoulder of rock, with the bridge below them in the sun.

"Yes," he said. "That's what Apollodorus says, when he's drunk. That he's not an engineer, he's a surveyor, and the emperor's not a soldier, he's a man who's decided that the Danube's the wrong border and the Carpathians are the right one and that the only way to move a border is to put a road through it that can't be rolled up." He gathered his reins. "Don't say that in the tent. The Senate still thinks this is about terms. Say it to me on rocks."

They went down. They crossed. He crossed on a road in the air over a river half a mile wide with the water going by white underneath between the ribs and the planks moving, very slightly, the whole length of it, like the mole in a swell, like the bridge of boats four years ago that the horses had been blindfolded for; and the horses were not blindfolded now, they walked, and the men walked behind them with their eyes open, which he had thought four years ago was the wrong way round and thought now, on the Syrian's road, was simply what happened when a thing had been built that a horse could believe in; and at the far gate-tower, in the shadow of the eagle, a big man with a beard and a Damascus face and lime on his hands to the elbow was standing with a tablet, counting the cohorts across, and looked up as the staff came through, and saw Sura, and said in Greek, without greeting, "Lucius. It holds. Tell him it holds. He's asked me four times by letter and I've told him four times and he'll believe it from you." And then the eyes went past Sura to the boy on the bad horse and the man said, "Who's that? He's counting my piers."

"Hermes's son. He counts everything. He's just told me your bridge is a tax-gate."

Apollodorus of Damascus looked at the freedman's son for a long moment with the lime drying on his arms.

"Is it," he said. "Well. He's right. Don't tell the Senate." And then, turning back to his tablet: "Twenty piers. Nobody else has counted them. They look at it and say *the bridge* and go across. He counted. Lucius, when this is over and you've got your province and your triumph, give me that one for a month; I've a forum to build out of the side of a hill in Rome and I'll want someone who can tell me what it weighs."

* * *


Aper found him on the bridge the second night, as Aper found everything.

He came along the roadway between the rails with a lamp he should not have had and a skin he certainly should not, and sat down on the planks beside Celer with his legs over the river, forty feet above water that could be heard and not seen, and said, "I've been made a tribune. Laticlave; no — angusticlave; I never remember which is the one for people like me. The thin stripe. My father wrote to a man and the man wrote to the emperor's man and I'm to go to the Fifth in the spring and stand in front of six hundred men who've been soldiers longer than I've been alive and tell them things. Drink."

Celer drank. The river went under them, all of it, the whole weight of the Danube, and the piers the Syrian had sunk in it stood against the weight and did not move, and the planks under them did not move, and he had come out here for that, to sit on a thing that did not move over a thing that did.

"I'll be bad at it," Aper said.

"You'll not."

"I will. I can't add and I can't shout. I'll be liked and I'll be bad at it, and in two years I'll go home to the salt and be a magistrate in Ariminum and marry somebody's daughter, and in twenty I'll be fat, and I'll tell people I sat on Apollodorus's bridge with the man who weighed the gold." He drank. "You'll weigh gold. I know it. Everyone on the staff knows it. Sura looks at you the way my father looks at the salt-pans."

"That's not a kind thing to say about either of us."

Aper laughed, out over the river, loud, so that the sentry on the far tower called out and Aper called back his name and the sentry said, *oh, it's you, sir,* in the voice everyone used about Aper, and let it go. "That's the first joke you've made in three years. Write it down. No — you'll have it in your head; you have everything in your head." He was quiet, and then said, in a voice that was not for the sentry, "I'll miss the tent. That's all. I know it's not a thing men say. I'm from a salt-pan; we say things." He got up, unsteadily, with the lamp. "Come to the Fifth's lines when we cross. I'll have a tent to myself and nobody in it who can read my hand, and I'll be lonely and I'll pretend I'm not, and you can sit there and not talk, which is what you do, and I'll feel better."

He went back along the planks, with the lamp swinging, singing something from Ariminum about a girl and a salt-cart, badly; and Celer sat on the bridge a while longer with the skin, over the river, and found that the thing he had come out there to feel, which was the piers holding, had got mixed up with another thing, and he could not take them apart, and did not try.

The Spaniard had a legion.

That was the thing about the second war that was not in the first: the emperor's great-nephew by marriage, who had sat on the end of a couch on the Aventine and asked a dead Greek's name, was legate of the First Minervia, five thousand men and their auxiliaries, and was across the river ahead of the staff and up in the hills west of the king's city doing, by the sheets that came back, exactly what a legate of a legion in his first command was supposed to do, which was to be where he was sent and to win small things and send long letters about them in good Greek. Celer read the letters. Sura set them aside and Celer read them after, on the stool, and told Sura one thing, and the thing was always the same and he said it differently each morning so as not to be sent back to Rufus: *he's writing for a reader who isn't you.*

"Yes," said Sura, on the fourth morning. "He's writing for the box. He knows there's a box. He's known since he was quaestor; I told him, the way Philon told you, so he'd not find it by accident. Every letter that man sends from the Minervia is a letter he expects to be read in twenty years by someone deciding what kind of legate the emperor was. He's right to. I'd do the same. I *did* the same; it's in the third room with the wax unbroken." Sura did not move. "The difference is that I wrote for one reader and he writes for eleven, and one of the eleven is himself, and that's the one he can't stop performing for. Watch for it. It's the hole in him. A man who reads his own letters as posterity will is a man who can be made to do a thing by being told how it will look."

Celer watched for it. He saw it in August, when the Minervia took a hill-fort in the western valleys and the Spaniard's letter about it ran to four sheets and had, in the third, a sentence about the fort's garrison — *they fought as men fight who have been promised a thing and have ceased to expect it* — that was his own sentence, from a margin, from a granary, three years ago, with the words moved; and he sat on the stool with the sheet and felt a thing go through him that was cold and clear and that he had not felt before and recognised at once, because he had watched his father feel it across a lamp in a strong-room: it was the feeling of seeing your figure in another man's column, uncredited, and being *pleased*.

He did not tell Sura that one. He told Sura the Iazyges.

* * *

His father's letter came in September, to the camp on the far bank below the king's mountains, by the house's post through Philon, and it was two sheets, and the second was the one.

The first was the house. The *Isis* had made the bounty. The new man at the grain-office had learned what he was worth and was being paid it. Bassus's debts had come to Rufus and Rufus had come to Hermes, as foreseen, and Hermes had lent, against the Baian villa this time, with the slipped tile and all, and held, now, in his strong-room, a paper that said that if the young master did not pay by the Kalends of the third year the house of Hermes would own the headland that the house of Hermes had been walking up to on foot every April for sixty years to stand in an atrium and be called *my people*. *I'll not take it,* his father wrote. *I'll never take it. A freedman who forecloses on his patron's villa is a freedman the whole coast turns its back on and his son's ring with him. But I'll hold the paper. There's a thing about holding a paper that a man like Rufus understands in his legs before he understands it in his head, and I've watched him understand it, in my strong-room, on a stool I gave him, and it was worth the four hundred thousand and the olives and the rest. He sat down. He sat down in my strong-room. Your stepmother says I've not slept since for smiling and she's not wrong.*

The second sheet was shorter.

*The steward. You'll know he asked the girl a question in front of a praetor's man at the funeral. You'll know because you were in the street; Thallus has a boy who told him so, and told me, so that I'd know what Thallus knows. And you wrote to your stepmother, after, about an egg-seller's husband in the lower town and a quarter's payment for nothing, and she did it, and told me she'd done it, because she's a good woman and doesn't lie to me about things that cost money. I've let it stand. The man's paid. He'll be paid while he lives. That's done and I'll not undo it.*

*But hear me. Thallus asked the question for one reason, which is that Rufus owes me the villa and Thallus knows it and Thallus is a steward whose master has been bought and who needs, therefore, to find a thing the buyer wants that the buyer can't get from the paper. He's looked round the house of Laecanius for that thing and he's found it on the Caelian with a box of pins. He thinks the girl is a hook in you. He's wrong about what kind, but he's not wrong that she's one; a man who strikes his father for a sentence is a man with a hook in him, and I'd not have told Thallus that for the villa, and I've not, and he's guessed it anyway, because he's never once in his life stopped counting.*

*So. Do nothing. The egg-man's paid; that's the end of what you do. Don't write to the Caelian. Don't write to the Nola woman about the Caelian. Don't let the Spaniard, who asks for you every time he's made something, ask for you about this. The house is with us — Rufus is in my strong-room on a stool and his steward knows it and the girl is the empress's hair and the empress is the one person on that hill Thallus can't count, which is why he asked the question in front of a praetor's man and not in front of her. He's put it on a sheet. Let it sit on the sheet. A thing on a sheet that nobody picks up is a thing that didn't happen; you've been in Sura's boxes two years, you know that better than I do. Pick it up and it's a thing. Leave it and in three years Rufus has paid or hasn't and either way Thallus is an old man with a key to a house that isn't his and a question nobody's answering.*

*Do nothing. I've never asked you to do nothing before. I'm asking now. It's the hardest thing I know and I've done it for thirty years and it got you a ring. — Your father.*

*She'd say "and?" I know. I've thought about it in the strong-room and I think I've got it now: it means the sum's done and there's nothing left over. Well. There's something left over. There's the villa, and I'm not taking it, and there's you, and I'm not spending you, and that's the "and." That's a father's. Hers was a dying woman's. They're not the same and I'll not pretend they are.*

He read it twice, on a stool, in a tent, on the far bank, with the king's mountains above and the Syrian's bridge a day behind and the Spaniard's four-sheet letters in a box at his elbow; and he sat with it for a long time, and the sum came out the same three times, which was *do nothing*, which was right, which was true, which was the thing he was good at.

And he did nothing. He did not write to the Caelian. He did not write to the Nola woman. When the Spaniard came into the staff tent in the last week of September with lime on his boots from a visit to the bridge and said, in Greek, in passing, *Hermes's son — Lucius tells me the widow Tertulla's hair-girl is from your street; the Augusta's taken a fancy to her, she does the hair the old way, Plotina says she's the only one on the hill with the hands for it; is she as clever as the Augusta thinks?*, he said, *cleverer, sir*, and nothing else, and the grey eyes looked at him for a moment and went on, and he did nothing with that either. He put the letter in the box with the rest. He fed the Lares from Nola, which he had carried north again, in the tent, with bread, because a woman at a loom had said they liked to see a face; and he thought, doing it, that his father had said *a hook in you* and had been right, and that his father had said *do nothing* and had been right, and that he had a woman in three rooms over a wine-shop on the Aventine who knew he was kind and worked with it and whom he had bought because he could not bear to stand on a platform's edge, and a ring that weighed less than a denarius, and a stone with an *F* on it, and that none of these was the hook, and that the hook was a girl on a mole holding him off at the length of her arm and saying *not for nothing*, and that he had put it in the column, and that the column did not balance, and that he was going to go up into the mountains in the spring and write the inventory of a kingdom with it open, because that was what his father had asked, and his father had never asked him to do nothing before, and it was the hardest thing he knew, and he was good at it.
