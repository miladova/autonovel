# The News Ship

The first she knew of the emperor's death was that a man did not pay.

He had come down the stairs at the sign of the Dolphin at the hour when the lamps were being trimmed, a factor from Ostia with a Syrian's beard and an Italian's boots, and he had stood in the doorway buckling his cloak and looking at the harbour as if it had done him a personal injury. Her mother had said the sum. The man had said, "Nerva is dead," as though that settled it, and gone out into the dark with the price of two nights and a girl still owing.

Her mother had looked at the door for the space of a breath. Then she had said, "Fausta. Get the shutters."

She, Fausta, was ten that winter, and she had got the shutters, because that was the order of things in the house: you did the thing in front of you and you thought about the emperor later, when the lamps were out and there was nothing to do with your hands. She remembered the wood cold and swollen with the sea-damp, the bar that would not drop into its bracket until she leaned her whole weight on it, and the sound behind her of her mother counting what was in the box and not saying the number aloud.

That was the second of February, or the third. Afterwards she could never fix the day, because in Puteoli the days of that month ran together like the sulphur-smoke that came over the hill from the burning fields and lay in the streets until noon. What she fixed instead was the man's hand on the cloak-pin, and the way her mother had not called after him. Hilara called after everyone. Hilara had once followed a Cretan wine-merchant to the mole and taken his purse off him in front of the harbour guard, and been applauded. But she did not follow the Syrian. She let him go, two nights and a girl, and she said to the room — to Antiope, who was sitting on the bottom stair in her shift with her knees drawn up, to the two sailors from Alexandria who had stopped eating, to the lamp and the smoke and the cold — "Well. Now we'll see."

* * *

In the morning the whole town knew, and knew it in the way the town knew things, which was from the harbour inward. The couriers of the Rome road had come through in the night and gone on to Neapolis and the fleet at Misenum, and nobody of consequence had been awake to receive them except the duty officer of the harbour watch, and he had told the bakers. So by the time the sun was over Vesuvius and the fog was lifting off the water in rags, the bakers had told the fishwives, and the fishwives had told the porters, and the porters had told everyone, and the news had been improved in each telling, so that Nerva was by turns dead of a fever, dead of a fall, dead of fury at a senator, and dead of the oldest cause of all, which was that he had been old. He had reigned sixteen months. He had adopted a soldier in Germany to come after him. That was the part that mattered, and it was the part nobody could tell her anything useful about, because none of them had ever seen the soldier and most of them could not say his name twice the same way.

"Traianus," said Antiope, who could. She had it from a man who had it from a letter. "He's Spanish."

"He's not Spanish. He's Roman. He was born in Spain."

"That's what I said."

They were in the yard behind the house, where the Isis shrine was, and where the slops went out, and where Antiope liked to sit in the first sun with her hair loose, because it was the only hour she had to herself and she chose to spend it being looked at by nobody. She was seventeen and had been at the Dolphin two winters. In summer she went up to Baiae and worked the villas, and came back in October with silver in her hem and a tiredness that took until the Saturnalia to leave her face. Fausta had decided when she was eight that she loved her, and had not revised it.

"Spanish or Roman," Antiope said, "he's in Germany, and he's got the army, and that's the end of it. Your mother's worried about the wrong thing."

"She's not worried."

"She let that man go."

Fausta had no answer to this. She picked at the moss between the yard-stones with her thumbnail. The shrine was a niche in the wall with a painted goddess in it, the paint gone to a pink ghost, and a saucer of something in front of her that the cats had been at.

"She's thinking," Antiope said, "that when an emperor dies, the people who had his ear lose it, and the people who had their ear lose theirs, and so on down, until you get to a lodging-house in Puteoli, where a man who was going to pay decides he had better keep his money until he knows who his friends are. It'll be the same in a month. It's always the same in a month." She stretched. Her shift had slipped off one shoulder and she did not fix it. "I was in Rome when Domitian went. I was six. My mother says the price of bread didn't move."

"Your mother was a slave."

"So was yours, duck." Antiope smiled at her, not unkindly. "That's why we're having this conversation in a yard."

* * *

Her mother sent her up the hill at the third hour with a message for the house of Laecanius Hermes, and that was how the day acquired its shape, and, as it turned out, the years.

The message was about oil. The Dolphin took its lamp-oil from Hermes's warehouse on the emporium, as it took its wine and its fish-sauce and, in a sense, its existence, since the house and the warehouse and the woman who ran one and the man who owned the other all carried the same name, and the name was not theirs. They were Laecanii. The whole of the lower town, it sometimes seemed to Fausta, was Laecanii: the oil-man and the ship's-chandler and the woman who sold hot sausages by the baths, and the man who dug graves, and the two brothers who had a monopoly on the hire of mules for the Cumae road, and her mother, and herself. Every one of them had been owned once by the family in the big villa above Baiae, or was the child of someone who had, and every one of them wore the name like a brand that had healed over and could still be read in the right light. Some of them were rich. Hermes was rich. It did not matter. When Lucius Laecanius Bassus came down to the coast in April to open his villa, they all went up the hill to stand in his atrium and be looked at, the rich ones at the front and her mother at the back, and he called them *my people*, and they called him *patron*, and he was, and they were.

She did not think about any of this, at ten. It was the water she swam in. She thought about the hill, which was steep, and the message, which was that the last amphora had been short and her mother would pay for what she got and not for what was written, and about whether she would be allowed through to the back of Hermes's house, where the tutor had his room, or sent round to the kitchen door.

Hermes's house stood on the slope above the macellum where the streets began to straighten and the drains ran under the paving instead of down the middle of it. It was not a large house, by the standard of the villas on the point, but it was large for a man whose father had been a Greek bought in a lot of twelve at the slave-market in Delos, and Hermes had made sure that it looked larger than it was. He had put a porch on it with two columns, and the columns had capitals, and the capitals were, if you did not look closely, marble. On the lintel he had had cut, in letters that could be read from the street, *C. LAECANIUS C. L. HERMES AUGUSTALIS*, and the *L.* in the middle of that, which meant *freedman of Gaius*, he had had cut smaller than the rest. Not much smaller. Enough.

The doorkeeper let her through. She was known. Nicias had said once, in her hearing, that the girl from the Dolphin had a better memory than the boy, and the doorkeeper had decided on the strength of it that she was a person, in the limited sense in which a doorkeeper decides such things, and did not make her go round. So she came through the atrium with its little pool and its painted walls — a seascape, ships and dolphins, the paint fresh enough that the blue still smelled — and through the passage past the room where Hermes sat with his tablets when he was at home, which he was not, and into the back court where the tutor had his room, and where Celer was sitting on the step with a wax tablet on his knees, scowling at it as though it owed him money.

He looked up. He was eleven, and he had his father's face already, the long Greek face with the heavy brows, and nothing else of his father's, not yet. He had the look he always had when she arrived: that she was late, and that he had expected her, and that he was not going to say either.

"Nerva's dead," he said.

"I know."

"The new one's called Trajan. He's in Germany. He's got the whole army. My father says he'll come to Rome in the spring and there'll be games and the price of everything will go up." He turned the tablet so she could see it. On it, in his square careful hand, he had written out the emperor's names, all of them, *Imperator Caesar Nerva Traianus Augustus Germanicus*, and under them a list of figures. "That's what a modius of wheat cost when Vespasian came in. And Titus. And Domitian. Nicias had it in a book. It goes up every time."

"Why?"

"Because of the games."

"Why do the games make wheat dearer?"

He thought about that. It was the thing she liked in him, that he would stop and think when he did not know, instead of saying the first thing; it was what made him different from every other boy in the lower town and most of the men. "I don't know," he said. "I'll ask him."

"I've got a message for your father."

"He's at the warehouse. Give it to Daphnis." Daphnis was the doorkeeper. "Or tell me."

She told him. He listened with his head on one side and then said, "It wasn't short. The amphorae from that batch are all the small size; it's the Baetican measure, not ours. She's paying the same for less oil but it's not short, it's a different jar." He said this without malice, as a matter of fact, and she felt her face heat, because it meant her mother had been wrong, and it meant the boy on the step knew more about her mother's house than she did.

"She'll want to pay for the oil, not the jar."

"Then she should say that. Not that it was short." He made a mark on his tablet. "I'll tell him."

"Tell him she said *short*."

He looked at her. Then — and this was the other thing she liked in him, which she did not have a word for yet, and would spend twenty years finding words for, none of them adequate — he nodded, and rubbed out the mark, and wrote something else.

"All right," he said. "Short."

* * *

Nicias was asleep in his room with a book over his face. He was a man of perhaps fifty, thin as a lath, with the pale indoor skin of someone who had spent his life in the back courts of other men's houses, and he belonged, as it happened, not to Hermes but to the patron: he was one of Bassus's Greeks, lent down the hill at a rent that Hermes paid quarterly and complained about monthly. He had taught Bassus's son his letters and Bassus's daughter hers, and now he was teaching the freedman's boy, which he regarded as a demotion, and he had let the freedwoman's girl sit at the back because she was quiet and because it amused him, as he once told Hermes, to see what a mind would do with an alphabet when nobody had told it the alphabet was not for it.

He woke when Celer kicked the doorframe. He lifted the book off his face, and looked at the two of them, and said in Greek, "Well. The old man's dead."

"We know."

"Everyone knows. Even you. Sit." They sat. "Tell me who the new one is."

"Trajan. He's in Germany with the army."

"He is in Germany with *an* army. Two legions on the upper Rhine and the goodwill of the rest. It is not the same thing. If it were the same thing, Nerva would not have needed him." Nicias swung his feet to the floor and reached for the cup beside the bed. There was always a cup beside the bed. "Why did Nerva need him?"

Celer said, "Because the Guard didn't like Nerva."

"Why not?"

"Because he was a senator and he'd been put in by senators after Domitian was killed, and the Guard liked Domitian."

"Good. And so a year ago the Guard surrounded the palace and made the old man hand over the men who'd killed Domitian, and the old man, who was eighty and had no sons and no soldiers, did it, and then went up to the Capitol and announced that he had adopted the governor of Upper Germany as his son and heir, because the governor of Upper Germany had soldiers. And now the old man is dead, and the governor of Upper Germany is emperor of the world, and he has not seen Rome in three years and may not hurry to." Nicias drank. "What does that tell you?"

Celer frowned. Fausta said, before she had decided to, "That he doesn't need Rome. Rome needs him."

Nicias looked at her over the cup. It was a long look, and she could not read it. She was ten and she had been told her whole life that she was quick, by people whose idea of quickness was being able to add up a wine-bill, and she did not yet know that there were other kinds of quickness and that this man had spent his life watching for them in the children of the rich and had found it, as a rule, where it was no use to anyone.

"That's right," he said. "Rome needs him. Remember that, both of you. It's the only fact about an emperor that is always true." He put the cup down. "Now. Celer. The Baetican measure. Your father's warehouse. What is the ratio?"

They did ratios for an hour. She was better at them than he was and she had learned not to show it, mostly.

* * *

They went down to the mole in the afternoon because the Alexandrian fleet was expected, and because it was the thing you did in Puteoli, in those years, when anything happened: you went down to the water and looked east.

It was not the season for the grain fleet. That came in May, when the sea opened, two hundred ships together under sail off Capri, and the whole town went out on the mole and the roofs and the hill to see them come in, and the magistrates in their best togas went down to the harbour steps to receive the first master, and there was a holiday. February was the dead month. But there were always ships, even in the dead month — the fast couriers that ran the winter passage for the imperial post and whoever could pay to go with it, the Rhodian merchantmen that took the risk for the prices — and the harbour watch had said that one of these was in the offing, and had been since dawn, beating up from the south against a wind that would not let her in.

The mole ran out into the bay on its great arches, farther than you could throw a stone and then farther again, and from the end of it you could see the whole curve of the coast: Misenum with the fleet in its basin, and the point of Baiae with the villas climbing it, and the headland where the patron's house was, white among its cypresses, and beyond that the Cumae hills, and behind everything the smoke from the burning fields, going up straight into the cold. There were fishermen on the mole, and porters waiting, and the usual boys. She and Celer sat on the end with their legs over the edge and the sea moving green under their feet and watched the ship not come in.

"My father says," Celer said, "the new emperor will build a harbour at Ostia."

"There's a harbour at Ostia."

"A proper one. A basin, with a mole like this one, so the grain ships can go straight up to Rome instead of coming here and unloading and sending it on by barge. Claudius started one and it silted. My father says Trajan will finish it, because he's a soldier and soldiers like roads and harbours. And then —" He stopped.

"And then what?"

"And then nobody will come here." He said it with great flatness, looking at the sea. "That's what he says. He says he's got ten years to get his money out of ships and into land, and then it won't matter."

She did not know, then, what to do with this. She looked at the town behind them, the stacked white houses going up the hill, the baths, the temples on the forum terrace, the amphitheatre like a great cup set down on the ridge. It was the biggest place she had ever seen. It was the biggest place she could imagine, because she had not seen Rome and did not, at ten, believe in it except as a kind of weather that came from the north and changed the price of things. The idea that a soldier in Germany could decide, in a room, to make all of it not matter was so large that it went straight through her and out the other side and left nothing but the cold of the stone under her legs.

"Then we'll go to Rome," she said.

He turned and looked at her. The wind had put colour in his face and taken the scowl off it; he looked for a moment like what he was, a boy on a wall.

"Who's *we*?"

"Us. Your father. My mother. The whole name. If the patron goes up to Rome more, he'll want his people there. That's how it works. He takes you where he needs you."

"How do you know how it works?"

"Antiope."

"Antiope's a whore."

"Antiope's been to Rome," she said, "and you haven't," and that was the end of that, for the moment, because the ship had come about and the wind had gone round a point and she was coming in at last, low and black, with the spray going over her bows.

They stood up to watch her. Everyone on the mole stood up. There was no reason to; she was one ship, in February, with a dozen passengers and a cargo of whatever had paid the winter rate. But the town had had its news by road, which was no way for a port to have anything, and it wanted the sea to confirm it. So the fishermen and the porters and the boys and the two children of the Laecanii stood on the end of the mole and watched her come down the last half-mile under a scrap of sail, and when she was close enough that you could see the faces along her rail, somebody on board, a man in a cloak at the bow, cupped his hands and shouted across the water: "*Nerva's dead. Trajan's emperor. Rome's quiet.*"

And the mole let out its breath. That was the sound she remembered afterwards, more than the shout: thirty men letting their breath go, and one of them laughing, and the harbour watch starting to shout for the lines.

Celer said, beside her, so low she almost did not hear him over it: "He said *Rome's quiet*. That's the part my father will want." He had his tablet out again. He was writing it down.

* * *

They walked back along the mole behind the porters, who were already quarrelling over who would carry what, and at the harbour steps, where the mole met the land and the customs-house stood with its brazier going in the doorway, they found the patron's steward waiting for the ship.

She saw him before Celer did, because she had been taught to. Thallus was a man you learned to see early. He was Bassus's freedman — had been Bassus's slave, and his father's before him, born in the house — and he ran the villa at Baiae in the eight months of the year when the family was in Rome and ran the family's business on the coast in the four months when it was not, and he had, over thirty years of this, acquired the particular stillness of a man who has watched a great many people come and go and has outlasted all of them. He was not large. He was not, to look at, anything: a man of fifty in a good plain cloak, bald, clean-shaven, with small hands that he kept folded in front of him and a face that had been arranged a long time ago to show nothing and had set that way. He had a boy with him to carry things. He always had a boy with him; it was never the same boy.

He had come down, she understood, for the letters. A courier-ship in February meant the imperial post and whoever had paid to ride with it, and the patron's house in Rome would have sent down by the first vessel after the old man died, because the patron's house in Rome would need its steward on the coast to know certain things before the coast knew them. That was what Thallus was. He was where the family's knowledge went when it went south.

"Celer," he said, as they came up the steps. Not *boy*. He used names; it was one of the things about him. "And the Dolphin's girl." He did not use hers. "You've been out to see the ship."

"Yes, sir."

"And what did the ship say?"

Celer told him. The names, the order of them, *Rome's quiet*. Thallus listened with his hands folded and his eyes on the boy's face, and when it was done he nodded, once, the nod of a man ticking an item on a list he had already made.

"Good," he said. "Your father will want that. Tell him I'll be down to the warehouse tomorrow about the spring consignment, and tell him the master wants the villa opened early this year. The Ides of March, not April. He'll want to be on the coast when the new man comes south, if the new man comes south." He reached into his cloak and brought out a coin and put it in Celer's hand, and closed the boy's fingers over it with his own small dry ones. "For the news."

Then he looked at Fausta. He looked at her the way the men on the stairs looked at her in the mornings, except that the men on the stairs were doing a sum and Thallus was checking one he had done years ago. It did not take long. It never did.

"Tell your mother," he said, "that I'll be down on the day after the Nones. The usual hour."

"Yes, sir."

"And tell her the Syrian's gone to Rome on a horse he hired in my name, and I'll be adding what he owes her to what he owes me, and she'll have it by Saturnalia, less my trouble." He turned away, toward the ship, where the harbour watch had the gangway down and a man in a courier's cloak was already coming along it with a satchel held in both arms. "Go on, the pair of you. You're in the way."

They went. They went up the harbour street with the fish-stalls shutting and the porters coming behind them under their loads, and neither of them said anything until the macellum, where their ways divided, and then Celer opened his hand and looked at the coin in it.

"A denarius," he said. "For telling him what the whole mole heard."

"He's not paying for the news."

"What's he paying for, then?"

"For you to remember he paid." She heard her mother in her own voice and did not mind it. "Now you owe him. That's a denarius' worth of owing. It's cheap, for him."

Celer looked at the coin a while longer and then put it in his belt. He did not throw it in the harbour, which a different boy might have done, and he did not offer her half, which a different boy might also have done. He put it away and he said, "He didn't give you anything."

"He gave my mother a message."

"That's not the same."

"No," she said. "It's not." And she went down the hill to the Dolphin with the message in her, the day after the Nones, the usual hour, and the other thing, *less my trouble*, which was the first time in her life she had heard it said aloud that her mother's house was a thing Thallus took a cut of, and which she carried the way you carry a cup too full, carefully, looking at it, so as not to spill any before she had worked out what it was.

* * *

Her mother was in the kitchen when she got back, with her sleeves rolled and her hands in a basin, and she did not look round.

"The oil?"

"He says it wasn't short. He says it's the Baetican measure and the jars are smaller and you're paying the same for less but it's not short."

Hilara took her hands out of the basin and dried them on her apron, a long slow motion, and turned.

She was thirty-one that year, or thirty-two; she did not know her birth-year, having been bought at four, and had chosen one for the purposes of her manumission and stuck to it. She was small and very upright, with the broad cheekbones and the dark deep-set eyes that the Rhodians at the Dolphin said were Thracian and the Alexandrians said were Syrian and that Fausta had, looking in the bronze, decided were simply her mother's, because she had never seen them on anyone else. She had been handsome. She was still handsome, in the way of a thing that has been handled a great deal and kept its shape. Men who came down the stairs in the morning looked at her a certain way, and then looked at the girl, and did the arithmetic, and some of them said something and most of them did not. Fausta had been watching them do it since before she understood what the sum was.

"*He* says. Hermes says, or the boy?"

"The boy. He's going to tell his father I said *short*."

"Is he." Hilara considered her daughter. "Why?"

"Because I asked him to."

Something moved in her mother's face and was put away. "Well," she said. "He's his father's son, that one, and his father never did anything for nothing in his life. You remember that. You remember it the next time he does what you ask." She turned back to the basin. "And he's right about the jars, damn him. I knew it when I sent you. I wanted to see if Hermes would admit it or send me a cheaper price and call it a favour." She lifted out a wet cloth, wrung it, slapped it over the edge. "The Syrian's not coming back. I asked at the watch. He went up the Rome road this morning on a hired horse. So that's that."

"Thallus was at the steps. He says the Syrian hired the horse in his name and he'll add what's owed here to what's owed there and you'll have it by Saturnalia. Less his trouble." She watched her mother's back. "And he's coming the day after the Nones. The usual hour."

Hilara did not turn round. Her hands went on with the cloth. But there was a pause in them, the length of a breath, and Fausta, who had been listening to this woman's silences since before she could walk, heard in it the same note she had heard the night before when the Syrian went out of the door: not fear, exactly. The sound of a thing being added to a column.

"Less his trouble," Hilara said. "Yes. There's always that." Then: "Antiope says what?"

"That it'll be the same in a month."

"Antiope's seventeen." Hilara wrung out another cloth. "Listen to me. I'm going to tell you something and you're going to put it where you keep things. When an emperor dies, nothing happens to people like us. Not for a year. Not for two. The price of bread doesn't move, the ships come in, the patron opens the villa in April, everything's the same. And then one day you look up and it's all different, and you can't say when it changed, and everybody who could have told you is dead or in Rome. That's what an emperor is. He's the weather that comes after the weather." She set the basin on the floor. "The old one was a friend to the senators. This one's a friend to the soldiers. Bassus is a senator. Work it out."

Fausta worked it out. It took her a moment, because she had to go round by way of the oil and the Syrian and the boy on the step, but she got there. "If the patron goes down," she said, "we go down."

"If the patron goes down," her mother said, "we go wherever he's standing when he falls, and we're underneath. That's what the name's for." She looked at the girl for a long moment, and then, unexpectedly, reached out and pushed the hair back off her forehead with a wet hand. "Go and help Antiope with the beds. The Alexandrians are staying another night and they want the room with the window."

* * *

That night she lay in the little room under the stairs that was hers, that had been a cupboard, and listened to the house. It was a thing she did. A lodging-house at night was a kind of instrument, and if you lay still enough you could hear all of it: the Alexandrians arguing softly in the room with the window, and the creak of the stair when Antiope went up, and the different creak when she came down, and the sea on the mole, and under all of it the particular silence of her mother not sleeping, which had a quality to it that she could have picked out of a hundred silences.

She thought about the mole, and the shout across the water, and the thirty men letting out their breath. She thought about the boy writing it down. She thought, with the perfect selfishness of ten, not about the emperor, who was a name, nor the patron, who was a face she saw in April, but about the sentence *nobody will come here*, and about how he had said it looking at the sea, as if it had already happened and he had been there and come back.

She did not know that she would remember this night, and she would not have believed, if told, how much of what she was lay in it already: the shutter that would not bar until she leaned on it, the man who did not pay, her mother's hand in her hair, the boy's tablet. She thought she was waiting for her life to begin. She was wrong about that. It had begun on the second of February, or the third, and it had begun the way everything in her life would begin, with a man in a doorway deciding what he owed, and a woman letting him go, and saying *now we'll see*.

In the morning the price of bread was the same. In the morning the Alexandrians paid, in good coin, and went down to their ship. In the morning there was fog again off the water and smoke again off the hill, and a boy came down from the house above the macellum with a note in his square hand to say that his father would take back the Baetican jars at the price paid and send the Italian measure, *as a favour to the house of Hilara in consideration of long custom*, and her mother read it twice and laughed out loud, the first time in three days, and said, "Oh, he's *good*. He's good, that man. He's charged me for the favour and I haven't even seen the bill yet."

And then she said, "Give the boy a cake. No. Give him two. One for the tutor."

And Fausta gave him two, in the yard, by the shrine with the pink ghost in it, and he ate his on the spot and wrapped the other in a cloth with great seriousness, and said, "Nicias says to tell you the games make wheat dear because the emperor buys it to give away, and when he buys it the price goes up for everyone who has to pay. He says it's the first thing anyone should know about Rome and nobody does."

"What's the second thing?"

"He didn't say. He went back to sleep."

They looked at each other across the yard. The sun had got over the wall and there was steam coming off the stones. She thought — it was a child's thought, and complete, and she would never have it again in that form — that she was happy, and that it was because of the cake and the sun and the boy, in that order, and that it would always be like this.

"Come down to the water later," he said. "The Rhodian's unloading. There might be something."

"I've got the beds."

"After the beds."

"After the beds," she said, and he went, and she stood in the yard a moment longer with the cloth he had left on the step, and then she folded it and took it in, because it was his, and because that was the order of things in the house, and because she had not yet learned that there was anything else.
