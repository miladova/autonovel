## 1. The pitch

Puteoli, AD 98. Trajan has just become emperor. Two freedmen's children —
Fausta, the sailors'-lodging-house girl, and Celer, the shipping-fortune
boy — have been each other's only equal since they were six. Within the
year, the obligations their parents owe to the same declining senatorial
house pull them out of the bay and apart: him north to the Dacian war as
a patron's client, her to Rome into a great household whose rules she
does not know. Book one follows the ten years from Trajan's accession to
his Dacian triumph (98–107): the war that makes the Empire's fortune, the
city that rebuilds itself out of it, and two people who learn, in
opposite schools, what Rome does to the people it owns.


## 2. The POV characters (dual close third, alternating)

Laecania Fausta, 10 at the open, 19 at the close. Freeborn daughter of
a freedwoman; bilingual, literate by accident, quick by necessity. Wants,
at the start, nothing more than for the two houses she stands between to
stop reminding her which one she belongs to. Afraid of becoming her
mother: clever, owned, and still waiting for a man to die.
By the close she has learned that the great households are a harbour
like any other — everything has a price and a tide — and that the one
thing she cannot price is what she is willing to become.

Gaius Laecanius Celer, 11 at the open, 20 at the close. Son of the
Augustalis; the family's redemption, and told so daily. Wants to be the
first man of his blood whom no one calls a freedman's son. Afraid, under
the arrogance, that his father is right about everything — including
Fausta.
By the close he has seen a kingdom destroyed in detail and has found that
he is very, very good at the kind of work that destroys kingdoms on paper.

The chapters alternate but need not be even. Neither POV knows what the
other knows. The reader is the only person in the book who has both.


## 3. What stands in the way

- Lucius Laecanius Bassus, their parents' patron: an ageing senator with
  a villa at Baiae, debts, a son who needs a praetorship, and a legal
  claim on the services (operae) and deference (obsequium) of every
  freedman of his house and, in practice, their children. He is not
  cruel. He is behind on his own obligations and collects on theirs.
- Hermes, Celer's father: the plan for the family does not include a
  freedwoman's daughter of uncertain fathering, and he has the money to
  enforce it.
- Hilara, Fausta's mother: freed only in a draft will of a man who has
  not died; everything she has is provisional, including her daughter's
  freeborn status should anyone with money choose to question the
  manumission.
- The Dacian war itself, 101–106: two campaigns, a Danube winter, a
  kingdom erased. Italian boys who go north as clients come back as
  something else or not at all.
- Rome's great houses, where a provincial freedwoman's girl does not
  know the morning salutatio from the evening cena, cannot read a
  senator's wife's silence, and will be allowed exactly one mistake.
- The thirty-mile gap between what the two of them believe about each
  other and what is true, widening with every letter that does not
  arrive.


## 5. Period and place

Rome and the Bay of Naples, 98–107. Nerva dies January 98; Trajan,
in Germania, does not come to Rome until 99. Puteoli is still the great
port (Portus not yet rebuilt). The First Dacian War 101–102, the Second
105–106; Apollodorus's bridge over the Danube; Decebalus dead by his own
hand 106; the spoils (famously ~165 tons of gold) funding the Forum of
Trajan (begun 107) and the triumph of 107 with 123 days of games. Plotina
and Matidia hold a quiet, decisive women's court on the Palatine; the
young Hadrian is a Spaniard with a mocked accent whom Plotina favours.
Licinius Sura is the kingmaker. Pliny is consul in 100 and gives the
Panegyric. The freedman secretariat (ab epistulis, a libellis, a
rationibus) is being professionalised — the posts are passing from
imperial freedmen to equestrians, which is exactly the door a freedman's
son could be the last to slip through.

Sensory anchors: sulphur on the wind at Puteoli; the Alexandrian fleet's
arrival as a civic holiday; Baiae's villas seen from a fishing boat;
the Danube in February; the Esquiline houses at dawn with the clients
queueing; the Palatine's back corridors; the hill being cut away for the
Forum, dust over the whole Subura for years.

Period-register notes: no minutes/seconds, no "okay", no Christian idiom
(God, hell, pray in the Christian sense), no paper (papyrus, tablets,
parchment), no glass mirrors (bronze), no stirrups, no "sir". Greek is
the language of intimacy and education; Latin of law and power. Slaves
are furniture to the people who own them and the books should notice
that without comment.


## 6. Anything else

- Mature / 18+. Explicit sex on the page where it carries meaning —
  which in Rome it almost always does, because it is almost always
  about who owns whom. Written frankly, from inside the POV, never as
  decoration. Violence with weight and aftermath; the Dacian war should
  be unglamorous and specific.
- This is book one of a slow burn that runs three books. The
  friendship must be real and particular before it breaks. Do not rush
  the break. Do not hint at reconciliation. Do not let either of them
  pine on the page; let the reader do that.
- Both POVs must be intelligent. Fausta's ignorance of Rome is of
  customs, not of people. Celer's mastery of systems must have a cost
  the reader can see before he can.
- Register: literary, dry, physical. Comparables in shared/premise.md.
- No prologue. No epigraphs from Marcus Aurelius. No chapter that opens
  with weather. No character who explains the Empire to another
  character who already lives in it.
- Historical people are historical people: Trajan, Plotina, Matidia,
  Sura, Hadrian, Pliny, Apollodorus, Decebalus. They act within the
  record.
- The reader is also the commissioning editor and does not want
  spoilers. Keep all outcome-revealing material in the generated
  outline/canon files, not in shared/world.md or shared/premise.md.

## 4. What changes by the end

At the open: two children, one street apart, each other's only equal,
both still inside the bay and inside the friendship. Fausta is nobody's;
Celer is his father's.

By the close (107, the Dacian triumph):

- Fausta is inside the Palatine's women's orbit — not yet of it, but
  seen by it. She got there by surviving a great house that broke her
  once, by learning that a freedwoman's daughter who cannot be a wife can
  still be indispensable, and by one choice that cost her the person she
  had been protecting. She has chosen, knowingly, the first piece of
  herself she will trade. The lodging house in Puteoli is closed; her
  mother's manumission has been confirmed — at a price Fausta paid and
  her mother does not know about.
- Celer has come back from Dacia with a reputation, a wound that does
  not show, a patron's debt converted into a patron's gratitude, and a
  place in the secretariat — the last freedman's son through that door.
  He learned in the north that he is better at the paperwork of
  destruction than at destruction, and that he does not mind. He has
  written one document that ended lives he never saw, and it is the
  thing he is proudest of and tells no one about.
- The friendship is broken, and broken by both of them: he by a betrayal
  of omission, when he could have spoken for her and his father's plan
  and chose the plan; she by an act of commission, when something she
  did to secure her own footing in Rome — honestly, under pressure,
  without full knowledge — landed on his house and cost it badly. Each
  knows what the other did. Neither knows why. Each believes the other
  acted from contempt. That misreading is the engine of book two.
- Irreversible: they have each become the kind of Roman the other was
  raised to despise — she a creature of the households and favour, he a
  creature of the archive and the state. They meet, once, at the end,
  across the triumph, and it is already too late to be friends and far
  too early to be anything else.
- Rome's change: the Forum of Trajan has begun; Puteoli's decline has
  begun; the secretariat has passed to equestrians behind him; Plotina
  has noticed the Spaniard. The summit is visible from here.
