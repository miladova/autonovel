# Rome at the Summit — series foundations

> Working title. Spoiler-free: this file describes *who, where, when,
> and what kind of book*. It contains no plot. Safe for the reader.
>
> A historical epic trilogy. Mature / 18+ (themes, sex, language,
> violence). Dual close-third POV. Rome, AD 98–138, from Trajan's
> accession to Hadrian's death. Two children of freedmen from the Bay
> of Naples, best friends, pulled against their will into the centre
> of the Empire and made into two different kinds of Roman.

---

## 1. The premise in one breath

At the Empire's absolute height, two freedmen's children who grew up
inseparable in the port of Puteoli are taken to Rome by the obligations
their parents never finished paying — she into the great households and
the orbit of the imperial family, he to the Dacian war and then into the
imperial secretariat. Over thirty years and three reigns, Rome makes each
of them into something the other cannot forgive, and cannot stop wanting.

The arc, at the highest altitude only: **best friends → mortal enemies →
lovers**, burned slowly across three books.

## 2. What the trilogy is about underneath

**What you own, and what owns you.**

The two leads belong to the one class in Rome that has stood on *both
sides* of the line between person and property within living memory.
Their parents were owned by people who are still alive and still called
*patronus*. The children are freeborn citizens, legally clean, socially
branded. Rome is a machine that converts people into property and,
sometimes, back again — through manumission, marriage, adoption,
conquest, debt, favour. Everyone in these books is trying to own
something (a name, a rank, a person, an emperor's ear) and everyone is
owned by something they pretend is honour.

Secondary veins that run through all three books:

- **Two kinds of power.** She rises through *people* — households,
  patronesses, marriages, alliances, the women's rooms where succession
  is actually decided. He rises through *paper* — letters, petitions,
  archives, the freedman bureaucracy that holds the Empire's memory.
  Rome ran on both, and each despises and needs the other.
- **The provincial capital and the real one.** Puteoli, their home, is
  the Empire's great port in 98. Trajan's new harbour at Portus
  (finished c. 113) will slowly strangle it. Their hometown dies in the
  background as they rise in the city that killed it.
- **Peak as a vantage, not a plateau.** The reader knows this is the
  summit. The characters do not. Nobody in these books says "golden
  age".
- **Learning the rules by breaking them.** Her brief: a world whose
  customs, hierarchies and unspoken rules she does not yet understand;
  mistakes have consequences; every gain demands a sacrifice; the
  question is how much of herself she is willing to change to claim a
  place among the powerful. His brief is the mirror: he understands the
  rules too well, too early, and the question is what is left of him.

## 3. Period and place

### The window: 98–138

| Reign | Years | Texture |
|---|---|---|
| Trajan | 98–117 | The soldier-emperor; conquest of Dacia (101–102, 105–106); the Forum and Markets of Trajan rising on the spoils; the Parthian war; an emperor who is genuinely popular and genuinely absent. Empress **Plotina**, niece **Matidia**, grand-niece **Sabina** run a women's court of enormous quiet influence. |
| The succession | 117–118 | Trajan dies at Selinus in Cilicia; the adoption of Hadrian is announced by Plotina *after* the death; four ex-consuls are executed in Hadrian's first months. The most dangerous year in the century. |
| Hadrian | 117–138 | The travelling emperor; Greek-loving, learned, paranoid, brilliant; the Wall, the Pantheon, the villa at Tibur; Antinous; the Bar Kokhba war; the long grim business of choosing an heir. The chancery under Suetonius, then others. |

Book one lives in Trajan's first decade (98–107, from accession through
the Dacian wars to the triumph). Books two and three take the succession
and Hadrian's reign. Exact ranges are fixed when each book is seeded.

### Puteoli (Pozzuoli), Bay of Naples — where they are from

- The Empire's great commercial port until Portus supersedes it.
  Alexandrian grain fleet, Eastern luxury trade, Greek the street
  language as much as Latin. Syrian, Jewish, Egyptian communities; a
  famous Serapeum; the cult of Isis; the sulphur fields (Solfatara) that
  smell the whole town.
- **The freedman town.** Shipping fortunes made by ex-slaves; the
  *Augustales* — the freedmen's prestige priesthood, the only honour
  club the law lets them join; marble tombs along the roads bought by
  men whose fathers were sold. Petronius put Trimalchio's dinner here.
- **Baiae, next door.** The senatorial class at play: hot springs,
  villas on the water, boats, brothels, poets, debts. The children of
  Puteoli grow up watching Rome's masters off duty.
- The amphitheatre (among Italy's largest), the emporium, the mole
  into the harbour, the *macellum* with its market columns, the
  fishponds of the rich, Lake Avernus, the Sibyl's Cumae over the hill.

### Rome — where they are taken

Not postcard Rome. The Rome of these books is: the great houses on the
Esquiline and Caelian with their morning crowds of clients; the Palatine
as a working palace full of slaves and freedmen who actually run it; the
Subura; the Forum of Trajan being built out of the side of a hill; the
baths; the law-courts in the Basilica Julia; the women's rooms; the
archive; the *carcer*; the Tiber docks; and, later, Hadrian's villa at
Tibur and the long roads out to the provinces.

## 4. The two leads

Names are working names. The patron house is invented (a declining
branch of the real *Laecanii*, consular in Nero's day, faded by
Trajan's). All freedmen and their children carry the patron's nomen —
that shared name is the first thing anyone in Rome learns about them.

### Laecania Fausta — "Fausta"

- Born 88, Puteoli. Daughter of **Laecania Hilara**, a freedwoman of
  the Laecanii who keeps a lodging house for sailors and factors near
  the emporium. Father not named; the town has opinions.
- Grew up poor, bilingual (Greek at home, Latin outside), literate
  because the boy's father paid for a tutor and she sat in. Knows the
  harbour, the price of everything, how to read a room of drunk men.
  Knows nothing whatever about how the great live, except what she has
  seen from the Baiae shore.
- Defining trait: **she learns.** Fast, by watching, and she does not
  forget a slight or a kindness. Her weakness is the same muscle: she
  adapts so well that she can lose track of what she is adapting *from*.
- "Fausta" — *lucky* — is the kind of name a freedwoman gives a daughter
  born free. She will come to hear it as a joke, then as a dare.
- Her brief across the trilogy: a rise into the highest circles of
  Roman power from circumstances entirely beyond her control; the great
  households, shifting alliances, rivalries, the dangerous politics
  around the imperial family. Neither effortless nor assured.

### Gaius Laecanius Celer — "Celer"

- Born 87, Puteoli. Son of **Gaius Laecanius Hermes**, freedman of the
  same house, now a shipping *Augustalis* with a fortune, a marble tomb
  already built, and a plan: his grandchildren will be equestrians and
  no one will remember the name *Hermes*. The son got a Latin cognomen
  on purpose.
- Grew up rich in a town where rich is not the same as respectable.
  Tutored in Greek and Latin letters, rhetoric, accounts. Quick, dry,
  observant, with the specific arrogance of a boy who has been told his
  whole life that he is the family's redemption.
- Defining trait: **he understands systems.** He sees how a thing
  works — a harbour, a household, a war, a secretariat — faster than
  anyone, and can make himself necessary to it. His weakness: he
  mistakes being necessary for being safe, and being useful for being
  loved.
- "Celer" — *swift*. He is.
- His brief across the trilogy: north to the Dacian war as a boy under
  the patron's obligation; back from the destruction of a kingdom as
  someone else; then into the imperial chancery, the freedman
  bureaucracy where the Empire's memory is kept — power as letters,
  access, and knowing where everything is buried.

### The friendship

They were raised a street apart under the same patron's name, one in a
house with marble and one in a house with sailors. Hermes paid for the
tutor; Hilara's girl sat at the back. They were, from about six, each
other's only equal — the only other child who understood exactly what it
meant to be the freeborn child of a slave in a town that measured people
by both.

What binds them before Rome: a shared language (Greek-Latin-harbour),
a shared tutor, a shared knowledge of what the great look like naked at
Baiae, a shared contempt for being pitied, and the specific intimacy of
two children who have each seen the other's parent humiliated by the
same man.

What is already cracked before Rome: the class gap neither of them
admits; his father's plans, which do not include her; her mother's
house, which he has been forbidden to enter since he was twelve; and
the simple fact that he has always been the one with choices.

## 5. The two families and the patron

- **The Laecanii** (patron house): once consular, now a single ageing
  senator, **Lucius Laecanius Bassus**, with a villa at Baiae, debts,
  ambitions for a praetorian son, and dozens of freedmen whose services
  and legacies he regards as his by right. He is not a villain. He is a
  landlord of human obligations, and he is behind on his own.
- **Hermes's house** (rising): the shipping fortune; a second wife
  of good freeborn family bought with it; the *Augustales*; the tomb;
  the plan. Hermes loves his son as a project. He loathes Hilara for
  reasons the town suspects and the children half-know.
- **Hilara's house** (broken): the lodging house, the sailors, the
  factors, the girls who work upstairs some seasons, the Isis shrine in
  the yard. Hilara is clever, unsentimental, and still owned in every
  way that counts by a patron who freed her in his will's draft and then
  did not die.

*Why "against their will":* freedmen's families went where the patron
needed them. That is not a plot device; it is the law of *obsequium* and
*operae*. The pull to Rome is structural, which is what makes it an
epic rather than an adventure.

## 6. Real people who will be on the page

Treated as real people with the record respected: **Trajan**,
**Plotina**, **Matidia**, **Sabina**, **Hadrian** (from his twenties,
a provincial Spaniard with a Greek accent that the Senate mocks),
**Licinius Sura** (Trajan's kingmaker), **Sosius Senecio**, **Pliny the
Younger** (for one or two very good dinner scenes), **Decebalus** (seen
only from the Roman side), **Apollodorus of Damascus** (building the
Forum, the bridge, everything), later **Suetonius**, **Attianus**,
**Antinous**, **Servianus**. Invented people never do what the record
says a real person did.

## 7. Register and craft constraints

- **Dual close third**, alternating by chapter, not necessarily evenly.
  Past tense. No head-hopping within a chapter.
- **Mature, explicit, on the page.** Roman sex written as the Romans
  lived it — bound up with ownership, status and risk; between unequals
  far more often than equals; sometimes tender, often unlovely, never
  coy and never gratuitous. Violence with weight and aftermath.
  Language blunt without modern slang.
- **Slow burn means slow.** Across three books. The romance is earned
  through enmity, not despite it. No instant chemistry beats, no
  "they both knew". The reader should spend long stretches unsure that
  either of them deserves the other.
- **Layered.** Every major character has a reasonable position. The
  Empire is not evil, not glorious; it is a system that works, at a
  cost it has outsourced.
- **History as payload.** The reader of historical fiction comes partly
  to learn: how a freedman bought a tomb, how a will was witnessed, how
  the *salutatio* worked, what the Forum of Trajan cost and whom it
  cost, how letters moved, what a Dacian winter did to Italian boys.
- **Comparables for calibration:** *Memoirs of Hadrian* (Yourcenar) for
  intelligence; *Wolf Hall* (Mantel) for power as texture and intimacy
  with the powerful; Mary Renault for sexual frankness without
  sentiment; *I, Claudius* for court menace; Robert Harris's Cicero
  trilogy for pace.
- **Not this:** gladiator-as-hero; Christians as the moral compass;
  orgies as set dressing; the Senate as a chorus of toga'd villains;
  modern feminist speeches in Roman mouths; "the Empire is like America";
  a prophecy; a secret heir.

## 8. Spoiler policy for this project

The reader of this trilogy is also its commissioning editor and does
**not** want plot. All planning that reveals outcomes — endings, turns,
who betrays whom, when the enmity breaks — lives only in:

- the fenced `SPOILERS` block at the bottom of each book's `seed.txt`;
- the generated `outline.md`, `canon.md`, `events.md`, `timeline.md`,
  `shared/characters.md` (post-generation), and anything under
  `briefs/`, `eval_logs/`, `edit_logs/`.

Everything else in `shared/` is meant to be safe. If in doubt, don't
open it.
