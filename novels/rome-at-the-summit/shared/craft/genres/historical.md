# Historical fiction: what the reader comes for

The genre guide for historical fiction and its subgenres. Package-owned
prompt material: `autonovel refresh-templates` keeps it current. Every
command that shapes or judges a book whose genre is historical reads the
sections named for it (`autonovel genre show --book <name>` names this
file; `shared/craft/genres/README.md` says which command reads what).

**Why it exists.** In one book drafted with this pipeline, the cold first
readers marked the history paragraphs "skimmed", the judge listed period
glosses among the weakest sentences, and the editor's summary turned that
into "fewer explanation paragraphs". The author, a reader of historical
fiction, corrected it: part of why people read it is to learn some history
(real events and people, how people lived, worked and saw the world, and
places that still stand under the names they had then). The history may be
badly written or badly placed. It is not the fat. Nothing in the pipeline
said what this reader comes for, so every loop that reads for pace pushed
to cut it. This guide says it, with the evidence, and says what each
command does about it.

**The rule this guide adds to every other craft document.** In historical
fiction, the period is part of the payload. A history passage that a
reader skims is a delivery problem: move it to where it is at stake, give
it to a person who needs it, make it a scene or a clause. It is never
fixed by cutting the history. (Recaps of the story's own plot are not
history, and are still fair to cut. Nor is a second telling: a gloss, a
rule of the period or a historical fact is given once in the book, where
it first matters, and its repeats are cut like any other repetition,
`PROSE.md` §7 rule 9. The payload is the history, not the number of times
it is said; on one book the same explanation of who chose the Emperor
came four times in its first chapter, and the reader found it slow.)

## The promise

### What counts as historical fiction

- **The Historical Novel Society.** "To be deemed historical (in our
  sense), a novel must have been written at least fifty years after the
  events described. Or written by someone who was not alive at the time of
  those events, and therefore approaches them only by research." It counts
  alternate histories, pseudo-histories, time-slip novels, historical
  fantasies and multiple-time novels too (Lee, HNS, "Guide: our definition
  of historical fiction").
- **Sarah Johnson** (talk at the AWP conference, 2002, for the *Historical
  Novels Review*): "a novel which is set fifty or more years in the past",
  "one in which the author is writing from research rather than personal
  experience."
- **Joyce Saricks**, the readers'-advisory standard: set "before the
  author's lifetime or experience", with "accurate historical detail:
  geography, customs, beliefs, culture, society, habits, characters and
  events" as a defining appeal (*Readers' Advisory Guide to Genre
  Fiction*, 2nd ed., pp. 291–2, as quoted by NoveList).

### What its readers say they come for

The largest evidence is M. K. Tod's reader surveys (self-selected, about
85% women; 805 respondents in 2012, 2,440 in 2013, 2,033 in 2015):

- **The three top reasons, the same in 2012 and 2013:** "To bring the past
  to life, appreciating how people lived and coped in very different
  times"; "Because it's a great story"; "To understand and learn about
  historical periods without reading non-fiction." In 2012, 79.3% of women
  chose bringing the past to life as their top reason. In 2015, "TO BRING
  THE PAST TO LIFE is the dominant reason."
- **Among the most favoured kinds of story** (2012): "The life of a
  significant historical figure" and "The life of lesser known historical
  figures."
- **Readers in their own words** (2015): "My favorite thing about reading
  historical fiction is finding out about the every day lives of people in
  the past." "The 'educational' part is important to me." "My historical
  fiction reading motivates me to do further reading on time periods and
  people."
- **A national sample agrees.** In the American Historical Association's
  2020 survey of 1,816 US adults, of those who named historical fiction
  among their main sources about the past, 36% said it made them want to
  learn more about history "a great deal" and 42% "some" (Burkholder and
  Schaffer, *History, the Past, and Public Culture*, 2021).

Authors and editors say the same. Ken Follett: "people like the feeling
that they are learning something as well as enjoying the story" (*Gulf
News*, 2018). Susan Opie, formerly a senior editor at HarperCollins:
"readers want to come away from the novel feeling that they have been
entertained and that they've learnt something, as well" (Jericho
Writers). Sharon Kay Penman: "I see historical fiction as a means of
awakening curiosity about the past. Readers who might not buy a biography
will pick up a historical novel" (Medievalists.net, 2011).

### The payload, item by item

What the reader comes to meet, and what this pipeline must protect:

1. **Real events**, on the page: the election, the siege, the fire, the
   trial, played as scenes where they are the story, not reported.
2. **Real people**, met as people, in the room, with their recorded words
   and acts where the record has them (`shared/craft/CHARACTER.md` §5).
3. **Daily life**: how people worked (the tools, the trade, the day's
   hours), what things cost and how money moved, how far a day's ride
   went, what they ate and wore, how houses were heated and lit, the
   church calendar, sickness and death, law and punishment, and how news
   travelled and how long it took.
4. **How people saw the world**: what they believed, feared and took for
   granted; what was honourable, shameful or obvious to them. Readers rank
   "behaviour that is realistic to the time period" among the most
   important things that make characters come alive (Tod, 2015).
5. **Places**, under the names they had then: what a city looked like, who
   ran it, what it lived on, and, where it still stands, a picture exact
   enough that a reader who goes there will recognise it. Readers go: after
   *The Da Vinci Code* was published, visits to Rosslyn Chapel rose from
   38,141 to 79,916 a year, and 49% of visitors surveyed named the novel or
   its film as a reason (Rosslyn Chapel Trust, 2024). Cornwell's novels end
   with a note on "what one might see at the modern sites of the events
   described." Shrewsbury lays monk's-foot plates in its streets for
   walkers following Ellis Peters's Brother Cadfael; the National Trust
   publishes a guide to the settings of *Wolf Hall*.

### What readers object to: the dump, not the history

- **Inaccuracy is the larger complaint, three to one.** Asked what spoils
  historical fiction, 44% named inaccuracies ("seeing modern
  sensibilities in a historic setting, anachronisms, dialogue that does
  not fit the period, poor research, moving major dates"); 15% named too
  much detail ("stories weighed down with reams of historical detail,
  almost as though the author wanted to include every bit of research")
  (Tod, 2012).
- **What they mean by too much:** "I don't like authors who just put in
  'Wikipedia' paragraphs instead of building historical atmosphere." "The
  atmospheric details shouldn't be over-explained like a dictionary,
  either" (Tod's readers, 2012).
- **Daily life as a catalogue rates low; daily life in a person rates
  high.** In 2015, readers rated "learning about food, clothing and the
  way they live" among the least significant things that make characters
  come alive, and complex characters with behaviour true to the period
  the most. Daily life is wanted when it is a person's life, under
  pressure, not a list.

So the reader's complaint is never "there is history here". It is "the
history stopped the story", "it was a lecture", or "it was wrong". Each
has a remedy that keeps the history.

## Subgenres

The standard readers'-advisory taxonomy is Sarah Johnson's (*Historical
Fiction: A Guide to the Genre*, 2005; *Historical Fiction II*, 2009),
whose chapters are: traditional historical novels, multi-period epics,
romancing the past, sagas, Western historical novels, historical
mysteries, adventures in history, historical thrillers, literary
historical novels, Christian historical novels, time-slip novels,
alternate history and historical fantasy. The biographical novel has no
chapter of its own there: Johnson flags biographical novels within
entries, NoveList lists "biographical fiction" and "starring famous
figures" as appeal terms, and the critic Michael Lackey has defined it as
its own form. A book can be two of these at once: a biographical novel
with a thriller's engine is common.

Each subgenre below gives its promise, what it must deliver on the page,
and how it changes the payload above. The ids are what `project.yaml`'s
`subgenres:` takes (`autonovel genre list`).

### `biographical` — Biographical novel

- **Promise.** A real person's life, or its decisive stretch, from inside.
- **What it is.** Lackey: fiction "that names its protagonist after an
  actual biographical figure"; where the historical novel "emphasises how
  the moment (history) makes the human", the biographical novel
  "underscores how an autonomous individual can and does create the age."
  The real name "grounds the narrative more firmly in the concrete and
  puts some limit on the authors' freedom" (quoted in Ryan, *TEXT*, 2022).
- **The contract with the record.** Mantel: "don't lie. Don't go against
  known facts." "You must be able to justify your decisions to the
  well-informed." "The reason you must stick by the truth is that it is
  better, stranger, stronger, than anything you can make up" (Reith
  Lecture 4, 2017). Follett's rule for real people: "either the scene did
  happen, or it might have; either these words were used, or they might
  have been" (Penguin Random House Q&A). Invention goes in the gaps: the
  motive, the private talk, the why (`EDITING.md` §17.1–17.2).
- **Must deliver.** The person's defining recorded acts played on the
  page, not reported; the private cost of the public life; the people
  around them as people; a decision the reader watches being made under
  uncertainty, so the figure is becoming rather than a finished monument
  (`EDITING.md` §17.3, H6).
- **Payload weighting.** Real people and events lead; daily life arrives
  through the figure's work (a banker's ledgers, a painter's pigments).
  Readers rank this among the stories they most want (Tod, 2012).
- **Failures.** The monument; the chronicle of one documented episode per
  chapter (`EDITING.md` H4); the a-posteriori prophet who knows what the
  figure means to history (H7).
- **Comparables.** Mantel, *Wolf Hall*; Yourcenar, *Memoirs of Hadrian*;
  Graves, *I, Claudius*; Stone, *The Agony and the Ecstasy*; Vidal,
  *Burr*.

### `political-thriller` — Historical thriller (political)

- **Promise.** A contest for power against a deadline, in a past whose
  outcome the reader may know.
- **Suspense with a known ending.** Robert Harris, asked whether knowing
  the ending spoils it: "Not at all. One of the most successful postwar
  thrillers was 'The Day of the Jackal.' We all know that President de
  Gaulle was not assassinated. It doesn't stop it being thrilling… people
  waiting for the shoe to drop in a way is often a source of greater drama
  than when you don't know what's going to happen" (PBS NewsHour, 2018).
  The question becomes how, how close and at what cost (`EDITING.md`
  §17.6, §18.3; `DESIGN.md` §4).
- **Must deliver.** The thriller contract (`EDITING.md` Part IV;
  `DESIGN.md` §14): a hard date; an opponent with a plan the reader can
  watch working; the protagonist at someone's mercy; a reversal that
  breaks the plan; the plan repaired by something planted; a climax the
  protagonist decides; two endings. And the historical contract: real
  dates and outcomes honoured, the period made concrete.
- **Payload weighting.** The thriller's machinery must run *through* the
  period, not past it: the courier's road, the price of a horse, the hour
  the gates shut, how long a letter takes are the clock and the obstacles.
  Historical-fiction readers rank thrillers among their least favourite
  story types (Tod, 2012) while ranking the past brought to life first: a
  thriller that sheds the period to gain pace loses the reader it was
  written for.
- **Failures.** Pace bought by cutting the history; a clock paid in
  summary; an opponent who only reacts (`EDITING.md` TH1–TH7).
- **Comparables.** Harris, *Imperium*, *An Officer and a Spy*, *Munich*;
  Forsyth, *The Day of the Jackal*.

### `traditional` — Traditional historical novel

- **Promise.** A story of invented people set among real events, the
  period rendered whole.
- **Must deliver.** A protagonist whose private fate is bent by the public
  events (Lukács's middling hero, `EDITING.md` §17.3); real figures met at
  the edges; the period's daily life throughout.
- **Comparables.** Scott, *Waverley*; Follett, *The Pillars of the
  Earth*; Penman, *The Sunne in Splendour*.

### `adventure` — Adventures in history (military, naval)

- **Promise.** Campaigns, voyages and escapes: what soldiers and sailors
  did, carried and feared.
- **Must deliver.** The working detail of the fighting trade (drill,
  weapons, ships, supply, wounds); real battles from inside a unit; an
  ending note on the actual campaign. Men among Tod's 2012 respondents
  chose adventure and stories "with a military angle" among their top
  three.
- **Comparables.** Cornwell's Sharpe novels; O'Brian's Aubrey–Maturin
  novels.

### `mystery` — Historical mystery

- **Promise.** A crime solved inside the period with the period's means of
  finding out (no fingerprints, no police force where there was none).
  NoveList: "A whodunit set in the past that pays close attention to
  historical details."
- **Must deliver.** The mystery contract (fair clues, the reveal) and an
  investigation that teaches the period: who had authority, what counted
  as evidence, how a body was examined.
- **Comparables.** Peters, the Cadfael chronicles; Eco, *The Name of the
  Rose*; Sansom, the Shardlake novels.

### `romance` — Romancing the past

- **Promise.** A love story whose obstacles are the period's: rank, law,
  faith, war, money.
- **Must deliver.** The courtship conventions of the time and their
  penalties; the romance's ending (a union in historical romance; in the
  romantic historical novel the history may win).
- **Comparables.** Heyer's Regency novels; Gabaldon, *Outlander*.

### `saga` — Saga

- **Promise.** A family or a business followed across years or
  generations, living through the history that changes it.
- **Must deliver.** A question that spans the generations, or one per
  segment (`EDITING.md` §17.5); the house, the firm or the land as a
  character; the period's turns felt in the family's fortunes.
- **Comparables.** Galsworthy, *The Forsyte Saga*; Mann, *Buddenbrooks*.

### `multi-period-epic` — Multi-period epic

- **Promise.** One place across centuries.
- **Must deliver.** A recurring place-signature and a lineage the reader
  can follow; each era's daily life distinct.
- **Comparables.** Rutherfurd, *Sarum*; Michener, *The Source*.

### `western` — Western historical

- **Promise.** The American frontier as it was: land, law, labour and
  violence.
- **Comparables.** McMurtry, *Lonesome Dove*.

### `literary` — Literary historical

- **Promise.** The past as a way of seeing a life or an idea; language and
  interior first. The history is still payload; it is delivered through
  consciousness rather than event.
- **Comparables.** Mantel, *Wolf Hall*; Morrison, *Beloved*; Saunders,
  *Lincoln in the Bardo*.

### `christian` — Christian historical

- **Promise.** Faith as the story's question, in a past setting.

### `time-slip` — Time-slip and dual timeline

- **Promise.** A past and a present story that light each other; the
  present-day thread often visits the places the past one happened in.
- **Must deliver.** Each timeline its own stake; the link (an object, a
  house, a letter) discovered, not announced.
- **Comparables.** Erskine, *Lady of Hay*; Cunningham, *The Hours*.

### `alternate-history` — Alternate history

- **Promise.** One recorded outcome changed, and its consequences
  followed honestly. The reader must know the real history well enough to
  feel the change; the book teaches it.
- **Comparables.** Harris, *Fatherland*; Roth, *The Plot Against America*.

### `historical-fantasy` — Historical fantasy

- **Promise.** The real past with one magical or uncanny element let in;
  everything else true to the period.
- **Comparables.** Clarke, *Jonathan Strange & Mr Norrell*; Cornwell's
  Warlord trilogy.

## Research

`/autonovel:research` and `/autonovel:gen-world` read this section. Find
out, beyond the events and the people:

- **Daily life, as a checklist per major setting:** work and its tools;
  coins, prices, wages, credit and how money was carried or sent; roads,
  rivers, travel times and costs; food, drink and meals; clothes by rank;
  houses, heat, light and water; the church calendar and the hours of the
  day (bells, canonical hours, the calendar in use); sickness, medicine
  and death; law, courts and punishment; letters, couriers and how long
  news took between the book's cities; forms of address and titles.
- **How people saw the world:** what this society believed about God,
  money, rank, women, foreigners, the future; what was shameful, what was
  honourable; what an educated and an uneducated person of the time each
  knew.
- **Places, for the places ledger:** each major place's name then, size,
  who ran it, what it lived on, what it looked, sounded and smelled like;
  and, for every building, street or site that survives, its name today
  and what a visitor can still see. Mark what does not survive. The design
  and the back matter both use this.
- **The true but surprising.** Record any real detail a modern reader
  would take for an anachronism. Jo Walton's "Tiffany problem": the name
  Tiffany is medieval, but "the reader will be pulled out of the story by
  Tiffany" (*The Allusionist*, 2022). The draft can use such a detail
  only with a clause that makes it believable.

Robert Harris: "I generally know about ten times more than I'll ever use.
And then it's very important to forget it all, and just tell the story,
using only those facts that are relevant" (Walter Scott Prize interview,
2023). In this genre, "relevant" includes what the reader came to learn.

## Design

`/autonovel:design`, `/autonovel:gen-outline` and `/autonovel:cast` read
this section.

- **The ledger: `## What the reader comes to learn`.** The design lists,
  under four heads, what the book will teach, each item with the chapter
  that delivers it and how (a scene, through whose eyes, at what
  pressure):
  - **Events**: the real events the book plays on the page.
  - **People**: the real people the reader meets, and the scene where
    each is first met as a person.
  - **Daily life**: the work, money, travel, faith, food and law the
    reader will see in use, each attached to a character who needs it.
  - **Places**: each major place with its name then, its `set_piece:`
    chapter (`DESIGN.md` §12), and `still standing:` with today's name
    when it survives.
  Check it like plants and payoffs (`DESIGN.md` §8): every chapter
  delivers at least one item, through a person, at a moment of pressure;
  the book is not front-loaded (the first chapters cannot carry the whole
  ledger); and the climax's chapters still teach something, because the
  reader came for the period to the last page.
- **The genre contract** (`DESIGN.md` §14) adds the active subgenres'
  "must deliver" lists above, each with its chapter.
- **Biographical.** Decide the span (a life, or its decisive stretch:
  `EDITING.md` §17.5), which recorded acts are played as scenes, and the
  invention list (people, scenes and motives the record lacks), which
  becomes the historical note.
- **Thriller.** The clock and the obstacles are made of the period: the
  design names, for each act's deadline, the period fact that sets it (a
  vote's date, a road's length, a fair's closing day).

## Drafting

`/autonovel:draft`, `/autonovel:revise` and `/autonovel:brief` read this
section. The other craft documents still apply (`DESIGN.md` §12 place and
§13a terms; `PROSE.md`); this is how the period reaches the page.

1. **Through a person who needs it now.** The merchant reads prices, the
   courier knows the road, the newcomer is told what he must not do.
   Mantel: "The task of historical fiction is to take the past out of the
   archive and relocate it in a body" (Reith Lecture 4).
2. **At pressure.** Give the fact when it bears on the scene's stake: the
   gate shuts at dusk *and the rider is late*. Michelle Barker: "Make the
   information something that causes problems for the characters"
   (*Writers Helping Writers*, 2022).
3. **A clause at first use, once.** A term the story turns on is
   explained in plain words where it first appears in the book
   (`DESIGN.md` §13a), and not again: back after a long absence it gets a
   word of reminder at most. The explanation is the payload; the
   repetition is not.
4. **A sentence or two at a time.** Barker: "A sentence or two of
   information is enough." "Sprinkle it throughout a scene."
5. **Plain telling is allowed.** A short plain paragraph at an arrival or
   a turn, in the narrator's voice, is one of the historical novelist's
   tools (Emma Darwin, `EDITING.md` §17.7). The failure is the lecture:
   a paragraph that stops the scene to explain what nobody in it needs.
6. **Places walked, not surveyed** (`DESIGN.md` §12): the set piece seen
   through someone with reasons of their own, the place's name of the
   time, and, for a place that survives, the details a visitor would
   still recognise. Do not have the narrator step out of the period to say
   it "still stands today": that is a flash-forward (`EDITING.md` V3). The
   back matter says what survives.
7. **Real events as scenes** where they are the story; a report only
   where they happen elsewhere and arrive as news (and then the news
   arriving is the scene: who brings it, how late, what it costs).
8. **People of their time.** Behaviour, belief and speech true to the
   period, never modern attitudes in period dress. Penman: "I will not
   sensationalize history or create medieval characters with modern
   attitudes." The reader's largest complaint is "seeing modern
   sensibilities in a historic setting" (Tod, 2012).
9. **The true but surprising** gets a clause that makes it credible (the
   Tiffany problem, above).
10. **Never contradict the record** (Mantel, Harris: `EDITING.md` §17.1).
    Invent in the gaps.

Emma Darwin: "Your readers want to live and breathe history, but they
won't keep reading if the narrative grinds to a halt on a hill of
historical detail" (Jericho Writers). Both halves are the brief.

## The first reader

`/autonovel:first-read` and `/autonovel:draft`'s cold reader read this
section with `shared/craft/FIRST-READER.md`; it changes who the reader is
and adds to what they report.

- **Who the reader is.** An intelligent adult who reads historical
  fiction for pleasure, partly to learn the period, and who knows nothing
  about this one. They enjoy meeting real people and events, seeing how
  people lived, and recognising real places; they dislike lectures,
  anachronism and modern attitudes in period dress.
- **Add a section after "Couldn't picture": What I learned.** What you
  learned about the period in this chapter (people, events, how they
  lived and worked, how they saw things, places) that you enjoyed; and
  what you wanted to know more about.
- **Split "Skimmed or bored" in two.**
  - *Skimmed: it stopped the story.* The history came at the wrong
    moment, or as a lecture. Say what it was about and what would have
    made you want it (a person needing it, a scene, a shorter clause).
  - *Skimmed: I didn't want it at all.* Rare; say why.
- **What the writer does with it.** A history passage under either head
  is moved, given to a person, turned into a scene or a clause. It is
  never simply cut. An empty "What I learned" is as serious as a "no" to
  the page turn: the chapter has not delivered what this reader came for.
  A period fact under **Told again** is the exception that proves the
  rule: the reader already has it, so the repeat is cut and the first
  telling stays.

## Judging

`/autonovel:evaluate`, `/autonovel:review` and `/autonovel:reader-panel`
read this section.

### The `history` dimension (chapter mode)

Score how well the chapter delivers the period, 0–10, with
`{score, weakest_moment, fix, note}` like every dimension:

- **What it measures.** Real people and events met on the page; daily
  life seen in use by people who need it; places under their names of the
  time, pictured; how people saw the world, with behaviour true to the
  period; accuracy (no anachronism of fact, word or attitude); and the
  delivery: given at pressure, through a person, without stalling the
  scene.
- **9–10.** A reader learns several things about the period they enjoy
  and remember, every one through a person at a moment that matters, with
  no lecture and no error.
- **7–8.** The period is present and accurate; one passage stalls or is
  told where it could be shown, or the chapter teaches little that is
  new.
- **5–6.** The period is backdrop: named but not seen, or delivered in a
  block a reader skims; or one anachronism of attitude.
- **Under 5.** The chapter could be set anywhere, or it lectures, or it
  gets the record wrong.
- `weakest_moment`: the passage where the period is least delivered (a
  lecture, a backdrop, an error). `fix`: where it should go and through
  whom, never "cut it". When the chapter teaches little, the fix names
  what from the design's `## What the reader comes to learn` ledger
  belongs here.

### What the other dimensions must not mark down

- **A gloss is not weak prose for being a gloss.** `prose_quality`,
  `register` and `concreteness` judge how a period fact is written, not
  whether it is there. A plain sentence of period fact in the narrator's
  voice is a tool (Darwin), not a fault.
- **`three_weakest_sentences`** may quote a period passage only when it
  is badly delivered, and its fix is placement or rewriting, never
  removal.
- **`sense_of_place`** and **`history`** overlap on purpose: place
  pictured scores there; place taught (its name, who ran it, what
  survives) scores here.
- **`repetition`** marks down a period fact, gloss or rule told a second
  time, never one told once; its fix keeps the best-placed telling. A
  history passage is not "repetition" because the period is everywhere
  in the book; the same explanation given again is.

### Whole-book judges

- **`/autonovel:evaluate --full`** notes, under `theme_coherence` or
  `world_consistency`, whether the design's ledger was delivered across
  the book and whether the period thins in the last act.
- **`/autonovel:reader-panel`'s genre reader** is this genre's reader as
  the first-reader section describes, and answers: what did I learn, what
  did I enjoy learning, where did the history stop the story, and did any
  person act out of their time?
- **`/autonovel:review`** judges the period's delivery and accuracy as
  part of the book's achievement; it does not recommend cutting it.

## Cutting

`/autonovel:adversarial-edit` and `/autonovel:apply-cuts` read this
section.

- **Never `CUT` period content:** real events and people, daily life, how
  people saw the world, places and their names. A candidate that is
  period content delivered badly is type `MISPLACED`, with action `MOVE`
  (say where: the scene where it is at stake, the person who would notice
  or say it) or `REWRITE` (dramatise it, or compress it into a clause,
  with the rewrite). `apply-cuts` never removes a `MISPLACED` candidate;
  the brief carries it.
- **`OVER-EXPLAIN` is about the story, not the period:** explaining an
  emotion the scene showed, a plot point the reader already has, a motive
  twice. It does not apply to a period fact told once.
- **Still cuttable:** recaps of the story's own earlier chapters; the same
  period fact told twice (cut the worse-placed one: `REDUNDANT`); research
  that is wrong.
- **Exhibitionism.** Irving Stone warned against "See what I know!"
  (`EDITING.md` §17.4); in this genre the remedy is still `MISPLACED`
  (give it to a person, at a moment that needs it), because the reader
  wanted the fact, not the display.

## Editing

`/autonovel:developmental-edit`, `/autonovel:structure-review` and
`/autonovel:review` read this section with `EDITING.md` Part III
(questions H1–H8).

**H9. Does the book deliver what this reader came for?** Count, from the
pages: the real events played as scenes and those only reported; the
real people met as people; the kinds of daily life seen in use; the
places pictured, and which still stand. Compare with the design's ledger.
- *Fail:* the period is backdrop; or the history is front-loaded and the
  last act forgets it; or a reader finishes knowing little more than the
  outcome.
- *Fix:* name where the book could give the reader more of the period,
  and through whom. Recommending more is in scope; recommending less is
  not.

**H10. Where history is skimmed, is it placed and delivered well?**
- *Fail:* glosses stacked at a chapter's opening; a guidebook paragraph
  on arrival; a lecture in dialogue ("As you know"); the history in the
  same paragraphs as the plot recap.
- *Fix:* move it to where it is at stake; give it to a person; make it a
  scene or a clause. Never cut it as the remedy.

**H11. Do the people live inside their own time?**
- *Fail:* modern attitudes, modern idiom, modern assumptions about
  privacy, faith, rank or women, without the period's reasons.
- *Fix:* the belief of the time, given its reasons on the page, and the
  cost to a character who departs from it.

**Typical failures in this pipeline.** The judge marks glosses down as
prose; cold readers report history as skimmed and the editor turns it
into a cut; a thriller subgenre buys pace by shedding the period; places
are named but not seen; the narrator flashes forward to say what
survives. The editor's letter names each where it occurs and prescribes
delivery, not removal.

## Back matter

`/autonovel:appendix` reads this section.

- **The historical note** (author's note). What was invented, compressed
  or moved, and why; the main sources. Penman: "any changes I make with
  known facts are always relatively minor and I then mention the change
  in my Author's Notes" (Goodreads Q&A). Stephanie Dray and Laura Kamoie:
  the note should "explain our choices and changes to the historical
  record", "cite your sources and explain that the craziest things in
  your novel are true", and "you shouldn't apologize for them" (*Writer's
  Digest*, 2026). (Against: Emma Darwin, following John Mullan, finds
  such notes "sort of apologetic", though she wrote one; Darwin and
  O'Reilly, OpenLearn, 2017.) `EDITING.md` H8 is the check.
- **Places you can still see.** For each place in the book that
  survives: its name then and now, what to look for, and the chapters it
  appears in; and the places that do not survive, said plainly. Cornwell's
  notes do this; readers travel to these places (The promise, above).
- **The people in the book.** A large cast gets a cast list, real and
  invented, grouped by household or side, one line each with the chapter
  of first appearance (`/autonovel:appendix --sections cast`), as *Wolf
  Hall* does. It lets the narration introduce each person once.
- **The timeline, the people and the glossary** (`/autonovel:appendix
  --sections timeline,bios`, `/autonovel:glossary`) complete what the
  reader came to learn.
