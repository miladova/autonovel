# Chapter V — Twenty Digits of Lead

*Nineteenth Day Before the Kalends of February, AD 157 · Corvus*

Water that had left the snow-fed springs above Sublaqueum forty-five miles east two days earlier hit Corvus's groin like an axe-blade the instant he let go of the iron ladder-rungs.

For three breaths his healed left lung locked tight inside its dry linen rib-binding, refusing air while January cold drove straight through oiled mutton-tallow smeared over his calves and thighs. Above his head, through a round two-foot travertine inspection shaft cut into the paved service-lane behind Claudius's brick arcade, a pale coin of grey winter sky showed **Phileros**'s shaved chin peering down beside an urban water-castellarius whose keys jingled on a brass ring.

"Keep the horn lantern clear of the weir-lip, Sabine!" Phileros called down in Greek, voice echoing hollow and metallic off twenty feet of vaulted *opus signinum* waterproof cement. "You have until the turncock opens the morning street-sluice at the second hour—half an hour at most before the surge-head rises three feet and washes you down into the Colosseum drain! Measure the third bronze collar on the south wall and call up the stamp!"

Corvus hooked his left elbow round an iron cramp driven into the encrusted pink mortar to steady himself against the current, held the horn-paned tallow lantern high in his teeth by its leather bail, and felt under his belt for his dead father's folding brass *pes Monetalis*—twelve Roman inches subdivided into sixteen *digiti*, polished bright with brick-dust the night before while Lucida slept.

Around his waist roared Rome's hidden bloodstream.

Up in the sunlit squares men praised marble nymphs and spouting bronze Tritons without ever asking how nine aqueduct rivers stayed tame inside brick skin. Down here in the distribution *castellum*—a vaulted three-chambered settling tank twenty-eight feet long by fourteen wide, coated half an inch thick in petrified brown calcium sinter (*crustae*) deposited over a century since Emperor Claudius brought the water in on hundred-foot arches—there were no nymphs at all: only deafening white turbulence, freezing spray smelling cleanly of mountain limestone, and three horizontal rows of round bronze outlet-nozzles (*calices*) mortared straight through the southern travertine breast-wall at exact legal heights so public street-basins drank first, licensed bathhouses second, and private senatorial mansions third whenever summer drought dropped the head.

Senator Priscus's Caelian peristyle fountain and bath-cistern had lost half their pressure since the Kalends of January. Priscus's household plumber blamed winter frost cracking mortar up at the seventh milestone; Phileros—who trusted plumbers slightly less than Carthaginians—had paid the district *castellarius* eight silver denarii before sunrise to lift one sealed manhole stone and drop a skinny fifteen-year-old who knew gauges into the tank before the water-office clerks came on shift.

Corvus waded four slow paces south along the slippery limestone floor, feeling with bare numbed toes for broken glass or fallen iron clamps, leaning forty degrees forward into the rush pouring over the middle stone baffle.

At eye-level along the wet south breast-wall projected seven green-patinated bronze *calices*—mandatory nine-inch tubular collars of hard bronze, which Roman water-law required rather than soft lead at the tank exit because crooked plumbers could hammer a soft lead mouth wider with a wooden drift-pin overnight to steal double Caesar's grant, whereas cast bronze held its bore and carried the *curator aquarum*'s punched magistrate mark.

Corvus wiped slimy green water-weed off Collar Three—Senator Priscus's own licensed pipe—with his thumb and laid the brass caliper-jaws across the inner lip.

Five *digiti* diameter: a clean *quinaria*-and-a-half household grant, unobstructed bar a finger-ring of hard chalk crust inside the rim. Nothing wrong with Priscus's nozzle. Why, then, was the water-level dropping six inches below Priscus's intake lip every morning before dawn?

Shivering hard enough now that the leather lantern-strap vibrated between his molars, Corvus took the horn lamp in his left hand and waded six paces further east into the darker corner behind the sediment-pillar, where turbulent eddy-foam hid the lower masonry courses.

There, set four inches *lower* in the travertine wall than any private grant had legal right to sit—level with the public *lacus* street-fountain outlets so it drank full head even when upper pipes sucked air—protruded two bronze collars stamped in neat official letters:

*EX · INDVLG · IMP · CAES · T · AEL · HADR · ANTONINI · AVG · PII · CALIX · XX*

Twenty square digits apiece—already a princely commercial bath grant.

Corvus laid his brass rule across the mouth of the nearer bronze *calix*. Exact twenty-digit bore at the front lip, honest as Cato.

Then Corvus did what a plump sixty-year-old water-inspector standing dry on top of a ladder never did: he blew warm breath onto his blue right fingers, plunged his bare arm up to the shoulder straight into the roaring black mouth of the bronze collar against the suction—fighting the terrifying pull that wanted to wedge his wrist solid inside nine inches of cold metal—and walked his scarred thumb-knuckle inch by inch past the inner bronze flange where the lead *fistula* pipe took over inside the masonry wall.

Nine inches in, right where the cast bronze collar ended hidden inside four feet of concrete pier, **the wall vanished around his fingers**.

No narrow twenty-digit lead tube met the bronze back-ring. Instead someone had hollowed a concealed masonry pocket inside the core of the wall, soldered a flared bell-chamber directly behind the honest nine-inch bronze sleeve, and branched *two* monstrous lead *fistulae*—wide enough that Corvus could spread thumb to little finger without touching opposite walls, **fifty-digit pipes (*quinquagenariae*)** at least!—angling downward through the foundation rubble so Venturi suction pulled triple volume screaming out of the public tank without altering the visible front bore by a hair's breadth!

Even better: lead pipe-makers stamped the owner's name in raised letters along every ten-foot soldered seam before rolling the sheet. Corvus stretched two raw fingertips three inches further down the icy lead belly inside the wall-cavity, eyes shut tight against blinding spray, reading backward raised Latin capitals by skin alone the way he used to read unbaked brick-stamps in fog:

*P · VEDII · POLLIONIS · BALN · ET · FVLLON*

*Of Publius Vedius Pollio—Baths and Fulleries.*

Overhead a sharp double clang rang against the iron manhole rim: the *castellarius* striking his wrench in panic.

"Up, boy! Out of the tank!" Phileros's voice barked down the shaft, urgent now. "The turncock's crossing the archway from the Claudia spur—if he lifts the main gate-board while you're against that south wall, the head will pin your ribs flat to the bronze!"

Corvus yanked his right arm backward out of the suction—skin scraping raw red across the sharp inner edge of the bronze flange as rising pressure bit—snatched his brass rule into his belt, and lunged four surging strides through chest-deep white water as a deep subterranean boom shook the vault behind him: the upper sluice-gate lifting!

A solid green wall of Sublaqueum snow-melt burst over the primary stone weir, raising the chamber depth two feet in three heartbeats and spinning Corvus's heels clean off the slimy floor. His left hand missed the wet bottom rung; freezing water closed over his mouth and nose; then his scarred right fist hooked the second iron cramp by blind muscle memory, his bare toes jammed into a mortar-joint, and he hauled himself hand-over-hand up the iron shaft while foaming water roared four inches under his heels and Phileros dragged him out by both sodden armpits onto frosty basalt cobbles.

"Shut the stone!" the bribed *castellarius* hissed, dropping the three-hundred-pound travertine disc back into its recessed collar with an iron crowbar and kicking loose horse-straw over the wet rim ten paces before two municipal water-slaves came whistling round the brick arch with grease-buckets.

Backed flat into a recessed garden doorway out of sight, Corvus bent double with both hands braced on blue-mottled knees, coughing river-spray silently into his sleeve so his left lung wouldn't tear open again, water streaming off his ragged tunic onto January frost.

Phileros threw a dry coarse mule-blanket over Corvus's shaking shoulders and squinted sharply down at his bleeding right forearm where the bronze *calix* rim had barked three inches of skin.

"Well?" Phileros whispered. "Chalk-crust in our collar?"

Corvus straightened slowly inside the mule-blanket. His teeth wanted to hammer like castanets; he clamped his back molars together until the jaw-muscle bunched hard under his ear and shook his wet head once.

"Our collar's clean," Corvus rasped. "Twelve feet east, set four inches below legal private datum, sit two twenty-digit imperial collars soldered inside a hollowed wall-pocket onto twin fifty-digit lead *fistulae*. Drawing off public head day and night. Stamped *Publius Vedius Pollio—Baths and Fulleries*."

Phileros went wholly, marvelously still in the grey alley shadow. Even his three bone wax-scrapers stopped swinging against his cloak.

"Fifty-digit lead behind a twenty-digit bronze face," Phileros breathed, almost reverently. "Under the *Lex Quinctia de Aquaeductibus*, piercing or enlarging a tank-conduit behind the *calix* carries a hundred-thousand-sesterce public fine, summary tearing-up of every pipe on the culprit's land by the water-curators, and forfeiture of the corrupt *castellarius* to the mines."

"Don't spend the hundred thousand yet," Corvus said through blue lips, wrapping the scratchy blanket tighter round his chest. "If Senator Priscus sends the water-curators down that manhole tomorrow morning, Vedius Pollio pays fifty gold pieces to a night-mason tonight, fills the wall-pocket with quick-setting *pozzolana* mortar before dawn, and swears some dead plumber laid an old blind junction there under Hadrian. Take me inside to a brazier and give me a dry tile. I'll show Senator Priscus where the stolen water surfaces—and how to catch Vedius with the tap open."

* * *

For six weeks Corvus had seen nothing of **Senator Quintus Servilius Priscus**'s Caelian mansion except the paved service-vestibule inside the Clivus Scauri back gate, where junior copyists sat on backless pine benches rubbing chillblained knuckles over waxed boxwood leaves while kitchen-mules clattered past to the turnspits.

Today Phileros marched him—barefoot, hair plastered black to his skull, wrapped in a horse-smelling mule-blanket over a soaked Subura tunic—straight past the astounded *atriensis* into the winter heart of the house.

Even an austere Stoic consular's house on the Caelian Hill made Berenice's sixth-floor garret feel like a badger-hole dug under a root. No gaudy gilded ceilings or Syrian porphyry columns like the Ceionian palace two streets west: Priscus's *domus* was old Republican peperino stone, tall black-and-white geometric floor mosaics laid so true that Corvus's eye could not find a sixteenth-inch dip across forty feet, and dark wax ancestor-masks (*imagines*)—consuls and censors smoked brown by two centuries of hearth-fires—staring out of cedar wall-cupboards along the *alae* with hollow painted eyes.

In the glass-screened southern *peristylium*, where winter sun warmed clipped boxwood hedges and a dry marble fountain-basin sat pointedly silent in the center of dormant rose-beds, two men walked slowly side by side along the colonnade flagstone.

One was **Senator Priscus**, chewing dry fennel seed in his short unbleached *toga*. Beside him walked a slightly shorter man of fifty-six or fifty-seven wrapped in a coarse dark philosopher's *abolla* without gold clasp or ornament, ink-stains ingrained deep into the side of his right middle finger, iron-grey hair thinning across a broad, scarred Stoic brow: **Quintus Junius Rusticus**—grandson of Arulenus Rusticus whom Domitian had executed for loving liberty too loud, teacher and closest living conscience to **Marcus Aurelius Caesar**.

Standing three paces behind them holding a silver tray of untouched warm water-cups waited **Aulus Volusenus Niger**, clean-shaved and oiled in cinnamon myrrh, twisting his red carnelian *quadriga* ring—whose patrician jaw dropped half an inch when he recognized the half-drowned hod-boy from Festus's rhetoric gallery dripping grey aqueduct-water onto his uncle's spotless mosaic.

"Uncle," Volusenus said sharply, stepping forward to block the colonnade, "this is the insolent fuller's brat Festus threw out in November for barking in class! Phileros, have you lost your wits bringing street-filth onto the winter pavement?"

Senator Priscus did not look at his nephew; he looked at the widening dark ring of Sublaqueum water spreading round Corvus's blue bare feet, and at the three-inch raw scrape bleeding slowly down Corvus's right forearm.

"Did Festus throw him out for barking false law, Aulus, or true law?" Priscus asked flatly.

Volusenus flushed dull red to the roots of his oiled curls and shut his mouth.

Junius Rusticus stopped walking. True to everything Forum clerks whispered of him, Rusticus looked first at Corvus's bare calloused feet on the cold stone, second at the thin linen pleurisy-bandage showing through his torn wet neck-hem, and last into Corvus's grey-green eyes.

"Get the boy near the charcoal pan before you question him, Quintus," Rusticus said quietly—his voice surprisingly gentle, dry as old parchment, wholly free of senatorial boom. "A witness whose teeth chatter gives crooked numbers."

A slave dragged a heavy three-legged bronze brazier glowing with oak-charcoal two paces from the colonnade step. Corvus did not sit—nobody sat unbidden before two consular senators—and did not hold his numbed hands greedily over the coals either, because sudden heat on frozen fingers made them swell shut; instead he knelt on one knee beside the brazier where radiant warmth could reach his bound ribs slowly, took a broken terracotta garden-tile and a charred stick of willow-coal from Phileros, and waited until Priscus nodded.

Phileros gave the two senators a thirty-word summary in crisp Greek: Collar Three clean, two twenty-digit imperial collars four inches low in the east corner, hollowed masonry pocket behind the bronze, twin fifty-digit lead *fistulae* stamped with **Publius Vedius Pollio**'s bath and fullery mark.

Priscus's slate-grey eyes gleamed cold and hungry. "A hundred thousand sesterces statutory penalty under the *Lex Quinctia*, Junius—plus criminal *stellionatus* fraud on Caesar's grant! We lay information before Lollius Urbicus before the tenth hour and have the curators' crowbars in that wall by sunset!"

"Wait, patron," Phileros murmured, glancing down at Corvus's bent black head. "The boy says sunset loses the bird."

Priscus frowned down at Corvus. "Speak Latin, boy, and keep it brief. Why shouldn't the curators break that wall before dark?"

Corvus laid the broken terracotta tile flat across his thigh. Now that the cold lock had clicked behind his eyes, with two consular senators, Phileros, and sneering Volusenus all watching his scarred right thumb push black willow-charcoal across red clay, every trace of shiver vanished out of his wrist.

"Because the manhole sits in a public lane watched by two fuller's porters who take Vedius's copper, Senator," Corvus said cleanly. "Send municipal crowbars into the *castellum* alone, and before they chip through two feet of hard *opus signinum* mortar to expose the hidden lead bell, Vedius's night-gang cuts the receiving pipes fifty yards downhill inside his own private cellar, plugs the stub with quicklime, and swears in court that those fifty-digit *fistulae* are dead Hadrianic storm-drains he never drew a pint from. Without proving live flow *into* his cisterns at the same moment the tank is opened, your *Lex Quinctia* fine turns into a six-month boundary argument before hired arbiters."

Junius Rusticus leaned forward half an inch inside his coarse dark cloak, ink-stained finger resting against his beard. "How do you prove live flow inside a rich equestrian's locked cellar without a magistrate's search-warrant, which Roman law grants for stolen goods, rarely for water?"

Corvus drew three sharp black lines across the red clay tile: one high tank on the Caelian crest, two plunging pipes dropping eighty vertical feet down the northern slope toward the Subura trough, and a tiny square at the bottom.

"You don't enter his cellar at all, Senator," Corvus said. "Water going into a bathhouse or fullery has to come *out* into the public street-sewer, and public sewer-grates belong to the aediles, requiring no private warrant. Two fifty-digit pipes running under twenty-two feet of head discharge roughly fourteen *quinariae* a watch more than Vedius's honest twenty-digit license allows. Tomorrow at the first hour, before bathers muddy the street drains, drop two pounds of crushed red Sinope ruddle-chalk or boiled alkanet dregs straight into the east corner of the Caelian *castellum* right in front of those two collars, while two sworn witnesses stand three hundred paces downhill at the sewer-outfall behind Vedius's Argiletum fulleries with a water-jar and a sand-glass. Within ninety breaths red water pours out of Vedius's private outflow grate at triple licensed volume, proving live connection, destination, and stolen cubic measure in one stroke before a single mason lifts a hammer."

Nobody spoke under the sunlit colonnade. Out in the winter garden a blackbird hopped onto the dry marble fountain-rim, pecked vainly at frost, and flew off over the cypresses.

Up behind his uncle's elbow, Volusenus had stopped twisting his carnelian ring; his heavy mouth wore the sour, baffled look of a man watching a street-dog open an iron strongbox with its teeth.

Senator Priscus cracked a fennel seed loud in the quiet, then looked sideways at Rusticus. "Gaius Cassius Corvus's boy," Priscus said; Phileros had told him the dead surveyor's name already. "Fifteen. Phileros has been paying him sixteen sesterces a week in the back vestibule to scrape wax."

"You have been overpaying for wax-scraping and underpaying for hydraulics, Quintus," Junius Rusticus said dryly. Yet when Rusticus looked back down at Corvus kneeling beside the glowing coals, there was neither warmth nor amusement in the Stoic's deep-set eyes, only a long, searching, almost sorrowful gravity, as though he were reading the last page of a scroll whose opening chapter pleased Priscus. "Your father surveyed land around Crustumerium, Lucius Cassius?"

"Until the clay-pit took him two summers back, Senator," Corvus answered.

"And you have already petitioned through Quintus's notary for a minor's stay against your father's bond, which Hermippus, steward to this same Vedius Pollio, bought up in November," Rusticus observed calmly. Not a question. "Be careful, boy. There is nothing wrong in defending your father's name or measuring crooked lead with a straight brass rule. Only I have watched clever hungry boys come up out of the country into Roman houses for thirty years. When you discover at fifteen that four lines of charcoal on a broken tile can pull down a man worth six million sesterces, it is very hard at twenty to remember why laws were written for anything except pulling men down."

Corvus kept his eyes steady on the Stoic's ink-stained hand. Inside his ribs, where December's pleurisy still left a dull hot stitch whenever he drew deep breath, he thought of Mousa's fingernails tearing his shin in the fog, of Volusenus's ivory stylus splitting his mouth while sixteen perfumed boys laughed, of Lucida stripping down to her thin linen chest-band in freezing dark to keep his lungs from drowning because they lacked nine sesterces for charcoal. It was very easy, Corvus thought silently behind polite grey-green eyes, for a man whose grandfather owned three villas and a seat in the Curia to warn barefoot boys against loving crowbars.

"A brass rule doesn't pull straight walls down, Senator," Corvus said aloud, voice respectful and flat as planed oak. "Only crooked ones."

Priscus barked a short, dry iron laugh, the first time Corvus had ever seen his patron's teeth, and turned to Phileros.

"Move him out of the service-vestibule into the inner *tablinum* copy-bench, Phileros. Thirty-two sesterces a week, eight denarii silver, plus a clean winter tunic, woollen socks, and shoes from the household chest so he stops bleeding river-lime over my mosaic. Keep him off the public court-lists until June while the Subortanae brick-trust brief ripens; I don't want Vedius's thugs recognizing their runaway trench-witness before we drop red chalk down their pipes. And Aulus," Priscus glanced coldly back at his frowning nephew, "take this boy's tile upstairs and learn why Julian's Edict beats cinnamon hair-oil before the May docket opens."

Volusenus bowed stiffly from the neck, took the charcoal-marked garden tile out of Corvus's cold hand with two fingertips as though touching a dead rat, and stalked indoors without looking down.

* * *

Next morning before the sun cleared the Praetorian ramparts, Corvus stood hidden inside the hooded woollen cowl of a Caelian fuller's porter thirty paces down the Argiletum drain-alley, holding a clear blown-glass Sidonian flask in one hand and a brass thirty-breath water-clepsydra in the other.

Beside the arched stone sewer-outfall where **Publius Vedius Pollio**'s largest commercial bath-and-fullery block discharged its waste into the vaulted *Cloaca* spur stood **Phileros**, two grey-bearded equestrian *recuperatores* from the *curator aquarum*'s roll wrapped to their noses in heavy Gallic cloaks, and a public notary holding a fresh three-leaved boxwood *testatio* open on his forearm. Nobody had knocked on Vedius's bronze street-doors; nobody had shown a warrant. Up on the Caelian crest three hundred yards south, Priscus's chief mason was standing over the lifted travertine manhole with a four-pound linen sack of pulverized Pontic Sinope ruddle—the blood-red iron-oxide earth carpenters snapped along chalk-lines—mixed thin in two buckets of warm brine so it wouldn't clump in cold current.

"First horn," Phileros murmured, watching the pale grey sky brighten behind a baker's smoke-stack. Up on the ridge a white linen kerchief dropped from the mason's raised arm.

Corvus unplugged the pin-hole of his brass timing-cup.

Forty breaths. Sixty breaths. Out of the two-foot iron-barred outflow arch beneath Vedius's foundation stones poured clear, steaming overflow water—already running four inches deep across the channel floor, far too brisk for an honest twenty-digit night trickle. One of the shivering equestrian witnesses stamped his boots on the frost and muttered into his beard that Phileros had dragged them out of warm beds on a Greek's pipe-dream.

Corvus did not look up from the iron grate. "Seventy-eight," he counted under his cowl. "Eighty-four."

At the eighty-seventh breath a faint rusty pink cloud bloomed round the iron bars.

Two heartbeats later the entire two-foot outfall surged thick, arterial **ox-blood red**, staining the frosted basalt channel scarlet from wall to wall and splashing red foam across the stone curb as fourteen stolen *quinariae* of Sublaqueum snow-water carried two pounds of Pontic iron-earth roaring straight out of Vedius Pollio's private bath-cisterns into the public street!

Both equestrian witnesses swore aloud by Hercules and bent low over the grate. Moving fast while the surge ran brightest, Corvus stepped down onto the wet inspection ledge with his hood pulled well forward over his scarred lip, plunged the clear glass Sidonian flask straight into the roaring scarlet spout behind the iron bars, corked it tight with red wax, and held it up to the grey dawn light beside his measured clepsydra line while the public notary's iron stylus flew across the boxwood leaves recording hour, outfall width, five-inch flow depth, and unbroken crimson discharge.

Before the notary had finished pressing the second witness's bronze signet into warm beeswax, a side door in the plastered bathhouse wall burst inward—some early furnace-slave down by the drain-sumps had seen blood-colored water swirling past his boots and run screaming upstairs that the nymphs were bleeding—and out onto the frosted cobbles shuffled **Hermippus**.

Vedius Pollio's chief steward had come down so fast he wore fleece-lined house-slippers under an unbelted squirrel-fur pelisse, an ivory ink-case still hooked by habit to his thumb, two cudgel-men in green-trimmed tunics crowding out behind him until they saw the purple-bordered equestrian stripes under the two witnesses' cloaks and checked their sticks halfway out of their belts.

Corvus stepped smoothly two paces backward behind Phileros's shoulder, face shadowed deep inside his coarse porter's cowl, watching through a three-inch wool slit as Hermippus's sharp Smyrniot eyes took in the scarlet gutter, the sealed glass flask in Corvus's hand, the public notary's triple-leaved *testatio*, and Phileros's polite, corpse-pale smile.

For two breaths Hermippus's grey goat-beard worked without sound. Unlike street-thugs such as Sticho or Mousa, Hermippus could multiply hydraulic cross-sections and statutory *Lex Quinctia* fines in his head as fast as Phileros.

"A burst fuller's madder-vat in Number Two court, worthy sirs," Hermippus tried smoothly in Attic-polished Latin, spreading both ink-calloused palms. "Winter frost splits terracotta dye-tubs every January; my bailiff was just sending for mortar—"

"Save the madder-vat for country magistrates, Hermippus," Phileros interrupted gently, tapping the wet waxed *testatio* with one bone scraper. "Sinope iron-earth settles in salt water within six hundred breaths; boil madder-root and it turns purple with alum, whereas iron-earth turns black with oak-gall. Two sworn witnesses on the Caelian crest watched four pounds of Sinope earth go down the municipal *castellum* shaft directly over your master's two twenty-digit collars eighty-seven breaths before it painted your sewer-arch red. Shall we walk up to the *curator aquarum*'s tribunal at the third hour so municipal crowbars can open that masonry pocket behind the bronze, or does Publius Vedius prefer to hear Senator Priscus's arithmetic in private before noon?"

Hermippus stared at the glass flask of red water, then once, narrow-eyed, toward the tall cowled porter's boy standing silent behind Phileros's elbow; Corvus kept his chin down and his scarred right thumb tucked inside his sleeve.

"My patron dines at home until the fifth hour," Hermippus said softly, every trace of oily warmth gone out of his throat. "If Senator Priscus's secretary cares to hold his notary off the *curator*'s steps until the sixth, plumbers will have the old Hadrianic masonry junction on the Caelian crest sealed flush with hydraulic lime before dusk."

"Have your plumbers bring thirty pounds of lead solder and close *both* concealed fifty-digit branches back to honest twenty-digit pipes while Senator Priscus's mason watches inside the tank," Phileros replied pleasantly, "and bring your own seal-ring to the Clivus Scauri at the fifth hour."

Corvus expected cheering back inside Senator Priscus's *tablinum* at noon—expected lictors, public exposure, a hundred-thousand-sesterce hammer brought down square on Vedius Pollio's skull.

Instead, sitting quietly at his new pine copy-bench in the warm inner alcove behind a lattice cedar screen while grinding fresh lampblack ink, Corvus heard Hermippus come and go in less than half an hour, leaving behind neither public prosecution nor silver fine.

When Hermippus's furred slippers had shuffled out the front vestibule and Priscus walked back into the *tablinum* alone with Phileros, the old consular held two items in his lean, blue-veined hands: the sealed glass Sidonian flask of settling red iron-silt, and a fresh, privately witnessed *cautio* signed with Hermippus's own emerald scarab acknowledging a "disputed Hadrianic conduit error" on the Caelian spur and waiving all counter-claims on the neighboring garden wall-support!

Priscus unlocked his iron-bound cedar *armarium* behind the ancestor-masks, set both the glass flask and the waxed *testatio* carefully on the middle shelf beside three bound bundles of *Figlinae Subortanae* brick-stamps, and turned the heavy Laconian key twice.

Corvus looked up over his reed pen, unable to stop the question rising into his throat before prudence caught it:

"You aren't laying the hundred-thousand-sesterce fine before the water-curator, Senator?"

Phileros sucked his teeth sharply in warning; Priscus raised one hand to let the boy ask.

"Suppose I fine Vedius Pollio a hundred thousand sesterces this afternoon, Lucius Cassius," Priscus said, leaning one lean hip against the locked cedar chest and cracking a dry fennel seed between yellow back molars. "Where does that hundred thousand go under the *Lex Quinctia*? Into the public *aerarium Saturni* to patch road-gravel, while Vedius writes off two weeks' Ostian grain-interest, hires three better plumbers next winter, and knows my cupboard is empty. Leave that red bottle sitting behind Laconian iron with two equestrian seals on the neck, however—where criminal *stellionatus* carries infamia and expulsion from the equestrian jury-panels whenever I choose to take the cork out—and how loudly do you think Publius Vedius Pollio barks when my brick-trust audit reaches Lollius Urbicus in June, or when his Caelian friends round young Lucius Commodus try buying votes in the next water-commission?"

Corvus stared at the locked cedar door.

Up in the sunlit garden yesterday Junius Rusticus had warned him against loving sharp knives that pulled crooked walls down. Today, inside the dim shadow of smoky consular masks, Quintus Servilius Priscus had just taught him something ten times colder and ten times more useful: **great men in Rome rarely pulled a crooked wall down when they could put a prop under it and own the man sleeping downstairs**.

"A spent arrow kills once," Corvus said slowly, testing the geometry aloud on his tongue. "A cocked bow commands the road all year."

Priscus gave a thin, wintry nod. "Finish copying Julian's third title on *negotiorum gestio* before the eighth hour, boy, then take your advance silver and get yourself decent lodging off that fuller's stair. My clerks don't sleep over poison-shops."

At the eighth hour—scrubbed clean, his raw forearm bound in pine-resin linen, wearing the unpatched dark grey Caelian woollen *tunica* and laced calfskin *calcei* over dry woollen socks, with **thirty-two sesterces** of advance silver plus his saved **twenty-four sesterces** from the waist-band making **fifty-six sesterces** jingling solid at his hip—Corvus walked down out of the Caelian trees into crisp afternoon sun.

Before climbing Berenice's stair, he stopped two streets west on the **Vicus Cuprius** where a clean, stone-vaulted cobbler's *insula* let dry second-floor rooms with real wooden window-shutters, a private iron door-bolt, and an honest terracotta smoke-flue for sixteen sesterces a month. He paid January's sixteen sesterces down on the cobbler's bench, pocketed the heavy iron key, bought half a roast kid's shoulder with rosemary and two honey-sesame cakes hot off a baker's shovel, and paused on a sunlit landing where a gap between two butcher's roofs gave a clear sightline down toward the crowded pantiles of the Argiletum.

He touched the clean grey wool over his chest, then reached inside his collar until his scarred right thumb met the rough, broken edge of his half of the *Figlinae Subortanae* clay brick-stamp hanging on hemp cord beside his skin.

Tonight he wouldn't hide a single coin in his waistband. Tonight he would climb those seventy-eight steps wearing real shoes, drop the heavy iron key to their own bolted door straight into Lucida's stain-shadowed palm, and carry their straw pallet out from behind Berenice's poison-shelves before dusk. He tucked the warm roast kid tighter under his elbow and started briskly down into the smoke.
