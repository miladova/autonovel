# Chapter XVIII — The Hairpin

*Seventeenth Day Before the Kalends of January, AD 159 · Lucida*

"Pull the third braid half a finger tighter above the left temple, Tyrian, so my cousin **Faustina** sees I haven't grown one grey thread since autumn while she waddles round the Palatine nine months swollen like a sow before market-day."

Nineteen-year-old Lucida stood behind the carved tortoiseshell chair in **Ceionia Fabia**'s heated Caelian dressing-chamber, combing warm quince-seed mucilage smoothly through three feet of pale red-gold patrician hair before coiling the plaits into the high, scalloped crown court ladies copied off Faustina's own bronze coin-dies—while making tiny, invisible improvements at the temples so Fabia's neck looked two inches longer than the Empress-to-be's.

Outside the double-glazed *lapis specularis* windows, a raw mid-December tramontana wind rattled dry laurel hedges under iron-grey clouds heavy with snow off the Sabine peaks; tomorrow night the **Saturnalia** candles would burn again, three full winters since fifteen-year-old Lucida had stood barefoot-damp on these exact mosaic hippopotami with Fabia's gold emerald needle denting her throat over twelve silver denarii!

Tonight Lucida wore a soft plum-brown woollen *tunica* woven in Tarsus, a delicate gold chain tucked beneath her collar, and clean kidskin half-boots paid out of a workshop and bakehouse fortune already worth **twenty-eight thousand sesterces** net above her nearly retired burial-guild advance—with **six hundred fifty-five unbroken mornings** (ANNVS II · DIES CCXC) standing solid on Sura's Fish-Market slate and her new two-thousand-*modius* stone grain-vaults on the Clivus Suburanus already roofed safe under fireproof brick arches!

Only old **Dorcas** sat muttering rheumatism-prayers over jewel-clasps in the far corner; over the past fourteen months since Trajan's Baths, Fabia had learned that nineteen-year-old Lucida neither flattered, gossiped, nor flinched, and talked in front of her mirror as candidly as generals talked over camp-maps.

"Look at the double-dyed Sidonian purple on this *stola*, Lucida," Fabia said, lifting a heavy, dark blood-violet silk hem toward the tall silvered bronze mirror while Lucida stitched the coiled red-gold crown fast with fine bone needle and red wool thread, Roman hairdressers sewed court coiffures rather than trusting loose pins alone—before sliding the famous six-inch green emerald *acus* diagonally through the top knot as an ornament and a dagger. "Sixteen hundred sesterces the pound my husband Quintillus paid for it, and tonight across the citrus table **Annia Galeria Faustina** will touch her own fat pearl belly three times an hour to remind every senator in the triclinium that *her* womb drops Caesars like figs, even if half the poor little mites die in their cradles before cutting teeth—whereas *my* betrothal ring went back into Hadrian's box when I was fifteen so Mark could marry his uncle's daughter instead!"

Lucida kept her fingers deft and feather-light round the emerald pinhead, watching Fabia's pale green-brown eyes narrow inside the bronze reflection.

 Everyone in Rome knew the whispered history: twenty-one years ago dying Emperor Hadrian had compelled Antoninus Pius to adopt seventeen-year-old **Marcus Annius Verus** alongside seven-year-old **Lucius Ceionius Commodus**, Fabia's little flesh-and-blood brother—binding Marcus by solemn betrothal to **Ceionia Fabia** herself so old Aelius Caesar's blood would sit beside Marcus on the Palatine! The week Hadrian's ashes went into his riverside mausoleum, mild-mannered Antoninus broke Marcus's betrothal to Fabia and married Marcus to Antoninus's own daughter **Faustina** instead, leaving proud, brilliant Fabia sidelined into marriage with Plautius Quintillus while watching clumsy-hipped, fertile Faustina wear Augusta's diadem!

"Empresses who carry ten children in twelve winters lean heavily on physicians and Stoic tutors, Domina," Lucida murmured quietly, touching a trace of crushed Syrian alkanet in sweet almond oil to Fabia's thin lips with an ivory spatula. "Whereas a brother who enters purple in full health remembers which sister kept his friends fed while others lectured him."

Fabia's eyes met Lucida's in the polished bronze—sharp, cold, and hungry.

"You see farther through brick walls than half my husband's cousins in the Curia, girl," Fabia whispered, leaning two inches back against Lucida's hands so even deafening-ear Dorcas across the chamber heard nothing. "Do you know why Marcus summoned Quintillus and Lucius to dine quietly up on the Palatine tonight instead of waiting out at Lorium for Saturnalia? Because seventy-three-year-old **Antoninus** couldn't sit a horse from his vineyard back to the portico on Tuesday without two chamberlains lifting his knees! He makes his valet lace thin splints of dried linden-wood inside his linen undershirt every morning before audience so the foreign embassies think old Father Pius still stands straight as a spear, and **Quintus Lollius Urbicus**, Marcus's pet iron dog at the Urban Prefecture, has dropsy swelling both thighs thick as wine-jars; Asclepiades's rival told my steward Urbicus won't live to see next autumn's vintage!"

Inside Lucida's chest, her calm pulse beat one swift, silent stroke faster.

*Lollius Urbicus dying before next autumn; Antoninus Pius laced upright in wooden splints at seventy-three.*

If Urbicus died first and Antoninus followed within the year—right around the final months of Lucida's **thousand-and-ninety-five-day** mill run!, every court warrant, every Subura building-commission, and every patronal shield in Rome would fly into the air like chaff in a threshing-wind!

"Quintus Servilius Priscus and sour-faced Junius Rusticus think we're blind," Fabia breathed, emerald earrings flashing green fire as she stood up in full Sidonian purple *stola* so Lucida could drape the gold-hemmed *palla* across her left shoulder. "Priscus has spent all autumn using that ruthless young Sabine hound of his—the tall nineteen-year-old who broke Spurius Carvilius and shut Vedius's Trans-Tiberim dye-pipes, to strip every equestrian moneylender friendly to my brother Lucius bare before the old man dies! Let them try. The Praetorian cohorts remember whose father wore purple first, and **Cornelius Repentinus** down at the Guard Prefecture doesn't read Epictetus in bed!"

Fabia turned round, caught Lucida's chin lightly between two unwarmed, ring-heavy fingers, without the needle-point this December, with genuine conspirator's intimacy—and dropped a thumb-sized rock-crystal scent-flagon mounted in solid gold leaf straight into Lucida's palm.

"Four hundred forty mornings left on those dusty Fish-Market millstones of yours before Trajan's statute makes you a citizen, isn't it, Lucida?" Fabia murmured. "Keep your ears open down around the Forum Piscarium and the Carinae this winter. Bring me one clear scrap of paper showing how Priscus and Rusticus plan to tie my brother Lucius's hands in the Senate when Lorium sends black riders, and the morning Lucius puts on Caesar Augustus's ring, **you won't need donkey-flour to break Publius Vedius Pollio's collar; my brother will sign your *restitutio natalium* in imperial purple ink before noon**, making you freeborn in law as if your mother had never seen a slave-ship!"

*Restitutio natalium*—imperial grant of simulated free birth, higher even than Trajan's freedwoman citizenship, wiping every trace of servile origin and Vedius Pollio's *ius patronatus* off the face of the earth in three strokes of Caesar's pen, **if she handed them Senator Priscus's throat—and Corvus's ruined career alongside it**!

"May the Emperor live long in health, Domina," Lucida answered in flawless, unblinking Caelian formula, bowing low over the gold-mounted rock-crystal bottle, "and may Lord Lucius's friends never lack clean oil when lamps burn late."

* * *

Stepping out through the Caelian service-postern into the first watch of the night felt like walking straight from a perfumed furnace into iron water.

While Lucida had been coiling Fabia's hair indoors, the low grey clouds off the Sabine mountains had broken into something Rome saw barely once every three or four winters: dry, silent **December snow**, sifting fine white powder down through the dark arches of Claudius's aqueduct and dusting the black basalt cobbles of the Clivus Scauri until passing mule-hooves sounded muffled as ghosts.

Wrapping her thick brown woollen outer cloak tight over Fabia's Tarsian tunic—the gold-mounted crystal bottle riding heavy as a lead plumb-weight inside her pocket, Lucida walked swiftly down the steep stepped cut between high peperino garden walls, escorted twenty paces behind by one of her burly Marsian bakery-porters carrying a shuttered horn lantern and an oak cudgel.

Halfway down the Clivus Scauri, her path took her directly past the austere grey peperino flank of **Senator Quintus Servilius Priscus**'s mansion.

Up behind a narrow second-floor library window—the exact room where Phileros kept his cedar chests, yellow bronze lamp-light glowed warm behind mica panes against the falling snow. Moving across that lighted rectangle for two heartbeats passed a tall, broad-shouldered silhouette with black hair bent low over an unrolled scroll beside a stooped older consular figure.

Lucida did not slow a single stride on the snowy steps; within five heartbeats the high garden corner cut off the window, and she descended into the smoky, noisy glow of the Subura trough where fried-pork sellers were already hanging pine garlands for tomorrow's Saturnalia and street-urchins screamed catching melting white flakes on their tongues.

Yet when she turned through the Fish-Market arch and gave three short raps on Barba's iron-bound oak postern, the warm courtyard inside held no holiday cheer.

Sixteen-year-old **Sura** unbolted the wicket immediately, pulling Lucida and the Marsian porter inside before shooting the heavy iron bar home with both scarred hands and holding her small black slate straight up under the doorway lantern!

Across the wax-wiped slate Sura had drawn a razor-accurate plan of Lucida's newly roofed **Clivus Suburanus** grain-vaults (*Lot Fourteen*) and the leaning, worm-eaten seven-storey timber fuller's *insula* looming five feet behind its eastern rear wall—with a jagged chalk earless stick-figure drawn right across the timber building's doorway and three words underneath:

*ONE-EAR BOUGHT BACK WALL TODAY · WATCHERS AT FISH-STALL*

Lucida's stomach dropped cold.

"Scaeva?" Lucida asked low, pulling off her snow-dusted hood. "How do you know Scaeva took over the rear tenement instead of just collecting Hermippus's rent?"

Sura flipped the slate over, writing fast in fierce, slanted chalk strokes:

*SAW HIM THROW OLD INSULARIVS OUT AT DUSK · PUT FOUR NEW CAPPADOCIANS ON TOP FLOOR WITH OIL-DRUMS · POINTED AT FELIX'S NEW BRICK ROOF*

Behind them near the glowing mouth of Oven One, sixty-four-year-old **Habinnas Barba** came shuffling out of the flour-bin arch wrapped in two sheepskin vests despite the eighty-degree oven-draft, and doubled halfway over against the oak weighing-table as a long, tearing winter cough racked his barrel chest!

Lucida crossed the stone floor in three strides, steadying the old Cappadocian's flour-white elbow before his forehead struck the scale-beam. When Barba pulled his rag away from his mouth and tried clumsily to tuck it inside his belt before she saw, the unbleached linen showed dull grey-pink speckles in the cresset light—the exact rusty winter warning Corvus had coughed three Saturnalias ago, laid over forty years of volcanic stone-dust and flour-paste!

"Don't fuss round my ribs like a hen, Tyrian; cold air got down the flue when the Marsians brought oak in," Barba wheezed fiercely, waving her hand off with stubborn soldier's pride as he straightened up toward the four Reatine mules already harnessed to begin tonight's **Day Six Hundred Fifty-Six** grind. "Read what the little mute wrote! Why the Hades does Vedius Pollio's one-eared butcher put four Cappadocians and oil-drums up in a condemned timber garret five feet behind old Ulpius Felix's new stone vaults?"

Lucida stared at Sura's chalk map in the red oven-glow, every wheel inside her head turning at ice-water speed.

Why put four thugs and lamp-oil drums inside a rotten seven-storey timber tenement **already flagged for demolition** by Corvus's sixty-foot building-commission?

Either Vedius had sniffed out, or merely suspected—that old Ulpius Felix fronted for Lucida's burial-guild circle and wanted a choke-hold right against her new two-thousand-*modius* wheat reserve... **or Vedius was preparing to burn down his own condemned seven-storey timber shell in dry weather before Urbicus's crowbars razed it**, collecting neighbor-clearance rights while framing surrounding bakeries and brick-plots in the smoke!

Not tonight in wet December snow; nobody burned green Subura timber in winter damp when roofs dripped slush. Vedius planted pieces six months ahead, for July or August when Dog-Star wind turned Rome into a tinder-box!

"Move only *half* our Sicilian reserve—four hundred *modii*, never the whole thousand, into the Clivus Suburanus tufa vaults this winter," Lucida instructed Barba and Sura quietly, wiping Sura's slate clean with her sleeve so no hired kneader saw Scaeva's name, "and brick up the two small rear ventilation-slits facing that timber alley three courses thick with iron shutters tomorrow morning! Now get upstairs beside the warm smoke-flue until the third watch, Barba; I'll weigh the first forty *modii* myself while Asclepiades's boy brings you hot hyssop-oxymel."

Barba grumbled fiercely in Cappadocian Greek, yet his knees were shaking enough under his sheepskins that he let Sura steer him up the warm loft steps toward hot barley-water while the four Marsian kneaders worked their paddles at the far end of the vaulted bake-hall.

* * *

At midnight—after the Marsians had racked the first two hundred risen loaves under damp cloths and gone into the warm inner kneading-room to eat their Saturnalia-eve garlic pork round a dice-board, snow began falling thicker through the square twenty-foot skylight opening in the center of the courtyard roof, hissing faintly in tiny white stars whenever stray flakes drifted near the warm stones round Oven Two's ash-pit.

Lucida sat alone on an upturned olive-wood mortar beside the banked red brick mouth of Oven Two, knees drawn up inside her cloak, tallying year-end silver columns on wax while listening to the deep, hypnotic *grrrm-grrrm-grrrm* of her four lava mills turning steadily in the rear shed.

Six weeks had passed since Corvus caught her waist over fifty-six feet of empty air on the Clivus Suburanus scaffolding and she told him to stay on his own side of the street. Six weeks without a single basket of peaches, without a note, without glimpsing his tall dark head once across the Forum—while up on the Caelian Hill tonight **Ceionia Fabia** had practically offered her imperial freeborn status on a silver platter in exchange for driving a knife straight into Senator Priscus's back!

Outside on the silent, snow-muffled alley stones behind the bolted oak postern, knuckles rapped low against iron-studded wood:

*Tap-tap.* Pause. *Tap.*

Lucida's stylus froze half an inch above the wax.

Nobody inside the walls of Rome knew that signal except two people; they had tapped *two-and-one* on hollow willow trunks along the Crustumerium ditch at ten years old whenever Corvus's drunken father came home swinging his leather belt or her mother's cough brought blood.

Slowly Lucida set down her wax tablet, walked across the flour-dusted basalt flags past the thin veil of falling white snow in the middle of the yard, and slid back the narrow bronze spy-plate at eye-level in the oak gate.

Two inches outside the iron grille stood nineteen-year-old **Corvus**.

He wore neither citizen toga nor Caelian commissioner's badge, only a dark, snow-powdered coarse woollen cloak pinned with plain iron at his throat, bare-headed so fine white December flakes tangled unmelted in his thick black hair and clung to his straight dark eyebrows. No litter, no slave, no lantern; his breath misted white in the freezing alley dark, and cradled in both bare, ink-and-brick-scarred hands he held a square unbleached linen napkin steaming faintly with heat.

For three heartbeats Lucida could have slid the bronze spy-plate shut and walked back to her ledger. Every smart, armored adult instinct she had forged over thirty-eight months in Rome told her to leave the iron bar down.

Her left hand reached down and lifted the heavy iron drop-bar silently out of its stone socket instead.

Corvus stepped inside out of the snow-wind, waiting until she dropped the bar back into place before speaking—taking in the four steady lava mills rumbling safe in the rear shed, the fresh **MODII CXV** chalked ready on Sura's morning board, and nineteen-year-old Lucida standing three feet away in her plum-brown Tarsian wool with red oven-light gilding her dark braids.

"Saturnalia tomorrow," Corvus said quietly, no clever Fronto periods, plain flat Sabine speech rough with cold. "Old Diodorus from Crustumerium brought dried autumn figs down on a firewood mule at dusk and baked six in fennel-dough with honey. Smelled like your mother's pan the winter we were twelve. Thought if I left them outside the gate, the Fish-Market dogs would get them before dawn."

He held out the knotted warm linen napkin without stepping past the snow-line unless invited.

Sweet, unmistakable smell of split purple Sabine figs blistered in wild honey and crushed fennel-seed drifted out through the warm linen—sweeping away Trajan's Baths, Laurentum surf, Horrea cedar-planks, and Caelian purple in one devastating rush straight back to two skinny children licking burned fig-syrup off cold thumbs behind a clay-clamp in Sabinum.

"Bring them over by Oven Two before you drip slush into my clean salt-bins," Lucida said, turning quickly toward the glowing brick arch so the red cresset-light wouldn't catch the sudden hot sting behind her eyelids.

They sat three feet apart on two upturned wooden flour-measures beside the warm brick cheek of the sleeping twelve-foot oven, watching white snow-crystals drift lazily down out of the black Roman sky into the center of the stone yard and vanish on warm basalt ten feet from their boots.

Corvus untied the linen corners between them: six fat dark Sabine figs split open in blistered fennel-crust, still piping hot from having ridden inside his wool cloak against his chest all the way down from the Carinae.

Neither of them spoke a single patron's name, not Priscus, not Fabia, not Crispina, not Vedius, not Felix, not Urbicus. By unspoken nineteen-year-old pact, every stone beyond this courtyard wall ceased to exist until the six figs were gone.

"Burned the bottom of the third one just like your mother always did when the brushwood popped," Lucida remarked gravely after her first bite, holding up the blackened crust-bottom between stained thumb and forefinger.

A real laugh—low, startled, rust-creaking from months of disuse, broke out of Corvus's chest, lighting his deep-set grey-green eyes warm in the coal-glow as he bit cleanly through a scorching fig and winced as hot honey scalded his tongue.

"You ate four out of six that December when my father broke his ankle in the boundary-ditch and swore a crow flew through the window and stole the fourth," Corvus accused, licking sticky dark fig-honey off the crescent brick-stamp scar across his right thumb-knuckle.

"Three and a half," Lucida corrected virtuously, hugging both wool-clad knees inside her cloak while warmth spread from the sweet hot fruit and the glowing brick dome right out into her fingertips. "The crow plainly took the half with the ants on it. How many clients' slaves are going to dump cheap clay *sigillaria* dolls on your Carinae doorstep at dawn tomorrow now that you're a terrifying Forum advocate?"

"Fourteen already tonight—all ugly little terracotta Herculeses with cross-eyes," Corvus admitted wryly, tossing a bit of dry fennel-stalk onto the glowing red ash-bed where it flared bright gold and vanished. "Saved the ugliest cross-eyed one in my pocket for Sura's shelf." He drew a tiny, lopsided three-inch painted clay gladiator out of his cloak-fold and stood it solemnly on the stone lip beside Oven Two, where it squinted fiercely across the yard like a drunken thumbsucker.

Lucida laughed aloud, soft, unguarded, clear in the snowy dark—leaning sideways to straighten the little clay gladiator's crooked shield so it wouldn't topple into the embers.

Turning her head back from the brick lip, she found Corvus no longer looking at the clay figurine.

He was sitting eighteen inches away on his upturned tub, melted snow glistening like tiny glass beads in his black hair, looking straight at her laughing mouth and throat in the red ember-light with an expression so stripped of courtroom iron, raw, starving, nineteen years old and lonely to the bone atop ninety-two thousand sesterces—that the laugh died clean out of Lucida's chest.

Slowly Corvus lifted his right hand, still warm and faintly sticky at the thumb with Sabine fig-honey—and brushed one single cold white snowflake that had blown sideways under the eaves off the dark braid resting over her left collarbone, right above the hidden olive-pip birthmark.

His scarred knuckles grazed the warm wool over her skin and stayed there, trembling once, just as they had trembled by the Janiculum tailrace at sixteen, except neither of them was sixteen now; both of them knew in their flesh what mouths and thighs did behind bolted doors when lamps burned low, and both of them knew that if Corvus leaned three inches closer across that upturned flour-tub tonight and put his scarred mouth on hers, neither Caelian palace nor twenty-eight thousand sesterces nor ninety-two thousand sesterces would keep their clothes on past three breaths!

Lucida's lips parted silently; her breath came warm and white against the back of his wrist; inside her pocket **Ceionia Fabia**'s solid-gold rock-crystal flagon pressed cold as an icicle against her hip—reminding her what Fabia had asked her to steal from this boy's house two hours ago!

Kiss him tonight while carrying Fabia's gold poison in her pocket and hiding Scaeva's four oil-drums behind Lot Fourteen, and she turned herself into a traitor worse than locker-woman Phryne!

Very gently, before her own traitorous hands could catch his cloak-collar and drag him down onto the warm flour-sacks behind the oven, Lucida lifted two fingers, wrapped them briefly around Corvus's scarred right wrist, pressed his palm once warm and hard against her own cheek for the space of two silent heartbeats, and set his hand back down on his own knee.

"The Marsians come out of the kneading-room at the third watch horn, Corvus," she whispered, voice aching rough in the quiet, "and snow's thickening on the Carinae steps."

Corvus sat motionless for three breaths, reading everything he couldn't reach in her dark amber eyes without anger tonight—only a deep, quiet, winter grief, then nodded once, stood up to his full six feet in the red glow, and pulled his damp woollen cloak-folds together.

"Tell Barba to drink hot squill-vinegar for that rattle I heard through the floor-vents," Corvus said softly at the postern before lifting the bar, "and keep an eye on the timber garrets behind Ulpius Felix's rear alley when dry weather comes. *Io Saturnalia*, Vespera."

"*Io Saturnalia*, Sabine," Lucida whispered back.

When the heavy oak door clicked shut and she dropped the iron bar back into its socket, she stood alone watching tiny white snowflakes drift into the warm stone circle where his boots had melted two dark wet prints—leaving the cross-eyed little clay gladiator standing guard beside Oven Two while four hundred thirty-nine mornings remained between them and the storm.
