# Chapter X — The Supper of the Dead

*Ninth Day Before the Kalends of March to the Kalends of March, AD 158 · Lucida*

Dead bakers smelled much cleaner than live ones: dry bone-ash, withered violets, and three-year-old Setine wine poured straight down terracotta pipes into their skulls.

Two miles south of the Porta Capena along the **Via Appia**, where tall cypresses hissed in the wet February night wind above marble roadside tombs, forty-eight living craftsmen of the **Collegium Aesculapii et Hygiae** reclined three abreast on stone masonry couches built directly over the subterranean *columbarium* vaults of their guild-brothers.

All round the plastered brick walls above their heads rose eight honeycomb tiers of arched niches, five hundred pigeonholes in all, each holding two lidded alabaster or grey clay ash-urns under a neat painted marble *titulus* plaque: *Marcus Ulpius Eros, baker, lived fifty-two years; Gaius Iulius Dama, harness-maker, freedman, never sued a neighbor.* Stuck into the mortar beside every third niche burned a tiny terracotta oil-lamp set out for the **Feralia**, last night of the nine-day February *Parentalia* when Roman families fed their ghosts lest hungry shades walk the streets howling before spring.

Before anyone touched the roast sucking-pig or warm chickpeas steaming on pine trestles down the center of the vault, **Gaius Salvius**—seventy-year-old *quinquennalis* president of the burial guild, a retired armorer of the Second Parthica whose bald skull bore a silver-plated dent from a Dacian club, walked solemnly to a lead-lined funnel set flush in the mosaic floor and tilted a full two-quart bronze pitcher of spiced honey-wine down into the earth below.

*"Drink warm under the grass, brothers, and grudge us not our bread above,"* Salvius chanted in rough camp Latin, while forty-eight scarred freedmen rapped bronze cups twice against stone couches and answered *"Salvete, Manes!"*

Standing back near the vaulted stair-foot beside wheezing **Habinnas Barba**, seventeen-year-old Lucida kept a modest dark blue woollen *palla* drawn respectfully over her braided hair—as custom required at rites of the dead, while holding under her left arm an iron-strapped oak coffer heavy with **two thousand four hundred sesterces** in clean silver denarii and four gold *aurei*.

Six months of waking before the third watch to decant Spanish oil on frosty quays, boiling Cyprian laurel-berry and Chian turpentine over backyard braziers until her knuckles blistered to fill **Ceionia Fabia**'s forty-pound Green-stable stoneware jars, paying Hermippus his cursed eighty sesterces silently on every single Kalends without touching Corvus's Caelian savings—all of it sat inside that twelve-pound oak box against her ribs.

And she was still **five thousand six hundred sesterces short** with eight days left until the Kalends of March handed Barba's four lava mills to Hermippus.

Down on the middle couch a squat vinegar-boiler wiped pork-grease off his beard and squinted through lamp-smoke toward the stair.

"Who's the girl with the money-box behind your elbow, Habinnas?" the vinegar-boiler called across the hall. "Guild bylaws fine any brother twelve sesterces who brings tavern-women down to the *Feralia* wine before the lamps go out!"

Forty-eight pairs of wine-flushed eyes turned toward the steps. Half of them belonged to old Eastern and Gallic freedmen who had bought their own freedom coin by copper coin over thirty years and trusted young women roughly as far as they could throw an ox-cart.

Barba cleared the dry flour-rattle from his throat and thumped his oak staff once on the mosaic. "No tavern-woman, Vibius! Daughter of a Syrian purple-dyer from Puteoli, co-lessee on my Fish-Market *pistrinum* starting next week, and she brought sixty gold pieces' worth of clean silver to put on the guild table beside an offer for our *arca communis* chest, if President Salvius gives her half a cup of sand to speak!"

Groans and suspicious mutters rippled along all three masonry *triclinia*. Up at the head couch beside the bronze statuette of Aesculapius with his gilded serpent-staff, old **Gaius Salvius** chewed a mouthful of crackling slowly, eyeing seventeen-year-old Lucida from mud-free boots to dark blue veil.

"Our burial endowment holds sixty-four thousand sesterces donated by Lady Salvia Marcellina in memory of her husband, Habinnas," Salvius rasped. "By imperial *senatus consultum* and our bronze foundation-tablet on that wall, capital sits locked at five percent a year on first-rank Italian farm mortgages—never lent out on shaky Subura bakehouses where one knocked-over oil-lamp turns six thousand sesterces to charcoal! Why waste good pork-heat listening?"

Lucida stepped two paces forward out of Barba's shadow into the open ring of terracotta tomb-lamps, letting the blue *palla* slip back off her crown just far enough that forty-eight hard-headed tradesmen saw neither cosmetics nor coy Caelian silk, only clean-braided black hair, work-broadened wrists, and steady amber eyes that didn't blink at five hundred dead men's urns.

She set her oak coffer down on the stone altar-step, unlocked the iron padlock, and tilted the lid open toward the torches so four gold *aurei* and five hundred silver *denarii* gleamed white and yellow in the vault.

"Because your five-percent Italian farm mortgage on Marcus Fulvius's olive-grove out at Velitrae hasn't paid silver since October when autumn hail stripped his trees," Lucida said clearly, voice pitched low enough not to echo off the brick barrel-vault, "and because I didn't walk two miles down the Appian stones tonight to borrow five thousand six hundred sesterces from your *arca communis*."

Salvius stopped reaching for his wine-cup. "If you aren't borrowing five thousand six hundred sesterces, girl, what are you asking?"

"I am offering to **sell this guild forty thousand two-pound loaves of clean Sicilian *panis secundus*** delivered across twenty-four months—starting on the Kalends of March, paid half in advance," Lucida answered.

Along the couches three bakers and two tavern-keepers sat bolt upright, wiping wine off their chins to do rapid street arithmetic on their fingers.

"Every master in this vault feeds between four and twelve apprentices, fullers' boys, muleteers, or forge-slaves every morning," Lucida continued, walking slowly three paces down the open mosaic aisle between the couches without touching a dish. "In the Subura and Velabrum today you pay **two copper *asses* the two-pound loaf** for Numerius Vettius's chalk-and-darnel sweepings bread that leaves your boys dizzy by noon—forty thousand loaves costing your households **five thousand sesterces a year**, ten thousand across two years! Advance my Fish-Market *pistrinum* **five thousand six hundred sesterces** out of your idle chest tomorrow before three guild *curatores*, secured first-lien behind no prior mortgage on four Orvieto lava mills, five sound Reatine mules, and two twelve-foot brick ovens, where my own **two thousand four hundred sesterces** sits in first-loss position underneath your silver, wiped out down to my last *quadrans* before this guild loses one brass *as*!"

"And what interest do we eat on five thousand six hundred sesterces?" demanded Vibius the vinegar-boiler, leaning over his cushion.

"Better than five percent paper, master vinegar-maker," Lucida shot back cleanly. "You redeem guild bread-lead tokens at Barba's counter every dawn at **one *as* and a *semis***—one and a half *asses* per full-weight two-pound Sicilian loaf instead of two *asses*, saving your forty-eight workshops twenty-five percent on daily slave-bread until every sesterce of principal is worked clean off! *Plus*, on all four annual *collegium* feast-days, *Cara Cognatio*, Violet-Day, Rose-Day, and the Emperor's birthday—Barba and I deliver **two hundred fresh white poppy-seed *siligo* loaves and four jars of honeyed wine gratis** to this vault for the living and the dead, adding twelve percent effective yield a year without violating one syllable of your charter against risky usury, because commercial advance-bread purchase for members' *sportulae* sits explicitly authorized in Column Three of your own bronze wall-tablet behind President Salvius's left shoulder!"

Every neck in the burial hall craned round toward the engraved bronze guild-charter bolted into the brick behind Salvius's couch, where three literate harness-makers squinting by torchlight read out *"ex reditu panes et vinum diebus sollemnibus dividantur"* and burst out laughing aloud!

"She read the bronze while we were pouring wine down the funnel!" somebody roared from the back couch, slapping his knee.

Old Gaius Salvius didn't laugh yet. He stood up slowly from the head couch, walked his stiff soldier's legs down the mosaic aisle until he towered six inches over seventeen-year-old Lucida, and looked down from her open box of gold and silver to her stain-shadowed thumbs.

"Clever tongue, dyer's daughter," Salvius rumbled. "Now answer an armorer's question without legal grease. Habinnas here has been coughing white paste into his beard for three winters; if his lungs stop in July, who keeps four donkey-mills turning a hundred *modii* every night against Hermippus's thugs—sixteen-year-old arms?"

Lucida did not drop her gaze a hair's breadth from the silver plate dented into the old legionary's skull.

"Every morning for the next **one thousand and ninety-five mornings**, President Salvius," Lucida said, quiet enough that men ten paces off stopped chewing to hear, "if Barba's ovens drop *one single modius* below a hundred between midnight and midnight, Trajan's citizenship count resets to nothing and a fat equestrian up on the Oppian Hill takes the clothes off my corpse when I die. Your guild stands to lose five thousand six hundred sesterces if those stones stop; **I lose my throat and my name**. Which of us do you think sleeps past the third watch?"

For five heartbeats Salvius stared straight into her amber eyes, measuring iron against iron, recognizing down in the bone of a sixteen-year-old girl the exact grim siege-discipline that kept frontier cohorts digging ditches in sleet—then turned toward the forty-eight couches and raised his scarred right arm.

"All brothers favoring twenty-eight hundred *denarii* advance bread-contract with Habinnas Barba and Lucida Tyria's daughter, subject to morning inspection of the four stones by Curators Vibius and Plautus—lift your right hands!"

Forty-five greased, flour-cracked, scarred freedmen's hands shot up into the torch-smoke toward the five hundred silent urns overhead.

Barba let out a hoarse Cappadocian war-whoop that dissolved halfway into a coughing fit, pounding Lucida's shoulder hard enough to stagger her an inch against her oak money-box; and for one warm, dizzying breath amid the smell of roast pork and dead men's violets, Lucida pressed her palm flat against the cold stone altar-step to steady her knees, eight thousand sesterces whole and clean in her hands without touching a coin of Corvus's Caelian future!

* * *

Within seventy-two hours—moving with the silent, ruthless speed of water filling a dry sluice before Hermippus's clerks realized their March foreclosure lamb had grown horns, Lucida spent almost every silver piece.

On the fifth day before the Kalends, flanked by Curators Vibius and Plautus from the burial guild and a public *tabellio* notary hired outside the Forum of Peace (never using Senator Priscus's clerks lest Corvus hear of her side-loan too soon, though Corvus rejoiced wildly upstairs that evening when she told him Barba's burial-guild had backed their *pistrinum*!), she counted **four thousand eight hundred sesterces** across Landlord Gaius Munatius's table—redeeming Barba's equipment-pledge in full and locking in a fresh five-year notarized *conductio* lease before Hermippus's factor even walked up the steps!

On the fourth day before the Kalends she walked down to the muddy cattle-pens of the **Forum Boarium** in pouring rain, bypassed three slick auctioneers trying to palm off broken-winded Campanian nags painted round the hocks with soot, and paid **one thousand nine hundred sesterces** cash to a Sabine drover from Reate for five iron-hooved, raw-boned three-year-old hill-mules that tried to bite her sleeve off when she checked their teeth.

On the day before the Kalends she unloaded **three hundred fifty *modii*** of clean, flint-hard amber Sicilian wheat directly off an Agrigentine coastal hull at the Emporium, probing every bottom sack-seam personally with a three-foot hollow bronze sampling-spear until neither clay-pellets nor weevils came up in the groove—and stood beside freshly shaved Habinnas Barba inside the marble-columned registry hall of the **Praefectus Annonae** near the Porticus Minucia while a bored imperial freedman clerk dipped his reed in red cinnabar heading-ink and inscribed Sheet Nineteen of the Region VIII bakers' roll:

*PISTRINVM PISCARIENSE · EXERCITORES HABINNAS BARBA LIB · ET LVCIDA LAT · IVN · QUOTIDIE MOD · C+*
*INITIVM TRIENNI EX EDICTO DIVI TRAIANI : KAL · MART · ORFITO ET PVDENTE COSS · [AD 158]*

*Kalends of March, in the Consulship of Sextus Sulpicius Tertullus and Quintus Tineius Sacerdos—First Day of Three Years.*

That night nobody in the Fish-Market bakehouse slept at all.

At the loud brass blast of the midnight third-watch horn from the Forum watch-post, Sura dragged the heavy oak bar across the courtyard postern, lit all six iron wall-cressets with pine pitch, and chalked across a freshly planed black slate bolted beside Oven One in neat four-inch capitals Corvus had taught her to square with a ruler:

**ANNVS I · DIES I**

Four blindfolded Reatine hill-mules leaned stout leather breast-collars simultaneously into four oak sweep-beams. Sixteen hundred pounds of Orvieto lava groaned, bit hard Sicilian kernel, and settled into a deep, continuous subterranean rumble, *grrrm-grrrm-grrrm*—that shook fine white *siligo* mist off the rafters like indoor snow.

Four hired free night-kneaders, broad-backed Marsian countrymen paid six sesterces a night plus two loaves so nobody chained human necks in Lucida's courtyard—worked rhythmic wooden paddles inside the circular stone kneading-tubs while Barba tended two roaring twelve-foot brick domes heated white with dry Sabine oak, sliding six-foot iron peels loaded with cross-scored two-pound round loaves (*panes quadrati*) in and out through shimmering waves of four-hundred-degree air.

Upstairs Corvus had stayed awake until the fourth watch helping haul sixty-pound wheat-sacks from the storeroom into the upper mill-hoppers before washing flour off his forearms to snatch two hours' sleep ahead of his own dawn *toga* preparations on the Caelian; down in the red heat Lucida worked stripped to a short knee-length baker's linen tunic bound tight under the ribs with hemp cord, bare feet planted wide on warm flour-slicked basalt, weighing every tenth batch personally on the certified municipal steelyard while entering sack-numbers in duplicate wax so no *annona* inspector on earth could challenge a single *sextarius* of her first hundred *modii*.

By the first pale streak of March dawn over the Fish-Market tiles, Sura stood on a wooden flour-tub cheering silently with both flour-white fists in the air as she chalked the final morning tally underneath *DIES I*:

**MODII · CVIII**

*One hundred and eight modii.* Eight clear *modii* above Caesar Trajan's line, and three hundred twenty warm, fragrant brown Sicilian loaves already stacked in willow panniers waiting for the forty-eight burial-guild runners lining up outside the bolted oak street-shutter!

"Unbar the front shutters, Sura!" Barba wheezed triumphantly, leaning on his long iron ash-rake with sweat cutting clean dark rivers down his flour-plastered chest. "Smell that crust, little Tyrian, not one grain of Vettius's dizzy-weed in forty baskets!"

Sura heaved up the iron drop-bar and folded back the wide street-shutters onto the noisy dawn cobbles of the **Forum Piscarium**—and stepped violently backward with a sharp hiss through her teeth.

Blocking the middle of the open archway in front of the waiting guild-apprentices stood **Gaius Ummidius Scaeva**, flanked by two bull-necked fullers' bailiffs carrying iron-shod ash cudgels low against their thighs.

Scaeva wore a heavy brown woollen military *paenula* damp with March river-fog; in his left hand he swung a hollow brass Kalends tally-tube from Hermippus's rent-office, and his single right ear twitched as his pale pig-eyes swept past the steaming willow bread-baskets to take in **all four lava mills thundering behind five sleek new Reatine mules**, four paid Marsian kneaders, and forty sacks of prime Sicilian grain stacked dry under canvas!

Upstairs in the loft directly over the arch, Corvus was still asleep for another half-hour before leaving for the Caelian, *thank every god in Syria*, because if Corvus looked out that upper shutter right now and saw Scaeva standing in their doorway, sixteen months of careful insulation blew apart in three breaths!

Lucida did not glance upward toward the loft beams once. Without hurry she wiped white dough off her palms onto her hemp apron-cord, walked to the low wooden counter dividing the shop-front from the cobbles, and took eighty sesterces—two gold *aurei* wrapped cleanly in oiled linen for her secret March Kalends patronal blood-tax, out of her inner pocket, sliding them silently under a folded bread-cloth toward Scaeva's hand so neither Barba nor the guild-boys behind him saw gold change palms.

Except Scaeva did not pick up the cloth.

Instead he planted one heavy hobnailed boot up onto Barba's clean stone threshold-sill, leaning across the bread-counter close enough that boiled garlic and sour wine blew into Lucida's face.

"Keep the two yellow chicks warm a breath, Tyrian," Scaeva rasped low in camp Latin. "Hermippus sent his clerk down to Munatius's house yesterday noon to take over four cold millstones on defaulted paper—and found **eight thousand sesterces** silver already stacked on Munatius's table and your name painted wet in red cinnabar down at the *Annona*! Where does a sixteen-year-old Junian slit who walked out of my master's garden twelve months ago without a copper *as* find eight thousand sesterces, unless she's holding back eighty percent of her dockside takings from her patron's ledger, or fronting stolen silver for some Caelian senator?"

Behind Lucida, old Habinnas Barba took two slow, silent steps closer to the glowing mouth of Oven One, his knotted hands shifting six inches down the shaft of his eight-foot iron ember-shovel.

"Tell my bailiffs to unhook two of those fat new Reatine mules right now as supplementary bond until Hermippus audits your silent partners' names," Scaeva ordered, jerking his scarred chin toward his two cudgel-men, "or we tip those bread-baskets into the fish-gutter and shut your shutters before Day One hits noon!"

Unhook two mules today, and tomorrow's grind dropped to sixty *modii*—killing her three-year citizenship run on its very first morning!

Lucida didn't raise her voice, didn't back away from Scaeva's garlic breath, and didn't call upstairs. Instead she reached behind the counter, lifted a heavy stamped lead customs-and-household seal hanging on green-dyed linen cord from a forty-pound sack of surplus bruised winter barley-bran left over from her **Domus Ceionia** stable-compoundings, and dropped the two-inch lead disc *clank* onto the oak board right beside Scaeva's fingers.

Stamped deep into the lead in crisp imperial-family capitals shone a prancing horse flanked by:

*EX · HORREIS · CEIONIAE · FABIAE · C · F*

*From the Granaries of Ceionia Fabia, Consul's Daughter*—sister to **Lucius Ceionius Commodus**, the exact golden prince whose ringed hand Publius Vedius Pollio spent twelve thousand sesterces on green parakeets trying to kiss!

Scaeva's eyes dropped to the lead stamp; his scarred jaw froze half-open.

"Eight thousand sesterces came from forty-eight sworn brothers of the *Collegium Aesculapii et Hygiae*, whose three *curatores* are standing six feet behind your heels waiting for their morning bread under imperial *senatus consultum* charter," Lucida said softly, so clear and venomous only Scaeva heard every word, "and our surplus bran and linseed-pressings go under contract to **Lady Ceionia Fabia's Caelian steward** for her brother Lord Lucius's *Factio Prasina* race-stables! Go ahead, bailiff: lead two mules out of a registered *annona* bakery carrying Ceionia Fabia's seal on the Kalends of March so I have to walk up the Caelian steps at the third hour and explain to Lord Lucius why **Publius Vedius Pollio**'s thugs stopped his stallions' hot bran-mash six weeks before the April Circus! Let's see whose ears Vedius slices off before supper—mine, or the fool who cost him his seat in Lucius's box!"

At the same instant she nodded once, half an inch—to Barba.

Old Barba thrust his eight-foot iron shovel deep into the glowing belly of Oven One, swung out ten pounds of hissing, white-hot oak coals, and dumped them in a blazing red ribbon straight across the stone gutter **three inches in front of Scaeva's greased boot-toes**, "clearing the ash-pit," as bakers lawfully did at dawn—sending scorching heat and flying orange sparks straight up Scaeva's shins!

Scaeva cursed, jerked his hobnailed foot backward off the threshold out of the white-hot coals, glared murderously from the smoking *CEIONIA FABIA* lead seal to the muttering crowd of burly burial-guild smiths and fullers gathering behind his cudgel-men with empty bread-panniers, and snatched the folded bread-cloth holding his regular eighty sesterces off the oak counter.

"Hermippus will verify every single name on that burial-club roll before the Ides," Scaeva snarled low across the glowing embers. "Set one toe outside your lane, little bitch, and Ceionia Fabia's horse-bran won't save your hide."

He spun on his heel and shouldered out through the Fish-Market crowd with his two cudgel-men behind him.

Immediately Barba swept the cooling coals calmly into the stone quench-trough, spat once into the steam, and started counting warm two-pound loaves smoothly into the burial-guild runners' baskets as if one-eared Praetorian killers visited every morning before breakfast.

Behind the high stone kneading-trough out of sight of the street, seventeen-year-old Lucida stood still for five breaths, staring up at Sura's chalked slate on the smoky wall (**ANNVS I · DIES I — MODII CVIII**, one thousand and ninety-four mornings still to survive!), then plunged both bare arms straight past her elbows into two hundred pounds of warm, rising wheat-dough, kneading hard with locked shoulders until the hot living gluten swallowed every tremor in her hands.
