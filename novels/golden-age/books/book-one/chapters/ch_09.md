# Chapter IX — Gout and Plautus

*Day Before the Ides of November, AD 157 · Corvus*

**Marcus Cornelius Fronto**, greatest living master of Latin eloquence in the inhabited world, lay propped on seven goose-feather cushions inside Maecenas's heated Esquiline conservatory cursing his own left knee in three dialects of Punic, Greek, and archaic Umbrian.

"Tell me, Victorinus—no, don't touch the footstool, if a fly lands on that woollen bandage my shin-bone splits like dry chestnut!, tell me why the immortal gods give smooth, unswollen ankles to every mule-driver in the Subura while turning an ex-consul's joints into bags of hot gravel!"

Standing quietly by the glazed terracotta *hypocaust* wall-flues with Senator Priscus's oilskin scroll-cylinder tucked under his left arm, seventeen-year-old Corvus watched the famous Numidian senator wince, hiss between magnificent white African teeth, and immediately dictate two lines of an exquisite letter to Emperor Antoninus Pius describing his knee-pain through three rare metaphors out of Plautus's *Pseudolus*.

Outside the tall mica panes, mid-November sleet blew slantwise across the high brick rampart of the **Horti Maecenatiani**, rattling dead plane-leaves over the ancient Servian agger where Horace and Virgil had once walked under Augustus. Inside Fronto's circular winter study, boxwood braziers and hollow tile walls kept the air hot as Cirta in June, thick with the competing smells of boiled henbane poultice, cedar book-oil, roasted Libyan figs, and five hundred priceless Republican papyrus rolls stacked in open pigeonholes round the room.

Corvus had turned seventeen five days earlier—wearing a plain unbordered tunic on workdays saving his white citizen *toga virilis* for Forum mornings, his shoulders had broadened enough hauling water-buckets and archive-chests that Phileros had let out his winter seams twice since autumn. For five months now he had spent two evenings a week after Caelian hours barking Demosthenes with round smooth river-pebbles under his tongue at old Gnipho's freedman's school; today Priscus had trusted him alone with a sealed packet of Cato the Censor's *Origines* excerpts Fronto wanted checked for an upcoming extortion speech before the Senate.

Before Fronto's secretary could take Priscus's cylinder from Corvus's hand, bronze folding-doors opened at the garden end of the conservatory and cold November rain-wind swirled in around **two imperial purple cloaks at once**.

Every secretary and pupil in the hot room dropped stylus and stood bowed—except crippled Fronto himself, whose lined, olive-dark Numidian face broke into radiant, almost fatherly delight as both adopted sons of the Emperor crossed the mosaic together to kiss his swollen knuckles.

"My two eyes!" Fronto cried out, forgetting his knee completely until he shifted half an inch and yelped *"By Hercules's club, kiss the cheek carefully, Mark, the elbow's full of scorpions today!"*

Corvus stepped half a pace back against the cedar book-presses, keeping his head respectfully inclined while missing nothing through lowered lashes. Up until today he had seen **Marcus Aurelius Caesar** and **Lucius Ceionius Commodus** only thirty yards off across crowded Forum basilicas or riding behind lictors in purple *trabeae*. Here, eight feet away across warm mosaic, stood the future of sixty million souls.

Marcus Caesar, thirty-six, dark beard lightly silvered at the chin-corners, wearing a plain, rain-dampened equestrian cloak over coarse unbleached wool—knelt right down beside his old teacher's footstool without caring for the henbane grease on the carpet, testing the heat of Fronto's flannel knee-wrap with gentle, ink-stained fingers and asking quietly whether the Pergamene physician had tried warm sulphur-water sponges before sleep. Tucked securely inside Marcus's belt-fold rode two battered, leather-bound Greek codices whose title-slips Corvus could read upside down from eight paces: **Arrian's *Discourses of Epictetus***, lent him by **Junius Rusticus**.

Behind Marcus strolled twenty-six-year-old **Lucius Commodus**, shaking bright November raindrops out of his gold-dusted curls like a wet retriever, smelling wonderfully across ten feet of hot room of Syrian spikenard, crushed mint, and wet stallion-leather, carrying in his own ringed hands a covered silver dish of braised Phasian pheasant nested on warm spelt-groats.

"Mark brings you cold water and Epictetus for your gout, Magister," Lucius said, setting the steaming silver dish right on Fronto's dictation-desk and lifting the lid so rich juniper gravy perfumed the entire study. "I brought a bird fat enough to grease both knees from the inside out, straight off my cook's spit! Eat three mouthfuls before your doctors starve you into a Stoic ghost like my brother here."

Fronto sniffed the juniper steam blissfully, then spotted the battered Greek *Epictetus* codices sticking out of Marcus Caesar's belt, and his mobile face fell into genuine, theatrical heartbreak that held real iron underneath the play-acting.

"Still chewing dry Cilician thistles from Quintus Junius Rusticus's mule-bag, Caesar?" Fronto groaned, tapping Marcus's leather notebook sorrowfully with one bandaged forefinger. "Twenty years I polished your tongue until you could make six hundred senators weep or march with twelve Latin words, and now Rusticus teaches you that eloquence is vanity, purple wool is dead sheep's hair smeared in shellfish blood, and an Emperor ought to write crabbed Greek maxims to his own liver at midnight! Tell me plain, Mark: when rebellious legions on the Danube or greedy tax-farmers in Syria need governing one day, will you read them syllogisms from Chrysippus? Even Jupiter thunders in rhythm; he doesn't mumble!"

Marcus smiled—a tired, luminous, slightly sad smile that crinkled the dark hollows beneath his sleepless eyes, and laid one thin hand warmly over his old master's bandaged wrist.

"Rusticus teaches me not to lie to myself while thundering, Magister," Marcus answered gently. "And today I didn't come to argue Zeno; I brought back your Cato roll—Wait: is that Quintus Servilius Priscus's seal over by the third press?"

Six pairs of eyes, Fronto's, Marcus Caesar's, lazy Lucius Commodus's, and three secretaries'—swung all at once onto seventeen-year-old Corvus standing flat against the cedar pigeonholes.

Corvus did not flinch, swallow, or shuffle his calfskin shoes. Inside his skull the cold surveyor's bolt slid cleanly shut: chin level, shoulders square, hands still on the oiled leather cylinder.

"Excerpts from the Fifth Book of Marcus Porcius Cato's *Origines* and the speech *De Sumptu Suo*, Caesar," Corvus said clearly, his deep voice, drilled five months over river-pebbles to strip away street-hiss without flattening its crisp Sabine bone—carrying clean as struck bronze across the warm room. "Copied and verified twice against Senator Priscus's Tusculan manuscript at the sixth hour."

Fronto blinked sharply through gout-tears, tilting his grey-curled Numidian head like an old hunting-eagle hearing a familiar mountain cry.

"Wait a breath," Fronto said, waving off the secretary who stepped forward to take the tube. "Don't hand it to Victorinus, unroll Column Two yourself, boy! Whose voice-school taught you to drop your diaphragm before initial *C* without hissing the *S*? And where did you get those vowels—Nomentum? Reate?"

"Crustumerium and Sabinum originally, Senator," Corvus answered, breaking the red wax seal cleanly with his scarred right thumb and drawing out the fresh papyrus roll without rustling the dry margins. "Old Antonius Gnipho's freedman has drilled my breathing since June."

"Sabine!" Fronto crowed, slapping his unbandaged right thigh hard enough to make Lucius grin around a mouthful of pheasant-wing. "Cato's own rocky turnip soil! Listen to this, Mark, listen to what real pre-Greek Latin sounds like before your Stoic dialecticians boiled all the marrow out of our nouns! Boy: read from Cato's *On His Own Expenses*, where the old Censor tells his corrupt enemies how his secretary reads out his clean account-book and then refuses to rub it out! Don't chant it like an Asianic flute-girl—read it as if four rich thieves were trying to send you to exile!"

Lucius Commodus leaned back against a porphyry pillar picking pheasant-meat delicately from small white bones, amused at watching a seventeen-year-old clerk thrown cold before two Caesars and Rome's supreme rhetor. Marcus Caesar folded both ink-stained hands quietly inside his coarse woollen sleeves, watching Corvus's eyes with calm, grave attention.

Corvus looked down once at the iron-black column his own reed had copied three hours earlier, Cato's famous *praeteritio* where the eighty-year-old farmer-statesman acts out ordering his clerk to read, stop, scratch out, no leave it in—then lifted his eyes off the papyrus straight at the empty marble floor between Marcus and Lucius, letting memory hold every syllable.

*"I ordered the boy to fetch the tablet where my speech was written down,"* Corvus began, keeping his voice low, dry, conversational—exactly as Senator Priscus had spoken six sentences over the Salarian orchard in May. *"Read out, I told him, what I spent on public banquets! Wait—don't read that; they don't care a fig whether I drank water at home. Read the paragraph where I refused bribes in Spain! No—rub that line clean off the wax too; they hate hearing what didn't stick to my fingers!"*

Then Corvus dropped his voice three notes deeper into his chest, not shouting, *cutting*, letting the hard Sabine *r*'s and *t*'s strike like a mason's chisel squaring Tiburtine stone:

*"Never mind—put the scraper down! Read every single figure from the top of the column to the bottom, if they force me! Better a citizen's honest ledger sound ugly to thieves today than stand blank when his children ask how he kept his house!"*

Dead quiet fell inside the hot conservatory, broken only by November sleet ticking sharp against the high mica windows.

Lucius Commodus had stopped chewing his pheasant-wing halfway to his mouth. Marcus Caesar looked sideways at Fronto, a faint flush of reluctant pleasure warming his pale Stoic cheeks despite himself.

Fronto let out a long, theatrical sigh of pure bliss, sinking back into his seven cushions.

"Hear the bone in those verbs, Mark?" Fronto murmured. "*Dele istuc... noli delere... recita ab initio!* Not one painted Greek adjective, not one perfumed circumlocution—pure iron nails driven straight through oak! What is your name, Sabine?"

"Lucius Cassius Corvus, Gaius's son, of the Crustumina tribe, Senator," Corvus answered, rerolling the scroll cleanly without his scarred thumb trembling once.

"Seventeen?"

"Five days past, Senator."

"Tell Quintus Servilius Priscus with my love," Fronto said, waving two bandaged fingers toward his desk-clerk, "that his seventeen-year-old copyist reads Cato cleaner than half the praetorian candidates snoring in the Curia! Victorinus: put Lucius Cassius Corvus down on the back-bench stool for Thursday sixth-hour declamations whenever Priscus can spare him, gratis, mind you, I don't charge Sabine turnip-boys fees so long as they bring me words out of Cato and never once say *essentially* or quote Chrysippus in my house!"

Corvus bowed deep enough to hide the fierce, sudden blaze behind his grey-green eyes. **Marcus Cornelius Fronto's own Thursday declamation bench**—where sons of consuls paid ten thousand sesterces a year just to stand near the door and hear the old Numidian dissect a courtroom proem!

Marcus Caesar paused beside Corvus on his way to the colonnade door a quarter-hour later, dark sleepless eyes resting for two quiet breaths on Corvus's scarred right thumb-knuckle and clean, mended grey tunic.

"Junius Rusticus spoke of a young surveyor's son at Quintus Priscus's house who measured water-pipes on the Caelian in January," Marcus Caesar said softly, remembering a single clerk's story ten months later out of ten thousand petitions! "Hold fast to Cato's clean ledger, Lucius Cassius. Sharp tongues are common in Rome; clean hands are rare."

"I will remember, Caesar," Corvus said, bowing low as imperial purple brushed past his elbow into the rain.

* * *

Out in the vaulted travertine carriage-porch of Maecenas's Gardens, where twenty red-cloaked German speculatores and torch-bearers stamped mud off their hobnails waiting for the two princes' litters, dusk was already falling thick and yellow-grey with freezing sleet.

Corvus lingered two paces back inside the dry brick door-jamb wrapping Priscus's empty leather scroll-case under his coarse winter cloak while Lucius Commodus paused laughing under the porch-arch to toss his leftover silver pheasant-dish to a hunchbacked Greek pantomime player waiting among his hangers-on.

Right behind the pantomime player, stepping nimbly down out of a gilded four-mule litter carried by tall Cappadocians in green-trimmed tunics, came **Publius Vedius Pollio**, accompanied by goat-bearded **Hermippus** holding a waxed umbrella over his master's oiled ivory skull!

Corvus drew one silent step backward behind a stacked pile of pine hypocaust-logs inside the shadowy porter's arch, pulling his dark wool hood low across his brow. Four paces away—close enough that Corvus smelled bitter almond hand-balm cutting right through wet mule-hair, Vedius bowed low over Lucius Commodus's extended ringed hand.

"My Lord Lucius! Your Syrian head-groom tells me *Volucer* ran six laps under twelve hundred breaths on wet sand yesterday—though some mysterious new supplier has started dropping green-sealed laurel-salve jars at the *Trigarium* gate behind my poor factor's back!" Vedius purred musically, smiling to show there was no malice in the world between sporting gentlemen. "Still, if the horse runs faster, we all win gold in April! Will you honor my Caelian heated bath after tomorrow's hunt?"

"Bring snow-pears and leave your gloomy water-lawyers at home, Publius, and I'll bring Petronius Mamertinus!" Lucius laughed, swinging agilely into his dry curtained litter as horns blew and the joint imperial escort clattered out through the Esquiline arch.

Standing under the dripping porch-eaves waiting for his own Cappadocians to turn his litter round in the narrow puddle, Vedius Pollio's velvet smile vanished the instant Lucius's purple curtains disappeared into the sleet.

"Find out before the Kalends whose cart drops those green-pitched forty-pound stoneware jars at the *Factio Prasina* gate, Hermippus," Vedius said softly, his light flute-player's voice flat as cold mutton-fat now. "Nobody undercuts my stable-factors with Lucius's grooms by accident; somebody on the Caelian is trimming my feathers."

"Scaeva already has a fuller's boy watching the Campus Martius hay-wharf, master," Hermippus murmured, consulting a damp pocket-wax leaf under the umbrella. "At least the dockside accounts came in clean on the Kalends—including your little Tyrian oil-filly from Subortanae. Twenty silver denarii down on the nail every month since March without missing an hour, and quiet as a mouse."

*Little Tyrian oil-filly from Subortanae. Twenty silver denarii down on the nail every month since March.*

Behind the stacked pine hypocaust-logs, seventeen-year-old Corvus stopped breathing completely.

For four heartbeats the wet courtyard, the clattering Cappadocian mules, and the sleet hissing down the gutter vanished into a white, roaring tunnel inside his ears.

*No.* Hundreds of Syrian girls worked oil-stalls down by the Emporium—No: *from Subortanae*! There had been **one** Tyrian girl at *Figlinae Subortanae*!

*Eighty sesterces every month since March.* March—the exact afternoon Scaeva raided Berenice's garret, when Lucida met him under the Arch of Titus with an empty cedar cash-box, swearing she had paid two hundred to buy Sura through a third-party Esquiline broker and eighty to clear Berenice's rent, and that Vedius had lost her trail completely!

Eight months, March, April, May, June, July, August, September, October, November—**six hundred forty sesterces** she had bled secretly out of her own fingers into Hermippus's palm every single Kalends behind his back, letting Corvus drop his forty-eight weekly sesterces proudly into their loft box and lecture her on Gaius's *Institutes* while Publius Vedius Pollio held an iron leash round her neck every thirty days!

Corvus's right fist clamped so hard around the pine log-bark in the dark that a dry splinter drove half an inch under his scarred thumb-nail without his feeling a sting. Only eighteen months of hard Caelian schooling kept him from lunging four paces out of the wood-shadow with his bare hands at Vedius's plump throat, where four Cappadocian litter-guards would have broken his skull on the cobbles in ten breaths and undone both their lives forever.

Vedius climbed smoothly into his gilded curtains; the Cappadocians hoisted the poles; the green-trimmed tunics vanished south toward the Oppian crest.

Corvus pulled the bloody pine splinter slowly out from under his thumb-nail with his teeth, spat it into the mud, and stepped out into the freezing sleet.

* * *

He did not take the short cut straight down the Clivus Suburanus to Barba's warm bakery loft—not yet, not while rage and humiliation hammered so hot behind his temples that he would have kicked the loft door open and shouted Vedius's name across Sura's pallet where half the Fish-Market might hear.

Instead he walked blindly east along the muddy outer bank of the ancient Servian rampart toward the **Porta Esquilina**, letting icy November sleet soak through his wool hood until his skull cooled back into stone.

Outside the triple travertine arches of the Esquiline Gate, past the old potter's burial-pits Maecenas had covered with garden soil, out where gravel cart-ruts forked toward Tibur and Praeneste between tall brick tombs and smoking lime-kilns—a knot of twenty shivering onlookers stood round four sputtering pitch-cressets in the mud.

Corvus slowed two paces off the gravel shoulder, rain dripping off his eyelashes.

Three muscular public executioner's slaves (*carnifices*) in filthy red-dyed leather aprons, forbidden by ancient custom to live inside the sacred *pomerium* wall lest their breath pollute citizens—had thrown a stripped, shivering man of about thirty flat on his back across a notched seven-foot oak crossbeam (*patibulum*) lying in the sleet beside an upright scarred post permanently planted in concrete beside the highway.

Around the condemned man's neck hung the whitewashed wooden *titulus* board naming his crime in two-inch black brush letters so every traveller riding into Rome could read Caesar's peace:

*C · MALLIVS · SP · F · LIBRARIVS · OB · FALSAS · TABVLAS · EX · LEGE · CORNELIA*

*Gaius Mallius, freeborn bastard's son, clerk—for forged public tablets under the Sullan Statute.*

Not a runaway slave. **A freeborn Roman citizen**, *Spurii filius*, no rich grandfather, no four-hundred-thousand-sesterce equestrian ring on his finger, counted among the *humiliores* whom second-century imperial rescripts punished with the *summum supplicium* once reserved strictly for slaves and rebels!

"Hold his left elbow flat on the notch, Dama—he's jerking!" the head *carnifex* grunted around an iron spike gripped sideways in his teeth, kneeling casually across Gaius Mallius's bare ribs in the mud as a butcher knelt on a calf.

Mallius wasn't shouting heroic speeches; he was weeping through broken teeth, pissing himself hot into the freezing sleet, begging in educated Forum Latin, better Latin than Volusenus Niger spoke!—for someone to fetch his cousin at the Porticus Minucia with six hundred sesterces before the hammer fell.

*Clank.*

Three flat, businesslike blows of a six-pound iron maul drove the five-inch square-shanked iron spike straight between the radius and ulna just above Mallius's left wrist into the wet oak *patibulum*.

The scream that tore out of Mallius's throat wasn't human speech anymore; it rose thin, ragged, and high as a hare caught in iron teeth, cutting clean across the hiss of sleet and the jingle of a rich merchant's covered carriage rolling briskly past through the gate ten feet away without a single curtain parting.

*Clank. Clank. Clank.* Right wrist nailed fast; four ropes hooked under the crossbeam; four *carnifices* heaving on pulleys to hoist the writhing white body eight feet off the mud until the mortise dropped with a wet *thud* onto the upright post—leaving Gaius Mallius hanging three yards above the highway gravel to take two winter days dying of cramp, sleet, and slow suffocation, for the exact crime **Marcus Tullius Dama** had escaped in May with a five-line clerical correction, and one-hundredth of the fraud **Publius Vedius Pollio** committed every morning before breakfast while eating snow-pears with Emperors' sons!

Sixteen-year-old Corvus stood alone at the edge of the torchlight with freezing mud over his calfskin ankles, forcing his eyelids wide open until every jerk of Mallius's nailed wrists burned permanent acid into the back of his brain.

Why had Lucida hidden Vedius Pollio's eighty-sesterce monthly chain from him for eight straight months?

Not because she loved lying to him. Because at seventeen **Lucius Cassius Corvus was still an ant below the four-hundred-thousand-sesterce line**, a forty-eight-sesterce-a-week clerk whom Vedius Pollio's friends could have nailed beside Gaius Mallius in the Esquiline sleet if Corvus swung first and missed!

Standing in the red smoke of the pitch-cressets while Mallius moaned high overhead in the dark, Corvus reached inside his wet tunic-collar and gripped his half of the broken *Figlinae Subortanae* clay stamp until its jagged edge bit hard into his scarred palm.

Junius Rusticus could keep his gentle Stoic sermons about clean poverty; Cato could keep his turnips.

In Rome there were only two nations under heaven: **men worth four hundred thousand sesterces who wore the narrow purple stripe and gold ring**, whom no magistrate on earth could flog or nail to wood, and **meat**.

"Four years," Corvus said aloud into the sleet, so low not even the nearest bystander heard him over the torches. "Before I turn twenty-one, I wear the gold ring—and I take every brick, pipe, and sesterce Publius Vedius Pollio owns."

When he climbed the outside brick stair to Barba's warm loft an hour later, soaked through and pale as chalk, Lucida looked up immediately from counting Green-stable jar tallies beside the charcoal pan, set down her stylus in alarm, and came straight across the warm tiles with a dry wool towel to rub his icy hair.

"Where were you in this sleet?" she scolded softly, pressing both warm, olive-oil-scented palms against his freezing cheeks to test for fever. "Did Fronto keep you till the second watch?"

Corvus looked down six inches into her fierce, watchful dark amber eyes, knowing every single secret silver coin she had paid on the Kalends since March to keep Scaeva away from his throat, and deciding in that exact heartbeat, with a coldness Senator Priscus himself might have admired, **not to tell her he knew** until he was rich and dangerous enough to cut her chain off in a single stroke without asking her leave.

"Fronto put me on his Thursday bench gratis," Corvus said quietly, wrapping his cold fingers briefly round both her warm wrists before letting go. "And Marcus Caesar knew my name."
