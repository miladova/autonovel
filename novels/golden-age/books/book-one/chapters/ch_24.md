# Chapter XXIV — The Old Man's Vintage

*Fourteenth Day Before the Kalends of November, AD 160 · Lucida*

Twelve miles west of Rome along the paved **Via Aurelia**, where low rolling Etruscan clay-hills smelled of wild fennel, bruised pine-needles, and distant Tyrrhenian salt, the master of sixty million human beings sat under a plain whitewashed timber grape-arbor splitting half-asses on a wax slate while wasps crawled undisturbed over his knuckles.

Lucida stood quietly in the dappled October leaf-shadow behind **Ceionia Fabia**'s camp-chair—four days past her own twentieth birthday now, wearing modest dove-grey Tarsian wool pinned high enough round her throat to hide the last faint shadow above her collarbone, **Day Nine Hundred Sixty-One** chalked safe back at the Fish-Market before dawn, staring through hanging purple clusters of late *amineum* wine-grapes at **Emperor Antoninus Pius**.

After four years inside Rome's crushing marble theaters, hundred-foot brick tenements, and gilded Caelian halls, imperial **Lorium** looked almost shockingly small: no soaring Hadrianic domes, no Egyptian obelisks, only the sunny, rambling country farmhouse where Antoninus had grown up as a boy seventy years ago and where he still preferred to live ten months out of twelve like a thrifty Sabine squire checking his own bailiff's turnip-weights.

Thirty paces across the stone-flagged press-yard, two giant twenty-foot oak lever-beams (*prela*) groaned rhythmically down upon stacked rush baskets of crushed grapes while eight bare-legged country slaves—and right in the middle of the splashing stone treading-vat beside them, **twenty-nine-year-old Lucius Ceionius Commodus** with his purple-bordered tunic hiked shamelessly into his belt, crimson must-juice staining both muscular thighs to the knee as he laughed and sang vintage catches!, trod red foam pouring sweet and heady down stone channels into sunken terracotta *dolia*.

Under the vine-arbor sat seventy-four-year-old **Antoninus** himself.

Tall, long-faced, silver-bearded like benign Father Numa on bronze medallions, he wore a plain undyed woollen tunic woven by women of his own household; before sitting down for afternoon petitions he had eaten two dry crusts of spelt-bread without wine—every palace servant knew the old man's habit, so hunger wouldn't weaken his patience or make his belly rumble impolitely in front of nervous provincial envoys.

Yet standing twelve feet behind Fabia's shoulder with a dyer's-and-healer's trained eye, twenty-year-old Lucida saw everything the bronze coin-makers left out.

Underneath that humble white wool tunic, whenever Antoninus leaned half an inch forward to take a scroll, **stiff flat strips of dried linden-wood** laced tight inside a linen bodice creaked faintly against his ribs—propping up a seventy-four-year-old thoracic spine curved so sharply by age that without wooden stays his chin would have rested on his breastbone! Around his ankles, loose soft felt house-boots hid swollen, doughy white flesh pitting deep above the leather laces; his breath carried across twelve feet of sweet grape-must the dry, unmistakable camphor-and-horehound odor of old men whose lungs filled slowly after midnight; and his pale blue eyes, gentle and shrewd as ever while questioning an Ostian salt-contractor over three missing amphorae, watered constantly at the outer corners.

At Antoninus's right elbow stood thirty-nine-year-old **Marcus Aurelius Caesar**, thinner and darker-ringed round the eyes than ever, dutifully blotting every rescript his adoptive father signed without once glancing out toward the sunny treading-vat where carefree Lucius splashed must; three paces left on a cushioned bench sat Marcus's wife **Annia Galeria Faustina**, fanning sleepy yellow-jacket wasps away from her plump eighteen-month-old toddler **Fadilla** while watching **Ceionia Fabia** across the arbor with flat, unblinking maternal hostility.

"Four years ago tonight," Fabia murmured so softly over her left shoulder that only Lucida holding her parasol-case heard above the creak of the oak wine-beam, "he walked three miles round those upper olive-terraces without a stick. Today two freedmen lifted him onto that cushion before the Baetican envoys came through the gate, and **Galeria Lysistrata**, his dead wife's Greek freedwoman who warms his feet and sells Praetorian centurionates behind Marcus's back—had the Pergamene doctor burn mugwort over his kidneys at dawn."

Out in the sunny yard Lucius Commodus vaulted laughing over the low stone lip of the treading-vat, rinsed crimson grape-skins off his calves with a bucket of cold well-water, and came striding barefoot up to the arbor carrying a foaming two-handled earthenware bowl of fresh, unfermented first-press *mustum* to offer the old Emperor for the traditional autumn blessing to Liber Pater.

"First blood off the south slope, Father!" Lucius called warmly, dropping effortlessly to one stained knee before Antoninus's chair without a trace of court stiffness. "Sweeter than Chian honey, pour three drops on the hearth-stone so frost spares the late olives!"

Old Antoninus smiled—real, paternal fondness warming his lined grey cheeks as he looked down at bright-curled Lucius, so different from overburdened Marcus, and set both veined hands on the carved oak chair-arms to stand upright for Liber's libation, waving away Marcus's instinctively outstretched hand with mild pride:

"An old farmer still pours his own vintage, Mark."

Antoninus pushed up onto both felt-booted feet, took the heavy clay bowl of purple must into his hands—

And **his swollen left knee folded inward like wet chalk**.

Before Lucius or Marcus could catch both elbows, seventy-four-year-old Caesar pitched sideways against the arbor-post; the linden-wood stays beneath his tunic cracked audibly against oak; the earthenware bowl shattered across the flagstones, splashing dark crimson grape-must like fresh arterial blood straight across **Marcus's white toga-hem and Lucius's bare insteps**, while a thin string of yellowish bile ran down Antoninus's silver beard as a sudden grey-lipped dizziness seized his heart!

Every voice in the press-yard died stone-dead. Even the slaves up on the twenty-foot oak press-beam froze hanging on their ropes.

For eight terrible heartbeats **sixty million people's peace hung crooked against a wooden post** while two Praetorian tribunes stepped instinctively six inches closer to opposite princes, and then old Antoninus drew one long, wheezing breath through clenched yellow teeth, wiped his beard dry himself with a corner of his plain wool sleeve, and forced a dry, steady countryman's quip past grey lips:

"Slippery wasps under a clumsy heel, boys. Pour Liber another cup off the beam; clearly the god wanted double measure."

Everyone laughed on cue—too loud, too brittle, as Marcus and tall chamberlains eased the trembling seventy-four-year-old Emperor gently back onto his cushioned carrying-chair and bore him indoors behind drawn linen curtains "to change his splashed boots."

Across the puddle of smashed clay and dark red grape-must soaking into hot dust between them, **Marcus Aurelius Caesar** and **Lucius Ceionius Commodus** stood facing each other for three silent breaths—crimson vintage staining both men's hems alike, before Marcus turned briskly indoors after the physicians without looking back, and Ceionia Fabia rose smoothly from her camp-chair, her green emerald hairpin flashing cold fire in the afternoon sun.

"Bring my wool wrap round the olive-walk, Lucida," Fabia murmured quietly. "The shade just turned very cold."

* * *

Behind the low stuccoed bath-wing of the Lorium villa, where gnarl-trunked century-old olive trees sloped down toward a muddy country lane crowded with couriers' tethered horses, twenty-year-old Lucida walked three paces behind Fabia and Dorcas—her eyes missing nothing in the lengthening golden shadows.

Coming fast up the gravel horse-path straight from a foam-lathered post-mule just ridden hard out from Rome walked **Quintus Junius Rusticus** in his coarse, clasp-free dark Stoic cloak, carrying a sealed leather dispatch-tube tucked beneath his ink-blackened thumb.

Blocking the narrow brick arch into the Emperor's private courtyard stood **Sextus Cornelius Repentinus**, newly elevated three months ago beside Furius Victorinus to command the **Praetorian Guard**, a thick-necked, pock-marked equestrian soldier of fortune with heavy gold rings biting into hairy fingers and the cold eyes of a camp Quartermaster who knew every senator in Rome whispered behind his back that old Antoninus's Greek concubine **Lysistrata** had put the Praetorian sword in his hand!

Fabia halted behind a clipped boxwood angle ten paces up the terrace, motioning Dorcas and Lucida still with two fingers downward.

"The Emperor is resting after the sun, Senator Rusticus," Prefect Repentinus said at the arch—courteous on the surface, planting two hundred pounds of Praetorian iron squarely in the Stoic's path. "If you bring urban dispatches from the Senate, my *cornicularius* will carry them in to Caesar Marcus."

Rusticus looked down first, true to lifelong habit—at Repentinus's gilded military boots, and never offered his hand.

"Letters from **Antioch** concerning King Vologases's horsemen on the Euphrates go into Marcus Caesar's own palm, Prefect, never through camp-clerks," Junius Rusticus replied in his quiet, dry parchment voice. "Keep your cohorts polished for parade; foreign frontiers belong to consuls."

Repentinus's pock-marked cheeks darkened dull brick-red as Rusticus brushed past his elbow through the arch without bowing, leaving the Praetorian Prefect staring after the Stoic's coarse cloak for three ugly breaths before Repentinus turned on his heel, glanced straight up the boxwood terrace toward **Ceionia Fabia**, and touched two ringed fingers significantly to his sword-belt in silent understanding!

Behind Fabia's shoulder, twenty-year-old Lucida stood motionless as carved olive-wood while two halves of a deadly puzzle clicked shut inside her head with an audible iron snap:

*Letters from Antioch concerning King Vologases's horsemen on the Euphrates...*

And three weeks ago down inside the flooded brick vault beneath Lot Fourteen, sliding out of **nineteen-year-old Corvus's** split leather satchel right beside her own name on a receivership sheet, **a flat oilskin packet sealed in pale pistachio-green Chian mastic wax**—the exact Levant resin Syrian couriers used from Antioch and Seleucia, stamped with Senator Priscus's private terebinth tree!

Corvus wasn't merely hunting crooked brick-stamps and aqueduct pipes for Quintus Servilius Priscus anymore. **Corvus was holding Priscus's and Rusticus's secret Syrian succession dispatches inside his own bag!**

* * *

Heavy autumn rain swept in off the Tyrrhenian Sea after dark, drumming loud on the low red pantiles of the **Mansio ad Duodecimum**, the private brick posting-house half a mile up the Via Aurelia where Ceionia Fabia's party lodged overnight because Faustina had pointedly packed every spare Palatine guest-chamber inside the Lorium villa with her own Annii cousins.

At the third watch of the night, after Dorcas had retired behind the inner partition and Marsian guards stood soaked under the stable eaves below, thirty-one-year-old **Ceionia Fabia** sat barefoot in a dark woollen night-robe beside a roaring olive-root hearth, turning her six-inch green emerald hairpin slowly end over end between both hands while rain streamed black down the shutter-cracks.

Across the small table twenty-year-old Lucida finished packing unused saffron and camphor salve-pots into oiled felt, waiting—knowing before Fabia opened her mouth that the bill for two years of Caelian protection had just come due.

"You saw his knees buckle over the grape-bowl," Fabia said quietly into the snapping olive-sparks.

"Linden-wood stays don't hold water out of lungs when January fog comes up the Tiber, Domina," Lucida answered without pretense.

Fabia stopped spinning the gold needle and laid it flat across the scarred pine table, pointing directly between them.

"Four months, five at the outside, if Lysistrata's Greek doctors bleed him carefully," Fabia said, low rapid patrician vowels stripped of every court curl. "When old Antoninus stops breathing at Lorium, Marcus holds the tribunician power already—nobody disputes Marcus's purple. What hangs on a razor's edge for **twelve hours** between Lorium's midnight courier and the morning Senate meeting is whether Marcus names my brother **Lucius** full co-equal **Imperator et Augustus** beside him, with half the armies, half the treasury, and the Eastern command against Parthia—or whether **Quintus Servilius Priscus** and **Junius Rusticus** stampede the Senate before dawn into voting Marcus sole Augustus and packing Lucius off to some toothless priesthood where Priscus's hounds can strip every friend of our house, including **Vedius Pollio**, **Cornelius Repentinus**, and everyone holding Dorcas's Caelian leases—down to bare bone!"

Lucida folded her oiled felt cloth neatly square, stain-faded thumbs calm on the table-edge. "Marcus Caesar loves his brother, Domina; anyone watching them over the spilled must today saw that."

"Marcus loves *duty* better than blood, and if Rusticus and Priscus hand him a *fait accompli* from the frontier legions swearing they will recognize Marcus alone, Marcus will sigh over Epictetus and cage Lucius 'for the peace of the Republic'!" Fabia snapped softly, leaning forward until emerald fire danced in her eyes across two feet of hearth-glow. "Repentinus's post-riders caught wind in September that Priscus and two ex-consuls have already slipped **ciphered Greek instructions to Antioch** behind Marcus's back, ordering senior tribunes of the Syrian legions to refuse my brother's name on the *sacramentum* oath! Priscus keeps *no* copy inside his Caelian *tablinum*, Repentinus's locksmith checked his cedar chest while Priscus dined out last Tuesday and found only brick-stamps and a dusty bottle of red ditch-water!"

Lucida's pulse did not jump by a quarter-beat; inside her head she saw the dusty Sidonian glass flask of red Sinope ruddle-water from Chapter Five sitting harmlessly in Priscus's chest while **the real Syrian lime-bark duplicate rode inside nineteen-year-old Lucius Cassius Corvus's satchel or Castor strongbox**!

"Why tell a Fish-Market baker high Senate secrets at midnight, Domina?" Lucida asked evenly.

Fabia smiled—cold, intimate, sister-to-sister across a gulf of ten million sesterces.

"Because **one hundred sixty-eight grinding mornings** still stand between your throat and Trajan's three-year citizenship roll, Lucida, putting your Day One Thousand Ninety-Six in **early April**, once Vestalia and guild audit holidays are reckoned!" Fabia murmured with surgical accuracy. "And because two weeks ago **Publius Vedius Pollio** dined with my brother Lucius and boasted over his third cup of Setine that long before March arrives, he holds a **sealed capital *veneficium* denunciation inside Vault Forty-Two at Castor** ready to pull old Berenice and *you* down into the executioner's pit the instant Repentinus takes emergency interregnum powers—wiping out three years of flour in an afternoon so Lot Fourteen and your Fish-Market mills fall straight into Vedius's lap!"

There sat every blade in the dark, counted down to the single day: **tenth of April, AD 161** for Day 1,095; Antoninus dying before spring; Vedius's Castor Vault Forty-Two; and Corvus's receivership net waiting on the other side!

"Whereas," Fabia whispered, sliding two smooth, unringed fingers across the pine to touch the back of Lucida's scarred hand, "you grew up in the same Sabine ditch as **Lucius Cassius Corvus**, Priscus's brilliant nineteen-year-old vault-keeper, whom half the Quirinal saw break glass in his bare fist for your name at Volusenus's supper in June! Bring me **one authenticated leaf or green terebinth wax-key of Priscus's Syrian treason** before the old Emperor dies: my brother Lucius rides straight into the **Castra Praetoria** with Repentinus, forces full co-equal **Augustus** purple on the Senate at sword-point, **burns Vedius Pollio's Castor Vault Forty-Two unopened**, signs your full Roman citizenship and freeborn *restitutio natalium* in wet imperial purple before sunrise, and hands you Ostian state grain-warehouses big enough to buy and sell ten men like Vedius!"

Outside in the dark courtyard a post-horse whinnied shrill against the pouring October rain; inside on the hearth an olive-root collapsed in a shower of bright gold sparks.

Twenty-year-old Lucida looked down at Fabia's six-inch green emerald needle gleaming on the pine planks between them—thinking of Corvus standing half-naked in the brick vault three weeks ago with blood from her own teeth running down his chin, saying *at least Gaius Cassius died a freeborn citizen under open sky instead of leaving his child kissing patrician hairpins in Caelian bedrooms*.

Hand Priscus's green-waxed Syrian letter to Ceionia Fabia tonight, and when Lucius Commodus and Praetorian Prefect Repentinus crushed Senator Priscus for treason, **nineteen-year-old Lucius Cassius Corvus, whose hand wrote the Greek cipher and whose box held the bark—would not go into polite island exile; still shy of the four-hundred-thousand-sesterce equestrian ring, Corvus would hang nailed by both wrists outside the Esquiline Gate in winter sleet**!

Even after everything they had torn out of each other's skin in the vault, even knowing he had written her bakery into his own receivership roll—the thought of iron spikes driven through Corvus's scarred right thumb turned Lucida's belly cold with fierce, instantaneous refusal.

*No.* She would break **Castor Vault Forty-Two** with her own wits first, keep all four Orvieto lava mills thundering day and night through winter frost across her last **one hundred sixty-eight grinding mornings**, and reach April a free Roman citizen **without selling Corvus's neck to Praetorian swords—so long as Corvus kept his hands off her ovens**!

"Priscus's young *cognitor* and I haven't spoken a civil word since August's rubble-spear, Domina," Lucida lied smoothly, lifting steady amber eyes into Fabia's gaze without giving either yes or refusal, keeping Fabia's protection warm through winter while guarding her own counsel. "Still, clerks drop wax shavings when winter fevers hurry them. If green mastic falls across my broom before spring, Domina will not hear of it second."

Fabia picked up her gold hairpin, slid it into her night-braid with a satisfied purr, and blew out the nearer lamp.

All night long while rain lashed the Aurelian road twelve miles from Rome, twenty-year-old Lucida lay wide awake behind her curtain counting backward from a hundred and sixty-eight, praying to no god on earth save iron mill-axles and dry Sicilian wheat that old Emperor Antoninus's wooden stays held his chest upright until April.
