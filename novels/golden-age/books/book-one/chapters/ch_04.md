# Chapter IV — White Lead and Rue

*Saturnalia, Eighteenth of December, AD 156 · Lucida*

On the second day of the Saturnalia, while half the Subura staggered blind-drunk down the steps in conical felt liberty-caps roaring *"Io Saturnalia!"* and rattling legal dice on every butcher's block from the Firewall to the Esquiline, Corvus began coughing rust onto the barley-straw.

At first dawn it had sounded only like the dry bark he brought home from the Caelian cisterns whenever Phileros kept him copying wax until dusk in an unheated vestibule. By the third hour, when Lucida knelt beside the wicker screen with a clay bowl of hot barley-gruel, he could not sit upright without catching his left ribs in both fists as if an iron spike had been driven between the fifth and sixth bones. His skin burned dry as kiln-brick under her palm; his lips were cracked dark at the left corner over the fresh white scar Volusenus's stylus had left in November; and when the spasm doubled him sideways, what came up onto the unbleached rag was thin, frothy, and streaked the color of corroded bronze.

Berenice stood over the screen chewing roasted pine-nuts out of an apron pocket, one milky eye aimed at the rafters, one black eye fixed on the rag in Lucida's hand.

"Pleurisy," the old woman said without emotion. "Side-fever. My second husband took it hauling snow-sacks down from Alba in Hadrian's tenth year. Drowned in his own chest-water on the fifth night and left me four brass pots and a lame mule."

"He isn't drowning," Lucida said, dipping a linen strip into cold vinegar-water and laying it flat across Corvus's forehead. Under the wet cloth his grey-green eyes were too bright, pupils wide, tracking shadows on the sloped pantiles that weren't there. "What cuts the phlegm?"

"Cretan hyssop boiled in squill-vinegar, hot linseed poultices changed every hour, and three bronze cupping-horns laid under the shoulder-blade by somebody who knows where the lung-vein runs without cooking the meat," Berenice answered, ticking items off horny yellow fingers. "Asclepiades the Greek down by the Temple of Peace has clean bronze bells and real Egyptian squill. Charges five silver denarii to climb six flights in Saturnalia week, plus two denarii for the syrup. Have you got twenty-eight sesterces sewn in your shift, little crow?"

Lucida knew to the single copper *quadrans* what they had.

Under the loose hearth-brick lay the bronze *signaculum* die and the five remaining Subortanae pine tallies—untouchable, because trying to peddle imperial brick-tallies on Saturnalia morning without Corvus's tongue would bring Scaeva down on their necks before noon—plus **eleven sesterces and two asses** left out of Corvus's Caelian copying-money after buying winter shoes, charcoal, and December straw-rent. Not knowing Corvus still had twenty-four sesterces stitched secretly inside his inner waist-band—the soaked tunic rolled tight under his aching head as a pillow—Lucida counted eleven sesterces against twenty-eight and came up seventeen short.

Behind the screen Corvus caught her wrist with dry, scorching fingers. Even half out of his head with side-pain, he had heard the sum.

"Leave the Greek," he rasped, every word sawed short by the knife under his ribs. "Linseed meal costs... four asses at the oil-stall. Boil that. Don't go out on the Clivus while the... fullers are drunk."

"Shut your teeth and breathe shallow, Sabine," Lucida told him, prizing his hot fingers gently open and tucking his arm back under the wool cloak so the draught off the dormer wouldn't strike his sweat. She stood up, stepped past the wicker screen to Berenice's marble tombstone slab, and looked straight into the old woman's good black eye. "Whose basket goes up the hills tonight?"

Berenice stopped chewing pine-nuts. Slowly she reached up to the top shelf behind the dried squid-bones and lifted down a square willow hand-basket lined in oiled purple linen. Inside sat three stoppered alabaster pots of *cerussa*—the velvety, dead-white lead-carbonate face-cream patrician women plastered over freckles and wine-veins—two little horn boxes of Syrian alkanet lip-red, and, buried underneath a false bottom of dried lavender stalks, **two thumb-sized phials of thick green glass** stopped with red beeswax over bladder-skin.

Rue-oil, black hellebore root, and Cretan savin, boiled down three-to-one. Five drops in mulled wine brought on a late moon-bleeding with cramps hard enough to make a woman bite wood; twenty drops emptied a three-month womb before morning—or stopped the mother's heart if her kidneys were weak and she drank the sediment.

Under the old Sullan statute on *venena*—poisons and secret drugs—a *humilior* caught selling an abortive draught that killed or sickened a citizen's wife didn't go before a polite civil arbiter; she went down into the Tullianum or out beyond the Esquiline Gate to feed crows.

"Myrtale went off at dawn with a trumpeter from the Castra Misenatium wearing his master's dinner-shoes," Berenice said low. "Up on the Caelian Hill, behind the arches of Claudius's aqueduct, stands the town house of **Lucius Ceionius Commodus**—old Aelius Caesar's boy—where his elder sister **Ceionia Fabia** keeps her winter *oecus*. Her chief tire-woman, a freedwoman named Dorcas with a mole on her nostril, ordered the three white-lead pots for the lady's Saturnalia masque tonight, and the two green phials for a chamber-maid who let one of young Lucius's chariot-friends lift her tunic in November. Twelve silver denarii on delivery. Ten for my box; two—eight sesterces—for the legs that carry it, plus whatever door-silver the tire-woman throws if you keep your eyes on the tiles and come back before the second watch."

Eight sesterces plus eleven made nineteen—still nine sesterces short of Asclepiades's twenty-eight.

"Give me three denarii out of the twelve," Lucida said flatly, picking up the porphyry muller to finish smoothing the third alabaster pot's paste with two practiced figure-eight turns, "and lend me the fourth against two weeks' lead-grinding, or take the basket up the Caelian ice yourself with one eye while forty thousand drunks throw torch-butts in the alleys."

Berenice hissed like an old goose, hand flying to her belt-pouch as if wounded. "Four denarii! Sixteen sesterces for an hour's walk? By Astarte, your mother bred a horse-leech!"

"My mother bred a girl who doesn't drop green glass in front of aediles," Lucida answered, wiping white lead off her knuckles with vinegar-tow so not a speck remained under her fingernails to poison bread later. Behind the screen Corvus coughed again—a wet, tearing hitch he tried to muffle into his forearm so she wouldn't hear—and Lucida's jaw went hard as river-flint. "Sixteen sesterces, paid directly into Asclepiades's hand the instant I walk back through that door with your nine denarii, Beroe. Deal or carry?"

Berenice glared from Lucida's chin to the muffled cough behind the reeds, spat a pine-nut shell into the charcoal pan where it flared yellow, and shoved the willow basket against Lucida's ribs.

"Lose one pot or let a *vigiles* corporal look under that lavender," Berenice whispered, "and don't bother climbing back up my seventy-eight steps, girl. Put on Myrtale's clean brown shawl over your head—and take the iron bodkin out of the wool-box. Saturnalia makes swine out of senators and senators out of swine; neither sort keeps its hands to itself after dusk."

* * *

Rome at Saturnalia dusk looked like a city sacked by comedians.

All down the Clivus Suburanus and up the paved rise toward the Carinae, every shop-shutter had been painted with red ochre garlands and hung with little clay figurines—*sigillaria*—sold for two asses apiece at street-corners as holiday gifts. Slaves walked five abreast down the middle of the basalt road wearing their masters' cast-off *synthesis* dinner-robes in scarlet, saffron, and leek-green, woollen *pileus* caps cocked over oiled curls, bellowing tavern catches at passing equestrians who, for five licensed days of December, had to grin and shout *"Io Saturnalia!"* back instead of calling lictors. At every fountain-basin men crouched ten deep round chalked gaming-boards, throwing ivory dice openly under the noses of sleepy urban watchmen; outside a butcher's stall near the Meta Sudans, two drunken fuller's boys were holding a squealing piglet over a brazier to singe its bristles live while a ring of painted Syrian flute-girls pelted them with walnut shells and snowballs scooped out of shaded garden ditches.

Lucida walked briskly along the wall-shadow with Myrtale's brown woollen shawl pulled forward over her dark braids, the willow basket hooked securely on her left forearm, her right hand inside her shawl closed round seven inches of three-sided iron mattress-bodkin.

Twice before she cleared the Colosseum valley men stepped into her path: first a towering German bodyguard from some senator's escort, reeking of spiced beer and mutton-fat, who caught a corner of her shawl laughing something thick in Rhine-Latin until she slipped out of the pin-fold, rapped the flat of her basket hard against his wrist-bone, and vanished behind four passing mule-litters; then, darker up the winding garden walls of the **Caelian Hill**, two teenage patrician boys in untied dining-slippers chasing a shrieking bath-girl with soot-blackened pinecones.

Up on the Caelian, the Subura's roar dropped below like surf under a sea-cliff.

Here high brick garden walls topped with broken glass and overhanging cypress boughs enclosed whole private acres of winter green. Lead water-pipes sang softly inside roadside shrines; torches burned clean olive-oil scented with frankincense in iron brackets beside bronze-studded postern gates—no smoky tallow or rancid *amurca* here—and through high clerestory windows glazed with translucent sheets of split Cappadocian *lapis specularis* drifted the shimmer of twenty-branched bronze candelabra, lyre-strings, and high, cultivated laughter.

At the service postern of the *Domus Ceionia* behind Claudius's great rusticated stone arches, Lucida showed the little lead tally-token Berenice had given her to a sleepy Cappadocian gate-porter wearing a gilt paper crown crooked over his bald pate.

"Dorcas's girl with the face-pots," the porter grunted, waving her down a whitewashed subterranean *cryptoporticus* warm from the bath-furnaces underneath. "Second spiral stair on the left, past the pastry-ovens. Don't cross the main peristyle or the steward docks Dorcas's wine-jar—the young master's got twenty Greens charioteers and six senators' sons drinking snowed Setine in the *triclinium*."

Lucida climbed the narrow travertine service-stair into warmth such as her skin had never felt in December.

Under the mosaic floors ran hollow brick *hypocausta* flues carrying clean wood-heat from invisible slave-stoked furnaces below, turning the very stone under Lucida's cheap leather soles gentle as May sunlight. Yet five paces down the serving-corridor behind the dining-hall curtains, she saw how Caelian warmth was stoked.

In a tiled scullery alcove off the pantry—four paces from silk-hung archways through which silver platters of stuffed dormice, sea-urchins packed in snow, and roast wild boar nested in dates were gliding out on the shoulders of garlanded boys—a fifty-year-old under-steward with a leather gag-strap wrapped round his own fist had a twelve-year-old Bithynian cup-bearer bent bare-backed across an upturned wine-amphora. The boy wore a holiday *pileus* liberty-cap still pinned absurdly to his curls; blood ran in four neat red ribbons down his pale ribs where a three-tongued whalebone whip (*scutica*) was coming down in rhythmic, practiced silence because he had chipped the lip of a murrhine dipping-ladle. Every time the whalebone bit, the under-steward shoved a vinegar-sponge against the boy's open mouth so no cry would spoil the holiday equality in the *triclinium* twelve feet away.

Halfway through the curtain-slit beyond the pantry, a corpulent grey-haired senator in a gravy-spotted *synthesis* lurched out onto the peristyle tiles, dropped to one knee beside a chased silver basin held by a kneeling girl no older than Sura, and stuck a flamingo feather down his own throat to bring up three cupfuls of purple Setine wine and half-digested thrush breasts so he could go back in for the mulled oysters; wiping his beard on the kneeling girl's clean hair-braids when she wasn't quick enough with the towel, he pinched her small breast hard enough to make her wince, belched *"Io Saturnalia, little mouse!"* and waddled back toward the lyre-music.

Lucida did not slow her step, did not stare, did not let her nostrils twitch at the mixed stench of warm vomit, roast boar-fat, myrrh smoke, and fresh blood on wet whalebone. She filed the layout of the pantry, the silver-closet lock, the three exits, and the face of the vomiting senator behind her eyes the way Corvus filed roof-joists, and tapped twice at the cedar door at the end of the women's corridor.

A sharp-nosed Greek freedwoman of forty with a black mole beside her left nostril—**Dorcas**—yanked the door open, snatched Lucida inside by the elbow, and shot the bronze bolt.

The dressing-chamber smelled like a summer garden locked inside an ivory box: crushed Campanian roses, cinnamon-leaf *malabathrum* worth three hundred denarii the pound, warm beeswax, and clean linen dried over myrtle-charcoal. Polished bronze mirrors four feet high stood propped on carved griffins' paws; clothes-presses spilled sea-purple Coan silks, saffron gauze, and pearl-sewn slippers across cushioned stools.

In the corner behind an embroidered screen, huddled small on a low footstool with both arms wrapped tight around her belly, sat a weeping sixteen-year-old slave-hairdresser (*ornatrix*) with a split lower lip and dark finger-bruises shaped like a man's grip high on her bare upper arm.

And in the center of the mosaic floor, seated upright in a high-backed chair of inlaid tortoiseshell while a second girl brushed out a heavy river of pale red-gold hair that fell below her shoulder-blades, sat **Ceionia Fabia**.

Lucida had expected some soft, painted palace doll. One glance killed that dead.

Ceionia Fabia was perhaps twenty-eight or twenty-nine, long-boned and straight-spined as a cavalry spear: daughter of Hadrian's dead first heir Caesar Aelius, sister to golden **Lucius Ceionius Commodus** whose drunken laughter echoed faintly across the courtyard from the men's hall, and—as every bathhouse gossip in Rome knew—betrothed at seven years old by Emperor Hadrian's own word to **Marcus Aurelius**, before old Antoninus Pius tore up the match to give Marcus his own daughter Faustina instead. Fabia had a pale, high-cheekboned face without a grain of white lead on it yet, eyes the cold grey-green of winter olive-leaves, and in her right hand she twirled a six-inch Roman hairpin (*acus*) of tempered gold set at the butt with a square-cut Scythian emerald, its point honed fine enough to pierce leather—the tool patrician mistresses kept handy to stab clumsy hair-slaves in the forearm when a curl fell crooked.

"That isn't Berenice's fat Greek slut," Ceionia Fabia said in low, clipped patrician Latin, never turning her head—watching Lucida strictly inside the tall polished bronze mirror.

"Myrtale took holiday fever, Domina," Lucida said smoothly, dropping her eyes the exact required two inches below Fabia's mirror-gaze while lifting the purple linen off the willow basket so Dorcas could inspect the three alabaster pots. "Berenice sent me so your house wouldn't wait. I ground the *cerussa* myself three times in rainwater and Chian vinegar; there isn't a grain of coarse grit in a hundred-weight."

Dorcas uncorked one alabaster pot, rubbed a smear of white cream between thumb and forefinger, sniffed for rancid pig-lard, grunted grudging approval, and reached hastily under the false lavender bottom for the two green glass phials.

"Wait," Ceionia Fabia said.

Dorcas froze with both green phials in her palm.

Fabia dismissed the girl brushing her red-gold hair with one flick of two fingers—*"Out into the passage, Phryne, shut the double doors"*—waited until the outer latch clicked, and only then turned in her tortoiseshell chair to look directly at fifteen-year-old Lucida.

"Come two paces into the candles, girl. Take that dirty wool off your hair."

Lucida let Myrtale's brown shawl slide back onto her shoulders and stepped to the edge of the Nilotic mosaic medallion, hands loose and empty outside her cloak—leaving the iron mattress-bodkin hidden in the shawl-fold.

Fabia's winter-olive eyes traveled from Lucida's thick blue-black braids down to the purple-brown dye-shadow ingrained along her cuticles, the calluses on her outer palms from Beroe's stone muller, and the cheap, newly patched leather shoes Corvus's November silver had bought.

"Syrian?" Fabia asked.

"My mother came from Tyre, Domina. Freed *inter amicos* by Aulus Lucidius at Puteoli. They call me Lucida."

"A Junian Latin," Fabia observed—not guessing, knowing every rung of Rome's human ladder by instinct. She tapped the square emerald butt of the six-inch gold pin once against her own chin. "Do you know what is in those two green bottles Dorcas is sweating over, Lucida?"

A stupid girl said *No, Domina, I only carry baskets*, which insulted a Caelian lady's intelligence and proved you lied under pressure. A greedy girl named the herbs to show how much blackmail she held, which got her dropped down an abandoned cistern in the Viminal gardens before sunrise.

Lucida looked straight at the mosaic hippopotamus under Fabia's hem. "Berenice told me the phials held bitter-almond water for winter chilblains, Domina. My mother told me when I was eight that a dyer's daughter who smells savin and pennyroyal through red beeswax has a blocked nose from October until May."

For three heartbeats the quiet in the warm, rose-scented room was absolute, broken only by the bruised slave-girl hiccuping behind the embroidered screen and a sudden distant roar from the men's *triclinium* across the peristyle where someone had smashed a wine-jar in applause for a charioteer's toast.

Slowly, Ceionia Fabia smiled—only with the corners of her thin, unpainted mouth, never reaching her eyes. She rose from the tortoiseshell chair, silk *stola* whispering over heated stone, walked right up to Lucida until her emerald-pinned fingers rested half an inch below Lucida's chin, and pricked the point of the six-inch gold *acus* lightly—just deep enough to dent skin without drawing blood—into the hollow of Lucida's throat, directly above the hidden olive-pip birthmark.

Lucida did not blink, did not swallow against the gold point, did not tighten a finger toward her shawl. Compared with Mousa's nails in river-mud and molten Sicilian sulfur hissing on Sura's bone, a patrician lady's hairpin in a warm room was theater—deadly theater if you flinched, coin if you stood still.

"Chilblain water," Fabia murmured, close enough that Lucida smelled honeyed Setine wine and crushed myrtle on her breath. "Good. Because my brother Lucius's friends drink carelessly in winter, and Senator Priscus's moralizing Stoic spies love counting kitchen scandals in our house whenever old Emperor Antoninus coughs at Lorium. If one word of chilblains ever walks down the Argiletum stairs, little Tyrian, I won't send watchmen after half-blind Berenice; I'll buy your dead patron's reversion off whoever holds it, open your ribs in my own cellar, and salt the meat. Understand me?"

"Clearly, Domina," Lucida said against the gold point. *(Filing away two names like hot gold coins dropped straight into her pocket: **Senator Priscus**—the name she had never heard Corvus speak, yet now locked forever in her ear alongside **Antoninus at Lorium** and **Fabia's brother Lucius**.)*

Fabia drew the needle-point back from Lucida's skin, turned smoothly to her dressing-table, and picked up not Dorcas's household copper purse, a little embroidered silk bag of personal gaming-money.

"Pay Berenice her twelve denarii, Dorcas," Fabia said over her shoulder, tossing **two extra silver denarii**—eight sesterces of clean holiday door-silver—straight through the candle-light.

Lucida caught both coins in one palm before they touched mosaic.

"Don't send the fat Greek next month," Fabia added, sitting back down before the bronze mirror and sliding the six-inch emerald pin through her twisted red-gold coil with one deft stab. "Send the girl with the blocked nose."

* * *

Coming down off the Caelian ridge after the first watch horn was twice as dangerous as climbing up at dusk, because by the second hour of darkness Rome's holiday wine had turned sour in forty thousand stomachs.

Lucida did not carry Berenice's nine denarii and her own two door-coins together in one fist. Before she stepped ten paces outside the *Domus Ceionia* kitchen postern into the frosty laurel lane, she knelt beside a dry boundary-stone, tucked Fabia's two personal silver denarii under her heel inside her right boot-lining, slipped Berenice's twelve denarii deep inside the knotted waist-string under her woollen apron, and set off at a flat, silent trot down the stepped cut toward the Velia and the **Templum Pacis**.

At the bottom of the Clivus Pullius, where pine-resin torches smoked black in iron wall-rings outside a three-arched wine-thermopolium, holiday riot blocked the cobbles wall to wall. A mule-cart loaded with Sabine firewood had lost an iron lynch-pin in the ruts; round the tilted axle two rival gangs of fullers' apprentices and butcher-slaves in scarlet liberty-caps were pelting each other with turnip-tops, greasy knuckle-bones, and blazing pitch-brands while an exhausted seven-man patrol of *vigiles* stood back under a portico awning letting them break their own heads so long as nobody set a timber shop-front alight.

Lucida flattened her back against a limestone baker's pier to slip behind the tilted cart, willow basket tucked tight against her ribs, right fist closed round the three-sided mattress-bodkin inside her shawl.

"Whoa there, little sigillaria-doll! Pay the bridge-toll in kisses or walnuts!"

Out of the torch-smoke lunged **Gaius Sticho's younger cousin Rufio**, an eighteen-year-old fuller's lout from the next alley over, stinking to heaven of vomited Falernian dregs and boiled sheep-trotters, his felt *pileus* crooked over one swollen eye. He did not recognize her under Myrtale's brown hood; he saw only a narrow fifteen-year-old waist and a purple-lined Caelian delivery basket that smelled rich with crushed lavender and sweet almond paste. His thick, lime-cracked left hand clamped straight onto her basket-rim while his right grabbed for the front of her shawl.

Shout, and four more fullers turned round to join the sport. Pull backward, and the willow handle tore open across the cobbles.

Lucida stepped *inward* under his raised elbow, exactly as Corvus had drilled her with a split reed behind the Tiber brick-clamps when they were twelve, drove her hobnailed boot-heel straight down onto the arch of Rufio's bare sandal-foot until cartilage popped, and pressed the needle-sharp triangular point of the seven-inch iron bodkin half an inch through his woollen tunic right above his bloated bladder.

Rufio froze with his mouth open, hot wine-breath steaming white against her eyebrow.

"Take three fingers off that willow rim before I count two," Lucida whispered under the roar of the street-brawl, keeping her elbow tucked low so not a bystander two paces off saw the iron buried in his belly-wool, "or you piss red wine through three holes until April, fuller."

Rufio's fingers sprang wide off the basket as though the willow were white-hot iron. Before he could curse or bellow for his mates, Lucida spun behind two rearing firewood-mules, vaulted a slick gutter of butcher's slush, and cut sharp left into the quieter colonnaded shadow behind Vespasian's **Temple of Peace**, where spice-merchants and Greek surgeons kept narrow vaulted *tabernae* beneath high brick arches.

Three bronze cupping-bells hung on an iron bracket above the fourth shuttered arch, lit by a single green-flamed tallow cresset.

Inside **Asclepiades of Pergamum**'s twelve-foot surgery, Saturnalia had filled every pine bench along both plastered walls with broken holiday meat. A cross-eyed Thracian *retiarius* trainee from the *Ludus Matutinus* sat straddling a blood-blackened oak stool having seven bronze skin-clips pinched shut across a torn shoulder while biting down on a folded leather strap; beside the door a drunken litter-bearer groaned over three dislocated fingers set in split reed splints; on the back marble table an apprentice boy pounded dried Egyptian squill bulbs and toasted linseed in an iron mortar, filling the low brick vault with sharp, vinegary steam that smelled of garlic, cautery-iron, and old blood.

Asclepiades himself—bald skull gleaming wet under the hanging bronze surgical lamp, oiled leather apron dark from chest to knee, clean short-paired fingernails moving swift and steady as a weaver's shuttle—snipped the knotted linen thread off the Thracian's shoulder with bronze shears and barked without turning round:

"No more broken noses tonight! Go stick your faces in the Meta Sudans snow-trough and come back on the Kalends with silver!"

"It isn't a broken nose, medicus; it's red side-fever on the fifth rib, three watches in, coughing bronze froth," Lucida said clearly from the doorway, stepping straight past the groaning litter-bearer to lay **two bright silver denarii**—Fabia's Caelian door-money from her boot—ringing flat onto the corner of his clean instrument-tray. "And five more silver denarii sit Upstairs on Berenice's marble slab the instant your bronze bells touch his back."

Asclepiades wiped his bronze shears on a boiled linen cloth and turned keen, pouchy grey eyes onto her face. He looked at the clean Caelian silver first, her mud-splashed hem second, and the steady set of her jaw third.

"Berenice the one-eyed Syrian up the Argiletum steps?" Asclepiades grunted. "Since when does that old scorpion spend twenty-eight sesterces on anybody's lungs? Is it a customer who swallowed her hellebore?"

"It's a sixteen-year-old Sabine clerk whose lungs earn thirty sesterces a week when they aren't full of water," Lucida lied smoothly adding a year and doubling Corvus's wage so the Greek saw a repeat-paying client rather than dying fuller's trash. "We have eleven sesterces plus Berenice's four denarii waiting upstairs right now—twenty-seven sesterces in silver and brass, plus these eight makes thirty-five. Take twenty-eight for the climb, the cupping, and a full pint horn of squill-oxymel, and leave me seven for Cretan linseed and oak charcoal. Wait until morning and he drowns, and you get nothing out of the corpse-cart."

Asclepiades's mouth twitched once at the corner. "Seventy-eight stairs on Saturnalia night with every landing slick with vomit, girl. Thirty-two sesterces."

"Twenty-eight," Lucida countered without moving an eyelash, laying two fingers back near the two silver denarii on his tray, "and I carry your bronze-box and linseed sack up all six flights myself while my hands are warm, and send every Caelian tire-woman who asks Berenice for tooth-tincture straight to your arch instead of Euelpistus down by the Forum."

Mentioning **Euelpistus**—Asclepiades's hated Cilician rival three streets west—did what begging never could. Asclepiades snorted through nose-hairs grey with cautery smoke, swept both silver denarii off the tray into his belt-wallet, and jerked his chin at the apprentice.

"Pack the three small bronze *cucurbitulae*, the scarifier, a pint flask of three-year squill vinegar boiled with Attic honey, and four pounds of crushed linseed meal in the felt satchel, boy, then bolt the shutter! Let's see whether this Tyrian mule climbs stairs as fast as she counts silver."

Twenty breaths later they were climbing the pitch-black Argiletum staircase two steps at a time—Asclepiades swinging his shuttered horn lantern ahead to kick sleeping drunks clear of the landings, Lucida hauling his twenty-pound felt instrument bag and Berenice's willow basket up behind him until sweat ran stinging salt into the corners of her mouth. Upstairs she slapped Berenice's twelve Caelian denarii onto the tombstone slab, collected her agreed sixteen back before the old woman's claw could close over the fourth coin, added eleven sesterces from under the loose hearth-brick, and counted the remaining five silver denarii straight into the Pergamene surgeon's clean palm.

By the third watch of the night, Berenice's smoky fourteen-by-eleven garret smelled like a legionary hospital tent.

While Berenice held the double-spouted tallow lamp close and Lucida pinned Corvus's shivering shoulders steady on his right side across the rush-mat, Asclepiades touched burning spirit-soaked lint inside three bell-shaped bronze cupping-glasses (*cucurbitulae*) and clapped them hard along the taut, sweating skin beneath Corvus's left shoulder-blade. Vacuum sucked purple flesh half an inch up into each hot bronze dome as the flame died. Corvus's teeth ground together hard enough to crack enamel; his right hand locked round Lucida's bare forearm until four white nail-crescents sank into her skin; not one cry came past his shut jaw.

"Strong ribs for a starved field-rat," Asclepiades muttered, tapping the middle bronze bell with a fingernail before releasing the suction with a wet *pop* and wiping dark congested blood-blisters clean with warm vinegar-wine. "Fluid hasn't turned to white pus yet; catch him two days later and you'd be hiring flute-players at the Libitina grove. Three spoonfuls of this squill-and-honey oxymel every watch when the cough rises; keep scalding linseed-and-fenugreek poultices strapped across the left ribs until the skin goes lobster-red; water boiled with barley, no wine at all, and don't let him chill between midnight and cockcrow when the sweat breaks, or the lung collapses inward."

Asclepiades packed his bronze bells into oiled felt, pocketed his silver, and went clumping down the dark pine ladders into the fading Saturnalia uproar below.

At the fourth watch, the dead black iron hour before winter dawn when even drunken fullers had collapsed in doorways and the street wagons had thinned to single night-soil carts, the crisis broke.

First came the shaking rigor: teeth chattering so violently Corvus couldn't swallow the sharp squill-syrup off the horn spoon without biting wood, ribs hitching in shallow, tearing gasps under the steaming linseed rag while cold December wind knifed through Berenice's unglazed dormer cracks. Their single fuller's cloak and Corvus's dry tunic piled on top weren't heavy enough; Berenice was dead asleep under her wolf-pelt across the garret with raw wool stuffed in both ears against tooth-ache; and they had only one measure of charcoal left in the pan, barely keeping the poultice-pot warm.

Lucida did not hesitate. She unpinned her outer brown woollen shift in the dark, stripped down to her thin linen chest-band and loin-cloth, crawled directly under the fuller's cloak onto the narrow barley-straw, and pulled Corvus's freezing, trembling length tight against the bare front of her own fifteen-year-old body: chest to his unpoulticed right ribs, thighs locked over his icy shins, both arms wrapped hard round his wet neck so his forehead buried itself into the warm hollow beneath her left collarbone, right over the dark olive-pip mark.

For half an hour he shook like a wet hound pulled out of January ice, muttering broken scraps of Sabine Latin and surveyor's numbers into her skin:

*"Eleven feet to Number Three... tell Lucida, no, tell Vespera the plank's greased... forty thousand bipedales, Phileros, count the stamps backward..."*

Lucida stiffened half a breath in the dark at the name *Phileros* and *forty thousand bipedales*, because Corvus had never told her about any Greek named Phileros or forty thousand bricks; he had told her he was sorting papyrus scroll-tags for an old bookseller on the Argiletum.

Her hand paused behind his burning wet hair. Down near his rolled outer tunic under his elbow, her bare knee brushed something hard and flat stitched inside the split waist-band: six round silver edges, unmistakable to fingers that counted copper in the dark every night.

*He kept back six denarii,* she thought in the pitch dark, listening to the rattle in his inflamed lung half an inch from her breastbone. *And he isn't working for a bookseller. He walked straight into some senator's brick-audit behind my back to keep me off the board.*

Hot salt anger made her back teeth ache for ten breaths; right behind it came animal dread as another racking cough convulsed his ribs against hers and hot sweat burst out across his neck, temples, and chest all at once, drenching both their skins wet as river-water.

The fever was breaking.

She grabbed the warm linen towel without letting cold air under the cloak, wiped his streaming face and throat dry so the chill wouldn't strike inward as the Greek warned, tilted two horn-spoonfuls of sharp squill-honey between his parted lips when his jaw relaxed, and pulled him straight back against her bare ribs, tucking the rough wool tight under both their chins.

His breathing slowed, still shallow and catching slightly at the bottom of the left lung, yet steady now, dry and temperate under her cheek instead of kiln-hot. Half-conscious between sleep and waking, Corvus shifted his bruised right hand until his scarred thumb found her shoulder-blade under the cloak.

"Vespera?" he whispered hoarsely, using the child-name nobody inside Rome had ever heard. "Thought I heard... Caelian bells."

"Go back to sleep, liar," Lucida whispered into his damp black hair, holding him tighter against her skin while grey winter light began to smear the dormer gutter outside and frost formed white along the rue-pots. Neither did she tell him about Ceionia Fabia's gold emerald pin at her throat, nor the bruised slave-girl weeping behind silk screens, nor whose Caelian silver had just bought the breath moving in and out of his lungs. Rome was already teaching both of them how to count with one hand under the table; yet as dawn broke grey over the Subura tiles and Corvus's heart beat slow and solid against her ribs, she swore by her dead mother's salt that whatever doors waited ahead, no plank, sickness, or senator would take him into the dark without her.
