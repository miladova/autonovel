# Chapter I — Ex Praediis

*Ides of October, AD 156 · Corvus*

Before the sun cleared the Sabine ridges, the river smelled of wet ash and dead lime.

Corvus crouched ankle-deep in the blue-grey trench at the lower edge of the clay-pit, four fingers of his left hand hooked around the cold brass plumb-bob in his tunic pocket, his right hand holding an iron kiln-rake steady across his knee. Above the reed-bank, mist hung thick enough to hide the ox-path from the drying sheds, though sound carried through river-damp as if across a cistern: the knock of a wooden trace-bar against an axle, the wheeze of a blown mule, and the flat iron cough of Gaius Ummidius Scaeva clearing phlegm into the sedge.

"Count the sheds first," Scaeva said up on the bank. His voice had the gravel of twenty years in the Praetorian cohorts before Publius Vedius Pollio bought his discharge and gave him a vine-stick tipped with four ounces of lead. "Do not fire the thatch until the tallies come out of the hut. The old bitch's executors want every stamped bipedalis accounted for before the auction on the Kalends, and I am not paying four sesterces a brick out of my own purse because you clumsy bastards burned the pine racks."

Beside Corvus under the overhang of cut clay, Lucida did not shiver, though her wool shift was soaked black to mid-calf and the October mud had hardened grey between her toes. She had their oilskin bag strapped flat against her ribs beneath an old fuller's cloak two sizes too broad in the shoulder. Her jaw was set slightly forward, her dark eyes fixed on the gap in the osiers where the towpath bent toward the Milvian Bridge. Under her left collarbone, hidden beneath coarse undyed wool, sat the dark mark shaped like an olive-pip; on her fingers, even in half-light, the nail-beds showed purple-brown from the walnut-hull and alkanet dye she had boiled two nights ago for the barge-men's woollens.

She touched two fingers to Corvus's wrist: once for the man coming down the plank-walk, twice for the dog behind him. Not a word. When you had lived since seven years old within stone's throw of the kilns, speech near strangers was coin thrown into the Tiber.

Corvus shifted his heel half a Roman foot backward until his sole found the buried oak sleeper he had laid there in July when his father's grave-mound settled. Five feet from the sleeper to the pug-mill trough; eleven feet from the trough to the mouth of Number Three kiln, where they had banked brushwood and olive-pomace at the third watch so the residual heat would keep the green tiles from cracking in the frost. His father used to beat that habit into his knuckles with a boxwood measuring-rod whenever the wine turned sour in him: *Never enter a yard without knowing its paces, Lucius; a man who guesses distances dies poor or dies indoors.* Gaius Cassius Corvus had known the exact width of every ditch between Crustumerium and the third milestone, and still died face-down under forty cartloads of slumped winter marl owing three thousand two hundred sesterces on a waxed tablet, so the maxim had holes in it. The pacing stayed.

"Corvus's boy!" someone shouted up by the drying-racks. That was Rufus, the cross-eyed tally-clerk who had come upriver from the Emporium last month when the bailiffs changed. "Ho, Sabine! Bring out the wooden press-frame and the new consular dies! Scaeva says if the girl's inside, fetch her out by the braid!"

Nobody answered from the hut, because the hut had held nothing since moonset except an overturned stool, three cracked cooking-pots, and half a bladder of rancid lard rubbed across the threshold to turn the mastiff's nose.

Up on the bank the dog snuffled at the grease, whined once, and fell to licking the sill. A boot landed in its ribs. Through a rent in the fog Corvus saw orange pitch-light flare against wet reed-thatch: one of Scaeva's three hired river-men—the broad-bellied Syrian called Mousa who worked the Sandapila wharf when debts were slow—stooping with a sulfur-spill at a clay lamp to light a tarred twist of hemp.

"Empty," Mousa called back. "Straw's cold. The little cunt's bolted."

"Look behind the tile-stacks," Scaeva answered. "A Junian Latin without travel-seal doesn't sprout feathers between dusk and cockcrow, and Vedius paid thirty gold pieces for the old dyer's reversionary chest. Find her, and leave her teeth in—she can still work an alum-vat."

Lucida's breath did not quicken against Corvus's shoulder; instead she reached into her girdle, drew out the curved neck-shard of a broken Spanish oil-amphora whose fracture she had honed the day before on sandstone until its edge could shave bristle off pork rind, and laid her thumb along the unglazed clay spine.

Corvus caught her elbow—not to stop her, only to angle them two paces left where the kiln flue breathed warm wood-gas across the trench and killed human scent. Inside the oilskin bag against her ribs lay four things worth both their lives and three men's necks besides: his own cedar birth-diptych (*tabula professionis*) proving Lucius Cassius Corvus, son of Gaius, of the Crustumina tribe, born free under the consulship of Titus Hoenius Severus and Marcus Peducaeus Stloga; a split terracotta master-impression from these very yards (*OPUS DOLIARE EX PRAEDIIS DOMITIAE LUCILLAE*); six waxed pine leaves recording four thousand roof-tiles and two-foot *bipedales* stamped three months ahead with next year's false workshop numbers; and the heavy round bronze *signaculum* die itself, cold as river-iron, stolen off Rufus's counting-table an hour before dawn while Rufus snored with his mouth open in sour Sabine wine.

Domitia Lucilla—mother to Marcus Aurelius Caesar, richest brick-mistress on the Tiber—had died before the spring floods dropped. For six months since, every sub-contractor, lease-farmer, and senatorial jackal along twelve miles of river clay had been tearing strips off the estate before the new trustees finished counting kilns. Scaeva's master Vedius Pollio had leased this strip through a straw-man, double-stamped imperial brick-loads for private Subura building sites, and meant this morning to burn the shacks, blame the dead surveyor's arrears for the missing tallies, and cart Lucida into Rome under an inherited patronal claim before anyone asked why dead men's kilns were still smoking.

Planks groaned overhead.

Mousa came down the slick timber gangway into the pit alone, holding the pitch-torch high in his left hand so sparks spat into the puddles, an ash-wood cudgel balanced in his right. He wore heavy hobnailed *caligae* laced halfway up scarred shins, no cloak, a leather apron stiff with dried river-silt. At the foot of the plank he paused, squinting past the pug-mill where an iron chain hung from the capstan beam.

"Boy?" Mousa said, almost friendly, dropping into dockside Greek—the flat Antioch vowels Lucida's mother had used when bargaining over madder root. "Come out if you're under the shed. Scaeva's only hot about the bronze die. Give back the die and the girl, and he knocks fifty sesterces off your old man's bond. Where are you going to run in October without shoes? Rome? Rome eats country puppies before the third hour."

Corvus looked at Mousa's knees. When a heavy man stepped off wet pine onto greased blue brick-clay, his weight went onto his back heel to keep his hobnails from skating.

Corvus let Mousa take two steps past the capstan. Then he hooked the iron kiln-rake low—not at the skull, which made a noise and glanced off bone, but flat behind Mousa's right ankle—and hauled backward with both shoulders braced against the trench wall.

Mousa's heels flew toward the fog. He hit the clay on his tailbone with a wet *whuff* that drove the Greek back down his gullet, the pitch-torch spinning sideways into the puddle where it hissed black without quite dying. Before Mousa could roll onto an elbow and roar, Corvus was up out of the cut, swinging the five-foot surveyor's iron measuring-link he had coiled under the rake-head, bringing the knotted brass ten-foot ring down across the bridge of Mousa's nose and mouth. Cartilage crunched like a trodden snail. Blood jumped black in the dying red sputter of pitch. Mousa grabbed blindly for Corvus's ankle, fingers astonishingly strong, hooking wool and skin together; Lucida stepped in from the left without a sound, drove the pointed heel of her amphora shard straight through the meat of Mousa's forearm between radius and ulna, twisted once until his fingers sprang open, and snatched up his dropped cudgel before the groan cleared his split teeth.

Up on the bank the mastiff barked—twice, ragged, furious.

"Mousa!" Scaeva's voice snapped sharp from the drying-sheds forty paces uphill. Boots thudded on packed turf. "Tiberius! Down the ditch—someone's by Number Three!"

"Go," Corvus breathed—not even breath, just lip against Lucida's cold ear.

They did not run up the bank where three men with vine-sticks held high ground. They went straight through the low brick arch of Number Three kiln's stoke-alley, ducking under the scorching sandstone lintel where banked olive-pressings glowed dull cherry behind loose firebricks. Corvus planted the heel of his iron rake against the dry key-brick holding the draught-damper, leaned all seven and a half stone of his starved fifteen-year-old frame into it, and popped the plug.

A lungful of white ash, choked carbon smoke, and dry olive-char belched backward out of the firebox straight into the trench mouth just as the mastiff bounded down the gangway ahead of the second bailiff. The dog hit the drifting ash-cloud and burst into frantic sneezing yelps; behind it Tiberius cursed by Hercules and shielded his eyes from stinging hot cinders.

By then Corvus and Lucida were out the leeward ash-vent, sliding belly-flat down the willow-root embankment to the Tiber's edge where an empty flat-bottomed reed-skiff—used by turf-cutters to mend fish-weirs—lay tethered by a rotten rushes-rope to an alder stump.

Lucida sliced the rush-knot with the bloody amphora shard before Corvus had both knees inside. He shoved off with the ash cudgel against gravel. Current took the stern immediately: autumn water swollen brown from three days' rain up in Umbria, carrying willow leaves, bloated sheep-gut from the upstream slaughter-yards, and rafts of yellow foam.

Fifty yards behind them on the bank, Scaeva burst out of the willow scrub, cloak thrown back, the stump of his missing left ear white with chill against his purpled cheek. He did not waste breath screaming threats; he dropped to one knee at the water's margin, squinted through thinning mist until he fixed Corvus's face and Lucida's dark braid clearly in memory, and raised two fingers to his own throat—a soldier's tally-mark, neat as chalk on a board.

Corvus dug the cudgel into the brown eddy to keep the bow pointed mid-stream toward the six stone arches of the Milvian Bridge looming out of the fog ahead.

Lucida sat cross-legged in the wet bilge opposite him, wiping Mousa's blood off her knuckles onto reed-straw, then checking the oilskin pouch under her shift. Her chest rose and fell fast, ribs visible at the open V of her coarse neck-hem, breath smoking white. For ten heartbeats she studied the smear of blood on Corvus's shin where Mousa's nails had torn him, then looked up into his face.

"You hit him with your father's ten-foot chain," she said in low Latin. "If you bent the brass ring, you'll cry about it until December."

Corvus checked the brass ring with his scarred right thumb. It had a fresh dent and one grey hair stuck in dark grease. "Ring's straight," he said. "His nose wasn't."

A sudden, fierce grin split Lucida's mouth—brief as flint-spark, gone before the shadow of the Milvian Bridge swallowed them in cold travertine gloom and spat them out on the Romeward side, where the Via Flaminia ran straight as a spear-shaft between painted marble tombs toward the smoke of a million cooking-fires.

* * *

They ditched the reed-skiff half a mile above the customs boom at the Navalia, because river-watchmen asked questions of barefoot fifteen-year-olds in unbranded boats, whereas nobody at a land-gate looked twice at two dirty country brats walking behind an Umbrian turnip-wagon on a holiday morning.

Or almost nobody.

Three hundred paces outside the Porta Flaminia, where the double file of senatorial tombs narrowed between dusty cypress hedges and roadside wine-booths, the tax-farmers of the *ansarium* market-toll had stretched a painted pine pole across half the paving stones. For three sesterces—one worn brass piece and two scarred dupondii unpicked from Lucida's hem with her thumbnail—an old freedman carter with a goiter swelling like a winter gourd under his chin had let them sling his spare willow turnip-baskets over their shoulders and walk beside his mud-caked ox-yoke.

"Keep your chins in the greens and let me do the cursing," the old carter muttered out of the side of his mouth as the queue inched toward the toll-table. His breath smelled of raw leek and rotten molars. "If the publican's clerk pokes the straw with his iron spit, don't flinch. Last month they found six bladders of untaxed Campanian perfume stuffed inside a dead sow's belly, and now every gate-bastard thinks he's Praetorian Prefect."

At the pine barrier sat a fat freedman *vilicus* under a striped awning, an abacus of grooved bronze across his knees, while two scarred Illyrian assistants in leather aprons thrust three-foot iron probing-rods deep into hay-loads, oil-panniers, and wool-bales to make sure no silk, spice, or unrecorded slave-child entered Caesar's customs boundary gratis.

The nearer Illyrian stepped up to the goitered carter's oxen, spat betel-brown wine-lees onto the basalt flags, and rammed his iron rod twice through the heaped purple-top turnips. The tip grated against oak floorboards. Then his pale eyes slid down to Corvus's torn shin—caked black now with river-silt over Mousa's nail-furrows—and across to Lucida, whose coarse fuller's cloak hung crooked over the flat bulge of the oilskin bag.

"Two asses on the roots, grandfather," the Illyrian said, resting the cold iron spit horizontally across Corvus's collarbone so he could not step past the ox-rump. "And what are these two? Untaxed field-stock going down to the Saepta dealers? Young Syrian bitch like that fetches sixteen hundred sesterces clean on the block if her teeth meet."

Corvus felt the blood drop out of his ears. If the clerk made them strip the fuller's cloak at the toll-table and found a thirty-pound bronze imperial brick-die stamped with Domitia Lucilla's dead name inside a runaway Junian Latin's shift, they would never even see the inside of the city wall; they would sit in ankle-irons in the Flaminian guardhouse before Scaeva finished his morning wine.

Before Corvus could reach for his citizen birth-tablet—which would only make a gate-clerk ask why a Crustumina citizen boy was hauling turnips barefoot beside an unsealed girl—Lucida shifted her basket off her hip, planted it straight down on the Illyrian's booted instep hard enough to make him grunt, and let go a burst of foul, singsong Sabine country-Latin thick enough to curdle goat's milk.

"Sixteen hundred sesterces? Tell him to pay you half on the nail, Lucius, and I'll ride into Rome in a cushioned litter instead of carrying this old skinflint's wormy turnips since the second watch!" She yanked down the collar of her rough shift half a thumb's breadth—not toward the oilskin bag, higher, showing the ugly purple-brown alkanet dye-stain smeared deliberately up her throat and ear-lobe like the weeping crust of fuller's scrofula—and coughed wetly toward the Illyrian's chin. "Go on, touch it, handsome! The fuller at Prima Porta docked my brother four asses yesterday because my neck-sores dripped into his white wool, and now the toll-man wants to marry me!"

The Illyrian snatched his iron probing-rod back as if her collar held scorpions, wiping the back of his hand across his mouth. Even the fat *vilicus* behind the bronze abacus waved two fingers in disgust without looking up from his wax roll.

"Two asses on the cart, take your rotten turnips and your scrofulous brats out of the road before you block Senator Bradua's outriders!" the *vilicus* barked. "Move the pole!"

The goitered carter flicked two copper asses into the wooden dish, cracked his goad over the near ox's horn, and rumbled through the stone arch of the Porta Flaminia. Twenty paces inside the gate, where the shadow of the old tufa wall gave way to roaring sun, the old man glanced sideways at Lucida's neck—where the purple-brown smear had already dried harmlessly into walnut-and-alkanet stain—and gave a wheezing, phlegm-thick chuckle deep in his gourd-throat.

"Scrofula," the carter said. "By Pollux. Girl, don't waste yourself on turnips. Buy a booth in the Vicus Tuscus; you'll own the street before your wisdom teeth come through."

"Keep the three sesterces, grandfather," Corvus told him, lifting both willow baskets back onto the tailboard as the wagon slowed behind a line of marble-sledges. "And if a one-eared man in a brown cloak asks at the gate-stalls whether you carried two passengers from the bridge, tell him we got off at the crossroads toward Tibur."

The old man squinted once at Corvus's bruised knuckles, once at the dent on the surveyor's brass ring peeping from Corvus's belt, and nodded without asking names. In Rome, curiosity was an expensive hobby for men with goiters.

It was past the fourth hour now. Sun had burned every shred of mist off the river-meadows, turning the sky over the Pincian gardens a hard, enameled blue that made every gilded bronze roof-ornament on the hills stab the eyes.

Corvus had seen Rome twice before from three miles off: a brown smudge of tile-smoke and white temple pediments beyond the river bends, never from inside its skin. Nothing his dead father had ever muttered over wine prepared a boy's ears for the noise.

Even with heavy freight wagons barred since sunrise, the Flaminian Way inside the tombs roared like a weir in flood. Thousands upon thousands of feet moved inward toward the Campus Martius: fullers in chalk-stiff tunics stinking of stale piss, Syrian litter-bearers in scarlet headbands trotting eight abreast with cedar poles grooved deep into calloused collarbones, country *ingenui* down from Tibur in scratchy holiday wool, painted boy-prostitutes from the Vicus Tuscus shading depilated cheeks with green parasols, scarred veterans of Antoninus's Mauretanian levies spitting olive-stones at stray dogs, amulet-sellers rattling bronze phalluses on leather strings for luck at the races.

And around all of it, framing the surging grease and wool and garlic-breath of the crowd, stood stone on a scale that made Corvus's surveyor's eye ache behind his brow. Porticoes a quarter-mile long rested on columns cut whole from single trunks of grey Egyptian granite and blood-streaked Phrygian marble; giant travertine water-arches strode overhead on seventy-foot piers, sweating green moss at the mortar joints while whole rivers ran captive above men's hats; bronze four-horse chariots crowned triumphal arches nobody underneath even glanced up to read; and rising to their left on its turf mound beside the river stood the great cylindrical tomb of Augustus choked in dark cypresses, while downstream across the water gleamed the newer, bigger drum Hadrian had built for himself and the Antonines, bristling with marble statues white as peeled bone.

"Shut your mouth, Sabine," Lucida murmured at his shoulder as they slipped under a portico hung with faded red awnings, "or a pickpocket will drop three dice down your gullet and charge you rent."

Yet her own eyes were wide, darting not at the pediments—she cared nothing for dead emperors' roofs—but at the women's hems, the gold hair-nets glinting inside open litters, the fat sausages hissing in lard over portable iron braziers, the money-changers' folding tables where bright silver *denarii* with old Antoninus's laurelled nose stacked twenty high beside little brass balance-scales. Corvus watched her take the measure of a silk-sleeved freedwoman buying roasted thrushes for four asses apiece without blinking; he could almost hear the iron abacus clicking behind Lucida's forehead. *Four asses for three mouthfuls of bird-bone. Two hundred thrushes make a winter coat.*

Press of shoulders carried them out from beneath the colonnade onto the open turf edge of the **Campus Martius**, north of the Trigarium horse-grounds, where eighty thousand people had wedged themselves forty deep along low pine barriers.

Corvus pulled Lucida up onto the stepped plinth of an old Republican boundary-altar warm in the midday sun so they would not go under the heels.

"Ides of October," a gap-toothed cobbler standing beside them said cheerfully, chewing roasted lupin-beans out of his tunic fold and spitting the skins onto the travertine. "First time in the City, country mice? Watch the right-hand trace-horse on the inner turn. My brother-in-law put eight sesterces on Polydamas's bays out of the Subura ward-purse. If the bay takes the outer rein and wins, we eat pork-udder tonight and nail the bastard's head above the Mamilian Tower before lamp-light!"

Out on the packed sand and turf course—no marble spina here like the Circus Maximus south of the Palatine, only ancient oak posts bound in bronze and a brick altar smoking with pine-resin and salt meal to Mars—four two-horse *bigae* stood trembling behind a stretched white chalked rope. Even at a hundred and twenty paces Corvus smelled horse-lather, crushed wormwood rubbed on wheel-hubs, and raw panic. The drivers wore short belted tunics dyed rust-red and leek-green, leather helmets strapped tight under their chins, curved knives tucked at their ribs to slice the body-reins if an axle splintered and dragged them.

A magistrate in a purple-bordered *toga praetexta* dropped a white linen *mappa*.

The rope snapped flat. Four pairs of chests lunged into leather collars at once; sand fountained backward in stinging arcs; eighty thousand throats let go a single tearing roar that vibrated inside Corvus's empty stomach.

Around the far post they went in brown dust, wheels hub-to-hub with three fingers of air between spinning iron tires. On the first turn an outside chestnut stumbled, slewed sideways, and took the trailing chariot-box across its hocks: wood cracked like dry pine in a kiln, a horse screamed high as a scalded girl, and the driver cut himself loose just in time to roll twice into the sand with his shoulder laid open to the red muscle while the remaining three *bigae* thundered past his heels without checking stride.

Lucida gripped the back of Corvus's tunic with one fist, leaning forward on bare toes, chin raised, lips parted. Not squeamish; she had watched oxen butchered since she could walk. What held her transfixed was the crowd on the wooden benches opposite—men in narrow purple equestrian stripes and spotless white *togae* leaning over the rail beside scarred butchers and pimps, all howling the same filthy camp-Latin at the bay pair surging half a length clear on the final straight, the right-hand trace-horse stretching its dark wet neck, nostrils flared crimson, foam flying off its iron bit as it carried the left-hand yoke-mate home across the chalk line.

The crowd's roar changed pitch, from gambling frenzy to something older, thicker, half-drunk on sun and iron.

Attendants of the *flamen Martialis* ran out in tucked-up white wool kilts before the winning bay had even stopped heaving. Nobody garlanded it with roses to lead it back to oats. Two bull-necked *victimarii* naked to the waist caught its bridle-rings on either side of the smoking turf altar; a priest sprinkled salted *mola salsa* grit and sour wine between its twitching ears while the horse rolled white-rimmed eyes at the smell of old blood in the grass; a third man stepped in from the flank with an archaic leaf-bladed spear—Mars's spear—and drove two feet of iron straight behind the bay's shoulder into the great vessels above its heart.

The horse dropped to its foreknees with a sound halfway between a cough and a trumpet-blast, shuddered along its glistening ribs, and rolled onto its side in the sand. Before its hind hoof stopped kicking, a butcher's cleaver flashed once at the croup: the dripping tail was hacked free at the root and handed straight to a waiting relay of fleet-footed temple-runners, who sprinted southeast across the Campus toward the Forum Romanum, running flat-out so fresh hot drops would still fall from the hair onto the hearth of the Regia before the blood clotted.

At the altar, the broader cleaver fell three times through neck-bone.

Up came the bay's severed head by the forelock, garlanded hastily with a wreath of hard baked loaves—*that no winter bin go empty*—and before the priest had stepped three paces back, the pine crowd-barriers cracked down the middle like rotten laths.

Two walls of men surged onto the sand from opposite sides of the field: from the south and west, young bloods, shop-freedmen, and shrine-wardens of the **Sacra Via** wearing yellow ribbons twisted around their biceps, fighting to carry the horse's head back to nail on the King's House in the Forum; from the east, pouring out of the narrow Argiletum throat, three hundred grease-armed fullers, cobblers, brick-carriers, and tavern-brawlers of the **Subura**, bellowing *"Turris Mamilia! Subura! Subura!"*

The gap-toothed cobbler beside Corvus vaulted off the altar-plinth straight into the press. Within five breaths the orderly holiday margin dissolved into a churning mill-race of elbows, cudgels, flying dust, and spilled lupin-sellers' trays.

Corvus caught Lucida's hand to pull her backward toward the Saepta colonnade—and stopped dead, cold water dropping straight down his spine.

Thirty paces left along the edge of the portico, standing three steps up on a travertine doorway above the crowd's shoulders so he could scan every young head bobbing toward the Argiletum exit, stood **Gaius Ummidius Scaeva**.

He had changed his mud-spattered cloak for dry brown wool; behind him waited two bull-shouldered urban runners wearing Vedius Pollio's braided leather wrist-straps, plus Rufus the cross-eyed tally-clerk, squinting frantically across the sunlit gap where turnip-carts and onlookers bottlenecked into the narrow street. Scaeva had figured it out before noon: two broke country rats with stolen brick-dies and a Tyrian mother's name could only go to earth in two places in Rome—Trans-Tiberim across the bridges, which he had already set men watching, or down the Argiletum into the Subura's warren. And he was standing directly over the throat of the funnel.

Rufus turned his crossed eye toward the Republican boundary-altar. For half an instant, sunlight flashed off the wet bronze *signaculum* corner pressing through the worn seam of Lucida's oilskin bag. Rufus's mouth opened; his arm lifted to point.

"Down!" Corvus snarled, yanking Lucida off the plinth not away from the riot, where the portico steps stood bare and open to Scaeva's runners, but **straight sideways into the sweating, howling wedge of Suburamen** surging east around the bleeding horse-head.

* * *

Inside the Subura phalanx there was no sky, only armpits, sour wine, fuller's-earth dust, flying spittle, and drumming hobnails.

A giant lime-burner with his nose broken flat across both cheeks had the bay horse's severed head hugged to his leather chest like a wineskin, loaves crushing into crimson horsehair while six Sacra Via ward-champions clawed over backs and skulls to wrench the bridle-strap out of his fists. Fists cracked against cheekbones; somebody went down under forty pairs of soles and was hauled up by his hair screaming curses by Bellona's tits; warm horse-blood smeared across Corvus's shoulder as the crowd-press slammed him and Lucida chest-to-back between two bellowing fullers reeking of fermented chamber-pots.

"Stay inside their elbows!" Corvus shouted into Lucida's ear, wrapping his left arm around her ribs over the oilskin bag while using his right shoulder like a wedge to keep them moving at the exact speed of the Subura vanguard as it battered southeast through the Pallacina alleys toward the Forum Transitorium.

Behind them, forty yards back and fighting cross-grain against five hundred furious ward-partisans, Scaeva's two wrist-strapped heavies tried to bull inward from the portico steps. One shoved a Subura cobbler aside by the face: a fatal mistake on the Ides of October. Three leather-aproned apprentices took him for a Sacra Via ringer and laid him flat across an overturned fig-barrow with three fists and an oak stool-leg before Scaeva could whistle them clear.

Still Scaeva came on along the higher shop-ledges, hopping from stepping-stone to threshold with the grim, unhurried economy of an old camp-sergeant who had hunted Dacian hill-runners through worse brush than this. Every time the alley widened where booksellers' stalls hugged the shadow of Minerva's colonnade, Corvus caught a glimpse of that scarred, earless profile gaining five paces along the wall-hugging flank.

Ahead rose the **Firewall**.

Corvus had heard barge-men talk of it over stew-pots; seeing it from the narrow stone canyon of the Argiletum made his chest contract. Behind the gleaming white Carrara marble columns of Augustus's Forum stood a sheer, windowless cliff of dark grey *lapis Gabinus* blocks, course upon cyclopean course rising a hundred Roman feet straight into the afternoon sky, banded by projecting travertine stringcourses like iron hoops around a giant's chest. Three smoky travertine arches pierced its base: the central *Arcus Pantani* choked solid now with the screaming tug-of-war over the horse's head, the narrower postern step-way on the north jammed with fleeing peddlers, a donkey wedged sideways under panniers of cracked vinegar-jars, and two Sacra Via toughs ambushing stragglers on the wet flagstones.

Corvus's eyes measured the jam in one heartbeat: the main arch would not move ten feet in a quarter-hour; Scaeva was twenty paces behind the jammed donkey on the right-hand cobbles and closing fast, leaded vine-stick raised; to the left, under a dripping brick butcher's-vault bridged overhead by a timber balcony, ran a five-foot drainage alley choked with slick offal-water that curved past the Firewall's northern spur straight onto the Clivus Suburanus.

They dove for the drainage cut.

A Sacra Via youth—nineteen or twenty, perfumed hair, yellow ribbon knotted round a muscular upper arm, drunk on Falernian and holiday blood—lunged out of the doorway thinking Corvus carried a Subura ward-purse under his arm, and grabbed Lucida's fuller's cloak with both hands, ripping the coarse fibula-pin clean through the wool so the oilskin bag swung exposed on its leather strap across her breastbone.

"What's in the sack, sewer-girl?" the yellow-ribboned youth laughed, twisting the strap round his fist while kicking Corvus's cut shin hard enough to drop him to one knee on slick sheep-paunch. "Let's see what Subura thieves—"

Corvus drove his head sideways into the youth's groin, clamping teeth through fine white wool into inner thigh until the dandy shrieked high as a gelded pig. Above him Lucida did not pull backward against the twisted strap; pulling backward choked you. She stepped *in* until her forehead touched the youth's perfumed chin, brought her honed amphora shard up flat against the leather strap—not cutting *her* strap, dragging the jagged terracotta edge hard across the back tendons of his knuckles—and when his blood-slick fingers spasmed open, drove her bare knee into his cods and shoved him backward onto the cobbles beside the panicked donkey's thrashing iron hooves.

"Up!" she snapped, hauling Corvus off the slime by his collar with bruising strength before Scaeva cleared the overturned vinegar-panniers ten yards behind.

Together they sprinted beneath the dark vault of the Firewall spur, crossing in six strides out of white marble Caesar-Rome into the other city.

Light dropped by half the instant they cleared the arch. On either side of the climbing **Clivus Suburanus**, six- and seven-storey tenements bulged outward on rotting oak corbels over the street like cliffs about to kiss, blocking the afternoon sun to a thin blue knife-slit overhead where wet washing—menstrual rags, patched soldier-tunics, yellowed fuller-cloths—dripped slow grey beads onto passers-by. The smell hit like an open palm: boiling goat-tripe, fried garlic, charcoal smoke pouring black out of windowless third-floor shutters because poor folk had no flues, sour *posca*, warm whore-perfume of crushed iris and cheap tallow from curtained ground-floor *cellae* scratched with charcoal prices (*FELICLA · ASSES II*), and under everything the deep, ancient ammonia-and-shit breath of forty thousand packed bodies living six to a room above shops that never shut.

Behind them through the arch, a roaring wave of triumph erupted: the Suburamen had torn the bay horse's head clear of the last Sacra Via blockers and came pouring up the Clivus around Corvus and Lucida, tossing baked-loaf crumbs in the air, pounding copper kettles, kissing bruised knuckles, sweeping the two fifteen-year-olds twenty alleys deep into the tangled hillside maze past the Vicus Patricius turn before Scaeva could even fight his way through the celebratory crush at the arch-mouth.

Halfway up a narrow stepped lane behind a smoky coppersmith's forge, where hammer-blows on bronze cauldrons drowned all talk and greasy black soot hung in the doorway, Lucida yanked Corvus sideways into a dark stairwell stinking of pickled cabbage and cat-piss.

For a long time neither spoke. Outside on the bright narrow steps, men went streaming uphill shouting *"Mamilia! Mamilia!"* after the bleeding horse-head; a woman leaned out a fourth-floor shutter above them, emptied a bucket of grey lentil-water onto the cobbles with a warning cry of *"Aqua!"* half a heartbeat too late, and laughed coarsest Greek when a passing muleteer cursed her dead mother's womb.

In the dark under the stair, Corvus leaned the back of his skull against damp tuff-stone and slid slowly down until he sat on his heels. His shin throbbed hot where Mousa's nails had furrowed it, and dried bay-horse blood had stiffened the wool over his collarbone into black crust.

Lucida dropped down beside him on the bottom step, knees drawn up, untying the oilskin bag with steady, stained fingers. One by one she touched its contents in the gloom: cedar birth-tablet; six waxed pine tally-leaves; heavy bronze *signaculum* die; and the cracked round terracotta master-impression from *Figlinae Subortanae*, already split three-quarters across its middle where it had hit the skiff-rib at dawn, bearing in raised backward letters around a carved pine-cone:

*EX PR DOMITIAE LUCILLAE · PAETIN ET APRONIAN COS*

She turned the cracked clay disc in both palms, braced her thumbs on either side of the fault-line, and snapped it cleanly in two.

Dust drifted onto her bare knees. She held out the right-hand crescent—*DOMITIAE LUCILLAE*—and dropped it into Corvus's palm, right over the pale half-moon scar on his thumb knuckle. The other half she slid flat inside her shift beside the remaining nine coins.

"Beroe's shop is above the third fullery past the Nymphaeum lane, if the old witch isn't dead," Lucida said, voice hoarse from dust and running, rubbing her nose with the back of her wrist. "My mother sent her two jars of Tyrian lees the summer before the cough took her. She'll rob us blind on straw-space, and if she smells silver she'll cut our purses while we sleep, so let me count out the asses before we knock."

Corvus closed his fingers around the rough baked clay until its broken edge bit skin. Outside the stairwell arch, two Subura street-whores in saffron shifts no thicker than onion-skin leaned against the coppersmith's doorpost sharing a clay cup of heated wine, laughing as one flicked a louse off the other's oiled shoulder into the gutter; farther up the hill, someone nailed Mars's dead war-horse above the tower beam to a thunder of stamping boots.

"Three thousand two hundred sesterces on my father's wax," Corvus said quietly, looking past the saffron shifts at the narrow strip of blue sky wedged sixty feet up between leaning tile-eaves. "And thirty gold pieces Scaeva says Vedius paid for your mother's chest."

"Six thousand two hundred, then," Lucida said, standing up and shaking turnip-dirt out of her skirt-hem with two brisk snaps. She looked down at him in the gloom—fifteen years old, barefoot, Mousa's dried blood blackening under her fingernails, eyes bright and utterly without self-pity. "Plus two pairs of shoes, and supper. Get up, Corvus. Nobody pays rent sitting on the bottom step."
