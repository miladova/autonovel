# Chapter VI — The Pear-Knife

*Fourth Day Before the Nones of March, AD 157 · Lucida*

Behind the great vaulted warehouses of the Emporium rose a mountain nobody had built by plan: a hundred feet high, half a mile round the skirt, composed entirely of fifty million smashed Spanish olive-oil amphorae stacked shard upon curving terracotta shard and dusted white every week with stinging quicklime so the rotting oil soaked into the clay wouldn't poison the southern wind across the Aventine.

Men called it **Mons Testaceus**—Potsherd Hill—and at fifteen Lucida loved it better than all the marble temples in the Forum, because marble temples only took copper out of poor folk's pockets, whereas broken Spanish clay paid thirty percent on a morning's walk if a girl got down to the travertine river-wharf before the customs clerks finished their breakfast pulse.

She stood on the slick limestone quay below the **Porticus Aemilia** in the raw March wind, wearing a stout brown woollen cloak pinned with brass and real hobnailed calfskin boots—bought out of Corvus's steady Caelian copying-silver—watching eight river-haulers (*saccarii*) swing fat, globular two-hundred-pound Baetican oil jars off a flat-bottomed Ostian tow-barge onto a bed of river-sand.

Every jar carried black brush-painted *tituli picti* on its neck naming the net oil weight, the Guadalquivir estate, the shipper, and the Corduba fiscal control mark. Once the state *annona* measurers decanted clean oil into sunken warehouse *dolia* inside the horrea, they smashed the empty clay bellies on the spot with iron mauls—because oil-soaked terracotta turned rancid in summer heat and couldn't be refilled—and mule-carts hauled the shards up the switchback ramp onto Potsherd Hill.

Except three jars out of every hundred cracked slightly along a lower rib during the tow upstream from Ostia behind straining ox-teams, weeping thick golden-green oil into their straw rope-jackets. State clerks refused "weepers" as short-weight; barge-masters hated carrying leaky clay back to Ostia; and **Gaius Baebius**, a squat Gaditane barge-skipper with a gold hoop through one nostril and salt-eczema on both wrists, had learned since February that the dark-eyed Syrian fifteen-year-old waiting by Bollard Twelve paid cash silver on the barrel-head without haggling past two breaths.

"Four weepers out of sixty-eight from Hispalis, Tyrian," Baebius grunted in Punic-accented Latin, kicking aside wet straw so Lucida could squat and press her thumb against the hairline crack in the fattest amphora's hip. "Top two-thirds clean green virgin pressing; bottom third cloudy with bilge-damp. Twenty-four sesterces the four, and bring your own skins before the wharf-wardens clear the berth at noon."

Lucida dipped her little finger through the unsealed bung-hole, touched one drop of cold Spanish oil to her tongue, and spat neatly into the brown Tiber. Good Guadalquivir fruit, pressed before frost, without turnip-seed adulteration; only the bottom palm-breadth smelled faintly of pitched pine hull.

Decanted carefully through clean linen before noon, those four cracked jars would yield nine *congii* of sweet upper oil—worth twenty-eight sesterces straight to Berenice and two Caelian bath-attendants for perfumed body-unguent bases—plus five *congii* of dark sediment and *amurca* dregs that the axle-wrights and Subura lamp-fillers bought for twelve sesterces cash. Forty sesterces out of twenty, less two sesterces for donkey-hire.

"Eighteen sesterces," Lucida said, standing up and wiping her oiled finger across the leather tongue of her own boot to feed the calfskin. "Number Three is split four inches up from the spike; half its bottom third is river-water already, Baebius, I can see the grey line sweating through the biscuit. Eighteen cash silver on the bollard right now, and my carter clears your sand before the fifth hour."

Baebius squinted at the damp ring on Jar Three, swore lazily by Melqart's balls, and held out a scarred palm. "Twenty. Five denarii flat, or I let the lime-boys smash 'em and write the loss off on the shipper's bond."

Lucida counted **five silver denarii** out of her inner belt-pouch into his palm—leaving **seventy sesterces** (17 denarii and 2 sestertii) tucked safe inside her bodice alongside the two hundred ten sesterces she and Corvus kept locked in their new iron-strapped cedar cash-box upstairs—and chalked her mark (*L · T*, sideways like a mason's square) across all four wet clay shoulders so no wharf-thief would roll them off while she fetched the donkey-cart.

For seven weeks now—ever since January when Corvus walked into their new second-floor shuttered room off the Vicus Cuprius wearing Priscus's clean grey wool and carrying thirty-two sesterces a week in copying pay, while Ceionia Fabia's tire-woman Dorcas doubled Lucida's Caelian cosmetic orders—life had felt almost dangerously wide. No more sleeping with rats gnawing lead under Berenice's poison-shelves, though they still rented storage-space from the old woman and kept their mouths shut about her jars; no more treading seven-day piss bare-thighed under Sticho's quince-rod; meat twice a week in their lentil-pot, dry firewood when Tramontana wind blew off the Sabine snows, **two hundred eighty sesterces** saved clean toward their first thousand.

Even the quiet ledger of small secrets sitting between her and Corvus since winter—she knowing he worked for **Senator Priscus** rather than any Argiletum bookseller though waiting for him to trust her enough to say the name aloud himself when Priscus's June embargo lifted; he knowing she carried alabaster pots into Caelian houses without knowing **Ceionia Fabia**'s emerald pin had pricked her throat—had seemed only like two coats worn against winter cold, harmless so long as both hands still put their silver into the same cedar box at night.

Then from behind the dusty white skirt of Monte Testaccio came two short, piercing thrush-notes blown hard through parted front teeth:

*Whit-whit! Whit-whit!*

Lucida spun round on the limestone quay so fast her woollen cloak slapped the mooring-bollard.

Running flat-out down the crushed-terracotta cart-track between two lime-wagons came twelve-year-old **Sura**.

Her flax hair stood wild with sweat and white fuller's-chalk; she had run all the way from the lower Subura across the Forum Boarium barefoot, slicing her left heel open on potsherds without slowing a stride; her madder-dyed linen burn-wrap had come half-undone down her scarred left forearm, and in her right fist she clutched a broken piece of slate and Lucida's own **iron-strapped cedar cash-box** from their locked second-floor room!

Lucida met her behind stacked oil-barrels out of Baebius's earshot, catching the panting, heaving child by both shoulders before she pitched face-first into the amphora grit.

"Breathe first," Lucida ordered low, dropping to one knee to shield Sura with her cloak while checking down the wharf-road for brown-cloaked runners. Nobody behind the lime-carts yet. "Did fire break out? Who broke our shutter?"

Sura shook her flax head violently, gulping air through her scarred tongue-stub with wet, whistling gasps, shoved the intact cedar cash-box safely against Lucida's knees—she had climbed down the outside courtyard drain-pipe into their room the instant boots hit the lower stair to save their two hundred ten sesterces before bolting!—and held up the smeared slate.

Scratched in frantic, jagged capitals with a lump of tailor's chalk were five words:

*ONE-EAR · TOOK BEROE · HORTI VEDII · NOW*

Ice water went straight from Lucida's throat into her soles.

*Scaeva.*

She flipped the slate over; on the back Sura had drawn a rough quince-rod and an ear-ringed stick-figure pointing uphill, followed by: *STICHO SOLD ME · RAN*.

Lucida's mind put all four pieces together in three heartbeats with sickening, crystal precision. Gaius Lucceius's fullery owed ground-rent to **Hermippus**, steward to **Publius Vedius Pollio**. When winter fuller-work slowed in February, Sticho and Lucceius had settled arrears by signing over small chattels—including a twelve-year-old mute Gallic sulfur-girl—and while Hermippus's clerks audited the fullery block's sub-tenants floor by floor from ground-vats to roof-pantiles, Scaeva had recognized old one-eyed Berenice's Tyrian ledger-marks from Puteoli, kicked in the sixth-floor fir door, and found where dead **Lucida Tyria**'s fifteen-year-old Junian Latin daughter had gone to ground!

Only two blessings stood between them and ruin right this instant: first, they had moved Corvus's cedar birth-diptych, the heavy bronze *signaculum* die, and the five remaining Subortanae brick-tallies out from under Berenice's hearth-brick six weeks ago, burying them deep inside an oiled amphora neck under a loose flagstone in a disused stable-arch near the Baths of Titus that neither Berenice nor Sticho knew existed; second, **Corvus was shut safely inside Senator Priscus's inner Caelian *tablinum*** until the ninth hour copying June briefs, where Scaeva had not yet linked him to Berenice's stair!

If Lucida bolted right now for Ostia with Sura and their two hundred eighty sesterces, Berenice—sixty years old, half-blind, terrified of the Esquiline crossbeam for her green savin-phials—would spill everything she *did* know before dusk: Corvus's full name (*Lucius Cassius Corvus, Gaius's son, Crustumina*), his tall Sabine frame, the Vicus Cuprius room where Corvus would walk home whistling at the ninth hour straight into Scaeva's leaded vine-stick!

Whereas if Lucida walked through Vedius Pollio's garden gate *herself* before the seventh hour—alone, clean-shoed, carrying silver—Scaeva stopped tearing up the Vicus Cuprius looking for her, Berenice kept her mouth shut about the Sabine boy, and Corvus stayed invisible behind Priscus's wall!

Lucida gripped Sura's chin firmly until the trembling girl locked grey eyes onto her lips.

"Listen to every word," Lucida said slowly and distinctly. "Take this chalk. Do *not* go back to the Vicus Cuprius room—Scaeva's dogs will be sitting on our bed-straw by noon. Go sit by the public goat-trough thirty paces outside the Clivus Scauri service-gate on the Caelian Hill—the big grey peperino house with six cypresses over the back wall. Wait there until Corvus comes out at the ninth hour. Write on your slate: *DO NOT GO HOME. VEDIUS HAS BEROE. WAIT BY TITUS'S ARCH AT DUSK.* If I am not at Titus's arch by the first watch horn, tell Corvus to take the buried jar and go straight to Phileros without looking back for me. Show me you understood."

Sura tapped four quick fingers against her own chest, drew an arch in the air, then caught a corner of Lucida's brown cloak in one fierce, dirty fist, shaking her head in silent terror while pointing at the cedar cash-box: *Don't take the silver to him—he'll steal it and chain us both!*

"A Junian Latin isn't a runaway slave, little thrush; he can't chain me without a magistrate's writ unless I run," Lucida lied calmly—knowing perfectly well what rich equestrians did behind high garden walls before magistrates ever heard a scream, yet refusing to leave Sura hunted as a runaway fullery-chattel or Corvus walking blind into an ambush. She pried open the cedar cash-box with her belt-key, dumped all **two hundred ten sesterces**—two gold *aurei* and two silver *denarii* plus small silver—straight into her own deep leather belt-pouch on top of her seventy sesterces, handed Sura the empty wooden box plus four copper *asses* for bread, kissed the flax crown of the mute girl's head once hard, and stood up.

"Go to the Caelian gate, Sura. Run along the open streets where watchmen stand, never the narrow cuts. Wait for Corvus."

Sura swallowed once, tucked the empty box under her burned arm like a legionary's shield, and vanished up the bright travertine steps toward the Aventine slope without looking back.

Lucida retied her dark braids smooth in a brass puddle-reflection, wiped every smudge of Spanish olive oil and potsherd dust off her boots with dry straw so she looked like a brisk fifteen-year-old Caelian tradeswoman rather than dock-prey, slipped her honed Spanish amphora shard out of her boot-top to drop it down a sewer-grate—because walking into Scaeva's hands carrying a concealed blade gave Vedius instant legal excuse to call the *vigiles*—and turned north toward the Esquiline gardens.

* * *

**Publius Vedius Pollio** kept his winter town-gardens high on the sunny southern brow of the Oppian spur, three hundred feet above the smoky stink of the Subura tenements whose ground-rents paid for his snow-pits, yet close enough that when the wind blew south off the Viminal he could watch his own roofs from a heated glass gallery without smelling a single chamber-pot.

Two scarred Cappadocian gate-keepers in green-trimmed tunics searched Lucida's cloak-seams and boot-tops roughly inside the lodge—finding nothing sharper than her brass cloak-brooch and the heavy leather purse at her belt, which **Gaius Ummidius Scaeva**, stepping out of the guard-room picking dried mutton from his molars with a bone toothpick, weighed appreciatively in one palm before handing back to his belt—no, wait: dropping straight onto a silver salver carried behind him!

Scaeva smiled down at her with genuine, almost paternal satisfaction, the white gristle-stump of his missing left ear gleaming in the garden sun.

"Didn't even cost me shoe-leather past noon," Scaeva said pleasantly. "Told Hermippus when we hauled the one-eyed Syrian witch downstairs that Tyria's little bitch wouldn't bolt while her dye-pots were still warm. Where's the long-legged Sabine brat who broke Mousa's nose with an iron chain at Number Three kiln?"

Lucida looked straight at the missing half of Scaeva's ear without blinking. "Dead of winter pleurisy before Saturnalia, bailiff. Coughed black blood five days in a doorway off the Campus and went into the Esquiline lime-cart with forty others. If you want his bones to beat for Mousa's nose, bring a shovel."

Scaeva studied her pupils for five slow counts, hunting the tiny flutter a liar gave when naming a live accomplice dead; Lucida thought steadily of the rust-colored blood on Corvus's rag in December and gave Scaeva flat, cold amber glass.

"Pity," Scaeva grunted, only half-convinced yet lacking any thread to pull while Berenice kept silent. "Saved me four ounces of lead on my stick. Walk ahead along the boxwood path, girl, and don't step on the crocuses; the master's mourning today."

Mourning, Lucida thought, half-expecting black cypress boughs hung for a dead wife or child.

Instead, inside a circular winter *heliocaminus*, a sun-trap pavilion paved in warm yellow Numidian *giallo antico* marble and walled to the south with tall bronze-framed panes of clear *lapis specularis* drinking the March sun, **Publius Vedius Pollio** reclined alone in a tunic of undyed Coan silk beside a tiny silver bird-cage inside which an emerald-green Indian parakeet lay stiff on its back with both scaly claws curled in the air.

On the wet marble floor ten feet below his couch knelt old **Berenice**, wrists tied loosely in front of her apron with hemp twine, a fresh purple swelling high on her left cheekbone where Scaeva's knuckle had introduced itself, her one good black eye darting warning lightning at Lucida for half a heartbeat (*I told them nothing about the boy!*) before dropping back to the stone.

Vedius Pollio was forty-eight or fifty, plump-jowled without fatness in the shoulders, skull shaved smooth and rubbed with oil until it shone like warm ivory, forearms depilated smooth as a bath-girl's and smelling across ten feet of warm air of crushed bitter almonds and snow-water. On a low citrus-wood table worth a farm in Sabinum stood a silver bowl of pale green winter Crustumian pears packed in clean mountain snow, and between his soft, manicured right thumb and forefinger gleamed a tiny folding fruit-knife with a handle of carved mother-of-pearl and a two-inch blade of surgical Noric steel.

He did not look up until he had pared one unbroken spiral of green peel from stem to blossom-end without nicking the white flesh underneath.

"Twelve thousand sesterces I paid an Alexandrian skipper at Puteoli for that bird last May, Scaeva," Vedius said, his voice astonishingly light, musical, almost melancholy, like a tragic flute-player resting between acts. "It could say *Ave Caesar* in Latin and *Drink, pretty boy* in Greek whenever Lucius Ceionius Commodus came to supper. Yesterday evening some careless furnace-slave let coal-damp leak through a cracked flue-tile under the aviary, and this morning: stiff as dried salt-fish."

"Should I have the furnace-slave flogged at the whipping-post, master?" Scaeva asked dutifully by the door.

"Flogging makes noise and ruins working backs," Vedius sighed, slicing a thin crescent of cold pear onto his tongue and chewing thoughtfully. "Dock the whole furnace-gang half their tallow-ration until June and sell the oldest one to the Ostian salt-pans; cold porridge teaches masonry care much better than leather. Now: is this Aulus Lucidius's little Puteoli legacy?"

Lucida stood upright three paces from the yellow marble step, hands folded still against her brown skirt, chin level.

Vedius turned dark, moist, intelligent eyes from the dead parakeet onto her face, appraising bone-structure, clean wool cloak, stout boots, stain-shadowed cuticles, and finally the heavy leather belt-pouch Scaeva had laid beside the snow-bowl.

"Fifteen," Vedius murmured, consulting no wax tablet, carrying ten thousand debtors and chattels straight inside that oiled ivory skull. "Daughter of Lucida Tyria, freed *inter amicos* at supper without the Praetor's rod. Scaeva wanted to cart you back up to Figlinae Subortanae in ankle-loops this morning to tread clay-slurry and work off the sixteen hundred sesterces in unperformed *operae* your consumptive mother died owing old Lucidius's estate, plus, let us say, four hundred sesterces damages for Mousa's punctured forearm and one missing workshop *signaculum* die."

"I know nothing of any workshop die, Dominus," Lucida said evenly, "and your river-man Mousa tried to drag a free Junian Latin woman into the reeds before dawn without magistrate's warrant or witness."

Scaeva took half a menacing step forward; Vedius raised two depilated fingers an inch off his knee, stopping the ex-Praetorian cold, and smiled at Lucida with genuine pleasure.

"Listen to the phrasing, Scaeva! *Without magistrate's warrant or witness!* Six months inside the Aurelian customs-posts and the ditch-sparrow talks like Julian's clerk!" Vedius sliced a second crescent of pear and pointed the wet two-inch silver blade straight at Lucida's breastbone. "Tell me how you earned the two gold *aurei* and seventy-odd silver sesterces sitting in that leather pouch since October, Lucida. And don't insult my afternoon by saying fullers' vats; fullers' girls have split heels and eight copper asses."

Lying about Corvus had cost nothing because Scaeva had no paper trail; lying about her own trade to a man whose steward owned half the Argiletum shop-fronts and dockside warehouses would get her stripped and sent back to the clay-trenches as worthless liar-stock within five breaths.

"Cosmetic lead-grinding and Caelian unguent deliveries for Berenice through January, Dominus," Lucida answered crisply. "And since the Kalends of February, buying cracked Baetican weeper-amphorae off Gaditane skippers at the Emporium sand-berths before the *annona* maulers smash them: decanting nine *congii* of clean upper oil for bath-unguents at three sesterces the *congius*, selling five *congii* of bottom *amurca* to wagon-wrights and lamp-boilers, clearing eighteen to twenty-two sesterces net on twenty invested."

Vedius Pollio stopped chewing mid-pear.

Slowly he lowered the mother-of-pearl folding knife onto the rim of the silver snow-bowl, leaned one plump elbow on his silk cushion, and looked at Lucida for the first time not as runaway livestock, as a balance-sheet.

"Scaeva," Vedius said softly without turning his head, "how much does a fifteen-year-old girl earn me treading wet marl at Subortanae before winter marsh-fever kills her?"

"Three sesterces a day less two asses barley-meal," Scaeva rasped unhappily. "Call it six hundred sesterces a year."

"Six hundred sesterces a year, and dead at nineteen leaving two rags and a tin spoon," Vedius murmured, shaking his oiled head at the sheer vulgarity of soldiers' economics. He looked back at Lucida, eyes shining soft and brown as velvet over iron spikes. "Whereas under the wise *Lex Iunia Norbana* of our ancestors, Lucida, so long as you remain a Junian Latin without citizen *ius Quiritium*, every oil-skin you decant, every lease you sign, every silver denarius you stack inside a cedar box over the next forty years comes home intact into *my* strongroom the afternoon your heart stops, provided I don't let clumsy bailiffs break my little golden goose in a brick-ditch first."

Cold sweat prickled silently down the hollow between Lucida's shoulder-blades, though her face gave nothing away. She had gambled walking through his gate that Vedius loved silver better than spite; watching him peel a third slice of pear while pricing her entire future life down to her burial shroud made her stomach turn colder than Mousa's cudgel ever had.

"Therefore," Vedius continued musically, "we make a civilized Roman arrangement. Old Berenice here goes back up her seventy-eight stairs with both eyes still in her skull, paying Hermippus double monthly shop-rent, forty sesterces on every Kalends, for the privilege of keeping her smelly little green bottles out of the Urban Prefect's fire. And *you*, Lucida, stay right here in Rome trading all the cracked Spanish oil-jars your clever Tyrian nose can smell, paying my steward Hermippus **eighty sesterces on the Kalends of every month** toward your mother's commuted *operae* and patronal respect, reporting your dwelling-street every quarter, never leaving the first milestone without Hermippus's lead seal. Miss one Kalends payment, or try creeping round a magistrate's clerk to beg re-manumission behind my back, and I auction your live *operae* contract to the sulfur-brothels behind the Circus Flaminius for ten years. Do we understand each other?"

Eighty sesterces every Kalends: twenty silver denarii a month bled straight off her wrists into Vedius's snow-bowl forever, plus every coin left at her death!

Still, Berenice lived, Corvus remained wholly unknown and untouched up on the Caelian Hill, and Lucida walked out free into the sunlight with her two hundred eighty sesterces minus the first month's eighty.

"We understand each other, Dominus," Lucida said, bowing her head two inches. "Take March's eighty sesterces out of my pouch on the table now, and give me Hermippus's receipt."

"Oh, the pouch on the table?" Vedius asked mildly, blinking as though reminded of a trifle. "Two hundred eighty sesterces, Scaeva counted. Eighty pays March's tribute, certainly. Which leaves two hundred, fifty silver denarii. Tell me, Scaeva: what did Hermippus value that runaway mute Gallic sulfur-slut from Lucceius's fullery at when Sticho signed her over against rent arrears this morning?"

Lucida's heart slammed once against her ribs like a dropped stone.

*No.* Sura was supposed to be sitting safely up by the Caelian goat-trough waiting for Corvus!

Outside on the gravel boxwood path boots scuffled, and one of Vedius's green-trimmed Cappadocian gate-keepers shoved **Sura** sideways through the bronze pavilion doors by her twisted thin arm, Corvus's empty cedar cash-box still clutched crushed against her chest, blood smeared dark from her cut bare heel across the yellow Numidian marble.

Sura hadn't been caught running *away*; Scaeva's second hound had spotted her fullery chalk-burn two streets below the Clivus Scauri before she ever reached Priscus's gate and intercepted her crossing the Colosseum valley. Sura's huge grey eyes met Lucida's in sheer anguish, terrified Lucida would think she had betrayed the message, and shook her head twice while tapping her empty mouth: *Slate smashed in the gutter before they grabbed me!*

"Two hundred sesterces on Lucceius's arrears sheet, master," Scaeva answered grinning, planting one heavy hand on Sura's flax skull to force her onto her knees beside old Berenice. "Damaged goods, no tongue and a five-inch sulfur-scar on the left arm, useless for house-service. Hermippus marked her down to go to Rufus's basement donkey-mill in Trans-Tiberim tonight to push the blind sweep-bar."

Every fuller's girl in Rome knew what Rufus's basement mill meant for a seventy-pound mute twelve-year-old: chained by an iron neck-ring next to mangy asses in pitch-black flour-dust eighteen hours a day until lungs clotted solid before fourteen.

Vedius Pollio did not even glance down at Sura's bleeding heel on his yellow marble; he looked exclusively at **Lucida's face** over the rim of his tiny silver pear-knife, testing with exquisite connoisseur's curiosity whether his new fifteen-year-old ledger-asset had a soft spot he could map and squeeze later.

"Fifty denarii," Vedius murmured softly. "The exact balance sitting in your leather pouch after March's eighty sesterces, Lucida. Keep your two hundred sesterces, good seed-capital for Spanish oil, and Scaeva sends the damaged Gallic piece across the river to push mill-timber tonight. Or leave the entire pouch on my snow-table, and Hermippus writes you a clean *chirographum* bill of delivery: not *mancipatio*, naturally, we'll save the five citizen witnesses for pricier cattle, call it *traditio* of a defective kitchen-scullion for two hundred cash, and you walk out owning one mute pair of hands and not a single copper *as* for supper. Entirely your commercial judgment."

Do not look at Sura. Look at Sura and he raises the price to three hundred on credit to put chains on next month too.

Lucida kept her amber eyes cold and flat on the mother-of-pearl handle of Vedius's knife, pretending to calculate flour-wages against oil-margins in her head for four slow counts, giving the fat equestrian the exact hard-nosed merchant hesitation that kept Sura's price at two hundred rather than letting him see her heart breaking behind her ribs.

"A mute girl eats four asses of barley a day and never blabs bath-unguent formulas to competitors," Lucida said crisp and dry as salt-cod. "Give me Hermippus's waxed bill of sale stamped with your house-ring, Dominus, and keep the two hundred, leather pouch included, provided you throw in six yards of coarse binding-cord so she carries Berenice's pots home."

Vedius laughed aloud, a clear, delighted ripple that shook his silk collar, folded the tiny silver pear-knife shut with a sharp *snick*, and tossed Lucida's belt-pouch into Scaeva's hands.

"Write the *traditio* tablet on the portico bench, Scaeva, and stamp it with the steward's ring! Oh, we are going to make each other wonderfully rich before you turn forty, little Tyrian, provided you don't catch winter pleurisy like your clumsy country friend!"

* * *

Twenty breaths later, outside the iron-spiked gate of the *Horti Vedii* where cold March wind whipped dust off the Esquiline cobbles, the bronze postern clanged shut behind the three of them: old Berenice limping on swollen ankles muttering Syrian curses under her breath, Sura clutching the stamped wax sale-tablet against her chest while silent hot tears cut twin clean tracks down her soot-streaked cheeks, and fifteen-year-old Lucida standing in the middle of the road without one copper *quadrans* left between her boots and the sky.

Except down on Emporium Bollard Twelve sat **four cracked Spanish amphorae already paid for at dawn**, chalked *L · T*, waiting to be smashed by the harbor wardens if nobody cleared the sand before sunset!

Lucida reached down, wiped Sura's wet cheeks roughly dry with the corner of her clean wool cloak, then knelt right on the cold basalt flags to bind Sura's bleeding heel tight in a strip torn off her own linen undershift.

"Stop leaking salt, little thrush; salt costs money," Lucida said fiercely against Sura's flax hair, feeling her own hands finally shake for three heartbeats now that Vedius Pollio's brown velvet eyes could no longer see them, before clamping both fists shut until the tremor died. "Berenice, go back up your seventy-eight steps, bolt your door, and pack every jar you want kept out of Hermippus's reach. Sura, limp slow behind me as far as the Velabrum cooper's yard so you can watch the skins while I haul."

She left her brass cloak-brooch in pledge with a squint-eyed Velabrum cooper who knew her face to borrow a two-wheeled oak hand-barrow and six clean goat-skins, then ran the empty barrow clattering down the river-road at a dead flat sprint while the March sun sank red-gold behind the Janiculum mills.

The harbor maulers already had their iron hammers raised two berths away when Lucida skidded onto the wet sand at Bollard Twelve!

Working side by side by dying river-light—Lucida tipping each two-hundred-pound Baetican clay belly carefully over a wooden cradle with braced thighs while bandaged Sura held folded linen strainer-cloths taut across the funnels—they drew off nine clear, fragrant *congii* of green Guadalquivir virgin pressing before bilge-water clouded the spout, and five thick *congii* of dark *amurca* dregs into separate tarred buckets.

She had no donkey to haul oil up the hills tonight; she sold wholesale right off the quay-curb by the first smoking dock-torches. Seven *congii* of clear green oil went straight to the bath-steward of the *Balnea Surae* coming down the Aventine steps with empty mule-panniers for **twenty-one sesterces** silver and brass; two *congii* she kept back for Caelian cosmetic base worth double once scented; the five buckets of *amurca* axle-grease went to the Ostian ox-carters' yard across the road for **eleven sesterces** copper; and she redeemed her brass cloak-brooch from the Velabrum cooper for one *sestertius* barrow-hire, walking away into the March dark with **thirty-one sesterces** clinking in a knotted corner of her apron and two *congii* of sweet oil slung over her own aching shoulder!

By the time the first watch horn blew across the Forum Romanum, however, a second crisis remained: Sura had never reached Priscus's gate, which meant **Corvus had walked straight home to their Vicus Cuprius room at the ninth hour**—to find either Scaeva's watchers or an empty room with its cedar cash-box gone!

Heart hammering in her throat, Lucida led limping Sura up the shadowed travertine pavement of the **Via Sacra** toward their emergency fallback point: the single tall Pentelic marble vault of the **Arch of Titus** crowning the Velia saddle.

Inside the deep, unlit coffered shadow beneath the carved relief of Titus's soldiers carrying the seven-branched golden candlestick out of burning Jerusalem, a tall figure in dark grey Caelian wool stepped silently out from behind a travertine pilaster with a heavy iron crowbar balanced ready in his scarred right fist.

Seeing Lucida's dark braids and limping Sura behind her in the moonlight, Corvus lowered the iron bar with an audible rush of held breath that misted white in the cold air. Slung over his left shoulder in a knotted sack were their two spare tunics, his surveyor's brass gauge, and the unearthed clay amphora-neck holding his cedar birth-diptych and the bronze *signaculum* die!

"Vicus Cuprius had two fuller's boys sitting on the wine-step across from our cobbler's door asking who rented the shuttered room," Corvus said low and fast, catching Lucida's free elbow in a grip hard enough to bruise just to make sure she was solid flesh and unhurt. "I saw Sticho's cousin Rufio spit when he spotted the cobbler, went in over the back bakehouse roof instead of the street door, cleared our clothes out in ninety breaths, and dug up the stable-arch jar on the way here. Where's the cedar box—and what happened to Sura's foot?"

Here, standing three inches from Corvus under the carved marble spoils of Judea while Sura leaned exhausted against cold travertine holding her stamped *traditio* tablet, came the parting of two roads.

Tell Corvus the whole truth, that **Publius Vedius Pollio** himself had held a silver pear-knife across a snow-bowl from her throat, confiscated two hundred eighty sesterces, and bound her under an **eighty-sesterce monthly Kalends tribute** plus quarterly patronal inspection, and what would Corvus do? Either he would tear open Senator Priscus's locked cedar cupboard prematurely to hurl the Subortanae brick-dies and Caelian water-flask at Vedius before June—destroying his own patronship and getting both of them crushed—or he would insist on paying her eighty sesterces out of his thirty-two-sesterce weekly clerk's wage, chaining his freeborn Crustumina rise to her Junian ankle-iron forever!

No. Her mother's defective manumission was *her* ledger to break, with *her* hands.

Lucida lifted the empty iron-strapped cedar cash-box from Sura's arm and tapped the stamped wax *traditio* tablet beside it.

"Hermippus audited Lucceius's fullery block for ground-rent arrears at noon," Lucida said—every word true so far as it went, trimmed clean as pared fruit. "Scaeva raided Berenice's garret hunting runaway Subortanae stock; Berenice told him you died of pleurisy in December, so your name is clean off his slate. Only Sticho signed Sura over to Hermippus's mill-broker for two hundred sesterces arrears, and our Vicus Cuprius lease-trail burned behind us. I used two hundred out of the box through an Esquiline broker to buy Sura's *traditio* outright before Rufus chained her in Trans-Tiberim, paid eighty to clear Berenice's shop-arrears and shut Sticho's mouth, and turned Baebius's four Spanish oil-jars down on the quay into thirty-one fresh sesterces before dark."

Corvus looked down from Lucida's calm amber eyes to small, trembling Sura—who held out the waxed *traditio* tablet timidly with her burned left arm so the tall Sabine boy could read Hermippus's seal for himself—and every hard, suspicious line round Corvus's mouth softened at once.

He knelt on one knee on the cold Via Sacra paving-blocks to bring his eyes level with the twelve-year-old mute girl's face.

"You climbed down the drain-pipe to fetch that cedar box out before Rufio's gang got upstairs, didn't you, Sura?" Corvus asked quietly.

Sura nodded twice, grey eyes wide in the moonlight.

"Then two hundred eighty sesterces was cheap," Corvus said simply, standing up and hoisting Sura's oil-skin off her narrow shoulder onto his own broad back alongside the clothes-sack. "We don't leave our own in blind mills. Come on—Phileros knows an old Cappadocian baker over behind the Forum Piscarium who lets dry loft-rooms above his brick ovens where fullers' dogs don't poke their noses, and ovens mean free heat through March."

*A Cappadocian baker with brick ovens,* Lucida repeated silently inside her skull as she fell into step between Corvus and limping Sura down the moonlit slope of the Sacred Way, feeling the thirty-one copper and silver coins clink steady against her thigh. Twenty-seven days until the Kalends of April brought Hermippus's hand back out for eighty sesterces; somewhere inside three hundred miles of Roman statutes there *had* to sit a road out of the *Lex Iunia Norbana* that didn't ask Publius Vedius Pollio's permission, and starting tomorrow morning among the flour-mills, she intended to find its price.
