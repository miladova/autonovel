# Chapter II — Oil of Bitter Almonds

*Sixteenth to Eighteenth of October, AD 156 · Lucida*

Seventy-eight steps went up from the street door to Berenice's garret: the first fourteen travertine, worn hollow in the middle like soup-spoons by a century of hobnails; the next thirty-two brick laid on edge and slimy with cabbage-grease; the last thirty-two unplaned pine ladders nailed between timber stud-walls thin enough that a man farting on the fourth landing rattled the clay oil-lamps on the fifth.

Lucida counted every riser on the way up because in summer, when the Subura burned, stairs were the difference between cooked meat and cold air.

Behind her on the narrow pine treads, Corvus climbed with one hand braced lightly against the lime-washed wattle partition, favoring his crusted shin without letting a single board creak under his weight. Even half-starved after a night spent wedged behind empty pickle-jars under a baker's awning, he looked up at the blackened oak joists above their heads with that concentrated, slightly cross-browed squint he got around bad carpentry, already calculating which cross-beam had rotted at the tenon and which way the roof would pitch when an earthquake or an overloaded water-butt brought the top two storeys down into the lane.

At the top landing there was no passage, only a low fir door patched at the bottom with hammered lead sheet to keep rats from gnawing through, and an iron ring worn bright by greasy thumbs. Smells seeped through the warped jamb before Lucida's knuckle touched wood: pounded bitter almonds, boiled vinegar, burnt hartshorn, beeswax, and underneath them all the sharp, coppery tang of pennyroyal steeping in an earthen pipkin.

Her mother's smell. Not the whole of it; Lucida Tyria had smelled of crushed murex shells and sea-salt too when she could get Syrian stock up from Ostia, before the fever shriveled her on a straw sack behind the brick-yards. Still, the bitter almond caught Lucida high at the back of the nose hard enough to make her jaw clench.

She rapped twice, then once, the way fuller-boys knocked when bringing clean wool-fat up the back stair so the aedile's market-inspectors down on the Argiletum would not ask why an unlicensed woman was boiling medicaments under dry roof-tiles.

Chains did not rattle inside; a wooden bar lifted without noise. The fir door opened four fingers wide, blocked by an iron wedge kicked against the floorboard, and one black Syrian eye—bright and hard as wet obsidian—studied Lucida from hairline to muddy instep while its mate, clouded milk-white with an old cataract from nose-bridge to temple, stared serenely past Corvus's ear at nothing.

"We don't buy rags before the Kalends," Berenice said in flat Subura Latin, making to shut the door. She was past fifty, thin-necked as a plucked heron, grey braids bound tight in yellow linen to keep stray hairs out of her unguent pots, two front teeth missing on the left where a drunken legionary had hit her during Hadrian's games.

"Lucida Tyria sent you two pitch-sealed jars of Sidonian purple lees in the consulship of Glabrio and Homullus," Lucida said swiftly in the Phoenician-salted Greek of the Tyre harbour-quarter, slipping three stained fingers into the crack—risking broken bones if the old woman kicked the wedge—just high enough that Berenice's good eye caught the purple-brown alkanet crescents under her nails. "And you sent back four ounces of Chian mastic and a pewter syringe when her womb bled after me, and still owed her six denarii on the tallow weight."

The door stopped moving.

Berenice's good black eye dropped from Lucida's face to the open V of her rough shift, just below the left collarbone where the small dark birthmark sat like an olive-pip against olive skin, then flicked sideways to Corvus standing half a step down on the steep pine ladder with his jaw shut and his right hand resting casually near the heavy bulge under Lucida's fuller-cloak.

"Six denarii?" Berenice cackled, a dry sound like dry beans shaken in a bladder, and kicked the iron floor-wedge aside with a deformed big toe. "Your mother died owing every muleteer between Tyre and Puteoli, little crow, and her tallow was a third mutton-suet whipped with lime-water! Come inside off the stair before the cobbler's wife across the air-shaft sells your faces to the ward-headman for two asses."

They stepped over the sill, and Berenice dropped the oak bar back into its iron brackets.

The *cenaculum* under the pantiles measured fourteen feet by eleven, sloped so low along the street eaves that Corvus had to duck his black head anywhere past the middle ceiling-beam. Light entered through a single unglazed dormer opening onto a lead rain-gutter where three clay pots of rue and savin shivered in the morning draught. Every square palm of wall-plaster and rafter space worked for its living. Dried poppy-heads, bundles of dried squid-bone for tooth-powder, bladders of goose-grease, cakes of red ochre from Sinope, and little blown-glass phials stopped with waxed linen hung from twine loops overhead. On a scarred marble slab salvaged from some broken tombstone sat a porphyry muller for grinding white lead, two bronze balances sensitive enough to weigh a dried lentil, and a charcoal brazier no bigger than a helmet over which a lidded clay pot breathed sour herbal steam.

Berenice did not offer them stools because there was only one stool, and she sat on it herself, spreading her patched brown tunic over bony knees while picking up an iron spatula to stir the pipkin.

"Shut your mouth if you're going to lie to me," she told Lucida in Greek, though she switched back to Latin on the next breath so Corvus understood he was being weighed too. "Tyria's girl. Fifteen now? Fourteen? And who's the long-shanked Sabine wolf-cub measuring my rafters with his eyes—your pimp, your brother, or the fool who knocked your belly up?"

"Corvus," Lucida said. "Gaius Cassius's son from the upper clay-pits. Freeborn, Crustumina tribe, clean birth-tablets. Nobody knocked my belly up, Beroe. Scaeva burned the shacks at Figlinae Subortanae yesterday before sunrise."

The iron spatula stopped halfway round the pipkin.

For three breaths the only sound in the garret was rain-water dripping into the gutter outside and the distant thump of fullers' poles six storeys down in the courtyard vats.

"Gaius Ummidius Scaeva?" Berenice asked softly.

"Missing half his left ear," Corvus said from by the low eaves, speaking for the first time. His voice was calm, pitched low so it would not carry through the lath-and-plaster wall into the next garret where somebody was snoring off the holiday wine. "Carries a leaded centurion's vine-stick. Works for Publius Vedius Pollio."

Berenice set the spatula down on the tombstone slab, wiped her palms on her apron, and stood up. Short as she was, her head nearly brushed the hanging poppy-bundles. "Out," she said. "Take the back roof-plank over the bakery tiles and break your necks in somebody else's region. Do you know whose bailiff collects the ground-rent on the three fulleries downstairs, you pair of milk-blind kittens? Not Vedius in his own name—no, great men keep clean fingernails—a freedman called Hermippus who drinks Vedius's bath-water! If Scaeva smells you under my tiles, my jars go out the dormer window and I spend winter sleeping under the Pons Sublicius!"

Corvus took half a step toward the door, pride stiffening his shoulders like an iron rod—he had never begged roof-space from anyone since his father died—and Lucida stepped flat across his toes before he could touch the bar.

"Wait," Lucida said. She reached inside her shift, bypassed the nine small coins—nine coins wouldn't buy Berenice's silence for an afternoon—and brought out one single waxed pine tally-leaf from the six they had stolen off Rufus's table, holding it tilted just long enough for Berenice's sharp right eye to read the bottom stamp, not long enough to read the brick-counts. "Scaeva isn't hunting two runaway field-mice for sport, Beroe. He doesn't know whether we went downstream to Ostia, across into Trans-Tiberim, or north up the Via Salaria. What we need is six feet of dry floor behind your screen until the November rains start, two fullers' day-tokens so we earn bread without showing papers at the gate, and salt for Corvus's shin. What *you* need is someone fifteen years old with nimble fingers and no name in the Argiletum to carry white-lead pots and sealed rue-phials up the Caelian back stairs where everybody already knows your cataract from twenty paces off."

Berenice's good eye narrowed to a black slit. "My errand-girl Myrtale carries my Caelian baskets."

"Myrtale has six copper *asses* of cheap Syrian iris behind her ears; I smelled her lingering on the third landing talking to two off-duty *vigiles* on our way up," Lucida lied smoothly, having smelled iris oil on a fat girl leaning over the third-floor rail and guessed the rest from the blue ointment smudge on the girl's thumb. "A girl who gossips with night-watchmen on the stairs while carrying pennyroyal to senators' daughters is going to put you both in the Esquiline lime-pit before Saturnalia. I don't talk to watchmen, I speak Greek, Latin, and enough Syrian to curse a mule, and I know how to grind *cerussa* wet so the lead-dust doesn't rot the lungs."

Behind her, Corvus said nothing at all, though Lucida felt the heat coming off his chest half a hand's breadth from her shoulder blade. He hated when she stepped into dirt to buy them room; she could feel his thumb rubbing that pale half-moon scar on his knuckle in the quiet. Let him hate it. Pride did not roof a garret.

Berenice stared at Lucida for a long count, then snorted through one nostril, reached out with horny yellow fingernails, and pinched Lucida's chin, turning her cheek toward the grey dormer light.

"Your mother had that exact lying mouth," the old woman muttered. "Sold lady Domitia's wardrobe-woman wild alkanet boiled in alum and swore by Astarte's dugs it was genuine double-dipped Sidonian shellfish, and never turned a hair while counting the silver. Sit down on the rush-mat, both of you, before that boy puts his skull through my tiles. Let me see what Vedius Pollio holds on your neck, girl. Did Tyria ever take you before the Urban Praetor's rod before the blood-cough killed her?"

Lucida dropped onto her heels on the worn rush-mat beside the charcoal brazier. Her wet wool shift stuck cold to her thighs.

"No," Lucida said. "Patron Aulus Lucidius signed a letter *inter amicos* at supper in Puteoli when my mother turned twenty-six, witnessed by five guests over mulled wine, and died of apoplexy before the autumn court session could confirm it by the rod. I was born four months after the letter."

Berenice clicked her tongue against her gap-teeth. Corvus knew the history already—they had turned it over fifty nights beside the kiln-ash like a sore tooth—yet hearing it spoken plain under a Roman roof made the walls feel narrower by half.

Under the *Lex Iunia Norbana*, a woman freed at supper among friends without the magistrate's rod was no slave: nobody could chain her ankles at a mill without cause or sell her naked off a wooden turntable in the Saepta. Yet she was no Roman citizen either. She was a *Latina Iuniana*, a Junian Latin. She might buy wool, bake bread, rent six feet of floor, pile up ten thousand silver denarii in an iron chest if her wits held out; and the hour her breath stopped, whether at sixteen of winter fever or seventy in silk sheets, the Roman law reached into her bedchamber, swept every copper *quadrans*, kettle, cloak, and brick she had ever bled to earn back into her dead patron's estate as if she had never been anything more than a clever slave minding her master's purse. And because old Aulus Lucidius's spendthrift grandson had drunk away his Puteoli villas three winters back and forfeited his pledged strongboxes to **Publius Vedius Pollio**, Vedius now owned the waiting hand at the end of Lucida's life—and claimed sixteen hundred sesterces in unpaid *operae*—freedwoman's labor-days—from her dead mother's account right now while she was still breathing.

"Live free, die slave," Berenice said matter-of-factly, picking up a lump of chalky white lead carbonate with iron tongs and dropping it into a stoneware mortar with three splashes of sharp vinegar. "Half the rich freedmen in Trans-Tiberim walk round in rings heavy enough to sink a skiff and still piss vinegar every winter wondering how to buy out their patron's reversion before apoplexy catches them. Sixteen asses a week for the corner behind the wicker screen, paid every eighth market-day, *nundinae*, on the nail, four asses extra if you burn my charcoal after the second watch, and you grind two pounds of lead-cake every evening before supper gratis. Touch one glass phial on my shelf without asking, and I don't call Scaeva; I put three drops of cold-pressed *hyoscyamus* in your lentil-pot and roll you both into the Cloaca grate myself. Show me your copper."

Lucida did not smile, did not thank her—thanking a Subura landlady told her she had asked too little—and unpicked four tarnished copper *asses* and one brass *dupondius* from her wet hem, laying six asses' worth flat on the marble tombstone slab.

"Four days on account," Lucida said. "And tell us which fullery downstairs takes day-hands without asking tribe-tablets."

Berenice swept the bronze off the marble into her pouch with one dry thumb, then tossed Corvus a lump of coarse grey rock-salt and a rag soaked in sour wine-vinegar. "Wash that leg before it stinks up my rue, Sabine. Gaius Lucceius's vats in the second courtyard down. His lead-treader got his kneecap split with a fuller's pole in yesterday's horse-brawl, so he's three pairs of legs short on Senator Regulus's dinner-cloths. He pays twelve asses from the fifth hour to dusk for boys, eight for girls, and docks two for urine-splash if you drop a hem. Leave your heavy bundle behind the loose brick under my hearth-ash; if you think I haven't eyes enough to see thirty pounds of stolen bronze pulling that girl's shoulder sideways, you're stupider than your haircuts."

Corvus looked down at the vinegar-rag in his hand, then across the brazier at Berenice's milky blind eye and glittering black one.

"If anything under that loose brick moves the width of a barleycorn while we're downstairs, grandmother," Corvus said very politely, wrapping the stinging vinegar cloth tight round his torn shin without a wince, "the Urban Prefect's clerks learn who sold abortive savin to Senator Bradua's niece before our ankles touch the first landing."

Silence dropped flat across the garret. Corvus didn't know Senator Bradua's niece from the Empress Faustina—he had plucked the name *Bradua* straight off the toll-collector's shout at the Flaminian Gate the day before—yet somewhere on the hills every rich family had a niece, and Berenice's good eye blinked once, slow as a lizard on hot stone, before breaking into a sharp, delighted wheeze.

"Oh, keep this one, little crow!" Berenice said, pointing her iron tongs at Corvus's chin. "He's got magistrate's teeth already—all bone and clean Latin while he steals your hearth-bricks! Go tread piss, both of you, before Lucceius hires street-Greeks."

* * *

One smelled Gaius Lucceius's *fullonica* before seeing the doorway, and three slick brick stairs down into the vaulted treading-hall was enough to coat the back of the tongue with salt ammonia for three days.

Along the street wall outside stood four sawn-off amphora necks bedded up to their bellies in mortar where every passing wine-drinker, mule-driver, and sausage-seller on the lower Clivus Suburanus stopped to relieve his bladder for free. Inside the dim brick arcade, rows of waist-deep terracotta tubs sunk flush into paved *opus spicatum* herringbone floors held that collected human piss aged seven days in the warm dark until its alcaline bite turned fierce enough to strip body-grease, banquet gravy, and lamp-soot out of forty-pound woollen *togae* and dining-couch covers.

Lucida hitched her coarse shift high into her waist-cord, knotted it tight between her thighs like a loin-clout so the hem could not drag, and stepped bare-legged into Tub Seven beside a sunken-chested Cappadocian treader old enough to have grey wool on his breastbone despite lacking forty years. Three tubs over, where the heavier, rougher *synthesis* dinner-garments soaked in thicker grey Cimolian fuller's earth and double-strength chamber-lye, Corvus gripped the horizontal oak hand-rail braced along the damp brick wall and began the steady, punishing fuller's march: left knee high, heel driving down through sodden wool folds, twist the ball of the foot to force liquor through the warp, right knee high, heel down, twist.

Within half an hour the warm, greasy liquid bit into every scratch from the river reeds and briars like hot needles. Within an hour the ammonia vapor rising between Lucida's thighs made her eyes run clear water down her cheeks and turned the inside of her nostrils raw pink.

Nobody sang work-songs there; men who opened their mouths wide over aged piss-vats coughed blood by thirty. Sound in the arcade was purely mechanical: the rhythmic *sluck-thump, sluck-thump* of twenty-four bare heels churning wet wool; the hiss of cold aqueduct water rushing from a lead spout into the three wide masonry rinsing-basins across the court; the sharp *whack* of willow switches beating dried fuller's-chalk out of finished cloaks under the sun-arch; and the pacing hobnails of Lucceius's overseer **Sticho**, a pot-bellied freedman with gold rings in both ears and a split quince-wood rod tucked under his armpit, walking the dry brick coping between the vats.

"Lift your heels, Seven, don't dance on top of the cloth like a tavern-whore saving her ankles!" Sticho barked above Lucida's head, rapping the quince-rod once against her oak hand-rail close enough to sting her knuckles with vibration. "That's equestrian table-linen from the Carinae, not mule-blankets! Miss one lamp-oil spot on the border and you pay four asses out of your eight!"

"Oil spot's already lifting, master," Lucida murmured without looking up, eyes watering, driving her small hard heel straight into the corner seam where olive-grease and spilled mulled wine had stiffened the linen yellow. Never look an overseer in the earrings when fifteen and bare-thighed in his tub; men like Sticho kept two uses for quince-rods after the shutters went up if a new girl looked either defiant or frightened. Look dull, work clean, count every exit.

Across the steam-haze beside the rinsing-basins stood the **sulfur-cage**: a six-foot bell-shaped dome woven of osier switches smeared outside with wet clay, over which damp white *togae* were stretched tight while a shallow iron pan of raw yellow Sicilian brimstone burned underneath, bleaching the wool dazzling snow-bright with choking blue fumes before the carders brushed up the nap with hedgehog skins and teasel-heads.

Tending three sulfur-pans at once was a girl smaller than Lucida—twelve, maybe thirteen, thin as a peeled willow wand, flax-pale northern hair hacked ragged above jutting collarbones, barefoot in grey ash. When a fuller-boy shouted to her to fetch fresh charcoal from the bin under the arch, the girl did not answer; when a rolling rinse-tub slopped icy water over her ankles, she made no sound at all, only sucked air silently past parted front teeth. Checking her mouth when she turned to lift a heavy damp *toga praetexta* onto the third wicker dome, Lucida saw the stub inside when the girl panted: tongue cut or burned short years back, or else born silent in some Gallic cattle-stead before the dealers drove her south.

Sticho had stopped by the rinsing trough to curse a Greek carder over torn fringe on a woman's palla, waving both stubby arms so his quince-rod clipped the wet shoulder of the overloaded third wicker dome.

One rotten osier rib snapped with a dry *pop*.

Ten pounds of wet woollen *toga* sagged inward toward the glowing iron brimstone pan. If burning sulfur caught dry-edged woollen nap on a magistrate's purple-bordered gown, forty denarii went up in stinking blue flame in ten heartbeats.

The mute flax-haired girl did not hesitate, because slave-children who let forty-denarius *togae* scorch got hung by their wrists from the beam and whipped with knotted ox-hide until their kidneys bled. She lunged sideways with both skinny arms, snatched the sagging wool clear of the blue flame with her right hand, and caught the tipping red-hot iron sulfur-pan on the bare inner skin of her left forearm to keep liquid burning brimstone from splashing across the skirt.

Skin seared with the wet, unmistakable *hiss* of fat hitting a griddle.

Even without a tongue the sound that tore out of the back of the girl's throat stopped six treaders mid-step: a thin, whistling animal screech that broke off into dry retching as the iron pan clattered onto the herringbone bricks, spilling blue-burning yellow crust harmlessly into the stone drain-channel while she clutched her smoking left arm against her belly, knees buckling onto wet brick. Molten sulfur stuck like burning pitch where it touched flesh; a blue bead still sputtered pale flame half an inch below her wrist.

Corvus dropped off his oak hand-rail in Tub Ten before anyone else moved, not shouting, reaching straight for a bucket of dry fuller's clay beside the coping, because throwing water on burning brimstone spread the blister; Sticho spun round in purple-faced fury under his fuller-chalk, snatching up the saved *toga* with one hand and raising the split quince-rod high with the other to beat the kneeling girl across her hunched shoulder-blades.

"Clumsy Gallic half-wit! Half a pound of Sicilian sulfur down the gutter and soot on the purple border—I'll take the hide off your—"

"Hit her on the stones while her arm's still smoking," Lucida called out clearly from Tub Seven, vaulted already onto the brick coping with ammonia dripping down her bare shins, voice flat and brisk as an account-clerk's, "and she drops her chin onto that purple stripe you're trailing in the soot, master Sticho, and then you aren't explaining three asses of spilled brimstone to Gaius Lucceius—you're explaining why Senator Regulus's hem has blister-grease baked into Tyrian dye that fulling-earth won't ever lift!"

Sticho's raised arm checked half an inch in mid-air. His small, red-veined eyes jerked down: sure enough, in his rage he had let two palms of the damp purple *praetexta* border droop dangerously close to the black charcoal smear beside the overturned pan. He jerked the cloth up against his fat chest as if bitten.

Before he could recover his breath to turn the quince-rod on Lucida, Corvus was already on one knee beside the convulsing girl, smothering the last sputtering crumb of sulfur on her forearm under dry grey Cimolian clay, pressing firmly to cut air off the burn without rubbing skin loose, then lifting the pan back onto cold stone with the toe of his foot.

"Who asked the eight-as street-bitch to count my wool?" Sticho snarled, stepping across the drain toward Lucida, quince-rod twitching down toward her bare wet thigh. "Get back in Tub Seven before I dock both your tallies to nothing and throw you in the alley!"

Lucida did not flinch back—stepping back put your heel in the vat—and instead dropped into her most deferential, honey-quick workshop voice, palms open so he saw the old dye-stains under her nails. "Dock me if I'm wrong, master! Look at the stripe under your thumb: genuine double-boiled purple goes black forever if vinegar or sulfur-ash hits it warm, unless you rub cold unwashed sheep's wool-grease along the white edge *first* to stop the bleed before rinsing. My mother dyed borders for Caelian houses twelve years. Give me a finger of raw lanolin from the carders' pot and clean vinegar, and I'll keep that border spotless *and* grease the mute's burn in the same turn so you don't lose a pair of hands before supper."

Sticho stared from the smudge near the purple hem to Lucida's stained cuticles, then down at the flax-haired girl crouched white-faced against the wall with her teeth clamped into her own knee to stop shaking. Greed and laziness wrestled visibly behind Sticho's sweaty brow: beating a mute slave cost nothing, except Lucceius skinned overseers who spoiled senatorial orders, and hiring another sulfur-tender before dark cost four asses.

"Five breaths," Sticho grunted, jabbing the quince-rod toward the carders' grease-crock under the arch. "Touch that border with dirty fingers and you leave your shift behind to pay the wool-bill. You—long-legs! Back in Ten, or you tread for love!"

Corvus caught Lucida's eye for half a beat as he rose from the brick, making sure Sticho's stick was down, before swinging smoothly back into Tub Ten without wasting a word.

Under the arch Lucida scooped two generous gobs of pale, rank-smelling unwashed sheep's *oesypum*—raw lanolin skimmed from first-boil fleeces, worth its weight in bronze up on the hills once Beroe perfumed it with rose-leaves and sold it back to matrons in alabaster pots as Athenian skin-balm. With a clean linen scrap and three drops of cold water she lifted the soot speck off the white wool a finger-breadth shy of Senator Regulus's purple stripe, easy as wiping dust off an apple, handed the unblemished *toga* to the hovering Greek carder, and dropped onto her heels beside the mute girl in the shadow behind the charcoal bin.

Up close, the burn along the inside of the girl's skinny left forearm ran five inches from wrist-bone toward elbow: angry blistered red round the edges, dead parchment-white at the center where the hot iron lip had kissed deepest, dusted grey with Corvus's clay. When Lucida rinsed the loose grit gently off with diluted cold vinegar, never breaking the risen water-blisters, the girl's whole ribcage locked rigid as plank-oak, grey eyes huge and dry in a soot-streaked face, waiting to be slapped for crying.

"Hold onto my ankle if you need to bite something," Lucida whispered against the flax hair, laying thick, chilled sheep's wool-fat cleanly across the raw skin and binding it loose with a torn strip of bleached rag ripped silently from the bottom of Lucceius's scrap-pile while Sticho's back was turned. She slipped an extra walnut-sized lump of stolen lanolin inside her own waist-knot for Corvus's shin that night. "Don't let them put cow-dung or cobwebs on it after the shutters close, hear me? Dung rots a burn green in three days. Keep the sheep-fat wet. What do they call you?"

The girl stared at Lucida's mouth, then lifted her unburned right hand and traced four quick, practiced Roman letters in the spilled fuller's-chalk on the brick floor between their knees. No illiterate cattle-child after all; someone had taught her tallies once before she hit the Subura vats.

*S · U · R · A*

Lucida rubbed her palm across the chalk before Sticho turned back down the coping, tapped two fingers against her own breastbone—*"Lucida,"* mouthed clear and slow so the girl could read lips—then pointed one chin-nod toward Tub Ten where Corvus's dark head rose and fell in steady, relentless rhythm above the steaming vat: *"Corvus."*

Sura looked from Lucida's face across the steam to Corvus, then down at the clean wool-fat bandage soothing the fire in her arm. Very deliberately, she touched two fingers of her right hand to her parted front teeth and blew a tiny, nearly inaudible double whistle—*whit-whit*, low as a hedge-thrush in thorn-bush—pointing one flick of her eyes toward Sticho's boots turning at the far end of the row.

Lucida was back chest-deep in Tub Seven, heels churning wet Carinae linen in stinking chamber-lye as if she had never left the rail, two heartbeats before Sticho's shadow crossed the tub.

At dusk, when the bronze tintinnabulum by the street gate clanged end-of-shift and legs came out of the vats wrinkled dead-white and raw-spotted to the groin, Sticho counted out twelve copper *asses* into Corvus's palm and seven—docking one out of pure spite "for vinegar-rag"—into Lucida's. Nineteen asses for half a day's skin burned in other men's piss: four sesterces and three quarters. Five loaves of coarse plebeian bread and a ladle of boiled chickpeas, with nine asses left toward next market-day's straw.

As they passed the doorway into the darkening lane, Sura crouched in the courtyard ash scraping cold brimstone crusts back into an iron pan with one hand; without lifting her flax head, she let go that tiny two-note thrush breath between her teeth as Lucida's shadow touched her bare shoulder.

Outside on the Clivus, lamp-lighters were reaching tarred tapers up to the bronze hooks beside the *popina* counters, where cauldrons of grey pulse and salt-pork rind bubbled over olive-stone embers. Before climbing the seventy-eight steps back to Berenice's mortar, they washed their burned legs to the knee at the public travertine basin at the corner of the Vicus Cuprius, standing in icy Marcia-water overflow beside two tired Syrian litter-porters rinsing mule-sponges. For four copper *asses* Lucida bought two scored round loaves still warm from a cellar oven and a twist of pickled sprats wrapped in fig-leaf, testing every loaf on the heel of her palm before paying so the baker could not palm off morning stale-crust on dusk light. They ate walking, tearing bread half-for-half without looking down, slicking salt fish-oil off their thumbs, stepping wide around the dark wet patches beneath overhanging windows—already moving like people born inside the wall rather than outside it.

* * *

Nobody who had never lain awake on the sixth floor of a Subura *insula* after sunset understood what Rome weighed.

All day long, while the sun stood above the hills, Caesar's old municipal law kept heavy freight-wheels outside the gates so senators' litters and advocates' chalked *togae* could pass without having an axle crush their insteps. The instant darkness fell, every gate from the Flaminia to the Ostiensis burst its dam at once.

Lying on thin barley-straw behind Berenice's tattered reed screen, back pressed flat against Corvus's warm spine so the single coarse fuller's cloak covered four shoulders instead of two, Lucida felt the whole timber skeleton of the tenement shudder and groan like a leaking grain-ship in winter swells. Six storeys straight down in the black canyon of the Argiletum, iron-tired ox-wagons hauling twenty-ton Luna marble drums for Antoninus's temple repairs, pine roof-trusses sixty feet long, casks of Baetican oil, and clanking carts of night-soil rumbled axle-to-axle over basalt paving stones. Torch-glare from the drivers' link-boys swept red across Berenice's smoky ceiling joists in rhythmic flashes; teamsters cracked leather whips and screamed obscenities in Oscan, Greek, and camp-Latin whenever two hubs locked on the narrow turn beneath the Firewall; every heavy jolt sent a dry trickle of old lime-mortar pattering like mice inside the hollow wattle wall beside Lucida's ear.

Across the room by the open dormer, Berenice sat wrapped in an old ragged wolf-pelt, holding a heated tile of smoking henbane-seeds under a funnel to her broken left jaw for the autumn tooth-ache. Autumn rain had begun to slant cold off the Esquiline, hissing faintly when stray drops hit her little brazier.

"Awake, Sabine?" Lucida whispered without turning over, feeling the steady, wide rise of Corvus's ribs behind her shoulder-blades. Even washed at the street fountain, both of them still smelled faintly of ammoniac fuller's-earth under sheep-fat and boiled chickpeas.

"Counting carts," Corvus murmured back, breath warm against the damp nape of her neck. "Nineteen stone-sledges in the last quarter-hour heading toward the Campus, all pulled by triple yokes. Somebody's laying heavy foundations north of Hadrian's portico before the winter frost locks the lime-pits."

Lucida turned inside the narrow cloak-fold until they lay nose-to-nose on the straw: three inches between their chins in the dark, the way they had slept in kiln-sheds and reed-huts whenever January wind came off Soracte, except here in Rome every inch of air felt denser, charged with iron and strangers' breath. In the red flicker passing across the rafters from a torch-wagon six floors below, she saw the dark purple bruise under Corvus's left eye where the Sacra Via dandy had grazed him, and the pale line of his mouth.

She reached down between them, found his right hand where it rested over the hidden floor-brick—even half-asleep he had moved their straw pallet directly over the loose hearth-tile hiding the bronze *signaculum* and his cedar birth-diptych—and rubbed warm stolen sheep's lanolin into the raw ammonia cracks between his fingers with slow, firm strokes.

Corvus let his breath out quietly through his nose. His fingers curled once around hers, rough skin catching rough skin.

"You shouldn't have stood up to Sticho over the mute girl," he said, so low Berenice by the dormer could not hear over the wagon-thunder. "If he'd swung that quince-stick at your ribs while I was three tubs off—"

"Then you'd have broken his kneecap with the clay-bucket and we'd be sleeping in the rain tonight," Lucida whispered back, pressing her thumb flat over his lips for half a heartbeat before he could lecture her. "I got us three ounces of clean sheep-fat, kept all eight asses bar one, and made an ally by the courtyard door who can whistle faster than Scaeva walks. Don't play bailiff with me, Lucius Cassius. Save the speech for the Forum."

His mouth twitched under her thumb—not quite a smile, something tighter and warmer. He shifted his hand up from hers, hooked one knuckle gently behind her braid to pull a stray wet strand of hair out of the grease on his shin, and left his palm resting flat against the base of her throat, right over the hollow where her pulse beat, half an inch above the invisible olive-pip mark under her shift. Neither of them pulled back. His palm was hot from fuller's-lime and fifteen years of knowing every bone in her face; for three wagon-passes below, neither spoke, listening to rain-water gurgle down the lead gutter outside.

"Tomorrow I go down past the Firewall to the Temple of Saturn," Corvus said into the dark. "Before the fourth hour, while the notice-boards are quiet. If my father's three-thousand-sesterce bond was bought up by one of Vedius's debt-collectors alongside your mother's chest, my name is already chalked on an agent's slate. Better I find the slate before the slate finds us."

"Go dirty," Lucida told him instantly. "Don't wash your tunic at the trough first. A boy in a washed tunic reading treasury boards looks like an heir or a debtor; a boy carrying a broken charcoal-hod looks like furniture."

Over by the dormer, Berenice lifted her jaw off the smoking henbane funnel, spat black saliva onto the wet lead gutter outside, and turned her one glittering obsidian eye across the dark garret toward the reed screen where their four bare feet stuck out from under the fuller's cloak.

"Whispering sums at fifteen," the old woman rasped over the rumble of timber-wagons shaking the floor-planks. "Listen to the pair of you—brick-mice planning which side of Caesar's cheese to gnaw first! Take an old Syrian's word before you wear out your knees on the stairs, children: Rome has two doors up out of the mud—the knife and the bronze scale. And neither one of them was built wide enough for two abreast."

Berenice pinched out her tallow wick between calloused thumb and forefinger, plunging the garret into pitch-black smelled through with bitter almond, burnt henbane, and cold October rain.

Behind the screen, Lucida reached inside her shift until her fingertips touched her half of the broken terracotta brick-stamp, sharp-edged and dry against her ribs; with her other hand she gripped Corvus's wrist under the rough cloak and held on hard until the wagons below blurred into sleep.
