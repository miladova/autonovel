# Chapter Fourteen

## Tiro

He went to Tibur on the day after the panel, on foot, at dawn, with a purse at his belt and a lie in his mouth.

"To see a man about a dog," he told Prisca, and she looked at him and said, "You have never owned a dog," and he said, "I am thinking of starting," and she said, "You are a terrible liar, Tiro," and he said, "I know," and went out.

The road to Tibur went up through the olive country and the stone quarries, and it took four hours at a good pace. He had walked it four times in eight years. He knew every milestone and the place where the stream came down under the road and the shrine of the fountain with the worn face, and he walked it now with his mind going round and round like a wheel with a broken spoke.

The smithy was at the bottom of the town, by the gate, where the carts came in.

It was a good smithy as smithies went: a stone shed with a wide door and a thatched roof and a yard with a well and a heap of charcoal and a goat tied to a post. There were two men working and a boy working the bellows, and the boy was fourteen and had his mother's hands, and he was taller than Tiro remembered and thinner, and there was a burn on his forearm from the sparks, and he did not look up when Tiro came into the yard.

Gallio the smith came out wiping his hands.

"You," he said.

"Me." Tiro put the purse on the bench. "I want to talk about the boy."

"The boy is not for sale."

"Everything is for sale."

"Not this boy. He is worth more to me in the shop than he is in silver. He has the knack. He can shoe a horse and he can temper a blade and he is not a fool, which is more than I can say for the last three I bought." Gallio looked at the purse, and then at Tiro. "You are his father."

"I am."

"You have been his father four times in eight years, and each time you have stood in that street and looked at him and gone away again. He told me. He said: *my father came and looked at me and went away.* He said it the way a man says the weather."

Tiro's face did not move.

"How much?"

"Twelve hundred."

"Last year it was six."

"Last year he was twelve and could not do anything but sweep. Now he can temper a blade." Gallio picked up the purse and weighed it in his hand. "You have two hundred here."

"I have two hundred and eleven."

"Then you have two hundred and eleven and the price is twelve hundred, and I am not a hard man, and I will take it in instalments, and it will take you five years, and in five years he will be nineteen and worth two thousand and I will raise the price again." He put the purse down. "That is the way of it. I am sorry. I am not a monster. I am a man with a business."

Tiro stood in the yard and looked at his son.

The boy had stopped working the bellows. He was standing with his hands hanging and his face carefully blank, and he was looking at Tiro the way a dog looks at a man who has kicked it before and might kick it again.

"Rufus," Tiro said.

"Sir."

The word went into him.

"Come here."

The boy came, and stood, and Tiro put his hand on the back of his neck, which was the only thing he could think of to do, and the boy endured it.

"I am going to buy your freedom," Tiro said. "I have been saying it for eight years and I have not done it, and I am going to do it this year. Do you hear me? This year."

"Yes, sir."

"Do you believe me?"

The boy looked at him.

"No," he said.

Tiro took his hand away.

"Then I will have to do it and not talk about it," he said, and the boy nodded, and went back to the bellows, and Tiro stood in the yard for a while and then took up his purse and walked back to Rome.

He was on the road by the third hour, and he was halfway home, at the place where the road bends and you can see the whole plain and the city lying in it like a stain, when a litter came down the road toward him with four bearers and a man walking beside it.

The man was Faustus.

The litter stopped. Faustus said something to the bearers and came forward on foot, and he was dusty and he had been riding, and he looked, for the first time in all the years Tiro had known him, like a man who was not entirely in control of things.

"Tiro," he said. "I have been to the house. They told me you had gone to Tibur. I have come after you."

"Then you have wasted a morning."

"I have come to make you an offer." Faustus fell into step beside him. "You went to the smithy. I know what he asked. I know what the boy is worth and I know that you will never have it, not in five years, not in ten, because a steward's peculium is a slow thing and because the domina pays you what she pays you." He took a folded paper from his sleeve. "This is the boy's manumission. It is signed by Gallio and sealed by the duumvirs of Tibur and it is dated the day before yesterday. It cost eleven hundred sesterces of my own money and it is in my hand, and the boy is free from the Kalends of August."

Tiro stopped in the road.

"You are a liar," he said.

"I am a great many things." Faustus held the paper up. "Look at it. You cannot read it, but you can look at the seal, and you know the seal of the duumvirs, and you know that I would not forge a public seal for a boy in a smithy." He put it back in his sleeve. "He is free. He does not know it. He will not know it until somebody tells him, and the somebody will be me, or you, or nobody at all."

"What do you want?"

"The box," Faustus said.

Tiro did not move.

"You know the one," Faustus said. "The small one, of myrtle wood, in the strongbox in her room. There is a letter in it, in the hand of my master, written seventeen years ago, and a stylus. I want the letter."

"You want me to rob her."

"I want you to save her." Faustus's voice was quiet and reasonable. "Think, Tiro. On the Ides she is to be tried. If she is convicted, the court will seize the house and everything in it, and the strongbox will be opened by the prefect's clerks, and the letter will be read out in the basilica. And what does the letter say? It says that a woman in this city took money to keep silence about a death in the house of the Claudii Corvi. And then the panel will ask: what death? And the answer will be the death of a consul, seventeen years ago, in his own shrine, with a pen in his throat. And she will be tried for that instead, and she will be executed, and every woman in that house will be sold, and your boy will be free and you will be dead." He paused. "Whereas if the letter is not in the box on the Ides, the clerks will find loan tablets and deeds and a purchase document and nothing else, and she will be tried for the death of Vettius, which she did not do, and she may even be acquitted."

Tiro looked at the road ahead, at the city lying in the plain.

"You are doing this to protect your master," he said.

"I am doing this to protect my master and myself and the house I have served for twenty years, and I am also telling you the truth, which is that the letter will destroy her." Faustus spread his hands. "You may believe both. A man may do a thing for a bad reason and it may still be the right thing to do. You have been in the world long enough to know that."

"And the boy?"

"The boy is free either way," Faustus said. "I have already paid. I am not a man who takes back a payment. Give me the letter and the paper is in your hand tonight; refuse me and the paper is still in my hand, and I will keep it, and when I go to Baetica in the autumn I will take it with me, and he will be free in law and a slave in fact, which is the worst of both."

They walked on in silence for a while.

"Why?" Tiro said. "Why do you care so much? You did not kill the old man. You took money from the son. You could go to the prefect yourself and tell him everything and be a witness and take your chances."

Faustus laughed. It was a small, dry, entirely mirthless sound.

"Because I am a freedman," he said, "and a freedman who informs on his patron is a dead man, and because in twenty years I have never had a thing of my own, and because—" he stopped.

"Because what?"

"Because when I was eleven years old," Faustus said, "I was sold into that house, and the old consul used to have me stand at the foot of his couch while he ate, and he would throw the bones on the floor for me to pick up, and he would laugh, and the lady of the house would look at the wall. And when he died I was twenty-four years old and I stood in the atrium with the others and I felt nothing at all, and then I went out into the garden and I found that I was crying, and I did not know why, and I have never known why." He looked at Tiro. "I did not kill him. But I have thought about him every day for twenty years, and when the son came to me with money I took it, and I have told myself ever since that I took it for the family, and it is a lie. I took it because I wanted him dead and I could not do it myself."

The road went down toward the city. A cart passed them, going the other way, and the driver called out a greeting, and neither of them answered.

"Tonight," Faustus said. "At the second hour of the night. Bring it to the shrine of the crossroads at the foot of the Palatine, and the paper will be in my hand, and I will take the letter and burn it in front of you, and you will never see me again."

He turned and walked back toward the litter.

Tiro stood in the road for a long time.

Then he went on into the city, and he came to the house at dusk, and he ate his dinner in the kitchen with the women, and he listened to them talk about the dinner and about the prefect's men and about the girl, and at the second hour of the night, when the house was quiet, he went up the back stairs to the domina's room.

The door was not locked. She had never locked it. She had said once that a woman who locks her door in her own house is a woman who has already decided that her household is her enemy, and she had looked at him when she said it.

He stood in the dark room and listened to her breathe.

She was asleep, on her side, with her hand under her cheek, and in the light from the window she looked younger and smaller and entirely without defences, and he had known her for seventeen years and she had never once let him see her asleep.

The key was on the table by the bed, on a cord.

He took it. He went to the strongbox and opened it, and the lock made a sound that seemed to fill the room, and he stood still with his heart going, and she did not wake.

The box of myrtle wood was at the bottom under the deeds.

He lifted it out. It was lighter than he expected. He stood in the dark holding it, and he thought about the letter inside it, in a hand he could not read, and about the fact that he was fifty-two years old and had never stolen anything in his life, and that the first thing he had ever stolen was the only thing his mistress had never told him about.

He put the box inside his tunic and closed the strongbox and put the key back on the table by the bed, and then he stood over her for a moment.

"Domina," he said, very quietly, and she did not wake.

He went out.

He did not go straight to the shrine.

He went up to his room first and took the box out of his tunic and put it on the bed and looked at it. It was small and dark and beautifully made, with a bronze lock and a lid that fitted so well that you could not see the seam, and it had been in her strongbox for seventeen years and she had put her hand on it in the dark, and he had watched her do it and had said nothing, because a good steward does not remark on what his mistress keeps.

He opened it.

The letter was folded twice, with the seal broken, and the paper had gone brown at the folds. There was a stylus beside it, bronze, worn smooth at the end, the sort of thing a schoolboy would have.

He could not read the letter.

He sat on the bed with it in his hands and turned it over and over, and the not being able to read it was, for the first time in his life, a physical thing, a wall he could put his hand on.

*She taught herself to read,* he thought. *She told me once. She said a man in a house taught her when she was seventeen and she learned in a fortnight and it was the thing that made her.*

He put the letter back in the box and closed it and put it inside his tunic, against his chest.

The shrine of the crossroads was at the foot of the hill, a little square building with a worn step and a niche with three figures in it and a lamp that somebody kept filled. Faustus was waiting with two men and a lantern.

"You have it."

"I have it."

"Give it to me."

"The paper first."

Faustus took the folded paper from his sleeve and gave it to him. Tiro unfolded it and looked at it, and could not read a word of it, and looked at the seal, and it was the seal of the duumvirs of Tibur, and he folded it again and put it inside his tunic.

Then he took out the box.

"I want to see it burned," he said.

"You will see it burned."

Tiro opened the box. There was the letter, folded twice, with the seal broken, and a stylus of bronze, worn smooth at the end, the sort of thing a boy would have in his satchel at school.

He looked at the stylus for a moment. Then he took out the letter and held it over the lamp.

And Faustus took it out of his hand.

"Thank you," he said, and put it in his sleeve, and nodded to the two men, and they came forward and took Tiro by the arms.

"What is this?"

"I told you I would burn it," Faustus said. "I did not tell you when."

"Give it to me."

"No." Faustus picked up the empty box and turned it over in his hands, and then put it on the step of the shrine. "You are going to go home and go to bed and say nothing. If you say anything to her, I will have the boy's paper revoked on the ground that it was obtained by fraud, and I will have him sold to a man I know in Puteoli who buys boys for the galleys." He turned to go, and then stopped. "Tiro. You did the right thing. You will not believe it tonight, and perhaps you never will, but you did. The letter would have hanged her."

He went away up the hill with his two men, and the lantern went with him.

Tiro stood in front of the shrine with the empty box in his hands.

Then he walked home, and let himself in by the kitchen door, and went up to his room, and sat on the bed, and took out the paper and looked at it for a long time in the dark.

He could not read it.

He could not read a single line of it. He could read four words: his own name, her name, and *the old man first*, which he had copied out on a tablet from the papyrus, letter by letter, in the dark. He had been a slave until he was thirty-five and nobody had ever taught him, and he had never minded, because a man who cannot read is a man who cannot be caught in a lie, and because a steward who cannot read the accounts is a steward who is trusted with other things. He had never minded in his life.

He minded now.

He sat on the bed with the paper in his hands and his hands shaking, and he thought: *I have given away the only thing she kept, and I do not even know what it said.*

He went down to the kitchen at the third hour because he could not sit still, and Prisca was there.

She was always there at that hour. She came down before the girls to light the oven and put the water on, and she had a shawl over her nightdress and her hair in a plait and she was not pleased to see him.

"You are up early."

"I could not sleep."

"No." She looked at him. "You look like a man who has been up all night doing something he should not have been doing."

Tiro sat down on the bench by the oven.

Prisca put the kettle on the hook and swung it over the fire and stood with her back to him, and the kitchen was quiet except for the sound of the fire taking hold.

"Prisca."

"What?"

"I want you to read something for me."

She turned around.

"You want me to read something."

"Yes."

"You have never asked me to read anything in eleven years." She wiped her hands on her apron. "You have never asked anyone to read anything. You told me once that a man who cannot read is a man who cannot be caught in a lie, and I said that a man who cannot read is a man who has to trust whoever can, and you said that you had never met anyone worth trusting."

"I remember."

"So what is it?"

He took the paper out of his tunic and put it on the table.

Prisca looked at it. Then she looked at him, for a long moment, and then she came and sat down on the bench opposite and picked it up and unfolded it and held it close to the fire.

"Where did you get this?"

"That does not matter. Read it."

"Tiro—"

"Read it."

She read it.

She read it slowly, moving her finger under the words, and it took her a long time, because it was a clerk's hand and the light was bad and because she had learned to read from an old woman in a laundry in Ostia and had never had much practice.

Then she put it down on the table.

"It is a manumission," she said.

"Read it out."

"*Gallio, smith, of Tibur, with the consent of the duumvirs, manumits Rufus, his slave, aged fourteen years, from the Kalends of August next, in consideration of the sum of eleven hundred sesterces paid by Faustus, freedman of Gaius Claudius Corvus.*" She looked up. "It is sealed by the duumvirs. It is a proper paper. He is free."

Tiro sat very still.

He had known. He had known since the shrine, since he had looked at the seal and had not been able to read a word of it. But there is a difference between knowing a thing and hearing a woman read it out in a kitchen at the third hour of the morning, and the difference went through him like a knife.

He put his hand over his face.

"Tiro."

"He is free."

"He is free from the Kalends of August." Prisca put the paper down. "What did you give him?"

He did not answer.

"Tiro. What did you give him?"

"A letter."

"What letter?"

"A letter out of the box in her room." He said it flatly, with his hand still over his face. "A letter from a man, seventeen years old. He said it would hang her if the court found it. He said that if I gave it to him he would burn it and she would be safe and the boy would be free, and I believed the first part and I wanted the second part, and I have been sitting upstairs for four hours trying to work out which of them I believed more."

Prisca was silent.

"You went into her room," she said.

"Yes."

"While she was asleep."

"Yes."

"And you took the one thing in this house she has never spoken about, and you gave it to the steward of the man who is trying to destroy her, and you did it for a paper." Prisca's voice was not raised. That was the thing about Prisca: she had run a household for eleven years and she had learned that the quiet voice does the work. "Tiro. She gave you a place when you had nothing. She gave you a name and a room and a seat at her table and she never once asked you what you had done before. I have been in this house eleven years and I have never seen her trust anyone the way she trusts you."

"I know."

"And you did it for a paper."

"For my son."

"Your son." Prisca stood up. "Your son has been four times in eight years, Tiro. I know, because you told me when you were drunk at the Saturnalia, and I have never told anyone, and I have thought about it every time you went to Tibur and came back and sat in the kitchen and did not eat." She put the kettle on the table. "You did not do it for your son. You did it because you could not bear to be a man who had done nothing for his son. There is a difference, and it is the difference between a father and a coward, and I am not sure which one you are this morning."

Tiro looked at the table.

"Neither am I," he said.

Prisca sat down again.

She was quiet for a while. The fire took hold of the wood and the kitchen began to be warm, and somewhere above them the house creaked, and in an hour the girls would come down and the day would begin.

"Are you going to tell her?" she said.

"Yes."

"When?"

"Before the trial."

"She will turn you out."

"I know."

"She will turn you out and you will have nowhere to go, and you are fifty-two years old with a scar on your face, and no one in this city will take a steward who has been turned out of the Myrtle for stealing from the mistress." Prisca looked at him. "I could read it to you again, if you like. The paper."

"No."

"Why not?"

Tiro looked up at her.

"Because I have heard it once," he said, "and I have waited eight years to hear it, and I thought that it would be the best morning of my life." He picked the paper up and folded it and put it inside his tunic. "And it is the worst. And I would like to sit in this kitchen for an hour before the girls come down and not think about anything at all."

Prisca got up and cut him a piece of bread and put it in front of him, and then she went back to the oven, and neither of them said anything for a long time.

At the fourth hour of the morning he heard the house wake: the kitchen girls, the water, the door, the fountain. He heard Antonia come down and speak to Prisca in the atrium, and he heard her laugh at something, and the sound of it went through him like a blade.

He went down.

She was in the atrium with a tablet in her hand, and she looked up when she saw him, and her face changed.

"Tiro. What is it?"

"Domina." He stood in front of her. "I have done a thing."

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she turned and walked to her room without a word, and he followed her up the stairs, and she opened the strongbox and knelt down and went through it, and came up with her hand empty.

She sat back on her heels.

"Where is it?"

"I gave it to Faustus."

She did not move. The morning light came in through the shutters and lay on the floor between them.

"Say it again."

"I gave it to Faustus. Last night, at the shrine of the crossroads. He gave me the manumission of my son, and I gave him the letter." His voice was steady, and he was ashamed of how steady it was. "He told me it would hang you. He told me that if the court seized the house the clerks would find it and read it out in the basilica and you would be tried for the death of the old consul. I believed him. I still believe him. And I wanted the paper for my boy."

Antonia looked at him.

"Seventeen years," she said.

"Domina—"

"Seventeen years, and I have told you everything. Not everything; I have not told you that. I have not told anyone that." She stood up, slowly. "I gave you a house and a name and a place at my table, and I have never once asked you what you did before I found you, and I have never once looked at you the way the men in the Forum look at a man with a scar. I trusted you with my life. I trusted you with the lives of every woman in this house." Her voice was quiet. "And you sold me for eleven hundred sesterces and a piece of paper you cannot read."

"I did it to save you."

"You did it for your son. Do not dress it up. I would have done the same for a child, and I would have hated myself, and I would have told you afterward, and I would not have stood in the atrium in the morning and said *I did it to save you*." She turned away. "Get out."

"Domina—"

"Get out of my sight, Tiro." She said it without raising her voice. "Go down to the kitchen and sit there and do not speak to me and do not leave the house. I will decide what to do with you when I have decided what to do with everything else."

He went.

At the door he stopped.

"There is a thing I have not told you," he said. "The papyrus. The one I took off the man in the alley. I could not read it, so I kept it. It has three words on it, in a bad hand, and I do not know what they are."

Antonia turned.

"Where is it?"

"In my room. Under the mattress."

"Bring it to me."

He brought it. She took it and unfolded it and read it, standing in the middle of her own room with the strongbox open at her feet, and her face did not change at all.

"What does it say?" Tiro asked.

"It says: *the old man first.*" She folded it. "It is a note from Faustus to his man, telling him the order of the deaths. Your master first, and then whoever was in the way." She looked at him. "You have brought me the one piece of paper in this city that could hang him, and you did it by accident, and you gave away the only thing I ever asked anyone to keep."

She put the papyrus in her sleeve.

"Go and sit in the kitchen," she said. "And Tiro—if you leave this house tonight, do not come back."

He went down the stairs and sat in the kitchen with the women, and Calybe gave him bread and he did not eat it, and the morning went on around him, and at the ninth hour a man came to the door with a message from the prefecture, and at the tenth hour the women began to scream.
