# Chapter Sixteen

## Marcus

He had made the plan for the Nones the way a young man makes a plan: thoroughly, and without any understanding of what could go wrong.

There was a horse at a stable behind the Temple of Vesta, hired for three days with money he had got by selling a signet ring that had belonged to his mother. There was a cloak and a pair of boots and a purse with forty denarii in it, and a knife, and a letter to the master of a boat at Ostia that had cost him two denarii and a great deal of charm. There was a route: out through the Viminal gate, north along the salt road to the crossing at Fidenae, then west to the river and down the Tiber to Ostia, four days, three if the roads were dry.

And there was the roof.

He had gone over it twice in the dark to be sure of it: out of his window onto the ridge, along the ridge to the corner above the fig tree, down onto the wall, and into the street. The drop from the wall was twelve feet and there was a water trough in the street below, and he had put a sack over the edge of the trough so that he would not make a noise.

He did all of it. He went out of the window at the second hour with his boots in his hand and his cloak over his arm, and he went along the ridge and down over the wall and into the street, and the two men at the gate never saw him, and he walked north through the sleeping city with his heart going like a drum.

He was at the Viminal gate by the third hour.

She was not there.

He waited in the shadow of a tomb on the other side of the road, which is what a man does when he is early and does not want to be seen, and the gate stood open with the watchman's fire burning in the niche, and the watchman was a fat man with a stick who sat on a stool and did not look at anything.

At the fourth hour a cart came through, and then two men with a lantern, and then nothing.

At the fifth hour the sky began to lighten.

He told himself that she had been delayed. He told himself that Prisca had caught her, or that the domina had kept her up, or that she had gone out by the kitchen door and had had to wait for a watchman. He told himself eleven different things, and each of them was reasonable, and underneath all of them was a thing he would not look at, which was that she had told him, in as many words, that she might not come.

*I will be there*, she had said. *And if you are not there, I will not come looking for you.*

He had said that he would be there, and he was, and she was not, and he stood in the shadow of a tomb on the Viminal road while the sky went from black to grey to the colour of a fish's belly, and the city woke up around him: a baker's cart, a woman with a bucket, a boy driving goats, a man selling hot wine out of a pot.

A watchman came past and looked at him.

"You waiting for someone?"

"Yes."

"They are not coming," the watchman said, with the cheerfulness of a man who has seen everything twice. "They never come. I have been on this gate nine years. I have seen eleven men waiting in the dark for someone who was going to come, and two of them came. The other nine stood there until the sun was up and then went home." He spat in the road. "Go home, sir. Whatever it was, it was not worth standing in the cold for."

Marcus went to the house.

He went the long way, by the Velabrum and up the Vicus Tuscus, because a man who is going to be seen standing in the street should not come by the alley. The street was empty except for a baker's boy and a woman washing the step of a shop, and the door of the Myrtle was shut, and there was no lamp in the niche.

He stood in the doorway of the fuller's shop opposite and watched the house for an hour.

At the fifth hour the kitchen door opened and a girl came out with a bucket and went to the fountain at the corner, and came back, and the door shut. At the sixth hour a man came up the street with a cart and stopped at the house next door and unloaded jars. At the seventh hour the door of the Myrtle opened and Prisca came out with a basket and went toward the market, and Marcus stepped out of the doorway and said, "Prisca."

She stopped as though she had been struck.

"You," she said.

"Is she awake?"

"Go away."

"Prisca—"

"Go away," Prisca said, and her face was white. "You are not to be here. She is not to see you. There is a thing happening in that house this morning and you are the last person in Rome who should be standing in this street." She took him by the arm and pulled him into the doorway, and her hand was shaking. "They have taken the cook. Maro took her three days ago and she has confessed and the confession will hang your—" she stopped herself. "And now there are two men in the alley behind the house who have been there since before dawn, and a litter at the corner, and the domina has been up all night, and the girl has been crying."

"What girl?"

"Leda." Prisca looked at him. "You do not know."

"Know what?"

"Go home," she said. "Go home, Marcus, go home, go home—"

The door of the Myrtle opened.

Two men came out with Leda between them.

She was not struggling. That was the thing he would remember: that she was not struggling, and that she was walking, and that her hands were folded in front of her, and that she had her shawl around her shoulders as though she were going to the market. One of the men had her by the elbow. The other walked behind with his hand near his belt.

There was a third man at the door, and it was Faustus.

Marcus started forward and Prisca caught at his sleeve and he pulled free, and he was across the street before anyone had time to think about it.

"Take your hands off her."

The man holding her elbow turned. He was a big man, forty or so, with the flat face of a man who has been in the army and has not enjoyed it, and he looked at Marcus the way a man looks at a dog in the road.

"Stand aside," he said.

"That is my—" Marcus stopped. "That is a free woman. She has been claimed free before the prefect. You have no right to take her."

"She is a witness in a capital case," said Faustus, from the door, "and she is being removed for her own protection, by order of the prefect's office, and here is the order with the seal on it, and if you lay a hand on my men I will have you arrested in the street." He was pale and he was sweating and his voice was not quite steady. "Marcus. Go home."

Leda looked at Marcus.

Her face was calm. That was the second thing he would remember, and he would think about it for years: that her face was calm, and that she looked at him the way you look at a person on the other side of a river.

"Go home," she said.

"Leda—"

"Go home." She said it clearly, and then she said, more quietly, "I am all right. They are taking me to a house in the country. There will be a maid and two men and I will be brought back for the trial." She paused. "Do not do anything stupid. That is the only thing I have ever asked you."

The man with the flat face moved her toward the litter.

Marcus took a step.

He did not decide to. It was the same thing that had happened in the stable yard with his father: his body did the thing before his mind had agreed to it, and he got a hand on the big man's shoulder and turned him, and the man let go of Leda's elbow and hit him in the stomach, once, with a fist like a mallet.

Marcus went down on the stones.

He heard Leda say his name. He heard Faustus say, "Get her in, get her in." He got a hand under himself and pushed up, and the big man hit him again, on the side of the head this time, and the street went away and came back, and when it came back the litter was moving up the hill.

He got up.

Prisca was there, and Tiro was there, and Tiro had him by the arm and was walking him into the alley behind the house, and Marcus was saying, "Let go of me, let go of me," and Tiro was saying nothing at all.

They got him into the kitchen and sat him on the bench, and Calybe's place at the oven was empty, and the fire was out, and there was a smell of burnt bread in the room that would not go away for a week.

Tiro put a cup of water in his hand.

"Drink."

"Get out of my way."

"Drink it," Tiro said, "and then I will tell you where they have taken her."

Marcus drank it.

"Ficulea," Tiro said. "A villa on the road, four hours north, with a wall and a gate. The domina has a man in the prefecture's clerks and he was told this morning. There are two guards and a woman, and the villa belongs to a freedman of the house of Corvus, and the order came from your father's steward and not from the prefect." He sat down on the bench opposite. "So now you know. And now I will tell you the other thing, which is that if you go out that door and up that road, you will be doing exactly what your father expects you to do."

Marcus looked at him.

"You know my father."

"I have been in this house seventeen years," Tiro said. "I know everyone."

"What does he expect me to do?"

"He expects you to go to Ficulea and make a scene at the gate, and be brought back, and be locked in your room until the Ides, and then married in the autumn." Tiro's scarred face was entirely without humour. "He is a man who arranges things. He has arranged this. He has put her somewhere he can produce her at the trial and somewhere you cannot get at her, and he has put a man on the road who will bring you home, and he is sitting in his study at this moment thinking that he has been clever."

"Then what should I do?"

"Nothing," Tiro said. "For three days. And on the Ides you should be in the basilica, in the crowd, where six hundred men can see the son of the consul-designate standing behind the woman his father is trying to destroy."

Marcus stared at him.

"That is not—"

"It is not a poem," Tiro said. "It is not a boy and a girl on a boat. It is a trial in a basilica and a city that loves a scandal, and the only weapon either of you has is that there will be people watching." He stood up. "You are a Claudius. You have a name that opens doors and a face that men recognize and a father who cannot afford to be seen to be cruel to his own son in public. Use it. Stand in the crowd on the Ides and let them see you, and when the girl is brought in to give her evidence, look at her, and let every man in that room see that the son of the house believes her."

"And that will save her?"

"No," said Tiro. "It might save her. It will not save the domina, because nothing will save the domina, and she knows it. But it might save the girl, because a witness who is believed is worth more than a witness who is not, and a girl who has a Claudius standing behind her is a girl who will be believed by men who are afraid of your father." He picked up the cup. "That is what I would do. I am a freedman and a murderer and a thief, and I have made a mess of my own life, and you should not take my advice about anything. But that is what I would do."

Marcus sat in the kitchen for a long time after Tiro went out.

Then he went home.

His father was in the tablinum with the door open, writing, and he did not look up when Marcus came in.

"You have been to the Vicus Tuscus," he said.

"Yes."

"You were knocked down in the street by a man of my household."

"Yes."

"Sit down."

"I will stand."

Corvus put down the stylus.

"She is at Ficulea," he said. "She is well. There is a maid with her and two men at the gate and a physician in the village who has been told to attend her if she is ill. She will be brought to the basilica on the Ides, and she will give her evidence, and she will be treated with every courtesy, and after the trial she will go to her mother at Surrentum with a purse from me." He folded his hands. "I have not harmed her, and I will not harm her, and whatever you think of me, I would like you to know that."

"I think you are a coward," Marcus said.

"Perhaps."

"You have taken a girl out of her house in the morning with two men and a litter, in front of the whole street, and you have put her in a villa in the country, and you are telling me it is for her protection."

"It is for her protection."

"From whom?"

Corvus looked at him for a long moment.

"From me," he said. "From my mother. From the man who paid for the poison and the men who would like to see the whole thing finished before the emperor comes home. From Maro, who would put her on the hook if he thought he could get away with it. And from you, Marcus, and your boat at Ostia, and your two knives and your purse of gold, because if you had taken her out of that house on the Nones she would have been a runaway slave in the provinces with a price on her head, and the first magistrate who stopped you would have been the end of it." He stood up. "I have done a great many things in my life that I am ashamed of. This is not one of them. This is the first thing I have done in a month that I can defend."

"Then let me see her."

"No."

"Let me send her a letter."

"No."

"Then I will go to Ficulea."

"You will not," Corvus said, "because I have given orders that you are not to leave this house until the Ides, and because there are two men at the gate, and because if you go over the wall they will bring you back, and it will be in the street, and it will be a scandal, and the scandal will be about you and not about her." He came around the table. "Three days, Marcus. That is all. Three days, and then the trial, and you may stand in the crowd and look at her as much as you like, and I will not stop you. After the trial she will be free and you will be married in the autumn and in ten years you will understand that I did the only thing that could be done."

Marcus looked at his father.

"I will never understand it," he said. "And I will never forgive you. And when you are consul, and when you are old, and when you are dying in that room upstairs, you will send for me and I will come, because I am your son and because I was raised to do my duty, and I will stand by your bed and I will not say the thing you want me to say."

He went out.

He went to his sister's room before the light went.

Caesia was sitting on the bed with her knees drawn up and a book open beside her, and she looked at him and said, "You are going to do something stupid."

"Yes."

"To Ficulea."

"Yes."

"Tonight."

"Yes."

She was quiet for a moment.

"Take me with you," she said.

"Caesia—"

"I mean it. I can ride. I can keep my mouth shut. I have four hundred sesterces of my own that grandmother gave me at the Saturnalia and a cloak and a good pair of boots, and if you go alone they will catch you at the gate before the second hour, and if there are two of us one of us can go round the back and make a noise and the other—"

"Caesia."

"I am fifteen years old," she said, "and in the spring I am going to be married to a man called Vinicius who has a nose like a shovel, and I have been in this house all my life and I have never done one thing that mattered." Her voice was shaking. "You are the only person in this family who has ever done a thing that mattered. Let me come."

Marcus sat down on the edge of the bed.

"If we go," he said, "and they catch us, they will not punish you, they will punish me, and they will do it by marrying you to somebody worse than Vinicius in a month. And if we are not caught, we will be in a fishing town in the provinces with no money and no name, and you will be the sister of a man who ran away with a singer, and no man will marry you at all." He took her hand. "I am not being noble. I am doing the sums. I have had a month in a room to do the sums."

Caesia looked at him.

"Is she worth it?" she said.

"I do not know what that means."

"Is she worth losing everything?"

Marcus sat with his sister's hand in his.

"I do not think it is a question of worth," he said. "I think a man is given a few moments in his life when he can be the person he actually is, and the rest of the time he is the person his family needs him to be. This is one of the moments. If I stay in this house and marry Vinicia Sabina and be a senator, I will be a good man and a correct one and I will think about this for the rest of my life." He let go of her hand. "I would rather be a fool in a fishing town."

Caesia wiped her face with the back of her wrist.

"You are a fool," she said.

"Yes."

"You are a better fool than anyone in this house." She got up and opened the chest and took out a purse and put it in his hand. "Four hundred sesterces. Take it."

"Caesia—"

"Take it, or I will throw it out of the window." She closed his fingers on it. "And when you are in the fishing town, send me word. Not to the house; to the wife of the bookseller in the Argiletum, her name is Vibia and she is a client of grandmother's and she is the only person in this city who can keep her mouth shut."

Marcus put the purse in his tunic.

"Thank you," he said.

"Go," said Caesia. "Go now, before I decide to be sensible."

He spent the afternoon in his room. He ate nothing. At the ninth hour he heard the house settle for the night: the slaves banking the fires, the doors being barred, his grandmother's step on the stairs, his father's voice in the tablinum.

At the second hour of the night he went out of the window onto the roof.

It was easy. He had done it as a boy, and he knew every tile. He went along the ridge and down over the wall by the fig tree and dropped into the street, and the two men at the gate did not see him because they were watching the gate.

He walked north.

He walked all night, along the Via Nomentana, past the tombs and the villas and the dark fields, and the moon went down and the road was black and the only sound was his own feet. He was twenty years old and he had been in the army and he had walked further than this in worse country, and he did not stop.

At dawn he came to Ficulea.

The villa was a low white building with a wall around it and a cypress at the gate and a boy sitting on the wall with a stick. Marcus stood in the road and looked at it, and the road was beginning to fill with carts, and a woman went by with a basket of eggs, and the sun came up over the hill behind the house.

There was a window on the second floor with a shutter open.

She was standing at it.

She saw him. He saw her see him. She did not wave; she put her hand flat on the shutter, and he put his hand flat on the air, and they stood like that for perhaps ten heartbeats.

Then a man came around the corner of the wall and saw him and shouted.

There were three of them by the time he reached the gate, and they were not gentle, and he did not fight back, because there was no point, and because he had seen her, and because in the whole of that night and that morning the only thing that had been in his head was the shape of her hand on the shutter.

They took him inside the wall.

The villa belonged to a freedman called Primus, who had been the steward of the house of the Claudii Corvi before Faustus and had retired to Ficulea with his savings and a garden and a wife who did not like him. He was sixty years old and he did not want to be in the middle of a scandal, and he stood in the yard with his hands folded and said, "Dominus, I am sorry, I did not know who it was," four times, while the two men held Marcus by the arms.

"Let me see her," Marcus said.

"I have my orders."

"Let me see her for one minute and I will go quietly and I will tell my father that you were civil."

Primus looked at the ground.

"There is a girl in the house who is to be sent to her mother," he said. "That is what I was told. That is all I was told. I have a wife and a garden and I have not been in Rome in four years." He looked up, and he was not an unkind man, and his eyes were unhappy. "Dominus, if I let you in, they will say I let you in, and I will lose the villa, and I am sixty years old."

Marcus stopped struggling.

"All right," he said.

They let go of him.

He stood in the yard and looked up at the house, and the shutter on the second floor was open, and Leda was standing at it with her hands on the sill.

She had been crying. He could see it from the yard. She had a shawl around her shoulders and her hair was down her back and she looked, in the morning light, like a girl in a painting of something very sad.

She said something that he could not hear.

"What?" he called.

She said it again, and he still could not hear it, and then the maid came and put her hand on her arm and drew her back from the window, and the shutter closed.

Primus's wife brought him water.

She was a thin woman with a hard mouth, and she brought it in a cup and stood over him while he drank it, and she said, "She is a nice girl. She has been no trouble. She cried all night and she would not eat, and this morning she asked me to send for a priest." She took the cup. "If you are the reason she has been crying, you should be ashamed of yourself, and if you are not, you should go home and leave her alone, because there is nothing you can do for her in a yard."

"I love her," Marcus said.

The woman looked at him.

"I loved a man once," she said. "He was a porter. He died of a fever in the winter and I married Primus in the spring, and I have been warm and fed for thirty years, and I still think about the porter." She turned away. "That is what love is worth in this world, dominus. It is worth a warm memory in a good marriage. Do not make it into a boat."

They put him on a horse and took him back to Rome.

One of the men was called Severus and he had been in the fleet and he talked the whole way, because a man who is riding four hours with a prisoner talks or he thinks.

"You are the son," he said.

"I am."

"Your father is a hard man."

"Yes."

"I was in the house at Ostia for a year, on the door, and I saw him twice." Severus shifted in the saddle. "He came down about a cargo and the steward had made a mistake in the manifest and your father had him up against the wall in the yard, not with his hands, with his voice, and the man was crying by the end of it. And then your father went out and got into the litter and I heard him say to the man beside him: *see that he has a good dinner.*" He spat over the horse's neck. "That is the thing about the great houses. They will break a man in the morning and feed him in the evening and think no more about it, and the man thinks about it for the rest of his life."

Marcus did not answer.

"Your girl," Severus said. "The singer."

"Yes."

"She is all right. I have seen her twice and she has a maid and the maid is a decent woman and the food is good." He looked at Marcus. "I am not supposed to tell you that. I am supposed to keep my mouth shut and take my pay. But I have a daughter of my own in Ostia and if a man was holding her in a villa in the country I would want to know that the food was good."

They rode on.

The sun came up over the hills and the road filled with carts and the dust came up, and Marcus rode with his hands tied to the saddle and his eye closing and the taste of blood in his mouth, and he thought about a boat, and about a warm memory in a good marriage, and about the fact that he had done everything a man could do and it had not been enough, and that this was the first time in his life that he had discovered that there were things a man could not do.

They came into Rome at the sixth hour.

His father was waiting in the atrium.

Marcus came in with his lip split and his eye closing and dust on his cloak, and Corvus stood by the impluvium with his hands behind his back and looked at him for a long moment.

"Did you see her?" he asked.

"Yes."

"Was she well?"

"Yes."

Corvus nodded slowly.

"Go to your room," he said. "You will be let out on the Ides."

Marcus went up the stairs.

At the top he stopped and looked down at his father standing in the atrium of the house of the Claudii Corvi, in the light from the impluvium, with the painted gods in the corner and the four hundred years of ancestors in their cupboards on the wall.

"You should have let me go," he said. "You should have let me take her and go, and you should have told my grandmother that I was dead, and you should have been consul and had your statues and your name on a bench in the Forum. It would have been a better life for everyone."

Marcus stopped with his hand on the door.

"There is a thing I want to say to you," he said, "and I am going to say it now, because in the autumn I will be on a ship and you will be consul and we will never say anything to each other again."

"Say it."

"You were a better man than your father," Marcus said. "That is the whole of it. You were a better man than him and you have spent your whole life afraid that you were not, and it has made you cruel in a way that he never was, because he did not care and you do." He opened the door. "I would rather have had a father who did not care."

"It would not," said Corvus. "But you are young, and you will not know that for twenty years, and by then it will not matter."

Marcus went into his room and shut the door.
