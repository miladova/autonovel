# Chapter Eleven

## Leda

The day before the litter came, she went to the prefecture to give her statement, and they took her down into the cells to show her what would happen to her if she did not.

She did not know that was why they were doing it. Tiro knew, and Tiro said nothing, because a freedman who tells a girl that she is being frightened on purpose is a man who has just made her more frightened. He walked beside her with his hand under her elbow and his scarred face perfectly still, and they went down the steps from the portico into the lower part of the building, and the air changed.

It was not the dark that did it. There was light: a lamp at the end of the corridor and a grille high in the wall. It was the smell. It was wet stone and old urine and something else, sweet and thick, that she did not recognize and then did.

There were eleven cells along the corridor. Some of them had men in them and some did not. The man in the third cell was singing, very quietly, a song about a girl at a well, and he stopped when he heard them and said, "Domina? Domina, is that you?" and Tiro said, "No," and the man said, "All right," and began to sing again.

A door at the end of the corridor was open.

Inside, a man was being questioned.

She did not see him. There was a screen of boards between the doorway and the room, and the clerk stood in front of it, and she saw the clerk, and she saw the lamp on the wall, and she heard. That was all. She heard the man's voice going up and then stopping, and the sound of a rope, and a man's voice, quiet and reasonable, asking a question. Then the other voice again, which was not words.

She stood in the corridor and looked at the floor.

"Miss," the clerk said. "This way. The prefect's clerk is in the room at the top of the stairs."

Leda did not move.

"Miss."

"What is he being asked?" she said.

"That is not your business."

"What is he being asked?"

The clerk looked at her, and then at Tiro, and then he said, in the voice of a man who has been in that building a long time and has decided that the truth costs nothing, "He is being asked whether he stole a jar of oil. It is worth nine sesterces. He will say that he did before the fourth hour, because everybody does, and then he will be sold to the mines for nine sesterces, and in a month he will be dead, and nobody will remember that he was asked anything."

Leda went up the stairs.

She gave her statement in a small room at the front of the building, with a table and two stools and a window that looked out at a wall, and the prefect's clerk was a different man from the one in the cells: younger, with a good haircut and an ink stain on his thumb, and he had a stool for her and a cup of water and did not look at her the way the other one had.

"Your name."

"Leda. I have no other name that I can prove."

"You are the singer of the house on the Vicus Tuscus."

"I am."

"You were in the passage behind the dining room at the second course of the banquet at which Gaius Vettius Proculus died."

"I was."

"Tell me what you saw."

She told him. He wrote it down in a neat hand, and when she had finished he read it back to her, and she said yes, and he said, "You understand that if you say something different in the basilica, this paper will be read out and the prefect will be entitled to ask why," and she said, "I understand."

He put the pen down.

"Miss," he said. "I am going to say something that is not part of my duty. The man who examined the cook is not in this building today; he has gone to Ostia on another matter. If you are asked to go down to the cells again, do not go. If a man in a uniform comes to your house and says the prefect wants you, ask for the paper. If there is no paper, do not go."

Leda looked at him.

"Why are you telling me this?"

The clerk looked at the door.

"Because I have a sister," he said, "and because I have been in this office six years, and I have written down about four hundred statements, and I have learned to tell the difference between a witness and a person who is going to be made into a witness." He stood up. "That is all. You may go."

She stood up.

"Thank you," she said.

The clerk did not answer, and he did not look up, and he was already writing something else.

On the way out she was sick in the street.
She was sick in the alley behind the prefecture, in the shadow of the wall, with her hand against the stone, and the world went white and then came back, and she could hear a woman selling something two streets away and a dog and a cart, all of it going on exactly as it had before, which was somehow the worst part.

Tiro stood beside her with his hand on her shoulder until it was over, and then he gave her a cloth, and said, "That is the appropriate response. I have been in that building four times and I have been sick three."

"What happens to the fourth?"

"The fourth time I was too frightened to be sick."

She wiped her mouth.

"How does anyone bear it?" she said. "How does anyone live in a city where that is in a building at the foot of the hill, and men go past it in the morning on their way to the market, and the prefect has his dinner at the ninth hour?"

Tiro looked at her.

"Most people do not go down the steps," he said. "And the ones who do, do not go twice."

That was the day before the litter came.

The litter came for her at the second hour, with two bearers and a slave woman, and it was a good litter, cedar and leather, with a curtain that could be drawn.

"They are not to come in," said Antonia, at the door. "You will go out to them. You will not bring anything with you but your shawl and the document."

"The document."

"The purchase document. I have told you what it says. You are to have it in your hand when you arrive, and you are to give it to him if he asks for it, and you are not to let go of it until he does." Antonia put a fold of papyrus into her hand. "And you are to remember that you are not a slave going to a master. You are a witness going to a panel, and the man you are going to see needs you, and a person who is needed may ask for things."

"What shall I ask for?"

"That is for you to decide," Antonia said. "You are twenty-one years old and you have been told what to do by other people since you were eleven. Today you will decide something yourself, and whatever you decide, I will stand by it."

Leda looked at her.

"Why are you being kind to me?" she said. "You have never been kind to me before. You have been just, which is different, and you have been careful with me the way you are careful with a good cup, and now you are kind, and it frightens me."

Antonia was silent for a moment.

"Because I have been sitting in a room with a dead boy," she said, "and it has occurred to me that I have spent seventeen years being careful with people, and that the people I was careful with are dead, and the people I was not careful with are also dead, and that the difference between them is not as large as I thought." She opened the door. "Go. And Leda—if they offer you a bath, take it. If they offer you wine, do not drink it. If they leave you alone in a room, count the doors."

The Claudii Corvi received her in the atrium, which was not what she had expected.

She had expected something like the house in the Greek novel: gold, purple, a hundred columns. What she found was an old Roman house with a black-and-white floor and a roof that had been mended in three places and a smell of wax and old wood, and a small shrine in the corner with the painted figures of the household gods, and a folded wool mantle on a bench where someone had been spinning and had put it down.

There were four people waiting for her.

The old woman sat in a chair by the shrine with her hands in her lap. She was small and straight in a dark stola, with white hair in a knot and a face like a closed door, and she looked at Leda for the length of a breath and then looked away, as though she had seen enough.

The girl was fifteen or so, and she was standing by the impluvium with her hands behind her back, and she was staring at Leda with open, unashamed curiosity.

The steward stood at the back with his hands folded. He was thin and neat and pale, and Leda knew him at once; she had seen him in the kitchen of the Myrtle four times, and she had been told that he was a banker.

And Corvus stood in the middle of the floor.

He was taller than his son, and greyer, and he had the same mouth. He was dressed plainly, in a white tunic with a narrow stripe, and he did not smile.

"Leda," he said. "You are welcome in this house."

"Thank you, dominus."

"You are not to call me that. Until the panel has determined your status, you will call me nothing at all, and I will call you by your name, and we will speak as two people in a room." He held out his hand. "May I see the document?"

She gave it to him.

He read it, standing, with the light from the impluvium on the papyrus.

"Massilia," he said. "Nymphodorus of Puteoli, dealer, to Antonia Cassia, for eleven thousand sesterces, warranted sound, no defects of body or character, origin Massilia, no known kin." He looked up. "You were not from Massilia."

"No."

"Where are you from?"

"Surrentum."

"Say the rest."

"My father's name was Vibius Rufus. He sold wine. Our yard was by the gate, with a wall and a vine over it, and there were amphorae in rows, and my mother's name is Vibia Prima, and my brother is called Gaius and he has a mark on his chin." Her voice was steady. "I was taken from the beach when I was eleven, by men in a boat who spoke Greek, and my brother ran, and I did not run, because I was eleven and a stranger had asked me a question."

The old woman by the shrine made a small sound.

Corvus folded the document.

"Thank you," he said. "That is what I needed to hear, and it is what you will say to the panel tomorrow, in those words, and they will believe you, because it is true and because truth has a sound that a lie does not." He handed the document back. "Now come with me."

He took her into a small room off the atrium, with a table and two chairs and a window that looked onto the garden. There was a bust of a man with a chipped ear on a shelf, and a set of wax tablets, and a box with a bronze lock.

"Sit down."

She sat.

"You saw a man in the passage on the night of the banquet," Corvus said.

"Yes."

"Tell me."

She told him. The dark tunic, the narrow stripe, the short hair, the shoulder turned away, the cup. She told it the way she had told Antonia and the prefect's clerk, in the same words, because she had decided in the litter that she would tell it always in the same words and never add anything to it, so that no one could catch her in a difference.

Corvus listened with his eyes shut.

When she had finished he was quiet for a moment.

"Now I will tell you what will happen," he said. "Tomorrow you will be brought before the panel. The prefect will ask you what you saw. You will tell him, as you have told me. And then Maro will ask you to describe the man's face, and you will say that you did not see it, and he will ask how it is that a girl who can describe the cut of a man's hair and the stripe on his tunic cannot describe his face, and you will say that the lamps were low and that he turned away."

"That is the truth."

"It is the truth, and it will not be enough. Maro will say that you are protecting the house, or that you have been instructed, and he will ask the prefect to have you examined under torture, because the law says that a slave's evidence is worth nothing until it is taken that way, and the prefect will agree, because he is a cautious man and because there is a consular dead and because a girl is a cheap thing to spend." He opened his eyes. "Unless you are free-born. If you are free-born, you cannot be examined except in the case of treason, and the whole thing changes."

"Then say that I am free-born."

"I intend to. But it is not enough for me to say it. I must prove it, and the proof is your mother's petition and the dealer's record and your own face and your own memory, and even then there will be a man on the panel who will say that the dealer's record is a forgery and that the girl has been coached." He leaned back. "So we will make it easy for him. You will say that you did not see the man's face, and that you cannot swear to the colour of the stripe, and that you were frightened and that the lamps were low, and that you have thought about it every night since and that you are no longer sure of anything except that there was a man and a cup."

Leda looked at him.

"You want me to say that I am not sure."

"I want you to say what a frightened girl would say."

"I am a frightened girl."

"Then say what you would say if you were frightened and alone and had no one to tell you what to say." His voice was quiet and patient. "Do you understand what I am offering you? If you do this, the panel will find that the evidence is insufficient, and the death will be recorded as a seizure, and you will be given to your mother, and you will go to Surrentum, and in a year you will be married to a man of your own class, and this will be a thing that happened to you once, in Rome, when you were young."

"And the man who carried the cup?"

"Will be dealt with."

"By whom?"

"By me."

Leda sat very still.

"You know who he is," she said.

"I know who paid him."

"And you will deal with him."

"I will deal with him," Corvus said, "in the way that such things are dealt with, which is quietly and completely and without a trial, because a trial would destroy the Senate and the city and my family, and because the man is the son of a consular and the courts of Rome do not convict the sons of consulars of anything." He said it without shame. "You are looking at me as though I were a monster. I am not. I am a man who has been given a great deal of power and a very small number of tools, and I am telling you what the tools can do."

"My lady's house is closed," Leda said. "Her door is shut and her women are frightened and her boy is dead on the Esquiline. If I say that I am not sure, then the panel will say that there was no man in the passage, and the prefect will say that the poison came from her kitchen, and the whole thing will be finished by the Kalends and it will be finished on her."

"Yes."

"And you will be consul."

"I will be consul," Corvus said, "and the city will be quiet, and the emperor will be pleased, and in ten years no one will remember any of it."

Leda stood up.

"I will not do it," she said.

Corvus did not move.

"Sit down."

"No."

"Sit down," he said, and something in his voice had changed, and she sat, because she was twenty-one and he was a consul-designate and because a room with a closed door is a room with a closed door.

"I am not asking you to lie," he said. "I am asking you to be uncertain, which you are, and to be frightened, which you are, and to let a panel of old men do what old men do, which is to find a way to be comfortable. That is not a betrayal of your lady. It is the only thing that will save her. If you go into that room and swear that you saw a man's face and name him, then Maro will say that you are a slave who has been coached, and he will put you on the hook, and you will say whatever he wants within an hour, and it will not be the truth, and the truth will be lost, and your lady will hang." He leaned forward. "Do you understand? The truth is not a sword. It is a heavy thing that you can only carry if you are strong enough, and you are not strong enough, and neither am I."

Leda looked at the floor.

"There is one more thing," Corvus said. "Your mother is alive, and she is poor, and she has spent ten years petitioning governors. If you go before that panel and make a spectacle of yourself, she will be told about it, and she will be told by men who will make it sound as though her daughter had become a whore in Rome and had lied to protect her whoremistress. That is the story that will reach Surrentum. Not the truth. The story." He stood up. "Think about what you want your mother to hear."

He went out.

Leda sat in the chair for a long time with the document in her hand.

Then the door opened and the girl came in.

She was fifteen, and she came in the way that young things come into rooms when they have been told not to, quickly and quietly and with her chin up, and she shut the door behind her and stood with her back against it.

"I am Caesia," she said. "You are the singer."

"Yes."

"You are the one my brother is in love with."

Leda said nothing.

"He does not say so. He does not say anything. He has been like a man with a stone in his shoe since he came back from Dacia." Caesia sat down in the other chair and folded her hands in her lap exactly as her grandmother had. "He is going to be married to a girl called Vinicia Sabina in the autumn, and he does not want to, and my grandmother says that what he wants does not matter, and my father says that in ten years he will be grateful. Will he?"

"I don't know."

"I think he will not," Caesia said. "I think he will be a consul and have a house full of statues and be very correct and very cold, and once a year he will drink too much at the Saturnalia and say something terrible to his wife." She looked at Leda. "Is it true that you were stolen?"

"Yes."

"From a beach?"

"Yes."

"With your brother on the beach?"

"Yes."

Caesia was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "I am sorry."

"Thank you."

"I do not mean it the way people mean it. I mean—" She stopped, and started again. "I have been in this house all my life and I have been told that we are the Claudii Corvi and that we are better than other people because we are older and because my great-great-grandfather was consul and because we do not make money in trade. And I have begun to think, this year, that it is all a story that we tell, and that the only reason it works is that nobody has ever made us stop telling it." She looked at Leda. "You are the first person I have ever met who did not believe it."

"I am not brave," Leda said. "I am frightened of your father and of the prefect and of the hook and of the dark. I have been frightened since I was eleven."

"Yes," said Caesia. "That is what I mean."

The door opened. The old woman stood in it, with the steward behind her.

"Caesia," she said. "Go to your room."

"Grandmother—"

"Go to your room, and do not speak to me of this, and do not speak to your brother of it either." The old woman's eyes came to Leda. They were pale and cold and entirely without malice, which was worse. "You will wait in the atrium. The litter will take you back."

Leda stood up.

"Domina," she said. "May I ask you something?"

"You may ask."

"Did you know? About me. Before all this."

Claudia Junia looked at her for a long moment.

"I knew that there was a girl in a house on the Vicus Tuscus," she said, "and that my son had been seen going into it, and that the woman who owned it had been in this house when she was a girl, and that nothing good would come of any of it. I did not know your name or your story, and I would not have asked." She stepped aside. "That is the truth, and you may make of it what you like. It is more than most people in this city will give you."

Leda went out through the atrium, past the shrine with its painted gods and the folded wool on the bench, and she was halfway across the black-and-white floor when she saw Marcus on the stairs.

He was in a tunic and no shoes, and he had come down four steps and stopped, and there was a slave behind him with his hand out, and it was clear that he had been told not to come down and had come down anyway.

"Leda."

"Dominus."

"Do not—" He came down two more steps. "Do not call me that."

"The old woman is behind me," Leda said, "and there is a man at the door, and if you come down those stairs they will take you back up them and there will be a scene, and I have had a great many scenes this year and I would like to get into a litter and go." She said it quietly and clearly, and she did not move toward him. "I am well. They have been decent to me. I am to go to Surrentum the day after tomorrow."

"Surrentum."

"My mother's town."

Marcus stood on the stairs with his hand on the rail.

"I have a horse," he said. "There is a boat at Ostia. I have it arranged; I have had it arranged for a month—"

"I know."

"Then why did you not come?"

"Because I am tired," Leda said. "Because I have been running since I was eleven years old and I have found that I do not want to run any more; I want to arrive. Because if I go with you I will spend the rest of my life being a woman who ran away with a senator's son, and if I go to Surrentum I will be a woman with a name." She looked at him. "And because your father would find us, and he would not kill me and he would not kill you, he would simply arrange things, and I have seen what happens to people when he arranges things."

Marcus came down the last step.

"Look at me," he said.

She looked at him.

He was twenty years old and he had a bruise on his cheek from a man in the street and his eyes were red, and he looked like a boy who has been told that the world is not what he thought.

"I love you," he said. "I have never said that to anyone. I am saying it now in my grandmother's atrium with a slave behind me and I do not care who hears it."

"I know," said Leda.

"Do you love me?"

"Yes." She said it without hesitating, which was the cruellest thing she did that day. "But I am not going to let it decide my life. I have had too many things decided for me by other people to give the last one away to a feeling."

The old woman's voice came from the door of the atrium.

"Marcus. Go upstairs."

He did not move.

"Go upstairs," Claudia Junia said, "or I will have Polio carry you."

Marcus looked at Leda.

"Surrentum," he said. "In the spring. I will come."

"Go upstairs," Leda said.

He went.

She watched him go up the stairs, and the slave went after him, and the old woman stood in the doorway with her hands folded and her face like a shut door.

"You are a sensible girl," Claudia Junia said.

"No," said Leda. "I am a girl who has been sold twice, and I have learned that a sensible girl is one who does what other people want and calls it her own idea." She picked up her shawl. "Your grandson will hate you for the rest of his life. I thought you should know that. He will be polite to you and he will come at the Saturnalia and he will hate you, and when you are dying he will sit by your bed and be correct, and he will not tell you anything true, and you will have nobody to blame but yourself."

She went out into the street where the litter was waiting, and she did not cry until the curtain was drawn.

She went back to the Myrtle and found Antonia in the courtyard with her sleeves rolled up, washing the fountain herself with a cloth and a bucket of water, which was a thing she did when she was thinking and could not sit still.

"Well?" she said.

Leda told her.

When she had finished, Antonia sat back on her heels.

"He is cleverer than I thought," she said. "He has found the one thing that will work, which is not a threat and not a bribe but a story. He has given you a story in which you are a frightened girl who is doing the best she can, and in that story you are not a coward, and he is not a villain, and the whole thing is very sad and nobody is to blame." She wrung out the cloth. "And it is a lie, because the man who paid for the poison is walking about the city, and the man who carried it is in the Tiber, and the boy who saw them is on the Esquiline, and somebody must be to blame, and it will be me, because I am the only one who cannot afford to be forgiven."

"What shall I do?"

"You will do what you decide," Antonia said. "I told you that this morning and I meant it."

"And if I decide to say what he asks?"

"Then I will lose," said Antonia, "and I will not blame you, and I will not speak to you again, because a woman who loses everything in Rome loses her friends first and her house second and her life last, and I will not have the strength to be gracious about it." She stood up and emptied the bucket into the basin. "But I do not think you will."

"Why not?"

"Because you are angry," Antonia said, "and anger is the only thing in this city that a poor woman can spend without a banker."

That night Leda sent a message to Marcus by the boy from the baths, and the message said: *The Nones. Before dawn. The roof, not the gate.*

And then she lay on her bed with the shell in her hand
She had been in the house on the Palatine for a day, and she had seen the shrine with the scratched gods, and the wool on the bench where the old woman had put it down, and the wax masks in their cupboards in the hall.

She had stood in a basilica and told six hundred men what a man did to her in a passage. She had walked down a corridor past a room where a man was being questioned. She had been given a cup of water by a clerk with an ink stain on his thumb.

And tomorrow she was going to a house on the Palatine where a man would ask her to say that she was not sure.
 and looked at the ceiling, and thought about Surrentum, and about a beach, and about a boy on the beach getting smaller.
