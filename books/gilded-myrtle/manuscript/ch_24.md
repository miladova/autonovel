# Chapter Twenty-Four

## Antonia

They buried Gaius Claudius Corvus on the third day, in the family tomb on the Via Appia, and nobody came.

That was the thing the city talked about for a week. Not the death; the death was expected, or at least it was the sort of thing that men had begun to expect, and the story of the hemlock in the shrine went round the Forum in an afternoon and was improved in the telling. It was the funeral. A man who had been consul-designate in the spring was carried out of his house on the Via Appia with his mother and his daughter and the household slaves behind the bier, and not one senator walked in the procession.

There had been a debate about it in the antechambers. Some men had wanted to go, out of decency or out of calculation; some had argued that to attend the funeral of a man whose conduct was before the Senate was to prejudge the question. In the end the Senate had done what it always did, which was nothing, and the nothing had been understood by every man in Rome as a message.

Antonia watched from the other side of the road, at the top of the steps of a wine shop, with her hood up.

She had no business being in Rome. The prefect had given her until the end of the month to be gone, and she had stayed, because there was a girl's body to be carried to Surrentum and a ship that did not sail until the Nones of August, and because she had found that a woman who has lost everything is no longer afraid of a fine.

The procession came out of the gate of the house at the fourth hour.

The bier was good. The family had not stinted on it; it was the same ivory and bronze couch that had carried his father, with a new cover, and the eight bearers were the household slaves, and behind it came Claudia Junia in black, walking alone, and Caesia, and then the household, which was eleven people now, and then nothing.

The wax masks of the Claudii Corvi went in front, nine of them, in their little cupboards, carried by hired men.

That was the thing that got into Antonia's chest and stayed there. The masks went first: the consul of the German war, the praetor of the Spanish command, the tribune who had died at Numantia, the one who had thrown himself on his sword in the latrine at Ephesus and whose name was never to be spoken in the atrium. Nine dead men in wax, with the paint flaking off their noses, carried through the streets of Rome by four hirelings at four sesterces apiece, in front of a bier that nobody was following.

She watched them go down the hill and turn toward the Appian gate.

Then she went to the house.

Claudia Junia received her in the atrium, in black, with her hair down, and she looked a hundred years old. She was sitting on the bench by the shrine with her hands in her lap and she did not get up.

"You have come for the will," she said.

"I have come for the letter."

The old woman looked at her.

"The letter you wrote me," Antonia said. "The one you have kept for eleven years. I want it."

"Why?"

"Because I am leaving Italy and I do not want it in this house."

Claudia Junia sat for a moment. Then she said, "In the box on the table by my bed. The key is under the lamp."

Antonia went up.

The room was the room of an old woman: a narrow bed, a stool, a chest, a shrine in the corner with a lamp, a wool basket with the spindle in it. There was a box on the table by the bed, and the key was under the lamp, and inside it there were papers: a marriage contract, a birth record, a bundle of letters in a hand that Antonia did not know, and one letter, folded twice, in her own.

She read it standing by the window.

*Claudia Junia. I have not spoken and I will not speak. I do not want anything from you or from your son. I am writing because I want you to know that I am alive, and because I have bought a house, and because it has a fountain in the courtyard with a bronze myrtle tree in the middle of it, and because I chose the myrtle myself. You will think that I am writing to frighten you. I am not. I am writing because there is no one else in the world who knows what happened in that room, and because a thing that is known by only one person is not a thing at all, it is a dream. A.C.*

She folded it and put it in her sleeve.

There was a second paper in the box, and it was not a letter; it was a codicil, in a clerk's hand, dated six days before the death, and it said that Gaius Claudius Corvus, being of sound mind, left to Antonia Cassia, freedwoman, the bronze stylus which was in the strongbox in his study, and to the guild of the bakers of the fourth region the sum of eleven thousand sesterces in the name of a boy called Felix, slave, deceased, and to his steward Faustus, freedman, nothing at all.

Antonia read it twice.

"Did you know about this?" she asked, when she came down.

"I know everything that happens in this house," said Claudia Junia. "I read it when the clerk brought it. It is not valid; a codicil made six days before death, when a man is known to have been shut in his study and not eating, will be contested by the heir, and the heir is a boy of twenty who is on a ship to Syria and does not care." She looked up. "But he made it. He sat at that table and he dictated it to a clerk, and he left a pen to a woman and eleven thousand sesterces to a dead boy and nothing at all to the man who went to the mines for him. That is the last thing my son did in the world."

"It is not nothing."



"It is a gesture," Claudia Junia said. "He was always good at gestures. It is what a man does instead of the thing itself."

Antonia stood in the atrium of the house of the Claudii Corvi and looked at the old woman on the bench.

"Your son was a coward," she said, "and he was cruel in the way that men are cruel who have never had to be anything else, and he stood in a doorway for three heartbeats when he was twenty-four years old and he never forgave himself for it, and he spent the rest of his life trying to be a good man in a family that did not believe in good men." She put the letter in her sleeve. "I hated him for seventeen years. I have stopped. It is not forgiveness; it is arithmetic. He is dead and I am alive and I have a girl's ashes to carry to her mother, and there is no time left for hating a man who is not here."

"And me?" said Claudia Junia. "Have you stopped hating me?"

"No," Antonia said. "You are still here."

Claudia Junia looked at her for a long moment.

"Will you write to me?" she said. "Once a year. One line. That you are alive."

Antonia stood in the door of the house of the Claudii Corvi.

"I will think about it," she said.

"That is not a no."

"No," said Antonia. "It is not a no. It is the most I have."

She went out.

She went out through the gate into the street.

Caesia was in the street.

She was standing by the cart with her hands folded, in a plain stola with no jewellery, and she had come out without a slave and without telling anyone, and when Antonia came through the gate she said, "I have four minutes. If my grandmother finds out I am here she will lock me in my room until the wedding."

"What do you want?"

"To give you this." She held out a small purse. "It is sixty sesterces. It is not much. It is what I have that is mine."

Antonia looked at the purse.

"Keep it."

"I am not giving it to you," Caesia said. "It is for the boy. The one from Tibur. He will need shoes and a cloak before the winter, and you will be buying wine and paying rent, and I know how a household eats money in the first year because I have watched my grandmother do the accounts since I was nine." She put the purse in Antonia's hand and closed her fingers on it. "I am not being kind. I am being practical. There is a difference and it is the only thing I have learned this year that is worth anything."

Antonia looked at her.

"You are going to be a dangerous woman," she said.

"I hope so." Caesia stepped back. "I am going to marry Vinicius in the spring and I am going to have a house and I am going to do what I said I would do. I am going to write to you. Not once a year; four times. And you will write back and tell me the truth about what it is like to be a woman with a shop in a town by the sea, because I have a suspicion that it is either much worse or much better than this house and I would like to know which."

"It is both," Antonia said.

"That is what I thought."

Caesia looked at the cart, at the women, at the boy asleep on the bundles.

"Did she suffer?" she asked.

"Yes."

"I thought so. I have been hoping she did not." She wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist, quickly, and straightened. "Goodbye, Antonia Cassia. You are the most interesting person who has ever been in this house, and my grandmother is right about you, and she is wrong about everything else."

They sold the contents at auction in the Forum, in the portico of the basilica, on a market day, and Antonia went to watch.

She did not know why she went. She had told herself that she would not; that a woman who has lost a house does not stand in a crowd and watch a man sell her tables. But she woke on the morning of the auction and found that she could not sit in the room above the fuller's shop, and she put on her shawl and walked down through the Subura and across the Forum and stood at the back of the crowd with the porters and the small dealers and the women who come to auctions to look.

The auctioneer was a small man with a good voice and a stick, and he stood on a box with the goods laid out on a trestle behind him.

"Lot eleven," he called. "Three couches, citron wood, bronze feet, the property of a lady of the fourth region. Nine thousand to start. Eight. Seven. Six and a half. Six. Do I hear five?"

They went for four thousand two hundred to a man in a green tunic who dealt in furniture on the Argiletum and would have them re-covered and sold in the autumn for nine.

"Lot twelve. One table, marble, with a lion's head. Two hundred. One fifty. One hundred. Sold."

"Lot seventeen. Eleven lamps, bronze. Forty sesterces the lot."

"Lot twenty-two. One silver service, sixteen pieces, the property of a lady of the fourth region. Twenty-two thousand to start."

The silver went for nineteen thousand to a freedman of the imperial household who had come with two slaves and a purse and who did not look at the pieces, only at the weight.

Antonia stood at the back with her shawl over her head and watched her life go across a trestle and be sold in an afternoon.

The fountain went for three thousand. The curtains went for six hundred. The mirror from her own room, the bronze one with the handle in the shape of a girl, went for ninety sesterces to a woman who was buying for a brothel in the Subura, and Antonia saw her hand it to a slave and say, "There. That will do for the upstairs room."

At the end, when the crowd had thinned and the auctioneer was drinking from a cup, she went up to him.

"The box," she said. "The wooden box with the tablets. Lot forty-one."

"That went an hour ago." He looked at his list. "To a man from the prefecture's office. Papers, deeds, tablets, contents unexamined, four sesterces."

"Who bought it?"

"A clerk. He said it was for the file." The auctioneer looked at her, and then at the shawl, and his face changed. "You are her."

"Yes."

"I am sorry, domina. It is the law. I am paid by the court."

"I know." She looked at the trestle, at the empty place where the goods had been. "There was a small bronze myrtle tree, from the fountain. Leaves on a stem. It was not in any lot."

The auctioneer looked at his list.

"It was listed with the fountain," he said. "It went with the basin. The man in the green tunic took the whole thing."

Antonia nodded.

"Thank you," she said, and went out through the portico into the Forum.

The house was sold at the end of the month.

It went for less than it was worth, because a house in which a consul has died twice is not a house that fetches a price, and because the buyer knew that the family had no choice. The buyer was a freedman of the imperial household who intended to pull down the old atrium and put up a block of flats, and Antonia heard this in the street from a man who had heard it from a mason, and she stood in the Vicus Tuscus and laughed until she had to hold on to a wall.

On the last night before she left, she burned the letter.

She did it in the yard behind the house of a fuller in the Subura, where the surviving women had been living since the fire: Prisca, Meroe, Junilla, the washerwoman, and the boy from Tibur, who had come down from the smithy with his chest on his back and had been given the corner by the oven.

There was a brazier in the yard and they had lit it for the evening meal, and Antonia took out the letter and the box of myrtle wood and put them both in the fire.

The box burned slowly, because myrtle is a hard wood, and it gave off a smell that she would remember for the rest of her life. The letter went up at once.

"What is it?" Prisca asked.

"A letter," Antonia said. "And a promise I made to a girl."

She watched it burn, and then she went inside and ate bread and onions with the women, and Meroe told a story about Alexandria that Antonia had heard four times, and the boy from Tibur fell asleep with his head on the table, and Junilla carried him to the corner and put a blanket over him.

The night before they left, the women sat up in the fuller's yard and argued about the future.

It was a warm night and they had brought the bench out and a lamp, and Marcella the laundress had given them a jug of her own wine, which was bad, and they drank it anyway. The boy from Tibur was asleep in the corner with his mouth open. The washerwoman had gone home. It was Prisca and Meroe and Junilla and Antonia, and a bowl of olives, and the smoke of the vats coming over the wall.

"There is a thing we have not decided," Prisca said.

"We have decided everything. We have decided the boat and the town and the house."

"We have not decided what we are going to do when we get there." Prisca put her cup down. "We have four hundred sesterces and five women and a boy. In Surrentum, that is a month, perhaps two. And then?"

"I will find work," Meroe said.

"At what?"

"I can dance."

"You can dance in Alexandria and in Rome, where there are eight hundred thousand people and a man will pay to watch a woman dance. In a town of six thousand on a bay, you will dance at the festival once a year and the rest of the time you will be a woman with a reputation." Prisca was not unkind; she was tired and practical. "We have all been in a house of women, and now we are going to a town where everyone will know it within a week, and there is no market for what we have been selling except the one we are trying to leave."

"So what do we sell?" Junilla said.

"We sell wine," Antonia said.

They looked at her.

"There is a widow in the town who has a yard and a son who runs a shop and a quarrel with the man who has four ships. There is no wine shop between the harbour and the gate, and the fishermen drink at the tavern by the mole, and the tavern is owned by a cousin of the man with four ships." She turned her cup. "I can buy wine. I have bought wine for seventeen years and I know what it costs and what it is worth and I know when a man is lying about a vintage. I can keep a shop. I can keep accounts. I have been keeping accounts since I was twenty-three."

"You can keep a house," Prisca said.

"I can keep a shop," Antonia said. "A shop opens in the morning and shuts at night and sells a thing that people need and does not require anyone to be charming after the second hour. I am forty years old and I have spent my whole life being charming and I am finished."

"And what are we?" Meroe said.

"You are free women with papers." Antonia looked at her. "That is not a small thing. It is a smaller thing than it should be, and it is more than either of us had a year ago. Prisca will keep the accounts, because she is better at it than I am and she enjoys it. Junilla will keep the door, because she is the only one of us who can look at a man and make him decide to go home. You will teach the daughters of the harbour families to dance, if their mothers will let you, and if they will not, you will learn to make the bread, because Calybe's bread was the best in Rome and she is not here and somebody in this town will pay for it."

"And you?" said Prisca.

Antonia looked into her cup.

"I am going to teach a boy to read," she said, "and I am going to put a stone on a hill, and I am going to find out what a woman is when she is not being watched."

The lamp guttered.

"That is not a living," said Prisca.

"No," Antonia said. "But it is a life, and I have discovered this year that they are not the same, and that I have spent forty years confusing them."

Meroe laughed, and then the others did, and Junilla said something about the wine being so bad that it made a person philosophical, and they sat in the yard until the lamp went out.

On the last day before they left, she went out to the potters' field on the Esquiline.

She went alone, in the morning, with a bunch of myrtle from a woman in the market, and she walked out through the Esquiline gate and along the road and turned off at the wall, and the man at the gate of the field took two sesterces and showed her the row.

There were a great many stones. That was the thing about the potters' field: it was full. There were stones the size of a hand and stones the size of a door, and some of them had names and some of them had only a number, and the grass was cut short between the rows by a goat that was tied to a post at the far end and looked at her without interest.

She found the stone.

She had paid for it in the month of his death, out of the money in the house, and she had not been back since the burning. Somebody had been putting flowers there; there was a bunch of dried lavender tied with a string, three weeks old at least, and a small clay cup.

Antonia knelt down and put the myrtle beside the lavender.

"I am going," she said.

The goat pulled at its tether and tore at the grass.

"I am going to Surrentum with a jar of ashes and five women and a boy, and I am going to keep a shop and sell wine and teach him to read, and in the spring I am going to put a vine over the door." She looked at the stone. "I do not know whether that is a good life. It is a life. I have found that it is more than most people in this city get."

She sat back on her heels.

"I am sorry," she said. "That is all. I have been sorry for a month and I expect I will be sorry for the rest of my life, and it will not do you any good, and I am saying it anyway because it is the only thing I have that is not for sale."

The goat came to the end of its tether and looked at her.

"You wanted a bakery," she said. "I would have bought you a bakery. If you had asked me, I would have said no, because a woman like me says no to a boy who asks for a bakery; she says, *wait until you are older, we will see, we will talk about it in a year.* And then in a year you would have been thirteen and I would have said the same thing, and in four years you would have been sixteen and I would have bought it, because by then you would have been worth it." Her voice caught. "You were worth it at twelve. I did not know. I am telling you now, and you cannot hear me, and that is the whole of it."

She got up.

At the gate of the field she stopped and looked back at the rows and the stones and the goat and the small grey stone with four lines on it.

"His name was Felix," she said to the man at the gate. "If anyone comes and asks, in a year, or in ten years, his name was Felix and he was twelve years old and he wanted to own a bakery."

The man looked at her.

"I will remember," he said.

In the morning they went to Ostia.

Eutychus came to the gate to see them off.

He came in a hired chair with one slave and a basket, and the basket had figs in it, and he stood in the road outside the Ostian gate with the dust of the cart traffic going past him and held it out.

"The third crop," he said. "The best of the year. I said the first was best because it was early, and I was lying; it is the third."

Antonia took the basket.

"You kept your word," she said.

"I am a practical man." He looked at the cart, at the women, at the boy asleep in the back among the bundles. "The papers are lodged with the prefect's office. Every one of them free, with a copy for each, and a note that any magistrate who questions them is to write to me. Nobody will question them." He put his hands behind his back. "The building is mine. I have had the masons in. We are putting in a furnace and a cold pool and a room for the women at the back, and there will be a small garden where the dining room was, and a myrtle tree in the middle of it, because there was one there before and it seems a pity to lose it."

Antonia looked at him.

"You are a strange man," she said.

"I am a man who has spent his life in accounts," said Eutychus, "and I have discovered at fifty-five that there is no line in any account for the thing I have been feeling since the basilica, and it is uncomfortable, and I would like it to stop." He held out his hand. "Domina. If you write to me from Surrentum I will answer, and if you need money I will send it, and if any man in that town troubles you, you will send me his name and I will have him audited."

She laughed.

"I will write," she said.

"See that you do." He looked at the cart. "You have four hundred sesterces and a jar of ashes and five women and a boy who cannot read, and you are going to a town where you know nobody except a widow who has been waiting ten years for a daughter who is dead. It is not a promising position."

"No," Antonia said. "It is the first honest one I have ever been in."

She got up on the cart.

They went out through the Ostian gate in the early morning with the dust coming up and the road full of carts and the Tiber on the left, and the city fell away behind them, and the women were quiet, and the boy woke up and looked at the river.

At the ninth hour they came to Ostia and the sea.

Antonia had not seen the sea since she was fourteen years old. She stood on the quay with the wind in her face and the smell of it, salt and tar and rotten weed and fish, and she looked at the water going out to the horizon, and she found that she was shaking.

"Domina," Prisca said. "Are you well?"

"I am well."

"You are crying."

"I know." Antonia wiped her face with the back of her hand. "I have not seen the sea since I was a girl. I had forgotten that it was so large."

The ship was a grain freighter called the *Providentia*, and the master was a small brown man with one ear who took passengers because they paid in advance and did not complain about the rats. They were given a place in the stern under an awning, with a chest for a table and a bundle for a pillow, and the women arranged themselves and their belongings and their food, and the boy sat at the rail and looked at the water.

They sailed on the evening tide.

Antonia sat in the stern with the jar in her lap.

It was a plain jar, of good clay, sealed with wax, and it had a label on it in her own hand: *LEDA, CALLED VIBIA RUFA, OF SURRENTUM, AGED TWENTY-ONE.* It was not the custom to put a name on a jar of ashes. The custom was to put them in the tomb and let the stone do the work. But the stone would be at Surrentum, and the journey was four days, and she had found that she could not put the jar in a chest with the bundles.

The ship went out past the lighthouse and turned south, and the shore went by, and the wind came off the land, warm and smelling of pine and dust and something else that she could not name.

Meroe came and sat down beside her.

"Tell me about the town," she said.

"It is on a bay," Antonia said. "There is a cliff above it and a temple on the cliff, and the town comes down to the water in steps. There is a wine quarter by the gate, where the barrels are, and a fish market, and a beach at the bottom of the hill with black sand. The water is very blue. In the summer the rich people come from Rome and put up awnings and complain about the heat."

"You were born there?"

"No. I was born in the Subura, in a room above a bakery, and my father was a grain factor, and he hanged himself in his shop when I was eleven." She looked at the water. "I have never been to Surrentum in my life."

Meroe was quiet for a moment.

"Then why are we going?"

"Because she is," Antonia said, "and because there is a woman there who has been waiting ten years, and because a town on a bay is as good a place as any for a woman with four hundred sesterces and a bad reputation to begin again." She looked at the jar in her lap. "And because I made a promise to a girl in a bed in a house on the Palatine, and I have kept very few promises in my life, and I intend to keep this one."

The wind came off the land, and the ship went south, and the sun went down behind the water, and the sky over the bay turned the colour of a peach and then of a bruise and then of nothing at all.
