# Chapter Ten

## Antonia

They laid him in the atrium with a coin on his eyes and a wreath of myrtle on his head, and the whole household came to sit with him, and the neighbours came in from the street, and by the ninth hour there were forty people in the house and the door had to be left open.

This was the custom and Antonia did it properly. The women washed him and dressed him in a clean tunic that Prisca cut down from one of Tiro's, and Meroe combed his hair with her own comb, and Calybe put a honey cake in his hand because he had liked them, and nobody said anything about the fact that a honey cake in the hand of a dead boy was a waste of honey. The lamps were set at his head and his feet. The flutes came at the tenth hour, two of them, played by a pair of brothers from the Subura who did it for four sesterces and did it well.

The neighbours stood in the doorway and looked and said the things that people say. They said that he was a good boy and that he had been quick, and that it was the city now, and that in their fathers' time a man could walk from the Esquiline to the river at midnight with a purse in his hand.

One of them, a woman of sixty who kept a bread shop two doors down, came in and took Antonia's hands and said, "Who washed him?"

"I did."

"Did you put a coin on his eyes?"

"Two."

"Then you did it right," the woman said, "and there is nothing else anybody can do, and do not let the priest tell you otherwise."

She went out, and Antonia stood by the fountain with her hands still warm from the woman's hands, and thought that it was the first kind thing anyone had said to her in a month. *He was a good boy. He was quick. I remember him running errands for the baker. It is the city. It is the city now. In my father's time you could walk from the Esquiline to the river at midnight with a purse in your hand.*

Antonia stood by the fountain and received them and said thank you, and her face was calm, and by the eleventh hour she had said *thank you* so many times that the words had stopped having any meaning at all.

At the eleventh hour Corvus came.

He came alone, on foot, with a dark cloak over his tunic and no lictor and no slave, and he stopped in the doorway of the atrium and looked at the boy on the couch, and then at her, and his face was the face of a man who has walked into a wall in the dark.

"I did not know," he said.

"He was twelve."

"I did not know that he was dead."

"No," Antonia said. "You knew only that he was alive and inconvenient."

She led him through the house to the counting room and shut the door, and they stood on either side of the table with the lamp between them, and the flutes went on in the atrium, thin and reedy, playing the old tune that is played at no other time.

"Tell me," she said.

He told her.

He told her about Faustus, and the money, and the man with the mark, and the confession in the tablinum, and the letter from Antioch, and the consulship.

He told it in order, like a man giving evidence, and when he had finished he put his hands flat on the table.

"That is all of it," he said.

"It is not all of it."

"It is all of it that I know."

"Then you know less than my steward." Antonia took the scrap of dark cloth out of her sleeve and put it on the table between them. "This was in the boy's hand. It is from a cloak of dark blue wool with a gold thread in the edge, and I have seen that cloak on the shoulders of your steward in the portico of Castor, and there are three men in Rome who can afford a cloak like that and two of them are in the provinces."

Corvus looked at the cloth.

"Where is the boy who did it?"

"Dead in the Tiber, two days ago. My steward killed him." She watched his face. "You are shocked. Do not be. Tiro has killed nine men in an arena for the entertainment of the people of Rome, and he killed this one in an alley in the dark because the man had a hand in the death of a child, and I did not ask him to and I will not punish him for it. If you want him, you may have him, and you may try him for the murder of a slave, and the city will laugh at you for a month."

"What do you want?"

"I want your steward in the prefect's cells by tomorrow night."

"Faustus has served my family for twenty years."

"Faustus bought the poison that killed a consular at my table and set a man to watch my door and had my lamp boy strangled in a yard. He has served your family for twenty years and in that time he has learned exactly what your family requires of him, and what it requires is that nothing is ever said out loud." She leaned forward. "You did not order this. I know you did not order this, because if you had ordered it you would have done it cleanly and you would have done it yourself, and you would not have left a cloak and a token and a woman in the Subura who remembers a burn on a wrist. Your steward did this to protect your name, and he did it in the way that servants do things for masters who never ask them what they are doing."

Corvus was silent for a long moment.

"If I put Faustus in the cells," he said, "he will talk."

"Yes."

"He will say that he took money from Publius Vettius to arrange the death of a consular, and that he did it without my knowledge, and that he used a slave of my household to carry the cup. And the panel will ask why a steward of the Claudii Corvi took money from a man to kill his father, and he will say that he did it for me, because that is what he believes, and because in twenty years I have never once told him not to." He pressed his hands flat against the table. "And I will be tried in the Senate for complicity in the murder of a consular, and my consulship will be gone, and my son will not marry, and my daughter will not marry, and my mother will die of it, and the name of the Claudii Corvi will be a joke in the Forum for a hundred years."

"Yes," Antonia said. "I know."

"You are asking me to destroy my family."

"I am asking you to be destroyed," she said, "instead of me."

The flutes stopped. In the silence they could hear the murmur of the neighbours in the atrium and somebody crying, and beyond that the noise of the street.

"How much?" Corvus said.

"What?"

"How much. For the boy's family, for the house, for the women, for the loss of the season. Name a sum. I will pay it out of my own purse and it will not be a small one, and we will call it compensation and it will be paid quietly and no one will ever know."

"He had no family. His mother sold him at seven for forty sesterces and went to Puteoli with a soldier, and I asked, because I am the kind of woman who asks." Antonia stood up. "You cannot pay for this. That is the thing you have never understood and the thing your father never understood and the thing every man in the Senate has never understood, and it is why the city is the way it is. You think that everything can be arranged. You think that there is always a sum, and if the sum is large enough, the thing is finished. And it is not finished. It goes on. It goes on in the woman who sold her son and in the man who carried the cup and in the boy in the yard, and it goes on in me, and it will go on in you, and no sum will ever make it stop."

Corvus looked at her.

"You have changed," he said.

"I have not changed at all. I have simply run out of the patience I used to spend on men like you." She sat down again. "What would you have me do?" Corvus said.

She looked at him.

"Go and look at him," she said.

"What?"

"Go out into the atrium and look at the boy on the couch. Not at me. Not at the papers on the table. Go and stand over him for a minute and look at what was done, and then come back in here and tell me what you would have me do."

Corvus stood in the door of the counting room.

"I have seen dead men," he said.

"Not this one."

He went out.

The atrium was full of the low sound of people being quiet together. The flutes had stopped; the two brothers had put them down and were eating bread by the door. The neighbours stood along the wall. The lamps at the boy's head and feet made a small yellow room in the middle of the larger dark, and there was a smell of myrtle and wet wool and, underneath it, the smell that Antonia had learned to recognize in the Subura when she was a child and had never got out of her nose.

Corvus stood at the foot of the couch and looked.

Felix had been washed and dressed. Somebody had combed his hair with water so that it lay flat, which it never had in life. He had a coin on each eye and a wreath of myrtle on his head that was too big for him and kept slipping sideways, and in his hand, because Calybe was not there to stop them, the women had put a honey cake.

His throat was the colour of a plum, in the shape of a hand.

Corvus looked at it for a long time.

He had seen dead men. He had seen them in Germany in the winter of the second year, when a party of his had been caught in a wood; he had seen a man with his jaw shot away and a man who had frozen sitting up against a tree with his eyes open. He had stood in the Forum and heard the death sentences read out and had voted for them. He had been in the room when his father's body was laid out, and had looked at the hole in the throat and had thought about the shape of the pen.

This was different, and it took him a moment to understand why.

It was the honey cake.

Somebody had put a honey cake in the hand of a dead boy because he had liked them, and it was, in the whole of that house, the most expensive thing anyone had done that day. It had cost four sesterces. It was going to be burned with him. It had no purpose at all, and no one would ever know about it except the women who had done it and the man who was standing over him now, and it had been done anyway, out of a habit of love that the city had no line for in any inventory.

Corvus put his hand over his mouth.

He had been beaten by his father twice in his life: once at nine, for laughing at a slave who was being flogged, and once at fourteen, for a reason he had never understood. He had been made to stand and watch the flogging at nine, because his father said that a boy who was going to be a senator should know what discipline was, and he had stood in the yard and watched a man's back open and had been sick afterward in the stable, and his father had never mentioned it again.

He had thought, for years, that the lesson of that afternoon had been about the slaves.

It had not been. The lesson had been about his father: that a man may do a thing in front of his son and go in to dinner afterward and talk about the corn supply, and that the son will learn the thing that was done and not the thing that was said, and that he will carry it into rooms for the rest of his life without knowing where he got it.

He went back into the counting room.

"Well?" Antonia said.

"I do not know what to say."

"No," she said. "You do not. That is the first honest thing you have said in this house." She was standing by the window with her arms folded. "Here is what will happen. You will not give me Faustus, because you cannot, and I will not go to the prefect with a scrap of cloth and the word of a dead woman in the Subura, because he will laugh at me and give it to Maro and Maro will hang me. So we will do it the other way."

"What other way?"

"I am going to give a dinner," Antonia said.

He stared at her.

"A dinner."

"On the Kalends. In this house. With the door open and the lamps lit and the couches out and the best wine, and I will invite every man who has ever eaten at my table. Nine of them will come. Perhaps eleven. Blandus will come because he owes me sixty thousand and I have given him the note, and Hostilius will come because he is afraid of his wife and a dinner is an excuse, and Sura's son will come because he owes me ninety thousand and hopes I will forget it, and there are four more who will come because they are curious, and one or two who will come because they are brave." She smiled. "And you will come."

"I will not."

"You will come," Antonia said, "because if you do not come, every man in that room will know that the consul-designate is afraid of a woman, and by the morning it will be in the Forum, and by the Ides it will be in Antioch. And because—" she paused. "Because you want to know what I will say."

Corvus looked at her for a long time. In the lamplight his face was tired and not young, and there was a greyness around his mouth that had not been there in the basilica.

"If I come," he said, "you will say nothing about the boy."

"I will say nothing about the boy."

"You will say nothing about Faustus."

"I will say nothing about Faustus."

"And in return?"

"In return," said Antonia, "you will do the one thing that a man in your position can do, which is to be seen to eat at my table on the Kalends, three days after my house was closed by the prefect, and to be seen to enjoy it. And the men who come will see you there, and they will think: the inquiry is not what it seems. And the prefect will hear that you were there, and he will think: the case is not what it seems. And the case will become a thing that nobody wants to touch, and it will die of being avoided, which is how most cases die in this city."

"That is not justice."

"No," Antonia said. "It is better. Justice would require the death of a consul's son and the ruin of a great house and the confession of a steward, and none of those things will happen, and a boy would still be dead. What I am offering you is survival, for both of us, and a dinner."

Corvus stood up. He walked to the door and put his hand on it, and then he stopped.

"How did you do it?" he asked.

"How did I do what?"

"All of this." He turned. "You came out of my father's house with a purse and a lie and nothing else. You were twenty-three years old. You had no family, no patron, no name, and a trade that the law despises and the Senate pretends not to use. And now you have a house with a fountain and a cook worth more than my steward, and half the Senate owes you money, and you have just invited a consul-designate to dinner and he is going to come." His voice was not friendly. It was not anything. "How?"

Antonia looked at him.

"Every morning," she said, "I got up before it was light. And I counted what I had, and I wrote down what I owed, and I made one friend and did one favour and asked for one thing, and I did not drink, and I did not trust a man who smiled at me, and I did not spend money on anything that could not be sold, and I did not let anyone see me cry, and I did that every day for seventeen years." She stood. "You have had a house and a name and a father's clients and an emperor's favour and you have spent your whole life being careful not to lose any of it. I have spent mine building something out of nothing. Those are not the same work, and the fact that you cannot tell the difference is why you are going to lose."

He opened the door.

"On the Kalends," he said.

"On the Kalends."

He went out through the atrium past the boy on the couch, and the neighbours made way for him, and old Rufus, who had been a client of his father's, said, "Dominus," and Corvus did not hear him.

They burned Felix at dawn on the Esquiline, outside the wall, in the potters' field where the poor are burned, and Antonia paid for a place at the front and for a stone.

The stone was small and grey, with four lines cut into it:

FELIX
SLAVE OF ANTONIA CASSIA
TWELVE YEARS
HE WAS QUICK

The stonecutter had asked her what she wanted it to say and she had thought for a long time and then said, *He was quick.* The man had looked at her and said, *Is that all?* and she had said, *It is more than most men have said of them.*

Tiro stood at the back with his hands folded. Calybe wept. Meroe wept and was angry about it. Leda did not weep at all, and stood with her hands at her sides and her face white, and afterward she went back to the house alone.

Antonia stayed until the fire had fallen in.

The potters' field was a flat, dusty place with a wall around it and a smell that got into your clothes. There were other fires going that morning: an old woman, a man with a crooked leg, a baby. The smoke went up in three or four columns, and the men who tended the fires went about their work with the calm of men who have seen everything, and a dog sat at a distance and watched.

Antonia stood in the smoke and made a list.

She made it in her head, the way she made every list: the names, the sums, the weaknesses. Blandus, who owed her sixty thousand and had promised to stand up. Hostilius, who was afraid of his wife and had a villa he could not afford. Sura's son, who was a fool and could be led. The praetor in the Subura, whose name she did not write down. Vipsania Marcella, who had been her friend for eleven years and had not called in nine days. Eutychus, who wanted the house and the list. Corvus, who would come to dinner. Publius Vettius, who had paid for the poison and was now walking about the city in a clean toga, waiting to inherit.

And Faustus.

When the fire had burned down she went back to the house and sat in the atrium and had the door opened, and the neighbours came in again, and she received them.

The inventory came on the day after the funeral.

There were three of them: a clerk of the prefecture with a tablet and a boy to carry the ink, and a man from the office of the city's property who had the flat, patient face of a man who has spent thirty years putting a value on other people's things.

They came at the second hour and Antonia received them in the atrium and said, "You will not go into the women's rooms."

"The law requires—"

"You will not go into the women's rooms," Antonia said, "and if you do, I will go to the prefect's house tonight and stand in his atrium and tell his wife what your clerk said to my doorkeeper, and I will do it loudly, and I will be arrested, and it will be in the Forum by the morning, and the prefect will have you flogged for making his life difficult in the fourth week of an inquiry." She stepped aside. "The rest of the house is yours."

They went through the rest of the house.

It took four hours. Antonia followed them. That was the worst of it: that she followed them, room by room, and watched a man with a flat face write down her life in a list.

*Dining room: three couches, citron wood, bronze feet, valued at nine thousand sesterces. One table, marble, four feet, with a lion's head. Eleven lamps, bronze, of which two are better than the others. One silver service, sixteen pieces, valued at twenty-two thousand. Curtains, six, of Egyptian linen.*

*Courtyard: one fountain, marble and bronze, with a myrtle tree, valued at four thousand. One bench, stone. Four pots, terracotta.*

*Kitchen: one oven, brick. One set of copper pans, eleven pieces. One mortar, one sieve, one set of knives.*

*Strongbox: one, oak, iron-bound, containing—*

Here the clerk stopped and looked at her.

"The box is to be sealed," he said. "The prefect's office will take an inventory of the contents in the presence of two witnesses."

"The contents are loan tablets and deeds."

"Then they will be listed." He wrote: *contents, to be inventoried.* "You may keep the box until the trial, on your recognizance, because the prefect has said so, and because you are not charged with theft." He turned the page. "The household."

Antonia said nothing.

"Eleven slaves," the clerk said. "Names?"

"Prisca, housekeeper. Calybe, cook, taken by the prefect's men and not returned. Meroe, dancer. Junilla, doorkeeper. Tiro, steward, freedman. Felix—" she stopped. "Felix, lamp boy, deceased."

"Value of the deceased?"

"Eight hundred sesterces."

The clerk wrote it down.

"Continue."

"Two kitchen girls, one washerwoman, one girl who carries the water, one boy who carries the water." She gave the names. "They are not for sale."

"Everything in this house is under the recognizance of the court until the trial," the clerk said, and wrote: *eleven slaves, to be valued at the conclusion of the inquiry.* He looked up. "Domina. The prefect has instructed me to say a thing to you, and I will say it now and then we will finish, and you will not answer me, because I am a clerk and it is not my business." He lowered his voice. "He says that a woman who keeps a house like this one should have had a friend in the Senate by now. He says that in nineteen years he has never seen a case in which a person of means had no one to speak for them, and that it is the strangest thing about the whole affair, and that it will be the thing that finishes you."

Antonia looked at him.

"Tell the prefect," she said, "that I have had a great many friends in the Senate, and that they were friends of the house and not of the woman, and that I have discovered this year that there is a difference, and that I am paying for it."

The clerk wrote something down that was not part of the inventory, and closed his tablet, and they went.

She walked through the house after they had gone.

The dining room with the garden painted on the walls and the birds among the leaves. The fountain with the bronze myrtle dripping into the basin. The little room by the stairs where a girl had slept for six years. The counting room with the tablets in their racks. The kitchen, where Calybe's rolling pin was still on the shelf.

She had built it out of nothing in seventeen years, and a man with a flat face had written it down in four hours, and the total had come to fifty-one thousand sesterces, which was less than she was owed and less than she had paid for it and nothing at all like what it was.

The women came to her that evening in the atrium, one by one and then all together, and asked whether they should go.

Meroe said it first, because Meroe was the bravest of them and had the least to lose, having a contract she could not read and four years of a plan. "Domina. If the house closes, we cannot stay in it. There is no money coming in and there is the rent on the shops and the wine merchant and the loan. If we stay, we eat the winter stores and then we have nothing, and in the spring we open with no stock and no money and a debt to a man who owns the ground under us."

"You have been talking to Zosimus."

"I have been talking to Prisca, and Prisca has been talking to Zosimus, and between the three of us we can do sums." Meroe folded her arms. "We are not leaving because we are frightened. We are asking whether we should leave so that the house can be shut properly and opened in the spring with something in the chest."

Antonia looked at them: Meroe, Prisca, Junilla, the two girls, the washerwoman, the girl who carried the water. Seven. Calybe in a cell. Tiro in the kitchen with the accounts. Felix on the Esquiline.

"No," she said.

"Domina—"

"No one leaves, and no one is sold, and the house does not close." She stood up. "I have been poor and I have been careful and I have been sensible, and every sensible thing I have ever done has been a way of being afraid in an orderly manner. This is not a year for sensible." She looked at them. "On the Kalends I am giving a dinner. The best dinner this house has ever given. The Falernian, and the turbot, and the lamps along the whole front, and every man who has ever eaten at my table is going to be invited, and some of them are going to come, and when they come they will be seen coming, and the whole city will see that Antonia Cassia is not hiding."

There was a silence.

"That is either very brave or very stupid," said Prisca.

"Yes," Antonia said. "We will find out on the Kalends."

That night she wrote the invitations herself.

She had a good hand. She had been taught it at seventeen by a young man in a house on the Palatine who had sat with her in a little room by the stairs and guided her fingers through the alphabet, and she had kept the hand he taught her, and it was the only thing of his that she had ever allowed herself to use.

She wrote eleven.

To Decimus Terentius Blandus, senator, at his house on the Caelian: *Antonia Cassia greets her friend. On the Kalends of July there will be dinner at the Myrtle, at the tenth hour, and the door will be open, and there will be the Falernian you liked and the cook has been told to make the turbot. Come, and bring no one.*

To Hostilius Rufus, knight: the same, with the addition of a private word about the loan, which he would understand.

To the son of Sura.

To the praetor.

To Vipsania Marcella, whose husband was a consular and who had not called in nine days: the same, and at the bottom, in a smaller hand, *I know why you have not come. Come anyway. I have never asked you for anything.*

To Eutychus: the same, and at the bottom, *Bring the figs.*

To Gaius Claudius Corvus, consul-designate: the same, and at the bottom, *The fourth couch, on the left, where you sat when you were twenty-four. The cushion has been kept.*

And then she sat with the stylus in her hand for a long time, and the lamp burned down, and outside the city went on with its noise, and she thought about the fact that she had lied to Corvus twice in one night and had done it without any difficulty at all, and that the lies had been the truest thing she had said.

She had told him she would say nothing about the boy, and she would say nothing about the boy.

She had told him she would say nothing about Faustus.

That one she had not meant at all.
