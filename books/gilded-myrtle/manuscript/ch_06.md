# Chapter Six

## Antonia

Eutychus came at the second hour, in a hired chair, with one slave and no escort, and he brought a basket of figs.

"From my place at Tusculum," he said, setting it on the table himself, before anyone could take it from him. "The first of the season. They are not as good as the ones from the coast, but they are earlier, and a man of my age has learned to prefer the early thing to the good thing, because he may not be alive for the good thing."

"You are fifty-five."

"I am fifty-five and I have a stomach like a bag of nails. Sit down, domina. You look as though you have been up all night."

"I have."

"Of course you have." He sat, uninvited, on the bench by the fountain. "I will not waste your time. You know what I am and what I can do and what I want, and I know what you are and what you can do and what you want. We are the two most practical people in this city and we may speak to one another without the usual noises."

Antonia sat down opposite him.

"Then speak."

"Three things." He held up one finger. "First: your house must close for a season. Not because I wish it. Because the prefect must be seen to do something, and the cheapest thing for him to do is to close a brothel. If your door is shut on the Ides, and there is a notice on it, and a fine is paid, then in the autumn you open again and the whole thing is a bad memory. If your door stays open, the prefect must do something larger, and larger things are harder to control."

"Second."

"Second: the girl." He held up another finger. "The singer. She is to be produced before the panel, and she is to say that she saw nothing in the passage. Nothing at all. Not a man, not a cup, not a shadow. The panel will accept it because the panel wants to accept it, and the death will be recorded as a seizure of the heart, which is what the physicians say when a great man dies inconveniently, and the whole affair will be finished by the Kalends."

Antonia looked at him.

"The girl saw a man," she said. "She has already told me so. She will tell the panel."

"Then the girl will be mistaken." Eutychus smiled, and it was a pleasant smile, and it did not reach his eyes. "A slave's memory is a fragile thing. It can be corrected. That is what the law is for."

"And the third thing?"

"The third thing is a list." He took a fig from the basket and turned it in his fingers. "I know what your house is. It is not a brothel; a brothel is a place where men go to be quick and ashamed. Your house is a place where men go to be slow and unwise. I know that senators have sat in that room and told you things they have not told their wives, their bankers or their gods, and I know that you have written some of it down, because you are a woman who has been poor, and a woman who has been poor writes things down."

"I have a good memory."

"You have a good memory and a locked box. I am not asking for the box. I am asking for a copy of the list of what you are owed. Names, sums, dates. The men who owe you money are the men who will vote as they are told, and I am in the business of knowing who votes as they are told." He put the fig in his mouth and chewed. "In return, I will make the prefect's inquiry a formality. I will find a man in Vettius's household who will confess to having brought the cup in, and the confession will be believed because he will be a slave and slaves confess, and the son will be grateful and the prefect will be satisfied and your house will reopen in the autumn with a new fountain."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you will be tried for the murder of a consular, and the fact that you did not do it will not save you, because the trial is not about what you did. It is about what you are." He dusted the fig seeds from his fingers. "You are a freedwoman who keeps a house of women. You cannot give evidence in your own defence. You have no family to speak for you, no patron who will stand up in court and vouch for your character, and no name that anyone will remember in a year. The prefect will find what the prefect needs to find. You know this. You have watched it happen to other women."

"I have."

"And yet you sit there with your hands folded."

"I am thinking," Antonia said, "about which of us is more afraid."

Eutychus stopped chewing.

"You are a rich man," she went on. "You are a freedman who has made himself the second most powerful man in the accounts of the empire, and you have a house at Tusculum with a garden that people are invited to admire. And every sesterce of it depends on one thing, which is that the emperor continues to find you useful. You have no name, no ancestors, no soldiers, no land that has been in your family for a thousand years. If Trajan dies tomorrow and Hadrian takes the purple, you will be a clever old man with a good garden and no friends, and the men who come to my house will not take your calls."

"There is a great deal in what you say," said Eutychus, "and none of it is new to me, and none of it will help you."

"I am not finished. You want the list because you want to know which senators are in debt, and you want to know it before the emperor comes back from the east, because the emperor is going to need money for the Parthian war and you are going to have to find it, and a man who can produce a list of indebted senators and their weaknesses is a man who can produce money out of a hat." She leaned forward. "You do not want to destroy me. You want to own me. And you have made a mistake, which is that you have come to me in the morning with a basket of figs and told me a lie about the girl, and the lie is the only thing in the whole of your proposal that tells me what you are afraid of."

Eutychus set the basket down.

"What lie?" he said.

"The man in the passage was not a slave of Vettius's household. He had short hair and a dark tunic with a narrow stripe, and he knew the passage, and he turned his shoulder when he saw the girl, which is what a man does when he is known in a house and does not wish to be. If he had been Vettius's man, she would have recognized him, because she had spent three hours in the same room as Vettius's people." She watched him. "He was your man. Or the son's man, hired through you. And you want the girl to say nothing because the girl's nothing is the only thing standing between you and a rope."

The fountain ran. Somewhere in the house a woman was singing at her work, badly, the way people sing when they think no one is listening.

"You have a great deal of imagination," Eutychus said at last, "and no proof at all."

"I have a witness."

"You have a slave. A slave who will be asked to repeat her story under the hooks, and who will not, because no one does." He stood up and picked up his basket, and looked at it, and set it down again. "Keep the figs. They will be a pleasant thing to have in the house while you are waiting."

"Eutychus."

He stopped at the door.

"If you are going to have me killed," Antonia said, "do it before the Ides. After the Ides I shall be at the baths every morning and it will be much more inconvenient."

He laughed, in spite of himself, and shook his head, and went out.

The counting room was at the back of the house, behind the kitchen, a small room with no window and a strong door and a table with a lamp and a set of abacus beads and a great many tablets stacked in racks along the wall. Antonia had it built in the second year, and she had built it before she built the dining room, which she had once said was the truest thing about her.

Zosimus was waiting in it. He was sixty, a Greek freedman of hers, bald, with the ink-stained fingers of a man who had done nothing else for forty years, and he had kept her accounts since the beginning, and he was the only person in the world who knew to the sesterce what she was worth.

"Tell me," she said, and sat.

"We have forty-one thousand sesterces in the chest, and eleven thousand in the hands of the money-changer by the Macellum, which is a convenience and not a risk. We are owed six hundred and twenty thousand, of which—" he ran his finger down a tablet—"four hundred and eleven thousand is out at interest, and two hundred and nine thousand is in the form of unpaid principal that is not earning, which is to say it is owed by men who cannot pay and who are, nevertheless, our friends."

"Names."

"Blandus, sixty thousand, no interest and no prospect. Sura's son, ninety thousand, secured on a farm in Campania that is worth perhaps eighty and which he will not sell. The knight Hostilius, forty thousand, at six per cent, who pays on time and is afraid of his wife. A praetor whose name I will not write down, one hundred and ten thousand, in a house in the Subura that is worth nothing. The rest is in small sums to small men, and it will come in over the year or it will not."

"And the debts?"

"Nine thousand to the wine merchant. Four thousand to the dealer in lamps. Twelve thousand to the builders, who are patient. And—" he hesitated.

"And?"

"Two hundred thousand to the bank of Tiberius Claudius Eutychus, at four per cent, of which one hundred thousand falls due on the Kalends of July and is secured on this house."

Antonia sat back. The lamp flickered in the draught from under the door.

"When did we borrow that?"

"Three years ago, when we bought the two shops in the Argiletum. You said at the time that a woman who borrows from a man like Eutychus is a woman who has given him a key to her house."

"I remember what I said."

"You said it would be cheaper than borrowing from a senator, because a senator would want a favour and Eutychus would only want money."

"I was wrong," Antonia said. "He wants both."

She sat for a while with her hands flat on the table.

"Here is what we will do," she said. "Send to Blandus. Tell him to come tonight, by the kitchen door, and tell him that if he is seen coming he will be turned away. He will come. He is the sort of man who will always come if he thinks there is a chance of something." She stood. "And Zosimus. Take the small tablets, the ones with the personal notes on them, the ones that are not accounts. Not the loan tablets. The other ones. Put them in the strongbox in my room and burn everything else in this room that is not a ledger."

"Domina—"

"Burn it. I will not be tried for what I know. I will be tried for what I am." She stopped at the door. "And Zosimus. If I am dead before the Ides, take the strongbox to Tiro, and if Tiro is dead, take it to the Vestals and ask for the chief vestal, and tell her that Antonia Cassia asked for the old kindness, and she will know what it means."

"There is no old kindness."

"No," Antonia said. "But she will take the box and she will not open it, because she is a woman who has spent her life not opening things, and it will be safe, and that is more than I can say for the house of any man I know."

Vipsania Marcella lived on the Caelian, in a house that had belonged to her husband's family for two hundred years and had been added to by four generations of men who could not agree about anything, so that the atrium was old Republican and the peristyle was Augustan and the new bath wing was the sort of thing that a man builds when he has made a great deal of money in Africa and wants everyone to know.

Antonia had been in that house perhaps fifty times in eleven years. She had dined in the peristyle. She had sat with Vipsania in the winter garden while the older woman complained about her husband's digestion and her son's tutors and the price of silk. She had been useful to her: a discreet place for a woman of her rank to buy a thing she did not want her husband to know about, and a source of gossip that was accurate and never cruel about anyone Vipsania liked.

She was kept waiting in the atrium for an hour.

That had never happened before. In eleven years she had never once been kept waiting in that house, and as she stood by the impluvium with the old Republican floor under her feet and the smell of the garden coming through the door, she understood that it was not an oversight.

Vipsania came at last.

She was forty-four, and handsome in the way that rich Roman women are handsome, with a great deal of hair and a great deal of gold and a way of holding her head that made her look as though she were listening to something a long way off. She came down the stairs with two of her women behind her and stopped at the bottom, and she did not come across the floor.

"Antonia."

"Vipsania."

"You should not have come."

"I know."

"I have been ill." The older woman's eyes went to the side, to the door, to the garden, to anywhere but her. "I have not been receiving. My husband has been—" she stopped. "You should not have come to this house. There are men who watch it. My husband has a position; he is on the board for the water; if it were known that I had received you—"

"Then receive me in the garden," Antonia said, "where the servants cannot hear, for five minutes, and then I will go, and I will not come again."

Vipsania looked at her.

Then she turned and walked into the garden, and Antonia followed her.

The winter garden was a green place with a pool and a hundred pots and a small bronze boy with a dolphin. Vipsania stood by the pool with her back to her, and her shoulders were shaking.

"I cannot," she said.

"You can. You are the wife of a consular. Your word is worth something in that basilica, and your husband's is worth more, and if you go to him tonight and tell him that he is to speak for me, he will do it, because he is afraid of you and because he has been afraid of you since the day you married him."

"Antonia—"

"Eleven years," Antonia said. "Eleven years, Vipsania. I have kept your secrets. I have kept the one about your son and the horse, and the one about the debt to the Jew in the Transitberina that your husband does not know about, and the one about the summer you spent at Baiae when your husband was in Africa. I have never asked you for anything. Not once. Not when the house was closed, not when the edict went up, not when a boy of twelve was carried out of a yard in the Via Tusculana." Her voice was quiet. "I am asking you now."

Vipsania turned around.

She was crying, and it was not the kind of crying that Antonia had seen in her own house, the useful kind; it was the ugly kind, with the mouth open.

"You do not understand," she said. "You have never understood. You are not like me. You have never had to be liked."

"I have spent my entire life having to be liked."

"No." Vipsania wiped her face with the back of her hand. "You have spent your life being necessary. It is not the same. A woman like me has no money of her own and no house of her own and no name of her own, and the only thing she has is the opinion that other people have of her, and she has to keep it the way a man keeps a wall. If I speak for you in that basilica, my husband will not divorce me; he is too afraid of my father's family. But every woman in Rome will know by the evening, and every one of them will say: *she has been going to that house for eleven years. What do you think she was buying there?* And my son will hear it at the baths, and my daughter's betrothal will be—" She stopped.

Antonia looked at her.

"And a boy is dead," she said.

"Yes."

"And I am to be tried for my life."

"Yes." Vipsania's voice was small. "I am sorry. I am truly sorry. I have not slept since it happened, and I have prayed, and I have made an offering at the temple of Juno, and it has not helped. I am not a brave woman. I have never been a brave woman. I have been a woman who was liked, and it has been enough for forty-four years, and now I find that it is not enough, and I cannot make myself into something else at my age."

Antonia stood in the winter garden of the house on the Caelian and looked at the woman she had known for eleven years.

"You will think about this for the rest of your life," she said.

"I know."

"Not tonight. Not next month. In ten years, when you are fifty-four and your son is in the Senate and your daughter is married and everything is exactly as you wanted it, you will be sitting in this garden in the afternoon and it will come to you that there was a week in the summer when you could have stood up and did not." Antonia turned to go. "And you will find that you cannot tell anyone, because everyone you know will agree with what you did."

Vipsania put out her hand.

"Antonia—"

"Do not."

"I will send money."

"Keep your money," Antonia said. "I have never taken money from a friend and I am not going to start on the worst day of my life."

She went out through the peristyle and the old Republican atrium and past the two women at the door, and the doorkeeper, who had known her for eleven years and had always called her domina, looked at the floor as she passed him.

She walked home along the Caelian in the heat.

Halfway down, in the shadow of the wall of the Claudian aqueduct, she stopped and put her hand against the stone, and stood there for a while with her eyes shut.

*Five,* she thought. *Blandus, and Hostilius if I press him, and Sura's son if I frighten him, and the praetor, if the praetor is capable of anything, which he is not. Four. And Vipsania was the fifth, and Vipsania is a woman like me, and I have known for a year that she would not stand, and I asked her anyway, because a woman who is drowning will ask anyone.*

She went on down the hill.

The edict came on the fourth day, and it was nailed to the door by a public slave with a pot of paste and a brush, while a small crowd gathered to watch and the neighbours came out onto their balconies.

It said, in the flat language of the prefect's office, that whereas a death had occurred in the fourth region of the city in a house of public resort, and whereas the inquiry was not yet concluded, and whereas the peace of the region was to be preserved, no house of public entertainment in the fourth region was to be open for the sale of food, drink or company until the inquiry was concluded; and that the owner of the house concerned was to remain in the city and to present herself at the prefecture when called.

There was a space at the bottom for the seal, and the seal was there, a bull in a gate.

Junilla read it, because Junilla could read, and then she came in and stood in the middle of the atrium with the paste still on her hands.

"They have shut us," she said.

"I know."

"For how long?"

"Until the inquiry is concluded."

"And when is that?"

"When the prefect concludes it," Antonia said.

The women were all there by then, standing in the atrium and on the stairs, Meroe with her hair half braided, Prisca with the keys at her belt, the kitchen girls with their arms wet to the elbow, Leda in the doorway with her lyre in her hands. Nobody said anything. Outside, the crowd was still there, and somebody laughed, and somebody else said something that made the others laugh, and a boy threw a stone at the door and it made a flat, stupid sound and everyone in the house flinched.

"Listen to me," Antonia said.

They listened.

"This is not the end. It is a bad month, and there will be a worse one after it, and then it will be over. Nobody is to leave the house after dark. Nobody is to speak to the men at the door. If a man comes to the door and asks for me, he is to be told that I am at the baths, and if he asks when I will be back, he is to be told that I did not say." She looked at each of them in turn, the way she looked at the fourth basin. "Junilla, take the notice down and burn it. Prisca, we will need the winter stores brought up now; the price of oil will go up when the inquiry is in the streets and I will not pay it twice. Meroe, you will practise in the courtyard every morning where the neighbours can hear you, because a house with music in it is a house that is not afraid. Calybe, we will eat plainly for a month and nobody is to complain, and on the Kalends we will have a dinner for the household, with the good wine, and we will invite nobody."

"And the girls who are not of the household?" Prisca said. "The ones who come in for the evenings?"

"Tell them to stay away until the autumn. Pay them for the month. In full."

"That is three thousand sesterces."

"It is cheaper than a girl who is frightened and talks to the watch." Antonia turned to Leda. "You. Come with me."

She took her into the counting room and shut the door.

"The panel sits in nine days," she said. "You will be called. My patron will be there—" she stopped, and corrected herself, because the word had come out of her mouth before she had thought about it. "Gaius Claudius Corvus will be there. He is the man who came to the basilica, the one whose son you have been meeting by the river, and he has known about you for some time. He will ask you questions. He will be courteous. He will be the most courteous man in the room, and he will try to make you say something you do not mean to say."

"I know," Leda said. "I have met his son."

"His son is not his father."

"No," said Leda. "That is what everyone keeps telling me."

Antonia looked at her for a moment. There was something in the girl's face that had not been there a month ago: a hardness, a thing shut like a door.

"You will tell them what you saw," Antonia said. "The man, the tunic, the cup, the passage. Nothing more. Not what you think, not what you guessed, not what I told you. What you saw."

"And what will happen to me afterward?"

"You will be given to your mother, if Corvus has his way. Or you will be kept as a witness, if the prefect has his. Or—" Antonia hesitated, and then said it, because the girl deserved at least one true thing. "Or you will be sold, if I lose, because everything in this house is mine and a woman who loses a case in Rome loses everything, including the people who trusted her."

Leda nodded slowly.

"Then I will not lose," she said, and went out.

Antonia stood alone in the counting room for a while. Then she went out into the courtyard, where the fountain was running and the bronze myrtle was dripping, and she looked up at the strip of sky above the roof, and she thought about the man in the passage with his short hair and his dark tunic and his shoulder turned away, and she thought: *Somebody in this city is being paid to have me destroyed, and it is not Eutychus, because Eutychus would have done it more cheaply. Eutychus is being paid too. Somebody has bought us both.*

She called Felix.

He came at a run, as he always did, with his hair standing up and a smudge on his cheek from the lamp.

"You remember the man I told you about," she said. "The neat one, with the tablet, who comes out of the kitchen door at night."

"Faustus," said Felix. "The steward of the house on the Palatine."

"Yes." She crouched down so that her eyes were level with his. "I want you to follow him."

Felix's face lit up.

"Follow him where?"

"Wherever he goes. He will not see you if you are careful, and you are small and you are quick and you know this quarter better than he does. You will not speak to him. You will not let him see your face. You will watch where he goes and who he meets and how long he stays, and then you will come back and tell me." She put her hand on his shoulder. "And Felix. If anything happens that frightens you, you will run. You will not be brave. You will run, and you will come home, and I will not be angry."

"I am not afraid of him."

"You should be," Antonia said.

She watched him go out through the kitchen door with his shoulders back and his chin up, twelve years old and eight hundred sesterces and worth more to her than the whole of the house, and she stood in the courtyard for a long time afterward with her hand on the rim of the fountain, listening to the water.

That night Blandus came to the kitchen door.

He came with his toga pulled over his head and his shoes in his hand and the look of a man who had spent an hour working out how to be seen by nobody, and he stood in the kitchen with the smell of wine on him and his eyes going everywhere, and he said, "I heard. I heard about the edict. I came at once."

"You came because I sent for you."

"That too." He sat down heavily on the bench. "Antonia. I cannot pay the sixty thousand."

"I know."

"I have until the Kalends of August. If I do not pay, the man in the Velabrum will take the house at Signia, and my wife will—" he put his face in his hands. "I have made a mess of it. I have made a mess of everything. I had a career, you know, when I was young; I was quaestor at thirty-one, and I had a wife with a good name, and I have spent twenty years being charming to people who could have helped me and never asking them for anything, and now I am fifty and I have nothing to show for it but a beautiful wife who despises me and two sons who are ashamed."

Antonia sat down across from him.

"Look at me," she said.

He looked up.

"You owe me sixty thousand sesterces. I am going to give you the note."

He stared.

"What?"

"I am going to give you the note, and I am going to write on it that the debt is discharged, and you will take it to the man in the Velabrum and show it to him, and he will understand that Decimus Terentius Blandus has a patron who is owed by half the Senate and he will wait." She took a tablet from the shelf and broke the seal on the note and wrote across the wax while he watched. "In return, you will do one thing for me."

"Anything."

"Do not say anything," Antonia said. "Not yet. When the panel sits, and they ask whether anyone in Rome can speak for the character of Antonia Cassia, you will stand up."

Blandus's face did something complicated.

"The panel is the prefect's," he said. "To speak for you there is to be marked."

"Yes."

"I am a senator."

"Yes."

"I have a wife and two sons and a name that has been in this city for four hundred years."

"Yes," Antonia said, "and you have sixty thousand sesterces of my money in your hand."

He looked at the tablet. He looked at the wall. He looked, for a long moment, at nothing at all.

"I will stand up," he said.

"Thank you."

"I am not doing it for the money."

"No," said Antonia. "You are doing it because you are a man who has spent his life being liked and has just discovered what it costs to be needed. It is not the same thing, and it is worth more." She stood. "Go out by the kitchen. And Blandus—do not tell anyone you were here."

He went.

Antonia sat alone in the kitchen with the lamp and the cold oven and the sixty thousand sesterces she had just given away, and she thought: *One. That is one man who will stand up. I need nine.*

She counted them on her fingers in the dark, and she got to four.
