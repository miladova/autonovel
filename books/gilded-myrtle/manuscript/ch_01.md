# Chapter One

## Antonia

Two hours before the banquet the cook discovered that the mice had got into the dormice.

They were kept in a clay jar in the back of the kitchen, in the dark, where a sensible animal would have stayed. Five of them, fat as thumbs, fed on walnuts and honey until they were worth forty sesterces apiece. By the time Calybe lifted the lid, four were dead in a corner and the fifth was sitting on the body of its brother with a look of profound and greasy satisfaction.

"Bring me the cat," said Antonia.

"The cat is also fat," Calybe said. She was a Bithynian with arms like a baker and a face that had never once asked permission. "The cat eats better than the kitchen boys. The cat will not trouble himself."

"Then bring me the kitchen boy."

The boy was brought. He was nine, and he had been told at the door that the mistress wanted him, which in that house could mean three or four things, and he had decided to cry on the way in the hope that it would help. Antonia looked at him for a moment.

"You will go to the market by the Circus," she said. "You will find the fat woman with the cages, the one who smells of nuts. You will say: *four for the Myrtle, and she will pay on the Kalends as she always does.* You will not run, because if you fall the jar breaks and then you will be the one in it. Do you understand?"

He understood. He went out with the jar under his arm and came back in an hour with four live dormice, a bruised shin, and the calm of a boy who has learned that the worst thing that can happen to him in this house is to be sent out again.

The house was at the top of the Vicus Tuscus where it bent toward the Velabrum, in the shadow of the Palatine but not of its grandeur. It had been a fuller's shop and two flats when Antonia bought it. Now it was three storeys of brick faced with good stucco, a walled courtyard with a fountain that had a bronze myrtle tree in the middle of it and water coming out of the leaves, and a dining room that could seat nine men on three couches and twelve if they were friendly. On the street side there was nothing to see but a plain door and a lamp niche. The door had no name on it. The name was not required. Men found it anyway, the way water finds a crack.

By the ninth hour the whole house smelled of cumin, pine smoke and roasting birds, and Antonia went through it the way a general goes along a wall, looking for the place where it would fail.

In the courtyard, two boys were scrubbing the fountain with sand. She stopped them. "The fourth basin," she said. "From the left. There is a line in the marble."

"There is no line, domina."

She put her finger on it. There was a line. The boy looked at the line as though it had insulted his mother, and went to get the pumice.

In the corridor behind the kitchen, Prisca had the linens out on a bench, counting them, and Antonia stood at her shoulder and counted with her, without speaking, until Prisca said, "Nine sets, and the tenth for the general if he brings his own man."

"He will bring two. The second one will be a spy for the prefect's office and he will drink nothing but water. Put him at the end, by the door, and give him the cup with the chip in it, so that we know which one he is when he goes."

"The cup with the chip is the good cup."

"Then give him the good cup," Antonia said, "and we will know him by that instead."

She went into the dining room. It was the one room in the house that she had spent money on without counting it first. The walls were painted with a garden that did not exist: myrtles and oleander and a trellis of vines, and among the leaves, small and exact, the faces of birds done so finely that men who had drunk a great deal would sometimes stand up to look at them. The ceiling was coffered and gilded, not thickly, but well. The three couches were of citron wood with bronze feet. There was no mosaic on the floor, only polished black stone, because a floor that showed the dust was a floor that made the servants work.

The lamps came in at the ninth hour, carried by Felix.

He was twelve, and small for it, a Subura child with a face like a question. He had come to the house at seven with the lamp trade, the way some boys come with the water trade, and Antonia had bought him for eight hundred sesterces and had never regretted it except when he talked. He carried the first lamp to the altar in the corner, where the little painted figures of the household gods stood among the flowers, and set it down, and said the words he always said, which were not the right words, since nobody had taught him any.

"Say it properly," Antonia said.

"I do say it properly."

"You say *little gods, don't let the roof fall on me.*"

"It is the same thing," said Felix, with dignity, "only shorter."

She cuffed him, lightly, on the back of the head, and he grinned and went to set the second lamp, and she thought, as she thought perhaps once a month, that if the roof did fall it would be a pity.

Tiro came in from the street with the accounts under his arm and the smell of the river on him. He was fifty-two, and he had been a gladiator in the reign of Domitian, and before that he had been a boy in a slave barracks in Capua, and before that nobody knew. He had a scar that went from his left ear to the corner of his mouth, which made him look as though he were always about to smile at something unpleasant. In seventeen years he had never once called her anything but domina, and had never once meant it.

"The fish is late," he said.

"How late?"

"The man says the roads are full of building stone for the new forum and the carts have the right of way. He says the stone is coming from Liguria and there is a great deal of it."

"The stone is not coming from Liguria. The stone is coming from the quarries at Luna and the carts are coming from Ostia and the fish is coming from the market, and the fish is late because the fishmonger is a liar." She took the tablet from him and looked at the figures. "What is this, here?"

"The wine. The Falernian."

"Nine jars."

"Nine. He would not break the lot. It is a good year and he knows what he has."

"Then he will sell it to someone else, and we will drink the Caecuban, and in the morning I will send him a note saying that I am sorry to have troubled him and that I hope his wife's cough is better, and he will send the Falernian at the price of the Caecuban, because he will think that I know something."

"You do know something."

"I know that his wife does not have a cough," Antonia said. "Put the Falernian away. It will be better in three years and so will he."

She went out into the street for the air.

The Vicus Tuscus at that hour was a river of people going the other way. Porters with bales, a woman selling boiled eggs out of a basket lined with straw, two soldiers of the urban cohorts walking with the unhurried arrogance of men who are permitted to carry sticks in a city where nobody else is. A knife grinder. A boy with a performing goat. The shops were shutting their shutters, and the smell was the smell of the city at evening: bread, urine, pine, the mud of the river, and somewhere close a great deal of garlic.

Across the street a man was standing in the shadow of a doorway, not buying anything.

Antonia looked at him the way she looked at the fourth basin. He was well built, a little gone to fat, with the haircut of a man who had been a soldier and the shoes of a man who was paid by someone. He was not looking at her. He was looking at the door of her house, which was the same thing, only stupider.

She went back inside and found Tiro.

"There is a man across the street."

"I know."

"How long?"

"Three days. He comes at the ninth hour and leaves at the eleventh. He is not very good."

"Who is he?"

"His name is Verus. He was in the seventh cohort of the vigiles and he was put out for taking money from a baker. Now he takes money from anyone. He has a wife and three children and he is behind on his rent, which is why he is not very good, because a man who is behind on his rent will take any job and any job pays badly."

Antonia considered this. "Give him a job."

Tiro's scar moved. "A job."

"Ten sesterces a day to watch the door and tell us who else is watching it. He will say yes, because ten sesterces a day is more than he is being paid to be stupid, and then when the man who hired him asks him what he has seen, he will tell him the truth, and the truth will be what I want him to hear." She turned back toward the dining room. "And Tiro. The fish."

"The fish is coming."

"Of course it is coming. Everything is coming."

The guests came at the tenth hour, in litters, in pairs, some on foot with a lantern boy in front and a slave behind, and the street, which had been emptying, filled again with the particular noise of rich men arriving: the creak of the carrying poles, the murmured instructions to the bearers, the soft explosion of laughter at the door that means nothing has been said yet.

Antonia stood in the atrium to receive them, which was not the custom. Custom would have had her in the dining room, or in a corner of it, or not at all; the wife of a house received her husband's guests and then withdrew, and the women of a house like hers were supposed to be a decoration laid on at the third course. She had tried it that way in the beginning, and had found that men will say things in front of a decoration that they will not say in front of a woman who is looking at them. So she received them in the atrium, in a plain white stola with no jewellery but two pearls, and she looked at each of them for the length of time it takes to blink, and each of them felt it.

Tiberius Claudius Eutychus came first, because Eutychus always came first. He was fifty-five, an imperial freedman, the secretary of accounts, which meant that the money of the empire passed through his hands in the way that water passes through a mill. He was small and neat and grey, with a face like a scholarship, and he wore a plain equestrian ring and nothing else that might be remarked upon. He had the manners of a man who had spent forty years being underestimated in a room and had long since stopped minding, because he won.

"Antonia Cassia." He took both her hands, which no senator would have done. "You are thinner."

"I am richer."

"That is not the same as being well."

"No," she said. "It is better."

He laughed, and went in, and put his hand on Felix's head as he passed, which Felix endured with the expression of a cat being blessed.

Decimus Terentius Blandus came next, a senator with a great name and a small fortune and a way of standing in doorways as though he were owed something. Behind him came Hostilius Rufus, a knight who had made his money in Spanish olive oil and had not yet learned to be quiet about it, and his son, and two men whose names Antonia knew but did not use, and Fannius, who was young and beautiful and entirely without money and had been invited because he made the room look better.

And last, with his own servant and his own cup, Gaius Vettius Proculus.

He was sixty-one and he looked older. He had been consul eleven years before, and there was a statue of him somewhere in the provinces with his name on it, and he had the thin, brittle dignity of a man who has been important for a long time and is beginning to suspect that he has stopped. His hair was white and cut close. His eyes were the colour of weak tea and they went around the atrium once, taking inventory, and stopped on Antonia.

"Domina," he said. "I have heard a great deal about you."

"From whom?"

"From everybody." He had a dry voice, like a knife on a whetstone. "It is the curse of my age that I hear a great deal about everybody and am acquainted with almost no one."

"Then you have come to the right house," Antonia said. "We are all acquaintable here."

He looked at her a moment longer than was polite. "I wonder," he said, and went in.

The dining room at the tenth hour, with nine men on three couches and the lamps lit and the first course coming in on a great silver tray: sea urchins, oysters from Baiae packed in snow from the mountains, eggs dressed with garum and pepper, thrushes in a paste of nuts and honey, and a dish that Calybe had invented and would not explain, which was made of something white and something green and tasted of the sea.

Antonia did not recline. She sat on a stool at the head of the middle couch, where the hostess sat if she was tolerated and the host sat if he was not, and she ate a little of everything and watched.

This was the work. Men thought the work of a house like hers was the wine and the women and the singing. The wine was a cost, the women were a cost, the singing was a cost. The work was the room. A senator who is worried about money will drink too much and talk about it. A senator who has just been passed over for a province will drink too much and talk about the man who was given it. A man who is being blackmailed does not talk at all, but he eats badly and looks at the door. A man who has done something he cannot undo will laugh at everything, a half-beat late.

By the second course she had learned that Blandus had borrowed sixty thousand sesterces from a man who was not a banker and could not pay the interest; that Hostilius had bought a villa at Tibur for a sum that had frightened even him; that Fannius had been offered a military tribunate and did not want to go; that Eutychus was pleased about something and would not say what; and that Vettius Proculus had not touched his wine.

He had a cup, his own cup, and his servant had filled it from his own jar, and he had lifted it twice to his lips and set it down both times without drinking.

"You do not like my wine," Antonia said.

"I do not like any wine."

"That is a misfortune."

"It is a discipline." He turned the cup slowly on the table. "I have a stomach that has declared war on me. My physician says I must choose between my digestion and my friends, and I have chosen my digestion, which is why I have so few friends left."

"Then why come out?"

"Because a man in a room learns things that a man at home does not," Vettius said, "and I am old enough to want to know things more than I want to be comfortable."

The second course: roast kid, a boar's head stuffed with larks, a turbot so large it came in on a board carried by two men, and with it the singing.

Leda sang after the fish, which was where she liked to be, because by then the room was warm and the men had stopped pretending to be interested in each other. She came in through the curtain at the far end with her lyre and stood in the pool of light by the fountain, and she wore green, and she did not look at any of them.

Antonia watched the room instead of the girl.

Nine men, and six of them had stopped chewing. Young Fannius had gone pink to the ears. Hostilius, who was fifty and had a wife and four children, was looking at Leda's hands with an expression that had nothing to do with music. Eutychus was watching Vettius. And Vettius was watching Leda as a man watches a door he thought had been locked.

She sang a song about a girl on a shore watching a ship go out, which was old and simple and had a tune that men remembered from their mothers, and she sang it in a voice that was not large but was absolutely certain, and when she finished there was that little silence that is worth more than applause and that no one can buy.

Vettius set down his cup. It had a chip in the rim, Antonia noticed, on the far side. He had brought his own chipped cup into her house.

"Where is she from?" he asked.

"Who?"

"The singer."

"Puteoli," Antonia said.

"Puteoli." He said the word as though he were tasting it. "She has the accent of the south. Not the city. The bay."

"She has the accent of wherever she was taught to have it."

"And her name?"

"Leda."

"Leda is a name for a slave."

"Most names are," Antonia said.

He smiled, without warmth, and lifted the cup, and this time he drank.

The third course was a dish of quinces and honey and a wine that Antonia had chosen because it was good and not because it was expensive, and by then Blandus was telling a story about the emperor's bridge over the Danube that had every detail wrong, and Hostilius was asleep with his mouth open, and Fannius had gone out to the courtyard with one of the dancers and had not come back.

Antonia went out to the little garden behind the kitchen, where the myrtle grew in a stone box and the air was cooler, and stood for a moment with her hands on the rim of the box. Behind her the house made its noise: laughter, a dropped cup, the lyre starting again, someone singing flat.

Vettius came out.

He had a servant's way of moving for a man of his rank, and she had not heard him until he was three feet away.

"You are not a woman who forgets," he said.

"I forget a great deal."

"Not faces."

"Faces least of all." She turned. In the dark he was only a shape with a white head. "What do you want, Vettius?"

"Straight to it. I like that. I have spent forty years in the Senate listening to men take an hour to say nothing." He put his hand on the myrtle box, close to hers, not touching. "I want the girl."

"Which girl?"

"The singer. Leda." He said it carefully, the way a man says a word in a foreign language. "I will pay well. Twice what you paid. Five times. I am not a man who haggles."

"You have a reputation for it."

"I have a reputation for many things." He looked up at the black square of sky above the garden. "I am sixty-one years old, domina. I have a house on the Caelian with eleven rooms in it and I rattle around in it like a stone in a jar. My son does not speak to me except to ask for money. My wife is dead nine years. I have a great many acquaintances and no one to talk to at night." He said it without self-pity, which was somehow worse. "And six years ago, at Puteoli, I saw a child in a dealer's yard who looked at me as though she knew me. I did not buy her. I have thought about it since."

Antonia did not move.

"That is a strange thing to want," she said.

"I am a strange old man. It is the only luxury I have left."

"There is no girl here who will be sold."

"Everything in this city is sold."

"Then you will have to find the man who is selling," Antonia said, "and it will not be me."

He was quiet for a moment. Then he said, almost gently, "I have heard that before, from a woman I knew. She said it about herself."

"Did she sell?"

"She was sold," Vettius said. "That is not the same, and I have never known which of us it was worse for."

He went back inside. Antonia stood in the garden until her hands were cold.

When she came in, the room had changed its weather. The laughter was too loud. Blandus had his arm around the neck of the man beside him and was telling him something that had made him go grey. Eutychus had moved from his couch to the stool by the door and was eating almonds, one at a time, watching.

And Vettius was standing in the middle of the room with his cup in his hand, not drinking, looking at Leda as she tuned her lyre.

"Sing the one about the ship," he said.

"I have sung it," Leda said.

"Sing it again."

She looked at Antonia. Antonia gave her nothing, which was the answer, and Leda bent her head and began.

She got four lines in.

Vettius made a sound. It was not a large sound. It was the sound a man makes when he has been surprised by something inside his own chest, and he put his hand flat against his sternum and looked down at it as though it belonged to someone else. His cup went over. The wine went across the black floor in a sheet, and the room came to its feet all at once, all nine of them, in the shuffling clatter of men who have eaten too much and are suddenly sober.

"Physician," said Antonia. "Sarpedon. Now."

Sarpedon was in the kitchen, drunk, and came in with his bag and the expression of a man being woken for a fire. He got Vettius onto the floor, and put his ear to the old man's chest, and put his fingers on the throat, and then he sat back on his heels and looked up at Antonia with the particular stupidity of a man who has just remembered how much he has drunk.

"He is dead," he said.

The room was very quiet. Somewhere in it a man was breathing hard through his mouth.

"He was drinking his own wine," said Blandus, too loudly. "His own cup. His own man poured it."

"Be quiet," Antonia said.

She knelt by the body. Vettius's eyes were open and his face had gone the colour of a plum. There was a little foam at the corner of his mouth, and his fingers were curled so hard that the nails had cut the palm. She had seen a man die of a stopped heart before, in the Subura, when she was a child; he had gone grey and quiet and there had been nothing of this purple, thrashing ugliness.

She looked at the cup. It lay on its side with the chip in the rim showing.

"Nobody leaves," she said. "Tiro. The door."

Tiro was already there, with two of the bearers and the boy from the kitchen who was big for his age. The guests were standing in a crescent around the body, their faces in the lamplight, and every one of them was calculating. She could see them doing it. She had built a house in which men calculated, and now the calculation had come home.

Eutychus had not moved from his stool. He set down the almond he was holding and dusted his fingers on his napkin, and looked at Vettius, and then at her. His face was sad, and patient, and entirely without surprise.

"Antonia Cassia," he said. "This will cost you a great deal."

"Will it."

"A consular, in your house, at your table, dead in his own cup." He stood up and straightened his tunic, which did not need it. "The prefect will be told before dawn. He will be told by three men, and each of them will tell him a different story, and he will believe the one that is most useful to him." He walked to the door and stopped. "I could tell him a story that is useful to you."

"At what price?"

"Not tonight," Eutychus said. "A price named over a corpse is a bad price. I will come tomorrow, when you have had time to be frightened."

He went out. Tiro looked at her, and she shook her head, and the door closed.

They put Vettius on a couch in the small room by the atrium, and closed his eyes, and Antonia sent for his servant, who had gone out into the street to be sick and had come back grey and shaking and had to be held up by two of the bearers.

"What did he drink?" she asked.

"The same as always. Water and a little wine. He had a bad stomach. He never took anything else."

"Nobody gave him anything?"

"Nobody." The man was crying, which in a slave is either the truth or a performance and cannot be told from the outside. "He would not take anything from anyone. He was afraid of—"

He stopped.

"Afraid of what?"

"Nothing, domina."

"Afraid of what?"

The servant looked at the floor. "He said there were men who wanted him dead. He said it in the spring. He said if he died in his bed his son would have to explain it, but if he died in the street it would be an accident. He said he would not eat or drink in another man's house." He wiped his face with the back of his wrist. "He ate and drank in yours."

Antonia went out into the courtyard. The fountain was still running. The bronze myrtle leaves were black with wet. Felix had fallen asleep on the bench by the door with his lamp still burning beside him and his mouth open, and one of the kitchen girls had put a cloth over him.

She stood there and looked at the water for a while, and then she went to her own room and took the key from around her neck and opened the strongbox.

It was a plain box, iron-bound, olive wood, and inside were the papers of the house: the loan tablets, the deeds, the manumission records, the purchase documents of eleven women and three men and a boy. Under all of it, wrapped in a piece of old linen, was a second box, small, of myrtle wood.

She did not open it. She put her hand flat on the lid and left it there.

In the morning the prefect's men would come, and Eutychus would come, and the city would come, and every one of them would be thinking the same thing, which was that a woman like Antonia Cassia had finally made a mistake and would now be made to pay for it. She knew how the story would be told in the Forum by the second hour. She had heard that story told about other women, in other years, and had been careful, then, to be on the other side of the room.

She thought of Vettius in the garden with his hand on the myrtle box, saying *everything in this city is sold.* She thought of the chip in the cup. She thought, with the part of her mind that never stopped working, that a man who brings his own cup into a house is a man who expects to be poisoned, and that a man who expects to be poisoned does not die of a heart that stops like a fist closing.

Then she thought: *his son*.

And beneath that, in the place where she kept the things she did not look at, another thought turned over and settled: that in twenty-two years the world had not changed at all, that men still died at other men's tables and women were still blamed for it, and that the only difference between the girl who had been sold at fourteen and the woman who owned this house was the number of people she could make pay.

She put the small box back and locked the strongbox and hung the key around her neck.

Then she called for Tiro.

"Go to the Caelian," she said. "Before the light. Find the son. Tell him his father is dead in my house, and that I am sorry, and that I will pay for the funeral."

"And if he does not believe you?"

"He will believe me," Antonia said. "He will believe me because he will want the funeral paid for. And then he will ask me for something else, and when he asks, I want you to look at his face and tell me what he asks for first."

Tiro nodded slowly. In the lamplight his scar made it look as though he were enjoying himself.

"And Tiro. Send Felix to me."

The boy came in with his hair on end and his lamp in his hand, though it was not needed.

"You will run an errand," she said, "and you will not tell anyone. Not the cook. Not the girls. Not Tiro."

"Not Tiro?"

"Not Tiro."

Felix considered this, which was his great failing. "Is it a bad errand?"

"It is a small errand. You will go to the shop of the man who sells the voting tablets in the Argiletum, and you will ask him whether he has any tablets in the new wood, the cedar, and he will say no, and you will come home. That is all."

"That is not an errand. That is nothing."

"That is the point of it," Antonia said.

He went out, disappointed, and she stood in the empty room with her hand on the strongbox and listened to the house settle around her: the fountain, a woman crying somewhere above, the distant clatter of the vigiles going by on the street with their buckets, the whole enormous animal of the city breathing in the dark outside her door.

*I have been careful for twenty-two years*, she thought. *And it has not been enough, and it never will be, because they do not require me to be guilty. They only require me to be convenient.*

She went to the window and opened the shutter.

Over the roof of the house opposite, above the black ridge of the Palatine, the sky was beginning to go the colour of a bruise.
