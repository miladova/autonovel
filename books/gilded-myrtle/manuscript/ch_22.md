# Chapter Twenty-Two

## Antonia

She was in Tibur when the messenger came.

She had been in Tibur two days, sleeping in the shed behind the smithy on a bench with her cloak over her, because she had not slept in a bed since the cells, and because the boy's papers had to be entered with the duumvirs of the town and the clerk was a slow man with a great deal of business. She had washed her face in the trough in the yard at dawn and had sat on the wall with the boy while the town woke up, and she had begun, in a slow and patient way, to teach him the letters of his own name.

R, she said, and drew it in the dust with a stick.

The boy drew it.

U.

He drew it.

They had got as far as the F when a man came up the road from Rome on a mule, and the man was one of the bearers from the house, and he was covered in dust, and he had ridden the mule into the ground.

"Domina," he said. "You must come."

Antonia stood up.

"What is it?"

"The girl. She has fallen. In the house on the Palatine, from the gallery, in the night, and she is alive, and the physician says she will not last the day." He wiped his face. "She is asking for you. The old woman sent me. She said to tell you that the girl is asking for you and that there is not much time."

Antonia looked at the boy on the wall.

"I have to go," she said.

"I know."

"I will come back."

The boy looked at her with the flat, patient, unsurprised look of a slave who has learned that people who say they will come back do not.

"All right," he said.

She took the mule.

She rode it hard down the Via Nomentana with the dust coming up around her and the sun going higher, and she came into the city by the Nomentan gate at the fourth hour and went up the Clivus Victoriae with the mule stumbling under her, and the streets were full and the people in them turned to look at a woman in a dusty stola on a blown mule.

She rode through the Nomentan gate at the fourth hour and up the Viminal and along the ridge of the Esquiline, and the city came up around her the way it always did: the smell first, then the noise, then the sheer press of people who had nothing to do with any of it.

There was a crowd outside the house on the Palatine.

Not a crowd of the kind that had burned the Myrtle; a smaller, quieter, better-dressed crowd, standing in the street and looking at the door. There were four litters at the corner and a group of young men in good cloaks who had plainly come up from the Forum to see whether there was anything to see.

A woman in the crowd said, "That is her. That is the one from the trial."

Antonia got down off the mule.

She went up the street with the dust on her and her hair coming down, and the crowd made way for her, and she heard them saying it as she passed: *that is the one, that is Antonia Cassia, she is the one who told the story about the consul, she is not permitted in Rome, look at her, she is not permitted in Rome and there she is.*

The door of the house of the Claudii Corvi was open.

There were people in the atrium.

The impluvium had been emptied and scrubbed, and there was still a dark stain in the seam of the marble that no one had got to, and a bucket and a cloth standing beside it, and a slave woman sitting on the bench by the shrine with her face in her hands.

Doris took her up the stairs.

The room was the best room, with the window that looked over the garden, and the bed with the carved footboard, and the good wool coverlet. They had put her in the bed and propped her up with cushions and there was a physician standing at the foot with his hands behind his back and his bag on the floor, and there was a woman in the corner with a bowl of water and cloths.

And Marcus was on his knees by the bed with his face against the coverlet.

Leda was lying with her head on the pillows and her eyes open.

Her face was unmarked. That was the first thing Antonia saw, and it was the thing she would remember: that Leda's face was unmarked and beautiful and entirely herself, and that the rest of her was a ruin. There was a splint on her left leg and a bandage around her middle, and blood had come through it in a small red flower, and her hands lay on the coverlet and they were the only part of her that moved.

"Domina," she said.

Antonia went to the bed and sat on the edge of it and took her hand.

"I am here," she said.

"You are dusty."

"I rode from Tibur."

"On a mule?"

"On a mule."

Leda laughed, and then stopped, and her face went white, and the woman in the corner came forward with a cloth, and Leda waved her away.

"It hurts," she said. "It hurts all the time and then it goes away and I think it has stopped, and then it comes back. The physician says my back is broken and that there is blood inside me and that it will not stop. He says I will sleep soon and not wake up. He says it in front of me, as though I were a piece of furniture. I would have liked him to lie to me. I have been lied to by better men."

"Leda—"

"I am not frightened," Leda said. "That is the strange thing. I have been frightened since I was eleven years old and now I am not frightened at all, and I do not know whether it is because I am dying or because I have finally stopped being a thing that things happen to." She turned her head on the pillow. "Marcus. Get up."

Marcus did not get up.

"Marcus."

He raised his head. His face was a wreck; his eyes were swollen and his nose was running and he looked, for the first time since Antonia had met him, like a boy of twenty.

"Get up," Leda said, "and go and stand by the window, and do not turn around until I tell you."

He got up and went to the window.

Leda looked at Antonia.

"I have three things to ask you," she said, "and then I will sleep."

"Ask."

"The first is my mother." Her voice was very low. "She is at Surrentum. There is a house in the wine quarter by the gate and her name is Vibia Prima and she has been petitioning governors for ten years. You will go to her, and you will tell her what happened to me, and you will not make it beautiful. You will tell her that I was sold and bought and that I sang in a house on the Vicus Tuscus, and that I was not a virgin when I died, and that I was loved by a boy of a good family who was not able to help me, and that I died in a great house on the Palatine because a rail was rotten." She stopped and breathed. "And you will tell her that I remembered the wasp song. Tell her that. She will know what it means. Tell her that I could not remember the words but that I remembered the tune, and that I sang it in a yard at Puteoli when a woman came to look at me, and that it was the only thing I had left of her."

"I will tell her."

"The second is the shell." Leda moved her hand on the coverlet. "It is in the chest, in the room. It is a cowrie, pink and white, and it has a worn place where my thumb goes. My brother gave it to me on the beach the morning the men came. Put it in my hand when they burn me. Not on my eyes; the coin is for the ferryman. In my hand."

"I will put it in your hand."

"And my brother," Leda said. "Gaius. He is two years older than me and he has a mark on his chin from a fall off a wall, and he was on the beach when they took me, and he ran." She stopped. "I have thought about it for ten years. I have thought: why did he run? And then I have thought: he was thirteen years old and there were four men in a boat, and what else was he going to do?" She turned her head. "Tell him I do not blame him. Tell him I have never blamed him. Tell him that I remembered the shell and not his face, and that when I remembered his face again it was the best day of the six years."

"I will tell him."

"And tell him not to be sorry." Her voice was going. "Everybody in this city is sorry. The consul is sorry. The steward is sorry. My mistress is sorry. There is a whole city of people who are sorry and a boy in a yard and a girl on a floor, and it has not helped either of them." She closed her eyes. "Tell him to be glad. Tell him that I was in a house with a fountain and I learned to sing and I was loved by a boy, and that there was one afternoon when I was ten and we swam out to the rock and he held my hand in the water, and tell him to be glad about that and not sorry about the rest."

"The third is this." Leda looked up at the ceiling. "I want you to burn the box."

"What box?"

"The box you keep the letter in. The myrtle box with the letter from him and the pen he gave you, the one you have been carrying from house to house for seventeen years. I have seen you look at it. I have seen you put your hand on it when you thought nobody was watching, the way a woman puts her hand on a door." She turned her head. "Burn it. Not for me. For you. It is a stone in your chest and you have been carrying it since you were twenty-three and it will kill you the way it killed him, sitting in a room with it."

Antonia looked at her.

"I will burn it," she said.

"Say it as though you mean it."

"I will burn it," Antonia said, "and I will put the shell in your hand, and I will go to Surrentum and tell your mother about the wasp song."

Leda closed her eyes.

"Good," she said.

"Tell me about the house," Leda said.

Antonia looked at her.

"The house is closed," she said. "The court has taken it. The women are in the Subura, in the house of a fuller's widow, and they are well."

"And the cook?"

"Calybe is alive. She is in the cells and she will be brought to the basilica and she will be freed."

"And Tiro?"

Antonia did not answer.

Leda turned her head on the pillow.

"You are a bad liar," she said. "You have always been a bad liar. It is the only thing about you that is any good."

"The house burned," Antonia said. "The night after the first day of the trial. A crowd came up from the Forum and put a torch to the door and the roof went. The women got out. Tiro went back in for the strongbox and came out with the papers and the floor went, and he died in the street."

Leda closed her eyes.

"Did he suffer?"

"Yes."

"Was he afraid?"

"I do not know. The woman who was with him said he asked for me and said the box was out." Antonia's voice was steady. "And then he said something about a boy at Tibur."

Leda was quiet for a while.

"He was going to buy his son," she said.

"Yes."

"Did he?"

"Yes."

"Good," Leda said. "That is good. That is one thing."

She shifted on the pillow and her face went white, and Antonia took her hand and held it until it passed.

"There is a thing I have been thinking about," Leda said, when she could speak. "In the basilica. When I said the mark, and the whole hall went quiet, and I thought: they believe me. Six hundred men and they believe me. And it was the first time in my life that anyone had believed a thing I said without being paid to." She opened her eyes. "I am not sorry I did it. I want you to know that. Whatever happens to me, I am not sorry."

"I know."

"And I am not sorry about Marcus." Her voice was very faint. "I am sorry for what it will do to him. But I am not sorry. He was the only person who ever asked me what I wanted and then waited for the answer."

"I know."

"Tell him that."

"I will tell him."

The physician came forward and said something about the hour, and Antonia said, "Get out," and he looked at her and then at the woman in the corner and then went out, and the woman went with him.

Marcus stood at the window with his back to the room.

She slept for a while in the afternoon, and woke, and asked for water, and could not keep it down.

The physician came back at the ninth hour with a bag of instruments and stood at the foot of the bed and said, in the flat voice of a man who has decided that honesty is a form of professional integrity, that the blood inside her had not stopped, that there was nothing to be done that would not make the last hours worse, and that she might live until morning or might not.

"Get out," Antonia said.

"Domina, I have a duty—"

"You have a duty to a fee and you have been paid, and the fee was for the diagnosis and not for standing at the foot of the bed telling a girl she is going to die in the third person. Get out."

He went out.

Marcus had not moved from the window.

"Marcus."

He turned.

"Come and sit with her," Antonia said. "Not at the window. Here. Take her hand and sit on the floor if you cannot sit on the bed, and do not say anything clever, and do not tell her that it is going to be all right, because she knows and she has known since the fourth hour and she is not afraid." She stood up. "I am going to find the woman and get a clean cloth and I am going to be in the corridor. I will be back in a quarter of an hour. Do not leave her alone."

She went out and stood in the corridor with her back against the wall and her eyes shut.

*Twenty-one*, she thought. *Twelve and twenty-one. A boy and a girl. That is what this year has cost, and it is not the end of it, and I am forty years old and I have nothing, and I do not know how a person goes on from this.*

She went back in after a while.

The afternoon went on. The light came through the window and moved across the floor and up the wall. Somewhere in the house a door closed and a woman began to cry and was told to be quiet. Outside, the city made its noise: the carts, the hammers, a man calling a price, a dog.

Leda slept for a while.

When she woke her voice was weaker and her hands were cold.

"Tell me a thing," she said. "Tell me a true thing. Not a kind one."

"What do you want to know?"

"Was it worth it?" Leda's eyes were on her. "The house. The fountain. The dinners. Seventeen years of counting and being careful and not trusting anyone. Was it worth it?"

Antonia sat with her hand in both of hers.

"No," she said.

"No?"

"I built a house and filled it with women and made myself rich enough that senators came to my door, and I told myself that it was freedom, and it was not freedom. It was a room with a lock on the inside of the door and no windows. I was afraid every day for seventeen years. I was afraid of the prefect and the Senate and the man on the Palatine with the pen in his box, and I was afraid of the women in my own house, and I was afraid of you." She looked at the girl's hand in hers. "I bought you. I told myself I had saved you, and I bought you, and you sang for men for six years and I took the money, and I told myself that you were not being given to men, and it was true and it was a lie at the same time, and I have known it every night."

Leda was quiet for a moment.

"Then why did you do it?"

"Because I was fourteen when they sold me," Antonia said, "and nobody built a house for me. And because I found that a woman alone in this city has two choices: she can be a thing that men do things to, or she can be the woman who owns the house, and I chose, and I chose every day for seventeen years, and I chose it over and over again, and each time I chose it I lost something, and I did not notice what I was losing until a boy of twelve was strangled in a yard for carrying a lamp." She looked up. "It was not worth it. But I did not know that until it was too late to do anything but try to be a better woman with what was left."

Leda smiled.

"That is a true thing," she said.

"It is."

"Then I will tell you a true thing." Her voice was very faint now. "I loved you. Not the way I loved Marcus, and not the way I loved my mother, who I do not remember. I loved you the way a person loves the door of a house that is not on fire. You were the first person in six years who did not want to put their hand on me, and I would have died for you, and I have spent the last month finding out that you were not worth dying for, and I am glad. Do you understand? I am glad. Because if you had been worth it I would have died for you, and I would never have found out what it was like to be a woman who chose."

Antonia could not speak.

"Marcus," Leda said.

He came from the window and knelt by the bed.

"I am sorry," he said. "I am sorry, I am sorry—"

"Stop." She got her hand out from under the covers and put it on his head. "You did nothing wrong. You are the only person in this whole city who did nothing wrong, and you will spend the rest of your life thinking that you should have done something, and I am telling you now, while I can still tell you, that there was nothing you could have done." She stroked his hair. "Go to Syria. Do not come back for me. Do not be sad for too long. Be a good man, and be kind to your wife, and when you are old and somebody asks you about your life, tell them about a night in a portico by a river and a girl who was free for a month."

He put his face in the coverlet and wept.

Leda looked past him at the ceiling.

"Tell me the wasp song," she said.

"I do not know it."

"Neither do I." Her eyes were closing. "I only know the tune. Sing it. It does not matter if the words are wrong."

Antonia sang.

She did not know the tune either. She hummed something, a low thing with no shape to it, and after a while she began to make words up, about a girl on a shore and a ship and a summer, and it was a bad song and she sang it badly, and the light went off the wall and the room got grey.

At the end, Leda's hand went slack.

Antonia sat with her until the woman came back with the cloths and the bowl, and then she stood up, and her legs were not steady, and she stood in the middle of the room of the house of the Claudii Corvi and looked at the girl in the bed.

Marcus was on the floor with his back against the wall.

"She is dead," he said.

"Yes."

"She was twenty-one."

"Yes."

"I am going to kill my grandmother."

"No," Antonia said. "You are not."

"I am going to—"

"No." She turned to him. "You are twenty years old and you are the last man of the Claudii Corvi, and if you do that you will be tried and executed and the house will end, and she will have won. Do you understand me? She will have won. She has spent her whole life keeping that name standing, and you are the name now, and the only thing you can do that will hurt her is to live and be a better man than she is." She looked at him. "And because the girl asked you not to be sad for too long, and because she was right about everything except your grandmother."

Marcus looked up at her.

"You are going," he said.

"Yes."

"Where?"

"To Surrentum," Antonia said, "with a shell and a story."

They washed her in the room, because the house was in an uproar and there was nobody to tell them not to.

Doris brought the water and the cloths, and Antonia did it herself, with Doris on the other side of the bed, and they did it the way the women had washed the boy in the atrium on the Vicus Tuscus: the face first, and then the hands, and then the rest, with the door shut.

There was a great deal of blood. It had pooled inside her and it came away on the cloths, and there was a bruise on her hip the size of a man's hand where she had hit the edge of the impluvium, and the leg with the splint on it was not straight.

Antonia washed her and did not say anything.

*There is a way of doing this*, she thought. *There is a way of doing this that the women of this city have known for a thousand years, and it is not to weep while you are doing it, because the dead are not helped by tears and the living have work to do.*

Doris put a coin on each eye and closed them with her thumb.

"She should have a wreath," she said.

"There is myrtle in the garden."

"I will get it."

Antonia was alone with her for a moment.

She took the shell out of her sleeve. It was a small pink-and-white cowrie with a worn place where a thumb had gone, and she had carried it from the chest in the room to here, and she put it into Leda's right hand and closed the fingers over it, one at a time, the way you close the fingers of a child on a coin.

"Here," she said. "This is yours. It was always yours."

Doris came back with the myrtle and they put it on her head, and it was too big, the way the wreath on the boy had been too big, and it kept slipping sideways, and Antonia took it off and put it lower, on the brow, and it stayed.

Then she stood at the foot of the bed and looked at what was left of a girl of twenty-one who had been taken off a beach when she was eleven and had sung in a house on the Vicus Tuscus for six years, and who had stood in a basilica in front of six hundred men and said what she saw, and who was, in the whole of that city, the only person who had told the truth from beginning to end.

There was a knock at the door.

It was Caesia.

She came in with her hands behind her back and stood at the end of the bed, and she looked at Leda for a long time, and then she said, "I am sorry."

"Yes," Antonia said.

"I did not do anything. I want you to know that. I have been in this house all my life and I have never done anything, and now there is a girl dead in the best room and a woman going into exile, and I am going to be married in the spring to a man called Vinicius, and I will have a house, and I will be able to do things in it." She looked up. "What should I do?"

Antonia looked at her.

"When you have your house," she said, "and there is a girl in it who is frightened, do not ask her whether she is well. Ask her what she wants. And then find out who is preventing her from having it, and stop them." She turned away. "It is not a great deal. It is more than anybody in this house did for her."

Caesia nodded slowly.

"I will remember," she said.

She went out.

Antonia went down the stairs into the atrium, where Claudia Junia was standing by the shrine with her hands folded.

The old woman was very pale, and she was standing straight, and she had put on a clean stola, and she looked at Antonia the way she had looked at her the first time, thirty years before, in that same room, when Antonia was seventeen and had been brought in from the kitchen with her hands still wet.

"Where is she?" Antonia said.

"Gone," Claudia Junia said. "The slaves have taken her to be washed."

Antonia hit her.

It was not a slap. It was a closed fist, and it caught the old woman on the side of the face and knocked her against the shrine, and the little painted figures went over and one of them broke on the floor.

Claudia Junia put her hand to her cheek and looked at the blood on it, and then she looked up, and her eyes were the same pale colour as her son's.

"Are you satisfied?" she said.

"No," Antonia said. "I have been hitting people for a month and it has not satisfied me once."

"It was an accident."

"I know it was an accident. The carpenter has been to see the rail and he has told everyone in the street that the rail was rotten, and he is telling the truth, and it will not help either of us." Antonia stood over her. "You reached out and took her by the wrist and she pulled away. That is what happened. I have it from the girl, and from the rail, and from the fact that you are standing in this room at all, because a woman who pushes another woman over a rail does not stay in the house to be looked at."

Claudia Junia was silent.

"You will not be tried," Antonia said. "There will be no trial. A free woman fell in a great house and broke her back, and the carpenter will say the rail was rotten, and the prefect will not want another scandal in the same month, and the whole thing will be an accident in the record. I know this city. I know how it works. I have spent my whole life being crushed by it and I know exactly how much it costs to make a thing an accident."

"What do you want?"

"I want you to live," Antonia said, "in this house, with the rail, and the shrine, and the broken god on the floor, and your son, and your grandson. I want you to have exactly what you have been fighting for." She turned to go. "And when you die, and you will die in that bed upstairs, I want you to remember that the only thing you ever gave to anybody for nothing was a letter you did not answer."

She went out into the street.

The sun was going down over the roof of the temple of Apollo, and the whole of the Palatine was gold, and the city below was beginning to light its lamps.
