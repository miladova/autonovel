# Chapter Twenty-Five

## Antonia

They came into Surrentum on the fourth day, in the afternoon, with the sun on the water and the town coming down the hill in steps of white and ochre and the temple on the cliff above it.

Antonia stood at the bow with the jar in her arms.

She had expected to feel something. She had prepared herself for it on the last night, lying under the awning with the ship moving under her and the women asleep, and she had thought about the town and the beach and the black sand and the boat coming in with men in it who spoke Greek, and she had thought: *this is where it began.*

What she felt, when she saw it, was nothing at all. It was a town. It had a harbour and a fish market and a lot of steps and a smell of nets and lemons. There were gulls.

They came in on the tide and the master put them off at the quay with their bundles, and they stood in the sun on the stones with the whole of the town going up in front of them.

"Where now?" said Prisca.

"The wine quarter," Antonia said. "By the gate."

They found it in an hour. It was at the top of the town, where the road came in from the north, and it was not a quarter so much as a street: a row of yards behind walls, with vines over the gates and amphorae stacked in rows, and a smell of must and wasps. There was a shrine at the corner with a worn step and a lamp in the niche that somebody still kept filled.

The house at the end of the street had a wall of rough stone and a vine over the gate, and the gate was open.

Antonia stood in front of it for a long time.

"Domina," Prisca said.

"Wait here."

She went in.

The yard was small and paved with cobbles and there were amphorae stacked against the wall in rows, with the painted marks on their shoulders, and a vine over the gate, and in the corner a table with a jug and a knife and a heap of something drying in the sun. A woman was sitting on a stool by the table with a basket of beans in her lap, shelling them.

She was fifty or so, and brown, and thin, with grey coming into her hair and a face that had been worked on by the sun. She looked up when Antonia came in, and her hands went on shelling the beans.

"The yard is not for sale," she said. "If you want the wine, go to the shop at the corner; my son keeps it now."

"I do not want the wine."

"Then what do you want?"

Antonia stood in the middle of the yard with the jar in her arms.

"My name is Antonia Cassia," she said. "I have come from Rome."

The woman's hands stopped.

She looked at the jar.

She was very still for a long moment. Then she put the basket down on the ground, carefully, so that the beans did not spill, and she wiped her hands on her apron, and she stood up.

"You wrote to me," she said.

"I wrote to you in the spring. I told you that I was coming and that I had news of your daughter." Antonia's voice was steady. "The news is not good."

"I know. I knew when the letter came. A woman does not write a letter like that with good news in it." She looked at the jar again. "Is that her?"

"Yes."

Vibia Prima put her hand on the table.

"Sit down," she said. "Sit down before you fall down, you are grey as a fish. Sit on the stool." She pushed the basket aside. "Have you eaten? You have not eaten. There is bread and there is cheese and there is wine, and I will get it, and then you will tell me, and you will tell me all of it, and you will not leave anything out because I am a woman and it is not proper."

Antonia sat down on the stool.

The bread was good and the cheese was better and the wine was the wine of the yard and it was thin and cold and it tasted of the ground. Antonia ate and drank, and the woman sat opposite her with her hands in her lap, and then Antonia told her.

She told her all of it.

She had thought about how to do it, on the boat. She had thought about softening it, and about the words she would use, and about whether a mother should be told the whole of what a slave dealer's yard is. In the end she told it plainly, in the order it happened, because she had been a liar all her life and had found that the truth was cheaper and lasted longer.

She told her about the men in the boat and the yard at Puteoli and the block and the dealer whose name was Nymphodorus. She told her about the house on the Vicus Tuscus and the singing and the promise and the men who put their hands on the girl's wrist as she went by with the lyre. She told her about the dinner and the cup and the basilica, and about a room on the Palatine with a window that looked over a garden, and a rail that was four hundred years old and had rotted at the mortice, and a girl who lay in a bed and asked for three things.

Vibia Prima listened.

She did not weep. Her hands stayed in her lap and she did not interrupt, and when Antonia had finished she sat for a while and looked at the beans on the ground where she had put the basket down.

"The wasp song," she said.

"Yes."

"I made it up. When the children were small. There were wasps in the yard in the summer and they were frightened of them, and I made up a song, and it was a stupid song, it had no tune to speak of." She looked up. "She remembered it."

"She remembered the tune. She could not remember the words."

"No," said Vibia Prima. "Neither could I, by the end."

She got up and went to the other side of the yard and stood with her back to Antonia, with her hand on the wall.

"You bought her," she said.

"Yes."

"For eleven thousand sesterces."

"Yes."

"And you kept her six years and you took the money she earned and you did not write to me."

"No."

Vibia Prima turned around.

"I have been to three governors," she said. "I have written letters to the prefect's office in Rome and to the legate of the province and to a man I once knew in Puteoli who had a cousin in the household of the governor, and I have stood outside the basilica in Naples for four days in the rain, and in ten years nobody has told me anything, and now a woman comes into my yard with my daughter in a jar and tells me she bought her." Her voice was shaking. "What am I to do with that? What am I to do with you?"

"Nothing," Antonia said. "There is nothing you can do with me. I am not here to be forgiven; I am here because I promised her I would come, and because she asked me to tell you about the wasp song, and because you have a right to know what happened to your daughter." She stood up. "I have four hundred sesterces and a jar and five women and a boy in the street outside your gate, and I do not want anything from you, and I will go and find a room and I will come back tomorrow to ask what you wish to be done about the ashes."

Vibia Prima looked at her.

"You are a hard woman," she said.

"Yes."

"You will not last a month in this town."

"I have been told that before," Antonia said, "by a woman in a cell in Rome, and she was wrong, and she is dead."

She went to the gate.

"Wait."

Antonia stopped.

Vibia Prima came across the yard. She was a small woman with grey in her hair and beans on her apron, and she stopped in front of Antonia and looked at her for a long moment.

"Did she have a good voice?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Was she beautiful?"

"Yes."

"Did she have anyone who was kind to her?"

Antonia thought about it.

"Yes," she said. "A boy. He was young and foolish and he loved her, and he tried to take her away, and he could not, and he will be a senator one day and he will be sad about her for the rest of his life."

"That is not nothing," said Vibia Prima.

"No," Antonia said. "It is not nothing."

They buried the ashes on the third day, in the ground outside the town, on the hill above the sea, in a small plot that Vibia Prima had bought eleven years before with money she did not have, on the day after the girl was taken, because she had known even then that her daughter was dead and had wanted somewhere to put her.

There was no stone yet. The stonecutter in the town was slow and expensive and had asked what she wanted it to say, and Antonia had said, "Put her name and her age and the fact that she was loved," and the man had said, "Is that all?", and Antonia had said, "It is more than most women get."

The brother was there.

His name was Gaius and he was twenty-three years old and he had a mark on his chin from a fall off a wall, and he was a broad, brown, silent young man who ran the wine shop at the corner and had a wife and a child. He had been on the beach the morning his sister was taken. He had run.

He did not speak to Antonia at the funeral. He stood at the edge of the plot with his hands at his sides and his face set, and when it was over he went to the wall and stood with his back to them all and his shoulders shaking.

His mother went to him.

Antonia stood by the small grave with the jar in it and the sea below the hill and the gulls turning over the water, and Meroe and Prisca and Junilla stood behind her, and the boy from Tibur stood at the end of the row with his hands folded, because he had asked to come and she had let him.

She did not say anything. There was nothing to say that would not have been a performance.

Afterward she walked down the hill alone and stood on the beach.

It was black sand, as she had said it would be, and the water was very blue, and there were fishing boats drawn up in a row, and a man was mending a net on the stones. She took off her sandals and walked to the edge of the water and let it come over her feet.

This was where it had begun. Not for her; for a girl of eleven who had been asked a question by a stranger and had answered it. Somewhere along this shore, ten years ago, a boat had come in and a boy had run and a girl had not, and everything that followed had followed from that one moment of good manners.

She stood in the water for a long time.

Then she went up the beach and put on her sandals and went back to the town.

They took a house.

It was not a good house. It was two rooms and a yard above a fishmonger's on the street that went down to the harbour, and the roof leaked at the corner and the stairs were steep, and the rent was thirty sesterces a month, and Antonia paid the first month out of the four hundred and looked at the yard and thought: *a table there, and a bench, and a vine over the door in the spring, and a sign.*

She did not have a sign made.

That was the thing she had to learn, and it took her a year. She had spent her whole life building a thing that men came to, and she found that in Surrentum she did not want a door that men came to. She wanted a shop. She wanted a thing that was open in the morning and shut at night, that sold something people needed, and that did not require her to be charming after the second hour.

So she sold wine.

It was not a great wine; it was the wine of the yards on the hill, and she bought it from Vibia Prima's son at a fair price and sold it at a fair price and did not water it, and in the first month she sold eleven jars and in the fourth month she sold sixty.

The winter in Surrentum was not the winter of Rome.

It rained. It rained for eleven days in November and then stopped, and the town smelled of wet stone and woodsmoke and fish, and the sea was the colour of a knife, and the wind came down off the hill at night and got into the shutters and made a noise like a man breathing.

They were cold. That was the thing none of them had expected: they had spent their lives in a city where a brazier and a blanket were enough, and they had come to a town where the wind came off the water and found every crack in a wall.

Antonia bought a second brazier and a load of charcoal and a bolt of good wool, and they sat in the back room in the evenings with the shutters closed and the lamp lit and the four of them sewing and the boy reading out loud.

He read badly. He read the way a person reads who has learned late: slowly, with his finger, and he lost his place and went back, and every so often he would come to a word he knew and say it with enormous triumph.

"*The — ship — was — in — the — har — harbour.*"

"Good."

"*And — the — men — were — in — the — ship.*"

"Good."

"*And — there — was — a — storm.*" He stopped. "Is there a storm?"

"There is always a storm," said Prisca, from her sewing. "It is the second chapter."

The trouble came in the winter.

It came in the shape of a man called Aulus Vibidius, who was one of the two duumvirs of the town and the owner of four ships and the largest yard in the harbour, and who sent a slave to her door on a wet morning in December with a summons.

"You are to appear before the duumvirs at the third hour," the slave said. "On a complaint."

"Whose complaint?"

The slave looked at the ground.

"His," he said.

Antonia went at the third hour, in her good stola, with Prisca behind her.

The basilica of Surrentum was a small building with a tiled floor and a statue of Augustus that had lost its hand, and it smelled of wet wool and the sea. There were perhaps forty people in it, which in a town of that size was a crowd. The two duumvirs sat on a raised bench at the end, and Vibidius stood in front of them with his thumbs in his belt.

"The complaint," said the senior duumvir, who was old and tired and read from a tablet, "is that the woman Antonia Cassia, a foreigner resident in this town, has been selling wine without being enrolled in the guild of the wine sellers, and that her house is a house of ill repute, and that she harbours women of bad character, and that the peace of the town is disturbed."

Antonia looked at Vibidius.

"May I speak?"

"You may answer the complaint."

"Then I will answer it." She turned so that the forty people in the basilica could hear her. "I have sold wine in this town for five months. I have bought it from the yards on the hill, including the yard of Vibia Prima, and I have paid the market toll every market day, and I have the receipts, and my steward has them here. I have not watered the wine. I have not raised the price in the festival week when everyone else raised it. I have sold to fishermen and to the wives of fishermen and to two of the men in this room, and if any man here says he was cheated, let him stand up and say it."

Nobody stood up.

"As for the house," she said, "there are five women living in it and one boy, and we are all free, and the papers are lodged with the prefect's office in Rome, and the man who signed them is the secretary of accounts to the emperor, whose name is Tiberius Claudius Eutychus, and if the duumvirs wish to write to him, they may, and he will answer, and he will answer in a bad temper, because he is a busy man and he does not like to be troubled about a wine shop."

The old duumvir looked at the tablet.

"Have you anything to say to the complaint of ill repute?"

"I have this to say," Antonia said. "I kept a house of women in Rome for seventeen years. That is true. I am not going to stand in this basilica and pretend to be a widow from Naples. The town knows what I was; the town knew it in the first week, because a town this size knows everything in the first week." She looked at the forty faces. "I came here with four hundred sesterces and a jar of ashes and five women, and I have kept a shop and paid my debts and not asked anything of anyone. If that is not enough for the duumvirs, then they must tell me what would be, and I will consider it, and I will probably not do it."

There was a sound from the back of the basilica that was not quite laughter.

Old Vibidius turned around and looked at it, and then at her.

"You are a bold woman," he said.

"I am a woman who has been in a basilica before," Antonia said. "In Rome. In front of six hundred men. There was a consul-designate in the third row and a murderer in the aisle and a girl on the floor, and I am still here, and the consul-designate is dead and the murderer is on an island and the girl is on a hill above this town." She let that sit. "You have forty people in a wet room and a complaint about a wine shop. Do what you like."

The old duumvir put the tablet down.

"The complaint is dismissed," he said. "The wine seller will enrol in the guild and pay the fee, which is four sesterces, and the town will consider the matter closed." He looked at Vibidius. "Aulus, I have known you thirty years and you are a fool in a good coat. Go home."

Vibidius went out.

Antonia paid her four sesterces to the clerk and enrolled in the guild of the wine sellers of Surrentum, and the clerk wrote her name in a book, and that was the first time her name had been written in a public book since the prefecture of Rome had written it in a list of the condemned.

Outside, in the rain, Vibia Prima was waiting with a basket.

"You did well," she said.

"I have had practice."

"You have." The older woman turned and walked beside her up the hill. "He will not trouble you again. He is a coward and he has been laughed at in his own basilica, which in a town this size is worse than a fine." She shifted the basket to her other arm. "Come to supper on the Kalends. My son's wife is a good cook and there is a cousin of mine coming from Naples who is dull but has a boat."

Antonia stopped in the rain.

"Are you asking me to supper?"

"I am asking you to supper."

"Last year you would not let me in your yard."

"Last year you had my daughter in a jar," said Vibia Prima, and then, after a moment, "and now she is on the hill and I go up on the Nones with flowers, and you go up on the anniversary, and there is nobody else in this town who does." She started walking again. "I am not your friend. I want to be clear about that; you bought my daughter and I will not forget it, and there are days when I see you in the street and I have to go into a shop. But you kept your word to her, and you came to my yard and told me the truth, and you have been decent to my son, and in a town this size that is a kind of relation."

They walked up the hill in the rain.

"I will come to supper," Antonia said.

The shop opened on the Kalends of October, at the sixth hour, with a jug of the new wine and a bowl of olives and a broom.

There was no sign, but Prisca had painted the word VINUM on the plaster beside the door in red letters a foot high, and Meroe had hung a bunch of dried vine over the lintel, and the boy had swept the step three times.

The first customer was a fisherman called Sabinus who came in because he had been told there was a woman from Rome selling wine at a fair price, and who stood in the doorway and looked around the room and said, "Is this it?"

"This is it," Antonia said.

"Where are the tables?"

"There are no tables. There is a bench outside and a jug and a cup and a price on the wall."

The fisherman looked at the wall.

"That is a fair price," he said, surprised.

"It is a fair price for the wine. If you want to be cheated, the tavern by the mole will oblige you."

He laughed, and bought a cup, and drank it standing in the doorway, and then bought a second and sat on the bench outside, and by the ninth hour there were four men on the bench and a woman with a jug and a boy who had been sent for a quart and told to hurry.

At the eleventh hour a man came in who had been at the tavern by the mole and had heard, and who was drunk and wanted to make a point.

"They say you were a whore in Rome," he said.

The room went quiet. There were three men on the bench outside and the woman with the jug and Sabinus, who had not gone home.

Antonia put down the jug.

"I was," she said. "And now I sell wine in Surrentum, and I do not water it, and I do not cheat fishermen, and I am not going to be spoken to in my own shop by a man who has had four cups at the mole and cannot hold them." She took a cup from the shelf and set it on the counter. "That is two sesterces. If you want to drink it and be civil, sit down. If you want to say something else, say it in the street, where I do not have to listen."

The man looked at her.

Sabinus stood up from the bench.

"Come on, Gnaeus," he said. "You have had enough. I will walk you home."

They went out.

Antonia stood behind the counter with her hands flat on the wood and her heart going, and Sabinus came back in and put two sesterces on the counter and said, "That was well done."

"I have had practice," she said.

"You keep saying that." He picked up his cup. "What does it mean?"

"It means I have been in a great many rooms where a man said something and everybody waited to see what the woman would do." She put the coins in the box. "It is a thing you learn, and then you cannot unlearn it, and it is worth about as much as it costs."

Sabinus drank his wine.

"My wife died four years ago," he said. "I have a boat and a boy of nine and a house with a leaking roof, and I am not proposing anything, because I have heard what happened to the last man who proposed something to a woman from Rome." He put the cup down. "But if you ever want a boat to take you along the coast, or a man to stand on a bench in your shop when a drunk comes in and says something, I am on the mole at the fourth hour every morning."

Antonia looked at him.

"Thank you," she said.

He went out.

Prisca came out from the back room where she had been pretending to do the accounts.

"That was well done," she said.

"Everybody keeps saying that."

"Because it is true." Prisca looked at the box. "Eleven cups and a jug. Nine sesterces, less the four we paid for the wine, less the rent, less the toll." She did the sum on her fingers. "We have made a profit of one sesterce and a half and a bunch of dried vine."

Antonia laughed.

"We will do better tomorrow," she said.

They did.

Prisca kept the accounts, which she did very well and enjoyed. Meroe taught dancing to the daughters of the harbour families, which caused a scandal and then a waiting list. Junilla ran the door and, in the evenings, the room. The boy from Tibur learned his letters in a year and could read the wine lists and then the letters, and at fifteen he could write a fair hand, and Antonia taught him the way she had been taught: sitting beside him at the table with a lamp and a wax tablet, guiding his fingers.

She wrote to Eutychus and he answered, four times a year, in a hand like a clerk's, with news of Rome.

The baths had opened in the autumn. He had put a myrtle tree in the garden. He had been audited by the prefect's office and had come out of it with a commendation. Maro had been given a command on the Danube and had been killed in the second year by a falling tree, which Eutychus reported without comment and which Antonia read three times.

Faustus had died in the mines at the end of the first year.

Publius Vettius had been exiled to an island and had written a book about his father.

And Claudius Corvus's daughter had married, in the spring, a young man of an equestrian family, and the wedding had been small.

Marcus wrote once.

The letter came in the third year, in the winter, from a fort on the Euphrates, and it was four lines long. It said that he was well, that he had been made a prefect of a cohort, that he had heard she was at Surrentum and that there was a stone on the hill above the sea. It said: *I have not forgotten the portico by the river. I do not think I will. If you are ever in need of anything, write to me at this address, and I will send it, and I will not ask for anything in return.*

Antonia read it twice and put it in the box on the shelf.

She answered it in the spring, and she wrote about the wine and the weather and the boy who could now read, and at the end she wrote: *She asked me to tell you not to be sad for too long. I am telling you now because I promised her I would, and because I have found that a promise kept is a rare thing and I intend to keep the ones I have left.*

She did not write again, and he did not.

On the first anniversary of the burial she went up the hill alone at dusk.

The stone was set by then. It was small and grey and it said:

LEDA
CALLED VIBIA RUFA
OF THIS TOWN
AGED TWENTY-ONE
SHE WAS LOVED

She sat on the ground beside it with her back against the wall and looked out at the sea, and the light went off the water, and the gulls came in to the cliff, and the lamps began to be lit in the town below, one and then another and then a great many.

She thought about the boy in the yard behind the bakery.

She thought about Tiro in the rain, saying *I look like a man who can keep anything.*

She thought about a man sitting against an altar with his hands in his lap, saying *I am sorry that I stood in the doorway.*

She thought about a girl in a bed on the Palatine, singing a song that nobody knew the words to.

She thought: *In Rome, everything is sold. I have spent my life learning the price of things, and I have paid, and other people have paid, and the accounts have never balanced and they never will.*

And then she thought: *but a woman may keep a shop, and sell wine that is not watered, and teach a boy to read, and put a stone on a hill, and that is not nothing. It is very little. It is almost nothing. And it is mine.*

She got up.

The wind came off the sea, cold and salt, and the last of the light was on the cliff, and below her in the town the women were closing the shutters and the men were coming up from the boats, and somewhere a door opened and a voice called somebody in to supper.

Antonia Cassia went down the hill.
