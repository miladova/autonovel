# Chapter Twenty-One

## Leda

They took her back to the house on the Palatine after the trial, because there was nowhere else to take her.

The panel had found that she was free-born and that her mother was Vibia Prima of Surrentum, and Pedo had said that she would be sent to her mother at the expense of the court; and then a clerk had said that the arrangements would take some days, and Corvus had said that she could not be left in the cells, and Claudia Junia had said that the family would undertake to keep her until the ship sailed, and so it was decided, in the way that such things are decided, by men who were tired and wanted to go home.

They gave her a room on the upper floor with a window that looked over the garden.

It was the best room she had ever had. There was a bed with a carved footboard and a coverlet of good wool, and a chest with a lock on it, and a bronze mirror on a stand, and a small table with a jug and a basin, and a slave woman called Doris, who was old and deaf and kind and brought her food three times a day.

On the first morning she sat on the bed and did not touch anything.

She had been in the house a day and a night, and she had not seen Marcus. She had seen Caesia twice, who had come to the door and said, "Are you well?" and had stood in the doorway with her hands behind her back and had not known what else to say, and had gone away. She had seen Claudia Junia once, in the atrium, who had looked at her and said, "You will eat, and you will sleep, and you will not go out of this house," and had gone on up the stairs.

She had not seen Corvus at all.

The city was outside the window. She could hear it: the carts, the shouts, the hammers from the building works below the hill, the dogs, the children. It went on and on and it had nothing to do with her, and she sat on the bed with her hands in her lap and listened to it.

On the second morning Doris brought her bread and honey and said, "The lady says you are to come down to the garden."

"Why?"

"The air."

Leda went down to the garden.

It was a small garden, walled, with a fig tree and a bed of herbs and a bench and a statue of a satyr with his arm broken off. She sat on the bench in the sun, and she had been there perhaps a quarter of an hour when Caesia came out of the house with a book.

"May I sit?"

"It is your garden."

Caesia sat down at the other end of the bench and put the book on her knees and did not open it.

"My grandmother says you are to go to Surrentum as soon as the ship sails," she said. "She says it is arranged. She says your mother has been written to."

"Yes."

"Are you glad?"

Leda looked at the fig tree.

"I do not know," she said. "I have wanted it for ten years. I have thought about it every night. I have made up what my mother's face looks like and what the house looks like and what my brother will say when he sees me." She paused. "And now it is going to happen, and I find that I am frightened of it."

"Why?"

"Because I am not the girl who was taken from that beach," Leda said. "I am twenty-one years old and I have sung in a brothel for six years and I have stood in a basilica in front of six hundred men and said what was done to me, and the woman who is going to Surrentum is not the daughter my mother has been looking for. My mother has been petitioning governors for ten years for a girl of eleven." She looked at her hands. "What will she do with me?"

Caesia was quiet for a moment.

"My brother is in love with you," she said.

"I know."

"He is in his room. He has been in his room since the trial. My grandmother has a man at the door. He has not eaten for two days, and yesterday he broke the shutter, and they had to take the shutters off the window." She turned the book over in her hands. "They are going to send him away. To the army, in the autumn, to a legion in Syria, and my grandmother says that by the time he comes back you will be married in Surrentum and he will have forgotten."

"He will not forget."

"No," said Caesia. "He will not." She looked up. "I am sorry. I do not know why I am telling you this. There is nobody else in this house who tells the truth, and I have discovered this week that I am not very good at being lied to."

Leda almost laughed.

"Neither am I," she said. "I have been lied to by everyone who was ever kind to me. My mistress lied to me about my papers and told herself it was for my good. The consul lied to me in his study and told himself it was for the city. The steward took me out of my house in a litter and told me it was for my protection." She looked at the wall of the garden. "And the only person who ever told me the truth was a boy of twelve who is dead."

Caesia sat with her for a while.

Then she said, "I will come and see you tomorrow," and went back into the house, and Leda sat in the garden until the sun went off the wall.

On the third afternoon she went down to the tablinum and asked to see him.

The slave at the door said that his master was not receiving. Leda said that she would wait, and sat down on the bench in the corridor, and after a quarter of an hour the door opened and Corvus stood in it.

"You should be in your room."

"I have been in my room for three days." She stood up. "I am to go to Surrentum on a ship the day after tomorrow, and I am to be given a purse and a letter and a woman of the household to see me onto it, and I have been told all of this by your mother, and not one word of it by you." She looked at him. "You are the man who put me in that villa. You are the man who is going to stand up in the Senate in the spring. I have given my evidence. I would like to be told the truth about my own life by the person who has arranged it."

Corvus looked at her for a moment. Then he stepped back from the door.

"Come in."

The tablinum was a room full of paper. There were tablets in racks and rolls in baskets and a table with three inkstands and a lamp, and a strongbox under the table, and a bust of Augustus with a chipped ear, and the shutters were closed.

"Sit down."

She sat.

"You are going to Surrentum," Corvus said. "The ship sails on the day after tomorrow from Ostia. There will be a woman with you, and a purse of four thousand sesterces, and a letter to your mother in which I have said that you were recovered through the courts and that the family of Corvus regrets the delay and the distress." He folded his hands. "It is not enough. I know it is not enough. It is what I can do."

"Why are you doing it?"

"Because it is the right thing to do."

Leda looked at him.

"That is not the reason," she said. "A man like you does not do a thing because it is right; he does it because he has worked out that it is cheaper than the alternative." She sat back. "What is the alternative?"

Corvus's mouth tightened.

"The alternative," he said, "is that you remain in Rome, and my son remains in Rome, and the two of you do something foolish in the autumn, and I have to decide whether to disown him or have you removed from the city. The ship is cheaper." He looked at her. "You are a very direct young woman."

"I have been sold twice," Leda said. "It teaches you to ask the price."

The room was quiet. In the garden a bird was making a noise.

"May I ask you something?" she said.

"You may."

"Did you know what your father was?"

Corvus did not move.

"That is not a question for this room."

"It is the only room in this house where it can be asked." She leaned forward. "I have heard the servants. I have heard the story that goes round the kitchen when the family is not there: that there was a girl in this house before I was born, and that the old consul hurt her, and that she killed him, and that the son sent her away. I have heard it four times in three days from four different people, and it is always the same story, and every one of them stops when I come into the kitchen." She looked at him. "I stood in a basilica and told six hundred men what a man did to me in a passage. I would like to hear one true thing from somebody in this house."

Corvus sat with his hands on the table.

"Yes," he said. "I knew."

"Did you stop him?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because I was twenty-four years old," Corvus said, "and because he was my father, and because I had spent my whole life learning not to be the thing that stopped him, and because I stood in the doorway for three heartbeats and I did not move." He looked at the table. "I have not said that sentence out loud in seventeen years. I have thought it every day."

Leda was silent for a moment.

"Then you know," she said, "what it is to stand in a doorway and not move."

"Yes."

"And you did it again, this year, when a boy was killed in her house."

Corvus's head came up.

"I did not know about the boy."

"No," Leda said. "That is what everyone says. *I did not know.* The cook did not know what was in the packet. The steward did not know what the money was for. You did not know about the boy. Everybody in this city is standing in a doorway saying that they did not know, and the boy is dead, and the girl fell, and a woman is going into exile." She stood up. "I am not going to forgive you. I want you to know that. I am going to get on the ship and I am going to Surrentum and I am going to have a life, and I am not going to think about this house again, and the reason I am not going to think about it is not that I have forgiven anybody. It is that I have decided not to carry it."

Corvus stood up.

"That is wise," he said.

"No," said Leda. "It is the only thing left."

She went to the door.

"Leda."

She stopped.

"When you see your mother," Corvus said, "tell her—" He stopped, and looked at the table, and then at the shuttered window. "There is nothing to tell her. I am sorry. That is all. Tell her that a man in Rome is sorry, and that it is worth nothing, and that I know it is worth nothing."

Leda looked at him for a moment.

"I will tell her," she said, "that her daughter was in a house where a man was sorry, and that it was not enough, and that she should not expect anything from Rome."

She went out.

Marcus came to her at the second hour of the night.

She was awake; she had not been sleeping. There was a scratch at the door, and then the door opened, and he came in with a lamp shaded by his hand, and shut it behind him, and stood there looking at her.

He had a bruise on his cheek that had gone yellow at the edges, and his lip was healing badly, and he was thinner than he had been by the river.

"You should not be here," she said.

"I know."

"If they find you here—"

"I know." He set the lamp down on the chest. "I have three minutes. There is a man at the stair who is a client of my father's and who owes me a favour, and he will cough twice when my grandmother's door opens." He sat down on the floor with his back against the bed, exactly as he had in her room at the Myrtle, and it was such a familiar thing that she had to look away. "I have been going out of my mind in that room."

"Your sister told me."

"Did she?"

"She told me about Syria."

Marcus was quiet for a moment.

"There is no Syria," he said. "There is only my grandmother, and she has decided, and my father does nothing, because my father is finished. Did you know? He went to the prefect's office at the second hour and he was there until the evening, and when he came back he went into his study and he has not come out. The clerks are saying that the emperor will be written to. The consulship will be withdrawn. He will be expelled from the Senate, or he will be allowed to resign, and either way it is finished." He looked up at her. "He has not spoken to me since the trial. He stood up in that basilica and told six hundred men that a girl had been sent away from his father's house with a purse, and then he came home and shut the door, and I have not been able to ask him why."

"Because he is ashamed."

"Because he is a coward," Marcus said. "You told me that at the river and I did not believe you. I have believed it for a week."

Leda looked at him.

"There is a thing I have to tell you," she said, "and I would rather tell it to you now, in this room, than in a letter."

"Don't."

"I am going to Surrentum."

"I know."

"I am going to Surrentum, and I am going to find my mother, and I am going to be a woman in a town by the sea with a name and a mother and a brother, and I am going to be respectable." She said the word as though it were a stone in her mouth. "And I am not going to be the woman who ran away with a Claudius."

Marcus looked at the floor.

"That is not what I asked you," he said.

"It is what you asked me in the portico of Portunus, and it is what you asked me in my room at the Myrtle, and I have said yes to you twice, and both times I was lying." She sat up. "I was not lying because I did not want you. I was lying because I did want you, and because a woman in my position will say anything to a boy who is kind to her, because kindness is the only thing she has ever been given for nothing, and she does not know what to do with it." She reached out and took his hand, and held it hard. "I am not going to spend the rest of my life being the woman who ruined Marcus Claudius Corvus. I have been a thing that men did things to since I was eleven. I am not going to be that for you."

"Then I will come to Surrentum."

"You will not."

"I will come. In the spring, when the legion goes east, I will not go with it. I will sell the horse and I will come to Surrentum and I will stand in the market and I will ask for the house of Vibia Prima, and if your mother turns me out of the door I will sleep in the street."

Leda looked at him for a long moment.

"You are twenty years old," she said.

"Yes."

"And you have never in your life had to do anything you did not want to do, except the army, and you did that because your father's name was on the commission." She let go of his hand. "You will come to Surrentum in the spring, and there will be a letter from your grandmother waiting at the inn, and you will read it, and you will go back. Or you will come, and you will stay a month, and then the money will run out, and you will write to your father, and he will send it, and you will go back."

"Leda—"

"I am not saying it to hurt you. I am saying it because I have been poor since I was eleven years old and I know what money does to a man who has never been without it." She looked at him. "Go to Syria. Be a soldier. Be a consul. Marry Vinicia Sabina and be kind to her and have sons and grow old, and in twenty years, when you are in the Senate and there is a debate about something, you will remember a night in a portico by a river and you will be sad for an hour, and then you will go home to dinner."

Marcus stood up.

"How dare you," he said.

"I am telling you the truth."

"You are telling me the truth the way my father tells the truth." He was shaking. "You are telling me what is good for me. You are telling me what the world is. You are telling me to go away and be respectable, and you are doing it out of kindness, and it is the worst thing anyone has ever done to me."

Leda stood up.

"Then it is the last thing I will ever do to you," she said. "Go to your room, Marcus. And when they put you on the ship in the autumn, do not write to me. I will not answer."

He looked at her.

There was a scratching at the door, and then two soft coughs.

Marcus picked up his lamp and went out, and the door closed, and Leda stood in the middle of the room in the dark with her hands over her face.

She lay on the bed for a long time with her eyes open.

The house made its noises: a door, a chain, a slave's feet in the corridor, the water in the impluvium, the wind in the fig tree. Somewhere below, a man was laughing, and then he stopped, and the house was quiet.

She thought about Antonia Cassia in the courtyard with her sleeves rolled up, washing the fountain herself because she could not sit still. She thought about Prisca on the kerb in the street with her face in her hands. She thought about the boy in the yard behind the bakery and his eleven sesterces in a rag and the fact that she had never told him that she knew about the money, and that he had never told her about the bakery, and that they had both been keeping a small secret from each other in a house where everything else was known.

She thought: *if I had gone to the gate at the Nones I would be on a boat now.*

And then she thought: *no. I would be in a villa at Ficulea with a man at the gate, or I would be dead in a ditch on the road to Ostia, and Marcus would be in a cell, and there would be nobody to say the mark in the basilica.*

She got up and went to the chest and took out the shell and held it in her fist.

*Tomorrow I will ask the old woman for the money for the passage and I will go to Ostia by myself,* she thought. *I will walk if I have to. I have walked further than this.*

She did not sleep.

At the fourth hour of the night she got up and put on her shawl and went out onto the upper gallery.

It was a Roman house of the old kind, built around a small atrium with a roof open to the sky, and there was a gallery on the upper floor that ran around three sides of it with a wooden rail, and she stood at the rail and looked down into the dark of the atrium.

The house was quiet. Somewhere a slave was coughing in his sleep. The water in the impluvium caught the moonlight in a small square, and the painted gods in the shrine were black shapes in the corner, and the whole of the house of the Claudii Corvi lay below her like a thing asleep.

She thought about the room at the Myrtle with the window the size of a bread board, and the fountain with the bronze myrtle in it, and the women sleeping around the brazier at the Saturnalia, and Calybe singing in Bithynian.

She thought about the beach at Surrentum and her brother getting smaller.

She thought: *I will go tomorrow. I will ask the old woman for the money for the passage and I will go to Ostia in the morning and I will be a woman in a town by the sea.*

And then she thought: *I am twenty-one years old and I have no money and no papers and no trade but singing, and my mother has been looking for a girl of eleven for ten years, and when she sees me she will be disappointed.*

The gallery creaked.

She turned.

Claudia Junia stood at the top of the stairs in a dark mantle with a lamp in her hand.

"I thought it was the girl," she said.

"It is the girl."

"You are not asleep."

"No."

The old woman came along the gallery. She was small and straight and she moved without any noise at all, and the lamp in her hand made the shadows of the pillars go up the wall.

"You have been crying," she said.

"Yes."

"At your age one cries at night and is ashamed in the morning." She stopped three feet away. "I have come to tell you what will happen. There is a ship at Ostia on the day after tomorrow; it goes to Puteoli and then to Surrentum, and there will be a woman from my household on it with you, and a purse, and a letter to your mother. You will leave this house at dawn on the day after tomorrow, and you will not see my grandson again, and in a year you will be married, and in ten years you will be a wife in a town by the sea and this will be a thing that happened to you when you were young."

"I know."

"Good."

Leda looked at her.

"Why did you keep the letter?" she said. "The one she sent you. The one from my mistress, eleven years ago."

The old woman was silent.

"I read it," Leda said. "It was in the box in your room, and the box was on the bench in the atrium, and I have been in this house for two days and no one has told me anything, so I read it." She was not sorry. "It says that she has not spoken and will not speak and does not want anything from you, and that she is writing because she wants you to know that she is alive."

"I know what it says."

"Then why did you keep it?"

Claudia Junia looked at her for a long moment.

"Because she was twenty-three years old," she said, "and she had killed my husband, and she wrote to me and did not beg. I have been in this city for sixty-three years and I have been surrounded all my life by people who wanted something. My husband wanted my dowry. My son wanted my approval. My daughter wanted a husband. The priests wanted a donation. The clients wanted a loan. And once, in seventeen years, a woman wrote to me and said: *I want nothing from you.*" She held the lamp very steady. "I have kept the letter because it is the only thing in this house that was ever given to me for nothing."

Leda stared at her.

"You could have answered it," she said.

"Yes."

"You could have helped her."

"Yes."

"Why did you not?"

"Because she was a whore," said Claudia Junia, "and because if I had answered it she would have been in my life, and my son would have been in hers, and the whole thing would have come up out of the ground, and the family would have fallen. And because I am a woman who has spent her life keeping a house standing, and I have made a great many choices in sixty-three years, and that was one of them, and I have not been sorry about it for a single day." Her voice did not change. "I am not sorry now. I am telling you the truth because you are a girl of twenty-one who is going to a town by the sea and will never see me again, and because there is no one else in this house I can tell it to."

The gallery creaked again.

Leda put her hand on the rail.

"I am not going to Surrentum," she said.

The old woman's face did not move.

"You are going the day after tomorrow."

"No." Leda shook her head. "I have been sent from one place to another since I was eleven years old. I was sold at Puteoli and bought at Puteoli and taken to a house on the Vicus Tuscus, and then I was taken out of that house in a litter and brought here, and now I am to be put on a ship and sent to a mother I do not remember, and every one of those things was decided by a person who was being kind to me." She looked at the old woman. "I am not going. I will go to Surrentum when I choose and I will go by myself, and if I have to walk, I will walk."

"Then you will walk out of this house tonight," Claudia Junia said, "with no money and no papers, and you will be a free woman in Rome with nothing, which is the worst thing in the world to be."

"I would rather be that than be sent."

The old woman reached out and took her by the wrist.

It was not a hard grip. She was sixty-three years old and her hand was thin and dry and not strong, and she took Leda's wrist the way a woman takes the wrist of a child who is going toward a fire.

"Come away from the rail," she said.

Leda pulled her arm back.

It was not a violent thing either. She pulled her arm back and stepped away, one step, and her heel caught the hem of her shawl, and she put her hand out and it found the rail.

The rail was old.

That was what the carpenter said afterward, when they sent for him: that the rail was original to the house, that it was four hundred years old, and that the pegs had rotted in the mortice, and that a man leaning on it in the daylight would have felt it give. He said it in the way that a man says a thing when he has been asked by a great house to explain how a girl came to fall, and he said it more than once, and it was true.

The rail went.

Leda fell backward and the rail went with her, and she did not scream; she made a sound like a woman who has been surprised, and she put out both hands, and the old woman's hand closed on the air where her wrist had been.

It was two and a half times the height of a man from the gallery to the floor of the atrium, and she hit the edge of the impluvium.

She did not die.

That was the thing that everyone said afterward, in the house and in the street and in the Forum: that she did not die. She lay on the floor of the atrium of the house of the Claudii Corvi with her legs at an angle that a leg should not be at, and her eyes open, and she was breathing.

Claudia Junia stood at the rail with the lamp in her hand and looked down.

Then she began to scream for the slaves.

What happened next happened in a house that was already awake.

The slaves came from the kitchen and the corridor and the back stairs, and somebody brought a lamp, and somebody brought water in a jug, and a woman began to pray in a language that nobody in the house understood. Marcus came down the stairs three at a time and knelt in the water and put his hands under her head.

"Get the physician," he said.

"He has gone to the village."

"Then get a horse and go to the village and bring him back, and if he will not come, put him on the horse."

Two of the men went out.

Claudia Junia came down the stairs with a lamp in her hand and stood at the bottom and looked at the girl on the floor and the rail hanging loose above her and the broken pieces of wood on the stone, and her face in the lamplight was the face of a woman adding up a column of figures and getting a number she did not like.
