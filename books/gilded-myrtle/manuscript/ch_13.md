# Chapter Thirteen

## Corvus

The panel met on the fourth day after the Kalends, in the same room with the cut-away frieze and the hunting mosaic, and the girl was brought in at the third hour.

Corvus came early, and Pedo was in the corridor outside the room with a cup of water and a tablet, and he looked at Corvus the way a man looks at a bill he did not expect.

"You are early."

"I am early."

"Maro is already inside and he has been inside for an hour, and he has been reading his notes." Pedo drank the water. "He has a theory."

"What theory?"

"That you are protecting your own household and that the girl has been coached." Pedo put the cup down on the ledge. "He is not entirely wrong."

Corvus looked at him.

"I have told you the truth about everything that matters," he said.

"You have told me the truth about everything except the one thing that would end your career, and I have not asked you about it, and I am not going to ask you about it today." Pedo turned to go in. "But I will tell you this, Corvus, because I am sixty years old and I have nothing to lose and because you are going to be consul in the spring and you should hear it from somebody. There is a thing in your family that has been sitting in that house for seventeen years, and everybody in Rome knows that there is a thing, and nobody knows what it is, and when a thing like that comes out it does not come out neatly. It comes out in a basilica in front of six hundred people, and it takes a good man and a bad one and a girl and a cook and a boy with a lamp and it puts them all in the same room and makes them say what they know." He opened the door. "I have seen it three times in my career. It is always the same. And the man who thinks he has arranged it is always the one who is most surprised."

He went in.

The girl was brought in at the third hour.

Corvus had not slept. He had sat up in his study until the sky went grey with the stylus in his hand—not the stylus; a stylus, an ordinary one, with which he had written three letters and burned all of them. His eyes were sore and his mouth tasted of ash, and he had discovered at some point in the night that the worst thing a man can do is to sit alone in a room and remember his own face at twenty-four.

The girl came in with her hands folded.

She was dressed plainly, in a grey stola, with her hair bound up like a respectable woman, and she was frightened; he could see it in the way she held her shoulders. But she walked to the mark on the floor and stood on it and looked at the prefect, and when Pedo said, "Your name," she said, "I am called Leda. My mother's name is Vibia Prima, of Surrentum, and my father was Vibius Rufus, a wine merchant of that town, and I was taken from the beach there when I was eleven years old by men in a boat, and sold at Puteoli to the dealer Nymphodorus, and by him to Antonia Cassia six years ago."

The room was quiet.

Pedo looked at the papers in front of him. "You understand that if you are free-born, you are not subject to examination, and that if you are not, your evidence is worth nothing without it. You understand what that means."

"I have been told, dominus."

"Then tell us what you saw on the night of the banquet."

She told it.

She told it well. She told it in the same words she had used in his study, and she did not embellish, and when Maro interrupted her to ask how she could describe a man's hair and the stripe on his tunic and not his face, she said, "Because the lamps in that passage are low and because he turned his shoulder, and because I was not looking at his face; I was looking at the cup," and Maro wrote something down and did not ask again.

When she had finished, Pedo sat back.

"Thank you," he said. "You will withdraw."

"Dominus." She did not move. "May I say one thing more?"

Pedo glanced at Corvus, and then at Maro, and then nodded.

"The man who carried the cup was not of our house," Leda said. "I have thought about it every night for a month, and I have thought about the way he moved, and I am certain of it. He was not of our house, and he was not a stranger to great houses. He was a man who knew a passage he had never been in, which is a thing you can tell from the way a person walks. He went as though he had been told where to go." She paused. "And there was a mark on his wrist. On the inside, here. A burn, in the shape of an eight. I saw it when he lifted his hand to the curtain."

Corvus did not move. Maro's stylus went still. Pedo raised his head slowly.

"You did not say this before," Maro said.

"I did not remember it before."

"You remembered it now."

"I have been thinking about it for a month," Leda said, "as I said."

There was a silence in which Corvus could hear the clerk's pen behind him and the noise of a cart in the street and his own blood.

"Withdraw," Pedo said.

She went out.

Maro was on his feet before the curtain had fallen. "A mark on the wrist," he said. "A burn in the shape of an eight. That is a house mark, and there are perhaps forty households in Rome that use one, and I can have a list of them by tomorrow, and then I can have every slave in those households looked at by the end of the week."

"That is a great many slaves," said Pedo.

"It is a city of slaves."

"You will not," said Corvus.

Both of them turned.

Corvus heard his own voice with a kind of distant surprise. He had not decided to speak. He had stood up, and his hands were flat on the table, and he was speaking.

"You will not," he said again, "because a house mark is not evidence. It is a thing that every household in this city uses to keep its property from being stolen, and if you begin hauling slaves out of the houses of senators and knights and having them strip to the elbow in the street, you will have forty households at your door by the evening and half the Senate in the morning, and the prefect will be explaining to the emperor why the city was in an uproar for a burn on a wrist. And the man who carried the cup is dead. He was found in the Tiber nine days ago, and the prefect's office has the body, and it has no name and no mark that anyone thought to write down before it was buried."

Maro looked at him with an expression of profound and unpleasant interest.

"How do you know he is dead?"

"Because I made inquiries," Corvus said, "as any man on this panel would. The body was taken from the river at the fourth hour of the morning and buried in the potters' field with four others, and the clerk who took it down wrote *male, adult, no name* and nothing else." He turned to Pedo. "There is a girl's memory and a dead man and a stain in a cup. That is the whole of it. If we make a case of this, we will make it on the woman, because she is the only one whose name we have, and the city will be satisfied, and the Senate will be satisfied, and nobody will ever look for the man with the mark, because the case will be closed."

Pedo looked at him for a long moment.

"That is a remarkable speech," he said, "from a man whose steward has a house mark."

The room went very still.

"It is on the door of the house," Corvus said. "It is on the door of every house in Rome."

"Yes," said Pedo. "I know."

He stood up, heavily.

"We will adjourn until the Ides," he said. "On the Ides we will hear the case against Antonia Cassia, and we will hear it in the basilica, in public, because the city will have it no other way. And on the Ides, Corvus, you will not sit on this panel. You will sit in the body of the court with the rest of the Senate, and you will say nothing, and if I hear one word out of you that is not an answer to a direct question, I will have you removed by a lictor, consul-designate or no." He gathered his papers. "And Maro, you will find me the man with the mark. Not forty households. One man. You will do it quietly and you will do it in nine days, and if you cannot do it in nine days you will tell me so and we will go to the Ides with what we have."

Maro bowed his head.

"Yes, dominus."

They went out. Corvus stood alone in the room with the hunting mosaic and the cut-away frieze, and after a moment he found that he was shaking.

He did not go home.

He walked instead, for an hour, up the Clivus Victoriae and along the ridge of the hill and down again through the narrow streets behind the temple of Apollo, and the city went on around him in its ordinary way, and he found that he was looking at it as a man looks at a room he is about to leave.

There was a woman selling bread at a stall, and two boys playing at knucklebones in the gutter, and a man mending a shutter, and a dog asleep in the middle of the street with its legs in the air. There was a beggar at the corner of the Clivus who had been there for eleven years and whose name Corvus did not know, and he stopped and gave him a coin, and the beggar looked at the coin and then at him and said, "Are you all right, sir?"

"Yes," Corvus said.

"You do not look all right."

"I have had a difficult month."

"Ah," said the beggar. "Well. It passes."

Corvus stood in the street and looked at him.

"Does it?"

"Not the difficult month," the beggar said. "That stays. But the being a man in a good toga in the middle of the street looking at a beggar, that passes." He put the coin in his mouth and bit it and put it in his belt. "Go home, sir. Whatever it is, go home and eat something and sleep, and in the morning it will be the same but you will be stronger."

He went home.

He found Marcus in the stable yard.

His son was standing by the stall of his horse with a currycomb in his hand, and the horse was saddled.

"Where are you going?"

"To Ostia," Marcus said. "You sent me."

"That was a month ago. You went, and you came back in four days, and you have been in this city ever since, and I have known it, and I have said nothing." Corvus stood in the yard with his hands behind his back. "Put the comb down."

Marcus put the comb down.

"Faustus tells me that you have been buying things," Corvus said. "A cloak. Boots. Two knives. A purse of silver changed into gold at a moneylender in the Transitberina, which is a thing a man does when he does not want a transaction to be traced. And a passage on a boat at Ostia, in the name of a man called Verus, who does not exist."

Marcus said nothing.

"You are going to take her," Corvus said. "You are going to take the singer out of that house and put her on a boat, and you are going to go with her, and you are going to be a nobody in Massilia or Tarraco, and you think it is a story out of a poem and that it will end with the two of you on a beach."

"I think it will end with me being free," Marcus said.

"You will be a deserter from the legions, which is a thing that can be punished with death, and a fugitive from your own family, and a man with no money and a woman with no papers, in a town where you know nobody and where the first magistrate who asks who you are will be answered by a man who wants a reward." Corvus's voice was rising. "You will be back in this city within a year, and you will be back with a child, and then you will come to me, and I will have to decide whether to take you in, and I will take you in, because you are my son, and I will hate it, and I will hate you, and you will know it."

"Then do not take me in."

"Marcus."

"Do not take me in." Marcus's face was white. "You have spent your whole life arranging things. You arranged my education and my commission and my marriage and my sister's marriage and the roof of this house, and you have never once asked me what I wanted, and when I told you, you said that what I wanted would not matter in ten years." He took a step forward. "I have been in a country where men died in ditches for a pair of boots. I have seen a man's leg come off at the knee and heard him ask for his mother. I came back here and you told me that the seventh legion had been lucky in its officers, and then you asked me whether I had brought back any money."

"I did not know what to say to you."

"You could have said anything. You could have said: that must have been hard. I would have taken that. I would have taken a single word." Marcus's voice cracked. "I am not doing this because of a girl. I am doing it because I have spent twenty years in this house and I have never once been asked what I think about anything, and because the only person who has ever asked me a question and waited for the answer is a slave in a brothel, and because when I am with her I am not the son of Gaius Claudius Corvus, I am just a man, and I have found that I like him better."

Corvus struck him.

It was not a slap. It was a closed fist, and it caught Marcus on the cheekbone, and he went back against the stall and his head hit the wood, and the horse shied and stamped, and for a moment neither of them moved.

Corvus looked at his own hand.

"I am sorry," he said.

"No, you are not," Marcus said, and there was blood at the corner of his mouth. "You are embarrassed. That is a smaller thing."

The words went into him like a knife, because they were not his son's words. They were the words Antonia Cassia had said to him in the portico of Castor, and Marcus had never heard them, and could not have heard them, and yet they came out of his mouth in the same cadence and with the same contempt, and Corvus understood, in that moment, that he had spent twenty years teaching his son everything he knew about how to be a man, and that the lesson had been learned exactly and applied to himself.

"Take the horse," he said.

Marcus stared at him.

"Take it and go," Corvus said. "Not to Ostia. Not with her. Go to my farm at Tibur and stay there until the Ides, and do not come to Rome, and do not write to her, and do not send a message by any boy or any slave or any man in this city. If you do that, I will not have you followed, and I will not tell your grandmother, and when this is over we will find a way to live."

"And after the Ides?"

"After the Ides," Corvus said, "I do not know."

Marcus looked at him for a long moment. Then he wiped the blood from his mouth with the back of his hand, and took the horse's bridle, and led it out of the stall, and Corvus stood in the yard and watched him go.

His daughter was in the garden.

Caesia was sitting on the rim of the fountain with a book in her lap and her feet in the water, and she did not get up when he came out, and he saw that she had been crying and had stopped.

"Where is your brother?"

"Gone to Tibur. He took the horse." She turned a page without looking at it. "He came to say goodbye to me first. He stood in the door of my room and he said: *if she is harmed, I will never forgive any of you, and if I am sent away, I will not come back.*" She looked up. "Is he right? Is she going to be harmed?"

"She is being taken to a house in the country for her own protection."

"That is what grandmother says. Grandmother says a great many things in the voice she uses for the accounts." Caesia put the book down. "Father. I am fifteen years old and I am to be married in the spring to a man I have met twice, and I have been told all my life that I am not to ask questions, and this year I have found that I cannot stop asking them." She swung her feet out of the water. "Where is she?"

"At Ficulea. In a villa with a maid and two men."

"Against her will?"

"She is a witness in a capital case and the prefect's office has an interest in her." Corvus heard his own voice and was disgusted by it. "That is the legal position."

"That is not what I asked."

He looked at his daughter.

There was a thing in her face that he recognized: it was the look of a person who has discovered that the people around her are not what she thought, and who is deciding, minute by minute, what she is going to do about it. He had seen it in his own mirror at nineteen, in the year he had learned what his father was, and he had done nothing about it for twenty-two years, and he found that he could not bear to watch his daughter begin the same long silence.

"No," he said. "Not against her will exactly. She is being kept there because if she is free to go she will go, and if she goes I lose the only witness who can say that a man came into that house with a cup, and the case becomes a case about a whore who poisoned a consul, and the city has its scandal and my family has its ruin."

Caesia stared at him.

"Thank you," she said.

"For what?"

"For telling me the truth. Nobody in this house has ever told me the truth." She stood up and put her sandals on. "I am going to say something now and you are going to be angry, and I do not care. I have been in this house for fifteen years and I have watched grandmother run it, and I have watched you do what she says and call it duty, and I have watched my brother come back from the war and be asked whether he brought any money." She straightened her stola. "The girl is a free woman. The panel has said so. She has a mother in Surrentum who has been petitioning governors for ten years, and she is being kept in a villa in the country so that you can put her in front of a panel and make her say what you want. That is not the law. That is kidnapping, and it is being done with a seal and a clerk and a litter, which makes it worse."

"Caesia—"

"I am not finished. Marcus is the best man in this family and he is being sent to Syria because he fell in love with a singer, and I am to be married to a fish-sauce man's son because we need three hundred thousand sesterces, and you are going to be consul in the spring and the whole city will say what a fine upright man you are." She picked up her book. "I have decided that I am not going to be like grandmother. I am telling you now, so that you know. I am going to marry the fish-sauce man's son, because I have no money and no choice and I am not a fool, and then I am going to run my own house, and in my house nobody will be kept in a villa in the country and nobody will be told to stop asking questions."

She went into the house.

Corvus stood in the garden with the fountain running behind him.

*She will be a better woman than any of us*, he thought, and then, because he was his father's son and because he had spent forty-one years learning to put a thing down rather than examine it, he thought: *and she will be married in the spring, and by then this will be over, and she will never know how close she came to being right about everything.*

His mother was waiting in the atrium.

She was not spinning. She was standing by the impluvium with her hands folded, and she had the look of a woman who has been standing there for some time, and on the bench behind her there was a small wooden box that Corvus had not seen in twenty years.

"Your son is gone," she said.

"He has gone to Tibur."

"He has gone to the girl."

"No," said Corvus. "He has gone to Tibur. He gave me his word."

"His word." Claudia Junia's mouth turned. "You gave me your word, once, that there was nothing in this house that I needed to know. That was the night after your father died and I have kept it for seventeen years, and I have been a fool, but I have been a loyal fool, and I have kept this house standing while you have been in the Forum being upright." She put her hand on the box. "Do you know what is in this?"

Corvus did not answer.

"His papers. His letters. The physician's report, which says that he died of a stoppage of the heart, and which I dictated to the physician myself at the fourth hour of the morning, because I found him in the shrine with a hole in his throat and I knew what would happen to this family if it came out." Her voice was perfectly steady. "He was a hard man and a bad husband and a worse father, and he was my husband, and I have spent seventeen years making sure that his death was an accident. Do you understand what that has cost me? I have lied to my son and my daughter and my priest and my own sister, and I have done it every day, and I have never once complained, because that is what the women of this family do while the men are being upright in the Forum."

"Mother—"

"Be quiet." She opened the box. "There is a letter in here. It is from the woman. It came to me eleven years ago, when she bought the house on the Vicus Tuscus, and it says: *I have not spoken and I will not speak, and I do not want anything from you, and I am writing because I want you to know that I am alive.* I have kept it, because I did not know what else to do with it, and I have read it perhaps once a year, and I have hated her every time I have read it, and I have never been able to decide whether she was threatening me or whether she was being kind."

She held it out. Corvus did not take it.

"She is going to tell the truth at the trial," he said.

"Yes."

"She will say that my father was killed in this house, by her, in the shrine, and that I was there, and that I sent her away."

"She will say it," said his mother, "if she is alive to say it."

The words hung in the air of the atrium, among the painted gods and the cracked impluvium and the smell of wax and old wood.

"Mother."

"I am not asking you to kill her," Claudia Junia said. "I am telling you what is required. A witness can be discredited, and a woman of her condition can be discredited more easily than most, but a woman who stands up in a basilica and tells a story that is true, with details that only she could know, in front of six hundred men—" she stopped. "You have three days until the Nones and nine until the Ides. Use them."

"To do what?"

"To make sure that she is not believed." The old woman closed the box. "There is a girl in that house. The singer. The one who is your son's ruin and the only witness to the cup. Take her out of the house. Not by force, in the street, where there will be a crowd; take her quietly, as a witness, into the custody of the prefecture, on the ground that her life is in danger and that she is a material witness in a capital case. Put her in a house outside the city, with a guard and a maid, and let the woman spend the nine days before her trial looking for her."

"That is not lawful."

"It is entirely lawful," said Claudia Junia. "It is done every month. And it is the only thing you have left that is not murder." She turned and walked toward the stairs, and then stopped, with her hand on the rail. "Gaius. When your father died I sat in this room for two days and I did not weep, and on the third day I went to the shrine and cleaned the floor myself, because I did not trust the slaves to do it, and I scrubbed it with my own hands until there was no mark on the stone. I have never told anyone that. I am telling you now so that you understand that I know exactly what this family is, and exactly what it costs, and that I am not asking you to do anything I have not done myself."

She went up the stairs.

Corvus stood alone in the atrium for a long time.

Then he went to the tablinum and sent for Faustus.

"I want the girl taken out of the house," he said, "tonight, quietly, and put in the villa at Ficulea with two men and a woman, and I want her treated well, and I want her alive, and if she is harmed I will have your hands. Do you understand?"

"Yes, dominus."

"And Faustus." Corvus looked up at him. "When this is finished, you will go to my estate in Baetica and you will not come back to Rome, and you will not see me again, and the money you took from Publius Vettius will be taken out of your peculium and given to the guild of the bakers in the name of a boy called Felix, who was twelve years old, and if you tell me that you cannot afford it I will sell you."

Faustus's face did not change.

"Yes, dominus."

He went out.

Corvus sat down at his father's table and put his hands flat on it, and outside the window the light was going, and the city was beginning to make its evening noise, and somewhere a long way off a woman was singing at her work.

He thought about the girl in the green dress standing in the pool of light with her lyre, and about the mark in the shape of an eight, and about the fact that he had just ordered a witness taken out of her house so that his mother's seventeen-year silence would hold.

He thought: *I have become my father.*

The villa at Ficulea had four rooms and a wall and a gate and a woman who came in to cook. It had a well and a fig tree and a view of the hills. It was, in every particular, a decent place to keep a witness, and he had chosen it himself in one afternoon from a list of three, and had thought at the time that he was being careful and humane.

And then, because he was a man who had been taught since childhood that a thought like that is a thing to be put down and not to be examined, he took out a tablet and began to write out the arrangements for the villa at Ficulea, in the clear, careful, upright hand of the consul-designate of the year to come.
