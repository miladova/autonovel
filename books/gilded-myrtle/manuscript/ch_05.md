# Chapter Five

## Marcus

The baths of Trajan on the Oppian hill had been open three years and were already the largest thing of their kind in the world, and Marcus Claudius Corvus went there every morning that summer because it was the only place in Rome where nobody wanted anything from him.

He swam first, in the great cold pool under the glass roof, twenty lengths, until his arms ached. Then he sat in the warm room with the sweat running off him and let a slave scrape him with a strigil and listened to the talk.

The talk that morning was all of Vettius.

"He was poisoned," said a fat man on the bench opposite, with the confidence of a man who had been told a thing by a barber. "Aconite. They say his face went black and his tongue swelled out of his mouth."

"They say he was with a girl of seventeen."

"They say the woman did it herself."

"Why would she kill a paying customer?"

"Because he would not pay," said the fat man, and laughed, and the others laughed with him, and Marcus lay on the bench with his eyes shut and his hands loose at his sides and thought about how easy it would be to kill a man like that with his own strigil.

They went to the hot room, and then to the cold again, and then out into the sun in the exercise yard, where the young men of the city threw a ball about and wrestled and showed off their shoulders. Marcus wrestled a cavalry officer from the Syrian legions and threw him twice, and the man took it well, and they sat afterward on the step with their chests heaving and their hair dripping and talked about horses.

"You are Corvus's son," the officer said.

"I am."

"I knew your father in the east. He was on the governor's staff in the year of the Parthian business." He wiped his face with a towel. "He was not liked."

"He is not liked here either."

"No." The officer grinned. "But he was trusted. There were four men in that province who could not be bought, and he was one of them, and it made him difficult company and it made the rest of us careful." He stood up. "Give him my regards. Tell him Quintus Drusus remembers the night at Zeugma."

Marcus went home by way of the Forum, and stopped on the steps of the Basilica Julia to watch the building works, because a man can watch a crane for a long time without thinking.

He had met her in the spring, at the house on the Vicus Tuscus, at a dinner given by a man called Blandus who owed money to everybody and had decided that the cheapest way to be important was to be seen in interesting company.

Marcus had not wanted to go. He had come back from Dacia in the autumn and had spent the winter in his father's house being asked questions about the war by men who had not been in it, and by the spring he had developed a manner that his grandmother called sullen and that his father called sensible. Blandus had sent a note, and the note had said: *there will be nine men and no wives and the wine is good, and there is a singer who is worth the evening.*

He went because he had nothing else to do.

The house had surprised him. He had expected something gaudy and had found a plain door on a street of shops, and a courtyard with a fountain and a bronze myrtle tree, and a dining room painted with a garden, and a woman of forty in a white stola who had looked at him for the length of a blink and said, "You are Corvus's son."

"Yes."

"You have his mouth and none of his manners." She had turned away before he could answer. "Sit on the left, next to the poet. He is dull but he is not malicious, and the man on your right will try to sell you a share in a grain ship."

The singer came in after the fish.

She was in green, and she carried her lyre as though it were a part of her, and she stood in the light by the fountain and did not look at anyone, and when she began to sing the room went quiet in a way that Marcus had only ever heard in a temple.

He did not hear the song. That was the thing he was ashamed of afterward, when he tried to explain her to himself: that he had not heard the song at all. He had watched her hands.

Afterward, when the men were arguing about the corn supply and the poet had gone to sleep, he went out into the courtyard for the air, and she was there, sitting on the rim of the fountain with her feet in the water and her sandals beside her.

"You are not supposed to be here," she said.

"Nor are you."

"I live here." She looked up. "You are the son of the man on the Palatine."

"How does everyone know that?"

"Because you stand the way he stands." She kicked her feet in the water. "He came to a dinner here once, years ago, before I came. The cook remembers. She says he did not eat anything and left before the third course and did not thank her."

"My father does not thank cooks."

"No," said Leda. "I did not think he did."

Marcus sat down on the rim of the fountain, a little way along from her.

"I am sorry," he said.

"For what?"

"I don't know. For the cook."

She laughed. It was a small, surprised, entirely genuine sound, and he would remember it for the rest of his life, and he did not know then that he was going to spend years trying to hear it again.

They sat by the fountain for an hour. She told him about the house and the women and the rules: that Meroe was buying her freedom in four years and had a contract she could not read properly; that Prisca had a husband in Ostia and wept about him when she was drunk; that the cook was a Bithynian and could put a man on the floor with a rolling pin. She told him about the summer when the house was closed and they all slept in the dining room around a brazier.

She did not tell him about the yard at Puteoli. He asked, once, and she looked at the water and said, "I do not talk about that," and he did not ask again, and he was proud of himself for it, and it did not occur to him for a long time that not asking is the easiest kind of kindness and the least useful.

"Tell me about Dacia," she said.

"There is nothing to tell."

"There is always something to tell. That is what men say when the thing is bad."

He looked at the water.

"It rained," he said. "It rained for four months. We built a road and the road washed out twice, and a man in my tent died of a fever and we could not bury him for two days because the ground was water. And there was an afternoon in the autumn when a party of us went up a valley to look at a bridge and the woods were full of men, and we came back with eleven of us out of nineteen, and I have not told my father any of that because he asked me whether I had brought back any money."

Leda was quiet for a moment.

"Did you kill anyone?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Does it matter that I asked?"

"No." He looked at her. "It is a relief, actually. Everyone else asks me about the tactics."

She laughed again, and then the curtain moved and one of the women put her head out and said, "Leda. The domina wants the second song."

She got up and put on her sandals.

"Will you come again?" Marcus asked.

"To my own house?"

"To the dinners."

She looked down at him.

"You are a nice boy," she said, "and you are the son of a man who does not thank cooks, and you will be married in a year to somebody's daughter with a great deal of money." She picked up her lyre. "But yes. Come to the dinners."

He came to four dinners in the spring. He did not tell his father where he was going. He told himself that it was because his father would not approve of Blandus; he knew, by the third dinner, that it was because his father would.

In the afternoon his mother's cousin came, and there was a dinner.

It was at the house of Vinicius Sabinus on the Caelian, and it was not a dinner, it was an inspection. The house was new money and did not pretend otherwise: the atrium was painted with scenes from the Aeneid in a style thirty years out of date, the mosaic in the dining room showed a chariot race in colours so bright that they hurt the eye, and there were four silver cups on the sideboard that had been bought on the same day from the same shop. But the silver was good and there was a great deal of it, and the cook was better than his father's cook, and the wine was Falernian and there was no water in it.

Vinicius Sabinus was a heavy, cheerful, sweating man of fifty-five who had made his money in shipping and in the fish sauce that Faustus had mentioned, and he had a senator's ambition without a senator's name, and he wanted, very badly, for his daughter to be a Claudius.

The daughter's name was Vinicia Sabina.

She was sixteen, and she was not what Marcus had expected. She was small and dark and extremely self-possessed, and she had a wit that she used the way a small dog uses its bark, which is to say constantly and without much damage. She sat beside him at dinner and asked him about Dacia.

"Was it cold?"

"In winter, very."

"Did you kill anyone?"

The table went quiet. Her father laughed too loudly.

"That is not a question for a young lady," he said.

"It is a question for a soldier," said Vinicia, without looking at her father. "I am to marry him. I should like to know what he is."

Marcus looked at her. She looked back at him with her chin up and her hands folded in her lap, and he understood, with a lurch, that she was frightened, and that this was how she was frightened: with questions.

"Yes," he said. "In the second year. A man in a wood, and another at a river, and I do not know how many with a bow, because you do not."

"And did you dream about it?"

"Sometimes."

"Then you are not a liar," said Vinicia Sabina, and turned to her fish.

Afterward, in the garden, when the men had gone to their cups and the women had been left with the almonds, her father took Marcus aside and put a heavy arm across his shoulders and said, "She is a good girl. She is headstrong. Her mother was headstrong. You will be patient with her and she will make you a very rich man," and Marcus said, "Yes," and felt the arm on his shoulder like a beam of wood.

He did not go home. He went to the Vicus Tuscus.

The house had a back door in the alley behind the kitchen, and he stood in the alley for a quarter of an hour in the dark before he knocked, because he was not a fool, and because a man who is watched will see who is watching.

He saw the door open at the ninth hour and a man come out.

He was thin and neat and moved quickly, and he had a tablet under his arm, and he stopped in the alley and looked up and down it, and Marcus pressed himself into the recess by the wall and did not breathe. The man went past him within three feet, and in the light from the kitchen door Marcus saw his face: a soft, pale, careful face, with the look of a man who has just done something he was told to do and is pleased with himself.

Marcus did not know him. But he knew the walk. It was the walk of a man who had spent his life in the antechambers of great houses, and when he turned the corner at the end of the alley, he turned it like a man going back to a place where he was important.

Marcus waited, and then he knocked.

Prisca let him in with a face like a locked chest, and took him through the kitchen and up the back stairs and left him in the passage, and Leda came out of her room with a lamp.

"You are a fool," she said.

"I know."

"I told you not to come."

"I know."

"There was a man here tonight. One of the prefect's men, and another one in a good cloak who stood in the atrium and looked at everything and said nothing, and they are coming back tomorrow, and the day after that, and this is the worst possible night in the world to be found in this house with me." She was whispering so fast that her breath caught. "And you came anyway."

"I came anyway."

She looked at him, and then she put the lamp down on the chest and put her hands over her face, and he stood there with his own hands at his sides, and after a moment she took them away and laughed, once, at herself.

"Come in," she said. "Sit on the floor and do not make a sound."

Her room was the same as it had been: white walls, a bed, a chest, a stool, a mirror, a window the size of a bread board. He sat on the floor with his back against the bed and she sat on the bed with her knees drawn up and the lamp between them.

"Who was the man in the alley?" he asked.

"What man?"

"Thin. Neat. A tablet under his arm. He came out of the kitchen door at the ninth hour and went off toward the Velabrum like a man who had done a good day's work."

Leda's face changed.

"That is Faustus," she said. "He is your father's steward."

The lamp flickered. Somewhere below them a woman laughed, and a door banged, and the whole house creaked.

Marcus felt the floor go out from under him, very slowly, like the deck of a boat.

"You are certain?"

"I have seen him four times. He comes at night and he talks to Tiro in the kitchen and then he goes away. The girls think he is a banker." She was watching his face. "Marcus. What does it mean?"

"It means my father knows where I go." He put his head back against the bed. "It means he has known for weeks, and he has not said anything, which is worse than if he had."

"What will he do?"

"He will not shout. He never shouts." Marcus closed his eyes. "He will make a plan. He has been making plans since he was fourteen years old, and the plan will have eleven parts to it and none of them will be wicked, and at the end of it I will be married to Vinicia Sabina and you will be on a boat to Surrentum, and he will believe, truly believe, that he has done the decent thing."

"Surrentum," Leda said.

"He knows who you are. He has known for nine days." Marcus opened his eyes. "He told me this afternoon, on the way back from the Vinicius house. He said there is a woman in Surrentum called Vibia Prima who has been petitioning governors for ten years, and that the petition is in the archive, and that when he produces you before the panel he will produce you as a free woman recovered, and the house will be shown to have bought stolen goods in good faith, and your mother will have you back, and everyone will be satisfied."

Leda did not move.

"My mother," she said.

"Her name is Vibia Prima. She lives at Surrentum, in a house by the wine quarter, and she has a son—"

"Her son is called Gaius," Leda said, in a voice that was not her own. "He is two years older than me. He has a mark on his chin from a fall off a wall. He cannot swim." She was staring at the lamp. "I had forgotten his name. I have been trying to remember his name for six years and I could not, and I have been singing songs about a girl on a shore because I could not remember my brother's name."

Marcus reached out and took her hand.

"Then it is true," he said.

"It is true." She said it slowly, testing it. "I am not—" She stopped. "My father sold wine. He had a yard with a wall and a vine over the gate, and there were amphorae in rows, and in the summer it smelled of wasps, and my mother had a song for the wasps, and I could not remember the words." She looked at him. "I am somebody's daughter."

"Yes."

"And they are alive."

"Yes."

Leda put her other hand over her mouth and cried, and it was the first time he had seen her cry, and it was not like a woman crying in a play; it was ugly and wet and it went on for a long time. He sat on the floor and held her hand and did not say anything, because there was nothing to say, and because he had learned in Dacia that the thing a man does when someone is breaking in front of him is to stay.

When she had finished she wiped her face with the corner of the blanket and blew out her breath.

"All right," she said. "Then what do we do?"

"We go," said Marcus.

"To Surrentum?"

"To Surrentum." He was speaking quickly now, the way a man speaks when the plan is arriving in his head faster than he can examine it. "Not to the panel. Not to my father. To your mother. If we go to her ourselves, with the purchase document, and I stand before the duumvirs of the town and swear that you are free-born and that I mean to marry you—"

"Marry me."

"Yes."

"Your father will disinherit you."

"He will try. He cannot disinherit a son without cause and he will not air the cause in a court, because the cause is me running away with a singer, and a Claudius does not air anything in a court." Marcus was on his feet now. "I have eleven denarii and a horse. You have—"

"I have a shell and a lyre."

"Then we will sell the lyre." He laughed, and she laughed, and it was the laughter of two people who are very frightened and have decided to be brave. "There is a boat at Ostia on the Ides. A grain freighter that takes passengers, the *Providentia*, and the master is a man called Onasus who owes a debt to a friend of mine. It goes to Puteoli, and from Puteoli there are boats every day along the bay. It is three days. Four at the outside."

"And if your father sends after us?"

"He will send to the ports, and the ports will be watched by the time we get there, if he knows we have gone. That is why we go before he knows." He took her by the shoulders. "The day after tomorrow. At the fourth hour of the night. There is a door in the Servian wall by the Viminal gate that the watchmen use, and the man on it is called Petronius and he is drunk four nights out of seven and I have money for the other three. Bring nothing that will make a noise. Nothing that will weigh you down."

Leda looked at him.

"My mother is alive," she said, "and I am to run away from the only house that has kept me, with a boy I have known for four months, to a town I have not seen in ten years, on the word of a man who has never kept a promise to anyone."

"Yes."

"You are all I have," she said, "and that is a terrible thing to be."

"I know."

"You do not know. You are a Claudius. If this goes wrong you will be sent to the Wall and given a command and in ten years you will be a senator and this will be a story you tell at dinner. If it goes wrong for me I will be in a mill with a collar on, or in the Tiber." She stood up. "But I will be there. The day after tomorrow. At the fourth hour. And if you are not there, Marcus, I will not come looking for you, because I will be dead or gone, and either way there will be no point."

"I will be there."

"Say it as though you believe it."

"I will be there," said Marcus Claudius Corvus, and he believed it, and it was the last simple thing he believed for a long time.

He went out by the kitchen door at the eleventh hour and walked home through the streets with his hood up, past the closed shops and the watchmen and the dogs, and the city was quiet in the way that a city is quiet when it is full of people sleeping and a few people doing harm.

There was a light in his father's study.

He went up the stairs quietly and along the corridor, and the door was open a hand's breadth, and through it he saw his father sitting at the table with a lamp and a wax tablet and a cup of water, writing. He had taken off his shoes. It was the most human thing Marcus had ever seen him do, and it filled him with a sadness so large and so useless that he had to put his hand on the wall.

Then Corvus looked up and saw him through the gap in the door.

"Come in," he said.

Marcus came in.

"Sit down."

He sat. The room smelled of lamp oil and old paper. On the table beside the tablet there was a small box of dark wood with a bronze lock, and Marcus had never seen it before in his life, and he noticed that his father's hand was resting on it.

"Where have you been?" Corvus asked.

"You know where I have been."

"Yes." Corvus set down the stylus. "I have known for a month. I had hoped that you would tell me, and I had hoped that you would stop, and you have done neither, and now I find that I must do the thing I have been putting off."

"Which is?"

"Which is to explain to you what you are doing." His father's voice was quiet and reasonable and it was worse than shouting. "You are twenty years old. You have no money, no office, no reputation to speak of, and a name that will open doors for you exactly once, when you are introduced in the Forum as the son of your father. In four months you will marry a girl with three hundred thousand sesterces and a good temper, and in six years you will be quaestor, and in fourteen you will be consul, and in thirty you will be an old man in a house full of statues who has done nothing dishonourable and nothing remarkable, and the family will still be standing. That is the whole of it. That is what a Claudius is for."

"And if I do not want it?"

"Then you will want it in ten years, and it will be too late." Corvus leaned back. "The girl is not the problem. I have known a hundred men who kept a girl. Blandus kept a girl for nine years and his wife used to send her presents at the Saturnalia. The problem is that you are in love with her, and a man in love is a man who can be led by the nose by anyone who has her, and you are the son of a man who is on a panel of inquiry into a murder in the house where she lives."

Marcus's hands were cold.

"You are going to destroy her," he said.

"I am going to do my duty, which will involve the house being closed and its women dispersed. The girl will be returned to her mother, which is a mercy, whether she knows it or not. The woman Cassia will be fined, or exiled, or—" he stopped. "That is not your concern."

"She is a witness. She saw a man in the passage with a cup. If you put her in front of your panel and then hand her to her mother, she will be gone, and the man who killed Vettius will be safe, and the whole thing will have been for nothing."

Corvus was silent for a moment.

"You have been talking to her," he said.

"Of course I have been talking to her."

"About the case."

"About everything. She is not a piece of evidence, Father. She is a girl who was stolen off a beach when she was eleven and has been sold twice, and she remembers her brother's face and not his name, and she is frightened of you and of the prefect and of the hook, and I have promised her—" he stopped himself.

"Promised her what?"

"Nothing."

"Marcus."

"Nothing that concerns you."

Corvus looked at him for a long moment. Then he did something that Marcus had never seen him do: he poured a second cup of water and pushed it across the table.

"Drink," he said. "You look like a man who has been walking in the heat."

Marcus did not touch the cup.

"When I was your age," Corvus said, "there was a girl in this house. She was a slave. She was clever and she was angry and she had been badly used before she came to us, and I was—" He stopped. He turned the cup around on the table. "I was not kind to her. I was not cruel to her either. I was careless, which is what a young man is, and I have spent twenty years discovering that careless is a kind of cruelty and that the difference between the two is only who is keeping the accounts."

Marcus sat very still.

"What happened to her?"

"She was sold," Corvus said. "I sold her, and I gave her a purse, and I told myself that I had done a merciful thing." He picked up the stylus. "I have not seen her since. And I will tell you what I have learned, which is that a man may do a merciful thing and a wicked thing in the same hour and spend the rest of his life not knowing which one he did. That is not a reason to do nothing. It is a reason to do the thing that is required of you and to do it cleanly and without self-pity, and then to go on."

He looked up.

"The day after tomorrow," he said, "you will go to Ostia and you will meet the grain factor Manlius, who is a client of mine, and you will spend a month learning the shipping trade, which is a thing that every senator pretends to understand and none of them does. You will leave at dawn. You will not go to the Vicus Tuscus before you go, and you will not send a message there, and when you come back in the autumn you will marry Vinicia Sabina and you will never mention the singer again."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I will have you watched, and you will be prevented, and the girl will go to the panel in chains instead of in a litter, and she will be asked questions under torture, because a slave's evidence is worth nothing otherwise, and I will not be able to stop it." Corvus said it without pleasure. "I am not threatening you. I am telling you the shape of the world. I have spent my life in it and I know the shape of it, and you do not, and one day you will thank me."

Marcus stood up.

"I will go to Ostia," he said.

"Good."

"At dawn."

"Good." Corvus looked down at his tablet. "Close the door."

Marcus went out and closed the door and stood in the dark corridor with his fists clenched and his heart going like a drum, and he thought: *the day after tomorrow, at the fourth hour, at the Viminal gate.* And then, because he was his father's son and had been raised in a house where nothing was ever said plainly, he thought: *he will have me watched. He said so. He said so because he wants me to know, and because a man who is watched will do something foolish to throw off the watchers, and he will be ready for that.*

So he would not go to the Viminal gate.

He would go over the roof.

In the morning his father beat a boy in the atrium for dropping a cup, and Marcus stood and watched, as he had watched since he was a child, and said nothing, and afterward he went to his room and packed a bag with a cloak, a knife, two loaves, and the eleven denarii, and he lay on his bed with his eyes open and listened to the house, and counted the hours.
