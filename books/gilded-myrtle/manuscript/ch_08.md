# Chapter Eight

## Corvus

The prefecture stood at the foot of the Palatine, in the old house that had belonged to a family no one remembered, and the room in which the panel sat had been a dining room once. You could still see the place on the wall where the painted frieze had been cut away, and the floor was a mosaic of a hunting scene with most of the animals missing, and there was a smell in it of wet stone and hot lamp oil and the particular smell of a room in which men wait.

There were three of them on the panel.

Lucius Pedo Vergilianus, the prefect of the city, was a heavy, tired, careful man of sixty who had been a consul and a governor and had asked for this post because it was the last one and because it kept him in Rome. He sat at the head of the table with his hands folded and his eyes half shut and said almost nothing.

Caius Vibius Maro, of the urban cohorts, sat at the end with a tablet and a stylus and the air of a man who has already decided and is waiting for the evidence to catch up. He had a scar on his neck and a way of looking at a witness as though he were measuring him for a collar.

And Corvus sat in the middle, in a plain toga, with his hands on the table.

The business of the first day was the cup.

It came in on a tray, carried by a slave, with a cloth over it, and Pedo lifted the cloth himself. It was a common cup of red Samian ware, with a chip in the rim and a dark stain inside that had not been washed out.

"The physician," said Maro. "Sarpedon of Athens, slave of Antonia Cassia."

Sarpedon came in. He had been sobered up by somebody and he was not enjoying it. He stood with his hands behind his back and gave his evidence in the flat voice of a man reciting a lesson.

"The deceased was sixty-one years of age and had the appearance of a man who had been unwell for some time. He had eaten oysters, eggs, thrush, kid, turbot and a dish of quinces, and had drunk, by his own servant's account, three cups of wine and water in the course of the evening, which is nothing. When I reached him he was on the floor, insensible, with the pulse gone from the wrist and the throat. The face was discoloured, the eyes prominent, the mouth foaming. There was no wound. There was no disease of the heart that I could detect, and I looked."

"You looked," said Maro.

"I looked."

"Are you often drunk when you look?"

Sarpedon's jaw tightened. "I had been drinking. It was a banquet. I was not so drunk that I could not find a man's heart."

"In your opinion, what killed him?"

"In my opinion he was poisoned."

"With what?"

"With something that acts quickly and produces convulsions and a discolouration of the face. Aconite would do it. White hellebore would do it. There are a dozen things that would do it, and half of them can be bought in the Subura for the price of a dinner, and I could not tell you which it was without the stomach, and the stomach is—" he stopped.

"Where is the stomach?"

"Burned, with the body, on the fifth day, according to the custom."

Maro wrote something down. Pedo opened his eyes.

"Who washed the cup?" Corvus asked.

The room turned to him.

"The cup," he said. "It came from the table to this building in a locked box, and it is stained, and it has not been washed. Who decided that?"

"The steward of the house," said Maro, "at the instruction of the woman. She had it sealed and put in the strongbox before the body was cold. Which is either the act of an innocent woman preserving evidence, or the act of a guilty one preserving the appearance of it."

"Or the act of a woman who has been in business for twenty years," Corvus said, "and knows that in this city the man who holds the object decides what it means."

Pedo looked at him for a moment. There was something in the old man's face that Corvus did not like: not suspicion, exactly. Recognition.

"You have a theory, Corvus?"

"I have a question. The deceased brought his own cup. Why?"

"His servant says he was afraid of being poisoned."

"Then he was afraid of a particular person, or a particular house, and he went into that house anyway, and drank from his own cup, and died." Corvus turned his own cup around on the table, once, and stopped himself, because it was a habit of his father's. "I would like to know who he was afraid of."

"We have asked the servant," said Maro. "He says he does not know. He says his master did not confide in him."

"Then ask him again."

"We will ask him again," said Maro, "when we have him on the hook. He is a slave. His evidence is worth nothing until then."

Corvus looked at him.

"The man is a witness, not a suspect."

"Every slave is a suspect," said Maro, "until he has been examined."

Pedo cleared his throat. "We will come to the servants in due course. Today we will hear the woman."

They brought Antonia Cassia in at the third hour.

She came in through the door at the end of the room with her head up and her hands folded in front of her, in a plain white stola with no ornament but two pearls, and she was, Corvus thought, entirely composed. There was a scab on her lower lip, which he had put there, and which every man in the room had noticed, and which she had not attempted to hide with paint, and he understood, with a cold drop of something in his stomach, that she had left it showing on purpose.

"Antonia Cassia," said Pedo. "You are the owner of the house on the Vicus Tuscus known as the Gilded Myrtle."

"I am."

"You are a freedwoman."

"I am."

"Of what patron?"

"Of Gaius Claudius Corvus," Antonia said, "who is sitting at this table."

The room was very quiet.

Pedo's eyes went to Corvus. Maro's stylus stopped moving.

"Is this true?" Pedo asked.

"It is true," Corvus said. "I manumitted her seventeen years ago, in my father's house, before witnesses now dead. I have not seen her in the interval, and I did not know that she was the owner of the house in question until nine days before Vettius's death, and I have declared the connection to the prefect in writing." He heard his own voice, level and cold, and he was impressed by it. "I have no interest in her fortune and no wish to defend her. I am here because I was asked to be here."

"You might have mentioned it sooner."

"I might have," Corvus said. "I did not think it was to the purpose."

"It is to the purpose now," said Maro, with a certain relish. "A patron sits on the panel that tries his own freedwoman."

"A patron," said Corvus, "sits on the panel that tries the woman accused of poisoning a consular at her own table, and whose house is a public nuisance, and who has been cited not for a crime against me but against the city. If I were to withdraw every time a man in this room had a connection to a woman—" he stopped himself. "I will withdraw if the prefect wishes it."

Pedo looked at him for a long moment.

"No," he said. "Sit. I would rather have you where I can see you."

Antonia smiled, very slightly, at nobody at all.

They examined her for two hours.

Maro asked the questions. Where was she when the cup was filled? What did she know of the deceased? Had she quarrelled with him? Had he offered her money? Had he been refused? How many times had he visited her house? Who were her bankers? Who were her patrons? Had she ever been accused of a crime? Had she ever been flogged? Had she ever been sold? By whom, to whom, for how much? Was it true that a woman of her condition could not give evidence in her own defence, and did she understand what that meant?

She answered all of it. She answered the questions about her girlhood in the same flat voice as the questions about the wine, and Corvus sat and listened, and once or twice he looked at the wall.

At the fourth hour Maro said, "The singer. The girl called Leda. She is yours?"

"She is of my household."

"She is a slave."

"She is of my household," Antonia said, "and I have reason to believe that she was free-born, and that her mother lives at Surrentum, and that a petition for her recovery is in the archive of this prefecture."

Maro's stylus paused.

"Who told you that?"

"Gaius Claudius Corvus told me," Antonia said, "in the portico of the temple of Castor, four days ago."

Corvus did not move.

Pedo turned his head, slowly, like a man who has heard a noise in an empty house.

"Is this true?" he asked.

"The girl's origin is a matter of record," Corvus said. "The dealer Nymphodorus of Puteoli sold her to the house six years ago, and the document of sale gives her origin as Massilia, which is false. I have made inquiries. Her mother's petition is in this building."

"And you did not think to bring it to the panel?"

"I am bringing it now," Corvus said, "with the girl."

"The girl is a witness," said Maro. "The girl's evidence is the only evidence that anyone saw a man in the passage. If she is free-born, her evidence is worth something without the hook, and the whole case changes shape." He looked at Corvus with a flat, unfriendly interest. "You have been sitting on this for four days."

"I have been verifying it for four days."

"Yes," said Maro. "You have."

Pedo closed his eyes for a moment.

"We will adjourn," he said. "The girl will be brought before us tomorrow. She will be examined as a witness, and if her manumission or her free birth is established, she will be examined without the hook, and if it is not established she will be examined as the law provides." He stood up, heavily. "Corvus. A word."

The room emptied. Maro went last, with his tablets under his arm and his eyes going from one of them to the other, and the door closed.

Pedo stood at the window with his back to him.

"You are making this difficult," he said.

"I am trying to keep it from becoming a joke."

"It became a joke in the Forum nine days ago. You cannot keep a thing from becoming a joke; you can only decide whether you are inside the joke or outside it." Pedo turned. "I asked you onto this panel because you are severe and because you have no connection to the house. You told me that. You looked me in the eye and told me that."

"I did not know it was a lie when I said it."

"No," said Pedo. "That is what is interesting about you." He came back to the table. "I have known you twenty years. You are the most upright man in the Senate and the coldest, and I have never once seen you do a dishonourable thing, and I have often wondered why. Most men are upright because they are afraid of the gods. You are upright the way a man is upright who is carrying something heavy and has decided that the only way to carry it is not to put it down." He sat. "What is she to you?"

"Nothing."

"You struck her in the portico of Castor, in front of two clerks and a man selling sausages. That is not nothing; that is the opposite of nothing."

"I struck her," Corvus said, "because she said something true."

Pedo looked at him for a long moment. Then he laughed, without much humour, and rubbed his face with both hands.

"Get out," he said. "Bring me the girl tomorrow. Bring me a case that does not stink, or I will hand the whole thing to Maro and let him hang whoever he likes, and he will like the woman, because she is rich and he is not and because she looked at him as if he were a doorkeeper."

He went to the Caelian that afternoon, on foot, with one slave, and he did not send word first.

The house of the Vettii was on the slope above the Colosseum, and it was a house of the old kind with a bronze lion on the door and cypresses in the garden, and it had the particular smell of a house in which somebody has died: wax, and lilies going over, and the closed air of rooms that are not being used.

The doorkeeper said that his master was not receiving.

"Tell him Corvus."

"Dominus, he is not—"

"Tell him Corvus, and tell him that I am on the panel of inquiry into his father's death, and that if he does not receive me now I will come back with a lictor."

Publius Vettius received him in the tablinum.

He was thirty years old and he looked forty. He had the thin, strained face of a man who has not slept, and there was a cup on the table at his elbow and a set of accounts pushed to one side, and the room smelled of wine and dust.

"Corvus," he said. "You might have written."

"I might have." Corvus sat down without being invited. "I have come about your father's will."

"The will is sealed."

"The will is sealed until the estate is administered, which will be in the autumn, and I have no interest in the will. I have an interest in the fact that on the morning after your father died you sent a man to the Vicus Tuscus to ask whether the cup had been taken away."

Publius's face did not change. That was the thing that Corvus noticed, and it was the thing he would remember: that the face did not change at all, and that a man whose father has died is not usually so good at that.

"I sent a man to ask about the cup because my father was poisoned," Publius said. "It seemed to me the most important object in the city."

"It is a reasonable answer."

"It is the true one."

"Then answer another." Corvus leaned back. "Your father came to that house with his own cup and his own servant and his own wine, because he was afraid of being poisoned. A man who is afraid of being poisoned is afraid of a particular person. Who was your father afraid of?"

Publius picked up his cup and drank.

"He was afraid of everyone," he said. "He was sixty-one years old and he had spent forty years in the Senate and he had made an enemy of every man he had ever worked with. He thought the cooks were poisoning him. He thought the barber was poisoning him. In the spring he accused his own steward of selling his wine and putting the money in a box, and the steward was innocent, and my father never spoke to him again." He set the cup down. "He was my father and I will not speak ill of him in this house, but he was not a man who was easy to live with, and the last year was very bad."

"I am sorry."

"No, you are not." Publius looked at him. "You are on a panel. You are here to find out whether I killed him."

"I am here to find out who did."

"Then find out." Publius stood up and went to the window and looked out at the garden. "I will tell you what I know, and then you will go, and we will not have to do this again. My father had a freedwoman in this house, an old woman called Chloe, who had been his nurse when he was a child. She had a son, a man of thirty-five who works in the grain office. In the spring my father told me that he intended to leave Chloe sixty thousand sesterces and the farm at Signia, and that the son was to have a place in the grain office, and that I was to have the house and the debts and the name." He turned. "I was angry. I said things to him in this room that I am ashamed of. If you are looking for a man with a reason to want him dead, you have found one, and he is standing in front of you, and he is not going to lie about it to a man who will find out anyway."

Corvus looked at him.

"Where were you on the night?"

"At the house of a friend. Manlius Sura, on the Aventine. There were nine of us and we played at dice until the fourth hour." Publius's mouth turned. "You may ask him. He will tell you. He will also tell you that I lost a great deal of money and that I was in a foul temper, and that when the news came in the morning I wept in front of everyone, and none of it will make you believe me." He came back to the table. "Do you know what is going to happen, Corvus? Nothing. Nothing is going to happen. There will be an inquiry and a report and a woman will be blamed, because there is always a woman, and in the autumn I will take my father's seat and my father's debts and my father's house, and I will sit in the Senate in his place and listen to men who despised him tell me what a loss he was."

"That is a very bitter speech for a grieving son."

"I am not grieving," said Publius Vettius. "That is the thing I cannot say to anyone else. I am not grieving. I have been trying to grieve for nine days and I cannot do it. He was a cold man and a hard father and he made my mother's life a misery and he never once in thirty years said a kind thing to me that was not about the family, and now he is dead and I am supposed to be broken, and what I actually feel is that a weight has come off my chest and I am ashamed of it." He picked up the cup again. "There. That is the truth. Put it in your report."

Corvus sat with his hands on his knees.

"There is a mark," he said. "A burn in the shape of an eight, on the wrist of the man who carried the cup."

Publius stopped with the cup halfway to his mouth.

"Is there."

"The girl saw it. The singer."

"The singer." Publius set the cup down very carefully. "And whose mark is it?"

"That is what I am trying to establish."

"Well." Publius looked at him. "You had better establish it quickly, had you not, before somebody decides that the mark belongs to a man in a great house and the whole thing becomes a scandal that nobody can control."

The room was quiet. In the garden a bird was making a great deal of noise about something.

"I know what you are doing," Publius said. "You are sitting there in your good toga with your hands on your knees, and you are thinking that I did it, and you are also thinking that if I did it, it would be very convenient for everybody, because a son who kills his father is a scandal that can be contained, and a consul-designate whose steward kills a consular is a scandal that cannot." He smiled. "You are welcome to think it. But I will tell you something for nothing, Corvus, because my father is dead and I find that I no longer care what I say to anyone. Whatever I am, I am not the worst man in this city. I have been in this house for nine days with the walls, and I have had time to think about the difference between a man who does a wicked thing and a man who arranges for it to be done by somebody else and then goes to the Senate in a clean toga. I have decided that I am the first kind, and that I can live with it, and that I do not envy the second."

Corvus stood up.

"Thank you for your time," he said.

"Corvus."

He stopped at the door.

"When you find out who it was," Publius said, "and you will find out, because you are a thorough man and because you cannot help yourself, come and tell me. Not for justice. I have no interest in justice. I want to know what my father was worth." He picked up the cup. "It turns out that everything in this city has a price. I should like to know his."

Corvus went out through the atrium past the lilies and the doorkeeper and into the street, and he stood on the Caelian in the sun for a moment with his heart going in a way that he did not like at all.

*I do not envy the second,* he thought.

He walked home.

He sat in the tablinum until the light went, and then he called for a lamp and a cup of water and sat for a while longer.

The house was quiet around him. Somewhere above, his mother's shuttle went back and forth; the sound came down through the floor, steady as a pulse, and it had been the sound of his whole childhood, and he understood suddenly that it would be the sound he remembered when he was dying.

*She has been spinning for forty years*, he thought. *She has spun through three emperors and a civil war and the death of her husband and the marriage of her son and the birth of her grandchildren, and she has never once stopped, and I have never once asked her why.*

He got up and went to the door and looked up the stairs at the light in her room, and then he came back and sat down, and the letter came.

The letter came that evening.

It came in the hands of a young man of the imperial bureau, who rode up to the house on the Palatine at the ninth hour and asked for Gaius Claudius Corvus and would not give the letter to a slave.

Corvus received him in the tablinum and broke the seal and read it twice.

"Dominus?" said Faustus, from the door.

Corvus looked up. The lamp was between them.

"The emperor," he said, "has been pleased to designate me consul for the coming year."

Faustus made a sound. It was a small, undignified, entirely genuine sound, and then he came forward and took Corvus by the hand and kissed it, which he had not done in twenty years, and said, "Dominus. Dominus, this is—the family—your father—"

"Yes," Corvus said.

He sat down slowly.

The letter was three sentences long and the second sentence said that the emperor had been pleased to hear of his diligence in the matter of the death of a consular, and that he trusted the affair would be concluded without scandal to the Senate.

"There is a condition," Corvus said.

Faustus's face went carefully blank. "A condition, dominus?"

"Not stated. Implied." Corvus folded the letter. "The emperor is at Antioch. He has been at Antioch for a year and he is preparing for a war in the east that will cost more than the Dacian wars put together, and he needs the Senate quiet and the provinces calm and the grain fleet on time. He has heard that a consular died in a brothel and that a woman is to be tried for it, and he does not care whether she did it. He cares that in six months' time, when he comes back to Rome, nobody is still talking about it."

"And so?"

"And so I am to make it go away," Corvus said.

"Then it will go away."

"It will not go away by itself. It will go away because somebody makes it go away, and the somebody is me, and the making of it will be the first act of my consulship." He looked at the letter in his hand. "My father would have known how to do it."

"Your father," said Faustus, carefully, "would have done it without hesitating."

"Yes."

"Dominus—"

"Send to my mother," Corvus said. "Tell her the news. Tell her that the wedding will be in the autumn and that the consulship will be in the spring, and that I will want her to receive the wives." He stood. "And Faustus. Where were you on the night of the banquet?"

Faustus did not move. In the lamplight his face was the face of a man in a portrait: smooth, careful, entirely still.

"At the house of the Vestals," he said, "with the will. You sent me yourself."

"The will was sealed at the sixth hour of the morning. The banquet was the night before."

There was a silence. Somewhere in the house a door closed, and a woman laughed, and a dog barked in the street.

"I was at the Myrtle," Faustus said. "You sent me to find out about the girl, and I went, and I spoke to the steward in the kitchen, and I came away. That is all."

"You did not tell me."

"You did not ask." Faustus folded his hands. "Dominus, I have served this house for twenty years. I have kept your father's secrets and yours, and I have done things in your service that I would not have done for any other man, and I have never once asked you for anything. I am asking you now to trust me, and to let this matter be finished in the way that it must be finished, and to think of the consulship and of your son and of the name of the Claudii Corvi, which is a thing that will outlast all of us, and which is worth more than a girl and a woman and an old man in a brothel."

Corvus looked at him.

"Faustus," he said. "Look at me."

Faustus looked at him.

"Did you kill Vettius?"

"No, dominus."

"Did you have him killed?"

"No."

"Do you know who did?"

Faustus was silent for three heartbeats.

"I know," he said, "that a man came to me in the spring with a great deal of money and a story about a father who had ruined his son, and that I listened to the story and took the money and did not ask what it was for. I know that I am a fool, and that a fool is a man who takes money and does not ask what it is for, and that I have been a fool in your service and not against it." His voice was perfectly steady. "And I know that if this comes out, the name of the Claudii Corvi will be dragged through the courts by a freedman and a whore, and your consulship will be a joke, and your son will not marry, and your daughter will not marry, and your mother will die of it. So I have taken steps."

"What steps?"

"To keep it from coming out."

Corvus stood up. He was aware, distantly, that his hands were shaking, and he put them behind his back.

"Who was the man with the money?"

"Publius Vettius."

The lamp guttered. Corvus looked at the letter in his hand: three sentences, and the second sentence, and the trust of an emperor who was a thousand miles away and did not care.

"Get out," he said.

"Dominus—"

"Get out of my sight," Corvus said, "and do not speak to me until I send for you, and if you take one step in the direction of the Vicus Tuscus without my order I will have you crucified in the yard of this house, and I will do it myself, and I will tell my mother that you died of a fever." He heard his voice rising and could not stop it. "Do you understand me?"

Faustus bowed his head.

"Yes, dominus."

He went out.

Corvus sat alone in the tablinum for a long time after the door closed.

He thought about the fact that he had known Faustus for twenty years and had never once asked him where he slept.

He thought about the fact that a man may keep a household of thirty people and know the name of every one of them and know nothing at all about any of them, and that this was, in the whole of Roman life, considered a virtue.

Corvus stood alone in the tablinum for a long time. Then he sat down and put the letter on the table and looked at it, and thought about his father in the shrine with the stylus in his throat, and about the girl in the little room by the stairs who had looked up at him with a face like a woman doing sums, and about the fact that in seventeen years he had never once been able to decide whether he had saved her or bought her.

*I am to be consul*, he thought.

And then, because he was his father's son, he began to make a list.
