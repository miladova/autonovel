# Chapter Two

## Corvus

The first client arrived at the fourth hour of the night, which was an hour earlier than the law allowed and two hours earlier than was decent, and Gaius Claudius Corvus, who had not slept, heard him from the tablinum and knew at once that the day would be a bad one.

He was standing at the window with a cup of water in his hand. Below him the garden of the house of the Claudii Corvi lay in the grey light, a rectangle of clipped box and gravel with a fig tree in the corner and a small bronze Silenus, green with age, who had been falling over drunk for two hundred years. Beyond the garden wall the roof of the Temple of Apollo caught the first of the sun. The house had been in his family for four hundred and eleven years. He knew the number because his father had made him learn it, along with the names of every consul of the line, and the three who had been granted a triumph, and the one who had been condemned for extortion and had thrown himself on his sword in the latrine of a house in Ephesus, whose name was never to be spoken in the atrium.

Corvus had never thrown himself on his sword. He had done something worse, and he had done it quietly, and there had never been a day since when he did not know it.

He set the cup down and went to receive his clients.

They filled the atrium by the fifth hour, sixty-three of them, standing in their order: the equestrians first, then the men of the towns, then the freedmen, then the poor. Some were senators, and these did not stand in any order at all but moved about the room as though they had come by accident, which was the privilege of rank. The air smelled of their clothes. Corvus stood on the step of the tablinum and took each of them by the hand and said the same nine or ten things, in the same order, in the same tone, and each of them went away satisfied that he had been personally remembered, which was the only gift he had to give them and the only one they truly wanted.

Old Rufus, who had been a client of his father's, wanted a letter to the governor of Baetica about a lawsuit over an olive press. Corvus gave it.

A man named Caecilius wanted a place for his son in the vigiles. Corvus said he would see what could be done, which meant that he would not.

A woman wanted him to intercede for her brother, who had been condemned to the mines for a theft he had certainly committed. Corvus listened to her for as long as she spoke without weeping, and then said, "Come back in the Ides," which was not a promise but was better than nothing, and she kissed his hand, and he felt the wet of her mouth on his knuckles for a quarter of an hour afterward.

At the back of the atrium, by the door, a man with a purple stripe on his tunic was waiting with his hands folded, and had been waiting longest. Corvus came to him last, as he came to him every morning.

"Blandus."

"Corvus." Decimus Terentius Blandus smiled. He had a beautiful smile and no money, and Corvus had known him for thirty years. "I need a small thing."

"You need sixty thousand sesterces."

Blandus's smile did not move. "Have you been talking to my banker?"

"You have no banker. You have a Jew in the Transitberina who charges you four per cent a month, and you have a man in the Velabrum who is not a banker at all and charges you something else." Corvus walked him toward the door. "Sell the villa at Signia."

"The villa at Signia is the only thing my wife has left that she likes."

"Then sell her something else." Corvus put a hand on his shoulder, which was the Roman way of ending a conversation in a doorway. "I cannot help you, Decimus. I have not the money, and if I had it, it would not help you, because a man who cannot pay sixty thousand will need a hundred in a year. You have two sons and a name. Take the elder into the army. Sell the younger's betrothal to somebody's money. Do not come to me in the spring with the same face."

"You are hard," said Blandus, without rancour.

"I am accurate."

"You were not always accurate." Blandus looked at him a moment, and there was something in his eye that Corvus did not like. "You were a great deal more generous once. In the year of the German war. Do you remember? We were both young, and you had a—" he stopped himself, with the instinct of a man who has been in the Senate a long time and knows exactly how far a man may be pushed before he stops being useful. "Well. We were younger."

"Goodbye, Decimus."

"I am dining at the Gilded Myrtle tonight," Blandus said, from the doorway. "Do you know it? No. Of course you do not. I shall tell you about it tomorrow. It is a very interesting house."

He went out. Corvus stood for a moment in the empty atrium, and then went to find his steward.

Faustus was in the small room behind the kitchen that he called his office, with the household accounts on a board and a slave boy copying a letter at the other end of the table. He was a freedman of forty-five, thin, neat, with the soft hands of a man who had never carried anything heavier than a set of tablets, and he had been with the family twenty years. He rose when Corvus came in and stood until Corvus sat.

"The estate," Corvus said.

"It is not good."

"Tell me the number."

"Four hundred and ten thousand sesterces owing, at the Kalends of June. Of which eighty thousand is to the fiscus, on account of the loan your father took in the year of the Dacian war, and one hundred and twenty thousand to Volusius, and the rest to various." Faustus turned the board around. "There is the income from the farms at Tibur, which is eleven thousand this year and will be nine next, because the tenant is a fool. There is the rent from the shops in the Argiletum, four thousand. There is the house in Baiae, which costs us more than it brings. And there is the sum your mother brought, which is in trust to her and her heirs and cannot be touched."

"And what is the total?"

"Before the games, and the wedding, and the consular expenses—"

"Before the games, and the wedding, and the consular expenses."

"Perhaps sixty thousand in hand." Faustus folded his hands. "We are, in the technical sense, rich. In the practical sense, if you were to die tomorrow your son would inherit a name, a house full of statues, and a debt that would eat both."

Corvus looked at the board for a while. His father had left it so. His father had been consul, and had built a portico, and had bought a team of Spanish horses, and had died in his own house at sixty-two in a manner that everyone had agreed to call an accident, and had left the family with a name that would open any door in Rome and not one sesterce to pay the doorkeeper.

"There is the Volusian marriage," Faustus said, carefully.

"There is not."

"The girl's dowry would be three hundred thousand. The father has made it plain. He wants a senatorial connection and he is willing to pay for it. The son is a decent enough young man. He has been in the army. He is not a fool."

"He is an equestrian," Corvus said. "His grandfather sold fish sauce."

"His grandfather sold fish sauce very well."

"My daughter is a Claudius."

"Your daughter," said Faustus, with the freedom of a man who had known him since he was a boy, "is a Claudius with no dowry, in a house with a leaking roof and a debt to the fiscus."

Corvus stood up. "I will think about it."

"Your mother has already thought about it."

"Then I will think about it after my mother."

He went through into the peristyle, where the sun had reached the top of the columns, and found his son.

Marcus Claudius Corvus was twenty years old and had come back from Dacia in the autumn with a scar on his forearm and a way of standing that was different from the way he had stood before he went. He had been a tribune of the seventh legion, which had meant a year of watching men die of dysentery and one afternoon of watching men die of other things. He was tall and brown and quiet, and he had his mother's mouth. He was sitting on the edge of the fountain with his boots off and his feet in the water, eating bread.

"Get your feet out of the fountain."

Marcus got his feet out of the fountain.

"You are up early," Corvus said.

"I have not been to bed."

"Where were you?"

"With men," said Marcus. "You would not like them."

"I would not have liked the men I was with at your age either." Corvus sat down on the bench opposite him, and for a moment they were both quiet, and the water went on falling into the basin. "I have something to say to you. I have been waiting to say it for three days and I find that waiting does not improve it."

"Then say it."

"You will marry in the autumn."

Marcus chewed his bread and swallowed it. "Who?"

"The daughter of Vinicius Sabinus. The family is equestrian. They are rich. Her name is—" Corvus found that he did not know the girl's name. "It will be a good match."

"I see."

"You do not see. You will see. Her dowry will pay the fiscus and the roof and the games, and your sister will be married in the spring to a man with a name, and the family will go on, and in ten years nobody will remember where the money came from." He heard his own voice and hated it, mildly, the way a man hates weather. "You will be a consul. You will have a son. This is not a punishment, Marcus. It is a life."

"I know what it is." Marcus put the bread down. "It is what you had."

"It is what my father had, and his father. It is the only thing we have that the fish-sauce men do not." Corvus stood. "Do not marry her for love. Love is a thing that happens in a house after the marriage, if it happens at all, and it is not the point of the house. Do you understand me?"

"Yes."

"Do you?"

"I understand you," said Marcus, and something in his voice made Corvus stop, but he could not tell what, and he did not have the time to find out, because at that moment Faustus came out into the peristyle with the news that the Senate had been summoned for the second hour, and that there was a rumour in the Forum that a consular had died in a house on the Vicus Tuscus in the night.

The Senate met in the Curia Julia, in the shadow of the building works, and the noise of the works came in through the open doors all through the debate: the chink of chisels, the creak of the cranes, the flat concussion of a great block of marble being set down, the shouts of the foremen in the accents of the Greek islands. The new forum of the divine Trajan was rising alongside the old one at a cost that no one had been able to calculate and no one had the courage to ask about. It had a basilica with columns of grey granite sixty feet high, and a temple, and a triumphal arch, and a column with a spiral of soldiers carved around it that would stand, men said, as long as the city stood. The dust of it lay over everything. It got into the wine and the lungs and the folds of a senator's toga, and men who complained about it were careful to complain quietly.

Corvus took his place on the benches of the praetorians, three rows back from the consulars, which was where a man sat when he had been praetor and was waiting for the consulship and did not wish to seem to be waiting for it. Around him the Senate of Rome settled itself like a flock of birds: six hundred men in white wool, the poor ones in togas that had been turned and re-turned, the rich ones in wool so fine that it hung like water. Two hundred of them were important. The rest were there because a man who does not attend the Senate is a man who is not missed.

The debate was on the corn supply.

It had begun with a report from the prefect of the annona: the fleet had come in from Egypt with a good cargo, and the granaries were full, and the price of grain in the city had fallen by a third of a denarius. This should have been the end of it. Instead a consular named Sura had risen to say that the fall in price was a bad sign, since it would discourage the merchants, and that the state should buy the surplus and hold it; whereupon another consular had risen to say that Sura's family owned the largest granaries in Ostia, and Sura had risen again to say that this was a slander, and the Senate had settled happily into the only sport it truly loved, which was the destruction of a colleague's reputation by men who had known him since he was a boy in a nursery.

Corvus listened. He had learned, in nineteen years, that a man's power in the Senate was not measured by how often he spoke but by how many men looked at him while someone else was speaking. He counted, this morning, and found eleven, which was respectable, and three of those were men who owed him money, which did not count.

Halfway through the third hour a clerk came up the aisle and put a folded tablet into the hand of the presiding consul. The consul read it, and his face changed, and he passed it to the man beside him.

"Fathers," he said. "I am told that Gaius Vettius Proculus died in the night."

The Senate made the sound a Senate makes. It was not grief. Vettius had been a difficult man with a cold manner and a talent for making enemies out of men who had been his friends; but he had been consul, and he had been of the old stock, and the death of an old stock is the death of a wall that everyone had assumed would stand.

"Where?" someone called.

The consul looked at the tablet again. "In a house on the Vicus Tuscus."

There was a pause, and then the laugh came. It started at the back and ran forward in a wave: the short, ugly, delighted laugh of men who have recognized a joke that they can tell in the baths for a month. Corvus did not laugh. He sat very still, and the noise of the chisels came in through the doors, and he felt something cold move in his chest, like a key turning in a lock he had thought was rusted shut.

"At the Myrtle," said the man next to him, to nobody in particular. "They say he was at the Myrtle. Well. He was a widower."

"He was a fool," said another. "A man of his age and his rank, in that house, with that woman."

"Which woman?"

"Antonia Cassia." The man said the name with a certain relish, the way a man says the name of a horse he is thinking of betting on. "The one who owns the place. They say she has half the Senate in her book, and the other half in her debt."

"They say," said Corvus, "a great deal."

He heard his own voice, and it was level, and he was proud of it, and he hated himself for being proud.

The debate resumed. He sat through the rest of it and did not hear a word. He was aware, in a distant way, of the shape of the argument, of the corn, of Sura, of the votes; and behind it, in the place where he kept the things that were not permitted, a room was opening: a room he had not entered in seventeen years, with a black floor and a painted wall and a bronze lamp, and a girl of twenty-three on her knees with her back striped open, and his father's hand in her hair.

He had been twenty-four years old. He had come in at the wrong moment. He had stood in the doorway for three heartbeats while his father turned his head, and in the fourth heartbeat the girl had reached up and taken the stylus from the writing box.

The Senate voted. Corvus voted with the majority, because he had no idea what the question was and because the majority is always safe, and then he went out into the light.

Faustus was waiting for him at the foot of the steps, with two slaves and the litter.

"The prefect's office has sent a man," he said. "Pedo wishes to see you."

"About what?"

"About Vettius. He is forming a panel of inquiry. Three men. He wants a man of your rank on it."

"Why me?"

"Because you are known to be severe," Faustus said. "And because you have no connection to the house."

Corvus looked across the square at the works. A crane was swinging a block of marble through the air above the Basilica Ulpia, and the men below it were going about their business without looking up, which was either courage or habit.

"Tell him I am honoured," Corvus said, "and that I am unwell."

He got into the litter. Faustus walked beside it, and the bearers carried him up the hill, and the dust of the new forum settled on the roof of the old city behind them.

His mother was in the atrium when he came in, which meant that she had been waiting.

Claudia Junia was sixty-three years old and had been a widow for twenty-four years, and in that time she had run the house of the Claudii Corvi with the calm of a woman who had never once had to raise her voice. She was small and straight, with white hair in a knot and a face that had been beautiful and was now something more useful. She was seated on the bench by the shrine with her wool in her lap, spinning, which she did every afternoon and had done every afternoon for forty years, and which she did not do because the family needed wool but because a woman who is spinning cannot be asked to do anything else.

"You are late," she said.

"The Senate was long."

"The Senate is always long. It is the one thing the Senate does well." She turned the spindle. "I have had a letter from Volusius. He is growing impatient. He wishes to know whether we are serious."

"We are not serious."

"Then we are fools." She did not look up. "Your father left you a name and a debt, and you have spent nineteen years polishing the name and paying the interest on the debt, and you have a son who is a soldier with no money and a daughter who is fifteen and will be married in a year or two to somebody, and if you do not take Volusius's money you will take somebody else's, and the somebody else will be worse. That is not a moral question. It is arithmetic."

"Volusius's son is a decent man."

"Then it is a better match than most." She looked up at last, and her eyes were the same weak-tea colour as Vettius's, which was a thing Corvus had never noticed before and would not be able to un-notice afterward. "What is the matter with you? You look as though you have seen a ghost."

"I have seen a name," Corvus said. "Antonia Cassia."

His mother's hands stopped.

For a moment she did not move at all. The spindle hung from her fingers, and the wool went on twisting slowly, and in the silence Corvus heard a slave sweeping in the next room and the pigeons on the roof.

"Where did you hear that name?" she asked.

"In the Senate. Vettius died in her house last night."

"Ah." She began to spin again, more slowly. "Then the gods are just, and late, and in a manner that no honest man would choose."

"You know her."

"I know of her. Everyone knows of her. She is a woman who keeps a house of whores on the Vicus Tuscus and has made herself rich enough that senators go to her for money. That is all I need to know of her, and it is more than I wish to." Her voice was perfectly steady, and perfectly cold. "Why does it trouble you?"

"It does not trouble me."

"Gaius." She had not called him by his name in a year. "You are a poor liar for a man in public life. You always have been. Your father was a better one." She set the spindle down. "There is a thing in this family that nobody speaks of. I have known it for seventeen years and I have never asked, and I will not ask now, because I have found that a woman who asks questions in this house receives answers she must then live with. But I will tell you this. Whatever she was to you, she is nothing now. She is a brothel-keeper with a fortune and a Greek steward and no name, and you are a Claudius, and this family has survived three emperors and two civil wars and one of its own consuls dying in a latrine in Ephesus, and it will survive her." She picked up the wool again. "Do not go to that house. Do not send to it. Do not let your son go near it. And when the prefect asks you to sit on his panel, sit on it, and be severe."

"He has already asked. I refused."

"Then send word that you have changed your mind."

"Mother—"

"Do it today," Claudia Junia said, "before some friend of hers tells him why you refused."

He found his daughter in the garden.

Caesia was fifteen, and she had the family's thinness and none of its calm. She was sitting on the rim of the fountain with a book roll on her knees and her sandals kicked off, and when she saw him she did not get up, which was a thing he had stopped correcting two years before.

"Mother's been in the atrium all morning," she said. "She sent the cook out for almonds and told her to take an hour about it."

"Your grandmother is a woman of strong opinions."

"Your grandmother," said Caesia, "is a woman who wants me to marry a man called Vinicius who has a nose like a shovel and a father who sells fish sauce, and she has told me so eight times since the Kalends, and each time she has told me that I must be sensible, and each time she has looked at me as though I were a horse she was thinking of selling."

"Would you rather a man with a better nose and less money?"

"I would rather," said Caesia, "that somebody asked me."

Corvus sat down on the bench opposite her. The fig tree had put out its first leaves, hard and small and green. Somewhere behind the wall a woman was calling a child.

"Very well," he said. "I am asking you."

Caesia looked up, and for a moment her face was entirely unguarded, and he saw how young she was, and how badly prepared for any of it.

"Does he beat his slaves?" she said.

"I don't know."

"Does he drink?"

"I don't know."

"Has he a mistress?"

"I don't know," Corvus said. "I know that his father is rich and that his mother is dead and that he has been two years in the army on the Danube and came back with his health. I know that the match would save this house. I know that if I refuse it we will sell the farm at Tibur in the spring and the house at Baiae in the summer, and by the time you are twenty there will be nothing left of us but the name and the statues." He said it flatly, because she was old enough for flatness. "What I do not know is what kind of man he is in a room at night. Nobody knows that. Not his father, not his friends, and not his wife until the door is shut."

"Then how does anyone bear it?"

"They do not think about it until they must," he said. "And then they find that they can bear a great deal more than they thought."

Caesia was quiet for a while. She turned the book roll over in her hands. It was, he saw, a book of geography, with a map of the world drawn badly at the beginning: the Middle Sea in the middle, the lands around it like a stain, and at the edges the monsters.

"There was a girl," she said.

"What?"

"In this house. Before I was born." She did not look at him. "When I was small, before old Doris died, she used to tell me stories when she was doing my hair. She said there was a girl in this house who slept in the little room by the stairs, and that my grandfather was very fond of her, and that one night she killed him and ran away, and that everyone said it was a fit, and that my father—" she stopped.

"Go on."

"That my father sent her away," Caesia said, "and that he was never the same after."

The fountain went on falling into the basin. Corvus sat very still and heard, in the silence, the noise of the city beyond the wall: a cart, a dog, a man shouting a price.

"Old Doris," he said at last, "was a freedwoman of your grandmother's who drank, and who told you stories because children listen and because she had nothing else to do with her afternoons. She is dead. She cannot be corrected. That is the way with servants' stories." He stood up. "There was a slave in this house, and she was accused of a theft, and she was sold. That is all. If your grandmother heard you repeat what you have just said, she would be very angry, and I would be angry, and the girl would be—" he stopped himself. "Do not repeat it."

"No, Father."

"Caesia."

She looked up.

"Your grandfather," Corvus said, "was a hard man. He was a good consul and a hard man, and the two are not the same thing, and I have spent my life trying to be the first without being the second and I do not know whether I have succeeded." He put his hand on the top of her head for a moment, the way he had when she was small. "Marry the fish-sauce boy. He will be a senator before he is forty, and his nose will not get any worse, and if he beats his slaves you will find a way to make him stop."

"And if he beats me?"

The question was asked lightly, with a small smile, and it went into him like a splinter.

"Then you will come home," he said, "and I will do what must be done, and the family will survive it or it will not." He took his hand away. "But it will not come to that. We will be careful. Your grandmother will be careful. That is what we are for."

He went up the stairs.

He went up to the small room on the second floor that he used for his own, which had been his father's, and which he had never been able to make his own. There was a couch, a table, a cupboard of documents, a bust of Augustus with a chipped ear. There was a strongbox under the table, and in the strongbox, under a folded will and a bundle of letters from his brother in Baetica, there was a piece of soft leather, and in the leather a bronze stylus.

It was a common thing. A writing stylus of the sort that every schoolboy in Rome had in his satchel, four and a half inches long, with a flat end for smoothing the wax and a point for writing. The point had been cleaned. The rest of it had not; there was a dark stain in the groove behind the point, where the blood had gone in, and no amount of polishing had ever got it out.

He had taken it from the altar himself, before the slaves came in. He had wrapped it in his handkerchief and put it in his sleeve, and later, when the story had been told and the household had been told and the lie had been told, he had put it in this box, and he had never shown it to anyone, and he had never been able to throw it away.

He held it now, and turned it over, and the light from the window ran along the little bronze shaft.

*She was on her knees*, he thought. *And she was not crying.*

That was the thing he had never been able to explain to himself, in seventeen years: that she had not been crying. She had been flogged until the blood ran down her back and she had not cried then, and she had not cried when his father took her by the hair, and she had not cried when she killed him. She had looked up, and seen Corvus in the doorway, and her face had been the face of a woman doing sums.

He put the stylus back in the leather and the leather back in the box, and locked it.

Then he went to the door and called for Faustus.

"Two things," he said. "First, send to the prefect. Tell him I will sit on his panel."

Faustus inclined his head.

"Second." Corvus stood with his hand on the door frame and looked out at the corridor, at the painted panels of the old house, at the light falling on the floor where his father had walked. "Find out everything that is known about the woman who owns the Gilded Myrtle. Everything. Who her bankers are. Who her patrons are. What she owns and what she owes. And find out—" he stopped.

"Dominus?"

"Find out whether she has a girl in that house called Leda."

"Leda," said Faustus, writing it on his tablets. "May I ask why?"

"No," Corvus said. "You may not."

He closed the door and stood alone in his father's room, and outside in the city the hammers went on, and the cranes swung their blocks of marble through the air, and the new Rome rose stone by stone over the bones of the old.
