# Chapter Seventeen

## Antonia

She woke in the dark in a room above a fuller's shop in the Subura, with the smell of the vats coming up through the floor.

The room had one window and a bed and a chest and a lamp, and she had been in it for nine days. It belonged to a woman called Marcella who had been a laundress and was now too old to work and who had taken in Antonia and the women for nothing, because, she said, a woman who has been in the Subura does not forget the Subura.

Prisca was already awake. She had laid out the white stola the night before and had sponged the hem and hung it to dry, and there was a bowl of water on the chest and a comb and the two pearls.

"Domina."

"I am awake."

"You are not. You have not opened your eyes."

Antonia opened her eyes.

"Nine days," she said.

"Nine days."

"It feels like a year."

"It feels like nine days," said Prisca, "and you have eaten almost nothing and you have not slept more than three hours in any of them, and today you are going to stand in a basilica in front of six hundred men who have come to watch you hang." She put the bowl down. "So you are going to eat a piece of bread, and you are going to let me do your hair, and you are going to put on the pearls, and you are not going to say anything clever to the prefect, because men like the prefect hate a clever woman in a courtroom."

"I have nothing clever left to say."

"Good."

Antonia sat up.

The room was grey and cold and the window showed the top of the wall opposite and a slice of sky. Below, in the street, a man was shouting at a donkey.

"The girl is at Ficulea," Prisca said.

"I know."

"He will bring her this morning."

"I know."

"And the cook—"

"I know."

Prisca combed her hair and bound it up the way a matron's is bound, and put the two pearls in her ears, and stood back and looked at her.

"You look like a woman who is going to a trial," she said.

"I am going to a trial."

"You look like my aunt," said Prisca. "She was a hard woman and she buried three children and she never once cried in front of anybody."

Antonia stood up.

They went out at the second hour.

The street was full. The whole of the Subura knew by then, and the shopkeepers had come out to their doorways and the women were on the balconies, and when she came out of the door of the fuller's shop with Prisca and Meroe and Junilla behind her there was a noise that went up the street like a wind through a field.

She walked.

They came down the Argiletum and along the Sacred Way, and by the time they reached the Forum there were people in front of them and behind them and along the steps of the basilicas, and a man on the roof of the portico of Gaius and Lucius was calling out the odds to a crowd below him, four to one against an acquittal, seven to one against her leaving the city alive.

Antonia walked through them with her head up.

She heard the words. She heard all of them: *whore, poisoner, that is her, that is the one, she does not look like much, my cousin says she has a box of names, my cousin says she bought the cook's silence with a house, look at her hands, look at her hands, she has never done a day's work in her life—*

She had done nothing but work for twenty-six years. She had got up before light every morning since she was fourteen years old. She had counted and saved and carried and smiled and remembered and been afraid, and there had not been a single day in twenty-six years when she had not been working, and the man on the roof of the portico had never worked at anything in his life except finding out which way a crowd was going.

She stopped at the foot of the steps of the Basilica Julia.

"Domina," said Prisca. "You are shaking."

"I know."

"Look at me." Prisca turned her around by the shoulders. "You are Antonia Cassia. You built a house out of nothing in a city that eats women, and you have fed and housed eleven people for seventeen years, and you are the only woman in Rome who has ever made a senator pay interest." She gripped her arms. "When you go in there, do not look at the gallery and do not look at the prefect and do not look at the man on the Palatine. Look at the back wall, above their heads, and say what you have to say to the wall."

Antonia looked at her.

"When did you become the brave one?" she said.

"About nine days ago," said Prisca. "It was either that or weep, and there was nobody else to do it."

Antonia went up the steps.

They tried her in the Basilica Julia, in the great central hall, because the prefect's court could not hold the crowd.

The basilica was a hundred and eighty feet long and a hundred and seventy wide and had four aisles and a gallery, and on the morning of the Ides it was full by the second hour. Men had begun arriving before dawn. The advocates had their places on the steps; the clerks had their tables at the side; the money-changers had been moved out into the portico and were doing a fine trade in bets. In the gallery the women of good family sat with their slaves and their fans, and there was a great deal of noise and a smell of hot wool and garlic and the dust that rose off the floor when six hundred people shuffled their feet.

Pedo sat on a raised platform at the eastern end with the panel on either side of him. Maro sat at his right with his tablets. On his left there was an empty chair where Corvus had sat, and Corvus was in the body of the court, in the third row of the senatorial benches, with his hands on his knees.

Antonia came in at the third hour.

She came on foot, from the Vicus Tuscus, with Tiro on one side and Prisca on the other and Meroe and Junilla behind her, and the crowd in the street outside the basilica parted for them and then closed behind them and came in after them. She wore a plain white stola, no jewellery but the two pearls, and her hair bound up like a matron's, and she walked up the central aisle with her head up and her eyes on the platform, and she did not look at the people on either side.

She heard them. That was the thing nobody tells you: that you hear them. *That is her. That is the one. She does not look like a murderer. She looks like my aunt. Look at her hands. They say she has a box of names. They say the consul-designate—*

She stopped at the mark on the floor.

"Antonia Cassia," said Pedo. "You are charged with the murder of Gaius Vettius Proculus, consular, by poison, in your house on the Vicus Tuscus, on the sixth day before the Kalends of June; and with keeping a house of ill repute contrary to the peace of the city; and with the corruption of the morals of the young. How do you plead?"

"I plead not guilty to the murder," Antonia said. "I plead guilty to the house, because it is mine, and I have kept it for seventeen years, and I have paid the tax on it, and I have never made a secret of it to anyone who asked."

There was a ripple of laughter in the gallery.

"The plea is noted," said Pedo. "Maro. Begin."

Maro began.

He was a good speaker, which Antonia had not expected. He was not clever and he was not subtle, but he had a way of laying out a thing in order that made the order itself seem like proof, and he had a voice that carried to the back of the hall without effort.

He began with the dead man.

He described Vettius: consul, senator, soldier, a man of the old school, a widower, a father, a man of sixty-one who had gone to a house on the Vicus Tuscus to be entertained and had died there among strangers. He described the banquet in detail: the nine guests, the wine, the singing. He described the cup.

"The cup," he said, "was the dead man's own. He brought it with him. He had a stomach that had made war on him and he did not drink from the cups of other men. Think about that, fathers. Think about a man who has come to the end of his life and has learned to be careful, who brings his own cup to a dinner because he is afraid of being poisoned." He turned slowly. "And think about a house in which a man may be poisoned in his own cup, at the table of the woman who owns it, with nine witnesses and not one of them able to say who did it."

He called Sarpedon.

The physician was sober this time, and frightened, and he gave his evidence in a low voice and Maro made him repeat it twice. The discolouration. The foam. The absence of any disease of the heart. The impossibility of examining the stomach, because the body had been burned on the fifth day, according to the custom.

"Who ordered the body burned?" Maro asked.

"The family," said Sarpedon.

"Did the panel order it?"

"The family took the body. It is the custom."

"It is the custom," Maro said, "and it is also a very convenient custom for a woman who wishes the evidence to be in a jar on the Via Flaminia."

He called the clerk who had taken the cup.

He called the man who had carried the bier.

He called, at the fifth hour, the clerk of the cells, who read out the confession of Calybe.

The hall went quiet for that.

The clerk read it in the flat voice of a man reading a list of goods, and when he came to the end of it there was a sound from the gallery that was not quite a sigh and not quite a groan.

"The cook will be produced," said Maro.

They brought her in.

Antonia had prepared herself for it. She had lain awake for three nights preparing herself for it, and she had told herself that Calybe would be alive and would be broken and that it would be a terrible thing and that she must not weep. She was not prepared at all.

Calybe came in between two guards with her arms held and her feet dragging, and she was a different woman. She was grey. Her face had a slackness in it, and one of her eyes did not track, and her hands were bound in cloth, and she walked the way very old women walk, in little steps, and she was forty-four years old.

She did not look at Antonia.

"Calybe," said Pedo. "You are the slave of Antonia Cassia, a cook. You have confessed to putting poison in the cup of Gaius Vettius Proculus at your mistress's order. Is that confession true?"

Calybe stood in the middle of the floor and said nothing.

"Answer the prefect," said Maro.

"It is true," said Calybe.

"Say it in your own words."

"My mistress gave me a packet." Her voice was slow and there were gaps in it. "White powder. She said to put it in the cup. The old man's cup. I said I did not want to. She said—" she stopped, and her mouth worked. "She said a slave who will not do as she is told is a slave who is sold to the mines."

"Did you put it in the cup?"

"I put it in the cup."

"Where?"

"In the passage. Behind the dining room. Before the second course."

"Did anyone see you?"

"A man of the house saw me."

"Who?"

"I do not know his name."

"Was it the singer? The girl called Leda?"

Calybe's head came up, and for the first time she looked at Antonia, and Antonia saw in her face something that was almost a smile.

"No," she said. "It was not the girl."

Maro's stylus stopped.

"You said in your confession that a man of the house saw you and said nothing."

"I said a man of the house. It was not the girl. It was a man." Calybe's voice was firmer now. "It was not the girl and it was not the steward and it was not the boy. It was a man I did not know. I do not know his name. I do not know whose man he was." She turned her head, slowly, and looked at the crowd, and her voice went up. "It was not her. She never gave me anything. She never gave me anything in her life but a good kitchen and a bad temper and a place to sleep—"

The guards took her by the arms.

"Withdraw the witness," said Maro.

"The witness has not finished," said Pedo.

"The witness is raving. She was examined under the hook four days ago and she has been raving since. She has said three different things in three days. I have the clerk's record here—"

"Sit down, Maro," said Pedo.

There was a silence. Maro sat down.

Pedo looked at Calybe for a long moment.

"You have said that you put the powder in the cup at your mistress's order," he said. "And now you say that she never gave you anything. Which is true?"

Calybe stood in the middle of the floor with the guards holding her arms.

"I do not know," she said. "I do not know what is true any more. I have been in a room for four days and there was a man with a hook and he asked me the same question over and over and after a while I could not tell what I remembered and what he wanted me to remember, and I said what he wanted, and he wrote it down." She began to cry. "I have been a cook in that house for eleven years and I have never put anything in a cup but wine."

They took her out.

The hall was very quiet.

Antonia stood at the mark on the floor and her hands were shaking, and she put them behind her back.

"The court will adjourn for an hour," said Pedo.

In the recess they put her in a small room off the portico with a guard at the door and Tiro and Prisca inside, and Antonia sat on a bench and drank water and could not stop shaking.

"She did not do it," Prisca said. "She did not do it, domina, she has never—"

"I know."

"Then why did she say it?"

"Because a man put a hook in her and asked her the same question for four days." Antonia put the cup down. "And because for the first three days she said nothing, and on the fourth day she would have said anything to make him stop, and then they wrote it down and read it out in a basilica and six hundred people heard it."

"What will happen?"

"I do not know." Antonia looked at the wall. "Maro will say that she is raving because the hook has addled her, and the panel will believe him, because it is convenient, and because a confession that is withdrawn is worse than no confession at all. Or he will say that her second statement proves she is unreliable and her first statement is the true one, and the panel will believe that instead, because it is also convenient." She stood up. "Either way the room has heard a woman say that I put poison in a cup."

She went out into the portico. The crowd in the Forum was enormous; the trial had emptied the shops and the workshops, and there were people standing on the steps of the temple of Castor and on the roof of the portico opposite. She could hear them, a low continuous roar, like the sea.

Blandus was waiting for her by the column.

"You did well," he said.

"I did nothing."

"You stood there." He was pale and he had been drinking. "I have been in the Senate nineteen years and I have never seen a woman stand there."

"You said you would stand up."

"I will." He looked at the crowd. "When?"

"When they ask if anyone in Rome can speak for my character."

Blandus nodded slowly.

"Antonia," he said. "My wife has left the city. She has gone to her sister at Tusculum and she has taken the boys. She told me this morning that if I stand up in that basilica and speak for a brothel-keeper I am not to come home." He looked at her. "I am going to stand up."

"Blandus—"

"I am fifty years old," he said, "and I have done nothing in my life but be charming and in debt. And you gave me back sixty thousand sesterces because I asked you at a kitchen door, and you did not make me crawl for it, and you did not tell anyone." He straightened his toga. "I am going to stand up, and my wife will go to Tusculum, and in ten years I will be a man who was ruined by his principles, which is a better thing to be than a man who was never ruined by anything."

In the recess they gave her bread and she could not eat it.

She sat in the small room off the portico with the guard at the door and the noise of the Forum coming through the wall, and she thought about Calybe in the middle of the floor with her hands wrapped in cloth, and about the fact that the confession had been read out and six hundred people had heard it, and that in the whole of that hall there had been perhaps nine men who understood that a confession taken on a hook is not a confession but a sound.

Marcus was in the hall. She had seen him at the back, by the door, with his hood down; the son of the man who was trying her, standing at the back of the crowd like a client.

She had looked at him for a moment and he had looked back and neither of them had moved.

That was the thing she would remember about the first day, more than Maro or the cook or the crowd: a boy of twenty standing at the back of a basilica with his hands at his sides.

The court resumed at the eighth hour.

Maro rose and said that he had finished with the witnesses of fact, and that the panel would hear the evidence of the household, and that he would call, first, the singer.

There was a stir in the hall.

"The singer is not in the city," said Pedo. "She is in the custody of the prefecture at Ficulea and will be produced tomorrow morning."

"Then I will call the steward of the house," said Maro.

Tiro stood up.

He was fifty-two years old and had a scar from his ear to his mouth and had killed nine men in an arena, and he walked to the mark on the floor and stood on it with his hands at his sides.

"Your name."

"Tiro. Freedman of Antonia Cassia."

"Your trade."

"Steward."

"You found the body of the man Callistus in the Tiber."

"I did not."

"You reported it to your mistress."

"I reported a man dead in an alley."

"You killed him."

Tiro did not move.

"I did," he said.

The hall made a noise.

"You admit it."

"I killed him. He had a hand in the death of a boy of twelve in my house. I found him in an alley by the fourth shed and I put a knife in him." Tiro's voice was flat. "I am not sorry and I will not pretend to be. If the panel wishes to try me for it, I will stand here and be tried for it. But I will tell you what I found on him first."

Pedo leaned forward.

"What did you find on him?"

"A token with a name on it, and a note in a bad hand."

"What did the note say?"

"I cannot read," said Tiro. "I gave it to my mistress."

Every head in the basilica turned to Antonia.

Pedo sat back.

"Domina," he said. "Do you have this note?"

"Yes."

"Will you produce it?"

Antonia looked at Maro, and at Pedo, and at the crowd in the gallery, and at Corvus in the third row of the senatorial benches, who was sitting very still with his hands on his knees.

"Not yet," she said.

There was a murmur.

"Domina," said Pedo. "You are on trial for your life. If you have a document that bears on this case, you will produce it now."

"I will produce it when the man whose household it names is in this room and can answer for it." Antonia's voice carried. "The note is in the hand of a steward. It says: *the old man first.* I will produce it, and I will name the steward, when the panel is ready to hear the name, and not before."

"You do not get to decide when the panel is ready," said Maro.

"No," Antonia said. "But I have been in this city for forty years, and I have learned that a thing said at the wrong moment is a thing that can be denied, and a thing said at the right moment is a thing that cannot."

Pedo looked at her for a long time.

"The court will adjourn," he said, "until the second hour tomorrow. The witness from Ficulea will be produced. The domina will bring the note. And I will have order in this basilica, and if there is one more outbreak I will clear it and try the case in a closed room, and the city may say what it likes." He stood up. "Take her to the cells."

They took her to the cells.

It was a room under the basilica, with a stone floor and a barred window at the height of a man's chest, and a bench, and a bucket, and a lamp that was not lit. There was a smell of damp and of the river, and through the wall she could hear men talking and a man singing, very badly and very far away.

Antonia sat on the bench in the dark.

She thought about Calybe in the middle of the floor with her hands bound in cloth, and about the fact that a woman she had known for eleven years had been broken in a room with a hook, and that the breaking had been done lawfully, by men with a warrant, in the name of the city.

She thought about the trial. She had thought, in the months before it, that she would be frightened. She was not frightened. She was angry, in a way that was so large and so cold that it had stopped being a feeling and become a place she could live in.

She thought about Tiro, who had sold her and had stood up in a basilica and confessed to a murder to put the panel off its stride, and who had done it, she understood, as a man pays a debt.

She thought about the boy on the Esquiline.

At some hour of the night she heard the bolt go back.

The guard came in with a lamp and set it on the bench and went out, and a man came in and stood in the doorway with his hands behind his back.

It was Corvus.

"You should not be here," Antonia said.

"I know."

"If Maro hears of it he will say that the panel is corrupt."

"Maro is asleep," Corvus said, "and the guard has been paid by my steward, which is a thing I have never done before and will not do again." He came into the room. "I have come to tell you that the girl will be produced tomorrow, and that she will give her evidence, and that she has been treated well."

"I know. You told me."

"I have come to tell you the other thing." He stood in front of her. "My steward is going to be named tomorrow. You will produce the note, and Maro will read it out, and the panel will send for Faustus, and Faustus will be arrested, and he will be examined. He is a freedman and he can be examined, and he will be examined, because a freedman who is accused of murdering a consular is not protected by anything at all."

"Yes."

"And when he is examined," Corvus said, "he will say that he did it for me."

Antonia looked up at him.

"Will he be lying?"

"Yes." Corvus's voice was quiet. "He will be lying, and he will believe it, and there is nothing I can do about it, because in twenty years I never once told him not to." He sat down on the bench, at the other end, with his hands on his knees. "I have spent the whole evening thinking about what I will do tomorrow, and I have found that there is only one thing that will save my family, and it is a thing that will destroy you, and I have come here to tell you to your face that I am going to do it."

"What is it?"

"The letter," Corvus said.

Antonia did not move.

"The letter I wrote you seventeen years ago, which Faustus took from your steward at the shrine of the crossroads. It is in my study. It says: *take the purse and go, say nothing, and you will never see my face again; say one word and I will see you dead.*" He looked at her. "Tomorrow, when you produce your note, I will produce my letter. I will read it out in the basilica, and I will say that it was written to a slave who had stolen from my father's house and who was sent away with a purse for the sake of the family's name, and that she has been silent for seventeen years because she was paid to be silent. And every man in that room will believe it, because it is in my hand and because I am the consul-designate and because you are a brothel-keeper, and the note about the steward will become a thing that a guilty woman produced to save herself."

Antonia looked at him for a long time.

"You will destroy the only evidence that might have saved you," she said, "to destroy me."

"Yes."

"And your steward will still be arrested."

"Faustus will be arrested and he will be convicted and he will be sent to the mines or crucified, and the city will be satisfied that the poisoner has been found, and the woman who kept the house will be acquitted of the murder and convicted of the rest, and the case will be closed." Corvus's voice was very flat. "And I will be consul in the spring, and my son will marry, and my daughter will marry, and my mother will die in her bed, and the name of the Claudii Corvi will survive. That is what I have decided. I have spent the whole night deciding it and I have not slept, and I am not proud of it, and I am going to do it."

Antonia stood up.

"Then I will tell you what I am going to do," she said. "Tomorrow, when you read out your letter, I will tell them what happened in the shrine."

"You have no proof."

"I have the stylus," Antonia said.

Corvus's head came up.

"You have no stylus. You never had it; I took it from the altar."

"I have a stylus," Antonia said. "It is bronze, and it is worn smooth at the end, and it is the one you gave me when I was seventeen, in the little room by the stairs, when you taught me to write my name. It is in a box in my strongbox, and tomorrow, when you have finished reading your letter, I will hold it up in that basilica and I will say: *this is the pen that killed your father, consul-designate, and you have the other one in a box in your study.*" She smiled at him. "It is not proof. It is a thing. And a thing held up in a basilica in front of six hundred men is worth more than proof, and you know it, because you have spent your whole life in rooms where things were held up."

Corvus stared at her.

"It will destroy us both," he said.

"Yes," said Antonia. "I told you that in the shrine. You did not believe me."

He stood up. He went to the door and stood with his hand on it, and the lamplight was on the side of his face, and he looked, for a moment, like the boy she had known in the little room by the stairs.

"I did not want any of this," he said.

"I know."

"I wanted to be a good man."

"You wanted to be thought a good man," Antonia said. "It is not the same, and the difference is the whole of your life."

He went out.

The bolt went home. The lamp burned on the bench, and Antonia sat in the cell under the basilica and listened to the man singing through the wall, and after a while she found that she was crying, and she did not know whether it was for herself or for the boy in the little room by the stairs or for the girl who had been sold at fourteen and had believed, for a little while, that a man with a kind face would open a door.
