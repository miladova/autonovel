# Chapter Fifteen

## Antonia

They took Calybe at the tenth hour, in the kitchen, with her hands in the flour.

There were six of them: four of the urban cohorts and a clerk and Maro himself, who stood in the doorway with his hands behind his back and watched while they put the irons on her wrists. Calybe did not scream. She looked at Antonia, who had come running at the noise, and she said, "Domina, the bread," and Antonia said, "I will take the bread out," and Calybe nodded, and they took her away.

The bread burned.

Antonia stood in the kitchen and watched it burn, because she had said she would take it out and she had forgotten, and the smell of it filled the house and got into the curtains and stayed there for a week.

She went to the prefecture at the second hour and was kept waiting until the sixth.

The room was the same room with the cut-away frieze, and Pedo received her alone, without a clerk, which was a courtesy and also a warning.

"You have come about the cook," he said.

"I have come about the cook."

"She is being examined. It is lawful. She is a slave and she is the person who had charge of the food and wine in the house on the night in question, and the law requires that her evidence be taken under examination, because a slave's word is worth nothing otherwise. You know this. You have known it since you were a child."

"I know it." Antonia stood in front of the table with her hands at her sides. "I know that a woman who has been on the hook for an hour will say anything that the man with the hook wants her to say, and that what she says will be written down and read out in the basilica and believed by six hundred men who have never seen a woman on a hook. I know that this is not justice. I know that it is the law. I am not here to argue the law."

"Then why are you here?"

"To ask you what you want."

Pedo looked at her for a long moment.

"I want the case finished," he said. "I want the emperor to come back from the east in the spring and find the city quiet. I want the Senate to stop talking about a dead consular in a brothel. I want to retire in four years to a house in Campania with a garden and a library, and I want to die in my bed, and I want no one to say at my funeral that I was the prefect who let the city be made a fool of." He sat down. "That is what I want. It is not a noble list. I am sixty years old and I have stopped pretending."

"Then take the case away from Maro."

"I cannot."

"You can. You are the prefect."

"Maro has the ear of the emperor's secretary," Pedo said. "He has had it for nine years. He is a hard man and a stupid one and he believes what he says, which is the most dangerous kind, and if I take the case away from him he will write a letter to Antioch, and the letter will say that the prefect of the city is protecting a brothel-keeper, and I will spend the last four years of my life explaining myself to a man who is a thousand miles away and does not read carefully." He folded his hands. "I am sorry. I am not your enemy. I am a man in a bad position, and I am telling you the truth because you are the only person in this city who has ever told it to me."

Antonia stood very still.

"Then I will ask you for one thing," she said. "The girl. Leda."

"She is a witness."

"She is a witness and she is in danger. The man who paid for the poison is the son of the dead man and he is at liberty, and the man who carried it is dead, and the only living witness to either is a girl of twenty-one who has already told her story to your clerk." She stepped closer. "Put her in the custody of the prefecture. Not in a cell; in a house, with a guard, under your seal. If she is in your custody, no one can touch her, and she will be produced at the trial and she will say what she saw, and the case will be decided on the evidence instead of on the hook."

Pedo looked at her with something that might have been respect.

"You are a remarkable woman," he said.

"I am a woman who has run out of options."

"I will consider it."

"Consider it today."

"I will consider it today," he said. "Now go home, and do not come here again until the Ides, and if you send me another letter like the one you sent me on the Kalends I will have you arrested for insolence." He stood. "And domina—I read the letter. Twice. I have kept it. If the case goes the way Maro wants it to go, it will not help you, and if it goes the other way it will not help you either. But I have kept it, and I do not keep many letters."

She went out through the prefecture and down the steps into the Forum, and the sun was hot and the dust was thick and the city went on around her.

She did not sleep that night either.

She lay on the bed in the little room above the fuller's and listened to the city and thought about the ledger.

It had always been the ledger. That was the thing she understood at three o'clock in the morning in a rented room with no house: that she had spent twenty-six years keeping accounts, and that she had believed, in a way she had never said out loud, that if the accounts were kept properly the world would be fair. A woman borrows and repays. A woman buys a girl and teaches her to sing and does not sell her to men. A woman pays her debts and her people and her taxes, and in the end the accounts balance, and the woman is safe.

The accounts had never balanced. They had simply been kept by her, in her hand, in her own favour, and the world had gone on doing exactly what it liked.

*Very well*, she thought. *Then I will not keep accounts any more. I will do the thing that is in front of me and I will do it as well as I can.*

The confession was read to her three days later by a clerk in the portico of the basilica, because she was entitled to know the evidence against her, and because Maro wanted her to know it.

It was four lines long.

*Calybe, slave of Antonia Cassia, cook, questioned by the hook, says: that her mistress gave her a packet of white powder on the morning of the banquet and told her to put it in the cup of the consular Vettius, and that she did not wish to, and that her mistress said that a slave who refuses her master's orders is a slave who is sold to the mines. She says that she put the powder in the cup in the passage behind the dining room before the second course, and that a man of the house saw her and said nothing. She says that she is sorry, and that she did it out of fear, and that her mistress is a hard woman and a bad one.*

Antonia read it twice.

"Where is she now?"

"In the cells," said the clerk.

"Is she alive?"

The clerk looked at his tablets. "She was alive this morning."

Antonia folded the paper and gave it back to him.

"Tell Maro," she said, "that a confession obtained on the hook is worth precisely what the hook is worth, and that in the reign of Tiberius a man confessed under torture to having killed his own father, and then, when he was released and tried to go home, his father came to meet him at the gate." She turned away. "And tell him that I will see him on the Ides."

She went to see Calybe.

It cost her thirty sesterces and a promise to the gaoler's clerk, and it took two days to arrange, and in the end they let her in at the second hour of the night through a door at the back of the building, with a lamp, and up a flight of steps and along a corridor that smelled of the same thing the corridors had smelled of when Leda came down them.

Calybe was in a cell at the end.

She was sitting on a stone bench against the wall with a blanket over her shoulders and her hands in her lap, and her hands were wrapped in cloth. She looked up when the lamp came in, and for a moment her face was the face of the cook in the kitchen at the fourth hour of the morning, and then it was not.

"Domina."

"Calybe."

"You should not be here."

"I know."

"They will say you came to silence me." Calybe's voice was slow and careful, the way a person speaks who is choosing each word. "They will say you brought money. They will say—" she stopped. "I have said so many things in this room. I do not know any more which of them were true when I said them."

Antonia sat down on the bench beside her.

"Tell me what they did."

Calybe looked at the wall.

"They put me on the hook," she said. "Not the first day. The first day they asked me questions and I said I did not know and they wrote it down. The second day they asked the same questions and I said I did not know. The third day they put me on the hook and asked them again." She was quiet for a moment. "It is not like being hurt. I thought it would be like being hurt. It is like being in a place where there is nothing except the question. There is no floor and no ceiling and no yesterday. There is only the question and the man's voice asking it, and after a while you find that you will say anything that makes the voice stop, and the voice knows that, and it does not stop, and so you say more, and more, and in the end you say the thing that makes it stop."

Antonia sat with her hands in her lap.

"What did you say?"

"I said that you gave me a packet." Calybe's face worked. "I said it because he wanted it. And then he wrote it down and read it back to me and I said it was true, and it was not true, domina, it was not true, you never gave me anything, you never gave me anything in eleven years but a kitchen and a bad temper and a place to sleep—" Her voice broke. "And then I said it was not true and he wrote that down as well, and then he asked me again, and I said it was true."

"I know."

"They will read out the first one in the basilica."

"They will read out both," Antonia said, "and Maro will say that the second one proves you are unreliable, and the panel will believe whichever one is convenient. I know how it works. I have been on the other side of it for seventeen years." She took Calybe's wrapped hands. "You will not be tried. A slave cannot be tried for a crime committed at the order of her mistress. You will be held as a witness and then you will be returned to the household or sold with it."

"I know what sold with it means."

"You will not be sold. I have made an arrangement with a man who will keep the women, and there is a paper, and it is sealed, and you will be free." Antonia said it quietly. "I have given away the house for it. It is done. You will be free, and you will have wages, and there will be a kitchen, and it will not be my kitchen."

Calybe stared at her.

"You gave away the house."

"Yes."

"For me?"

"For all of you. There are eleven of you. Tiro counted this morning."

Calybe began to cry. She cried the way a big woman cries, without any grace, with her whole body, and Antonia sat on the stone bench beside her in the dark and held her hand through the cloth and did not say anything.

After a while Calybe stopped and wiped her face with the corner of the blanket.

"Domina," she said. "There is a thing I have to tell you and I have been sick about it for four days."

"What?"

"The morning of the banquet. I went out into the passage at the second hour to get the garum, and there was a man there." She stopped. "Not in the passage. In the little room where the cups are kept. He had his back to me and he was doing something at the shelf, and when he heard me he turned round and smiled and said, 'The wine is over there, mother,' and I said, 'I know where the wine is,' and I took the garum and I went back into the kitchen."

Antonia was very still.

"What did he look like?"

"Young. Thirty perhaps. Thin face. He had a cup in his hand." Calybe's hands were shaking. "He had a mark on his wrist, on the inside. A burn, in the shape of an eight. I saw it when he put his hand on the shelf."

The lamp guttered.

"That is the man," Antonia said.

"I know it is the man. I have known it for four days. I have been sitting in this room knowing it, and they came in and asked me whether my mistress gave me a packet, and I said no, and they put me on the hook, and it never once occurred to me to say: *there was a man in the little room with a cup and a mark on his wrist.*" Calybe began to cry again. "Why did they not ask me that? Why did they ask me the one question I could not answer and not the one I could?"

Antonia sat in the dark with the smell of the corridor in her nose.

"Because they did not want that answer," she said.

She stood up.

"Domina—"

"Listen to me." Antonia crouched down in front of her. "When they bring you into the basilica, and they read out the first confession, you will say: *that is not true, I said it on the hook, and I have told the domina what I saw.* And then you will say what you have just told me, and you will say it loudly, and you will describe the man and the mark and the little room and the cup, and you will not stop for Maro and you will not stop for the prefect and you will not stop for the lictors." She gripped her hands. "Can you do that?"

Calybe looked at her.

"I can try," she said.

"Then that is all," Antonia said. "That is all anyone can do."

She went out through the corridor and down the steps and into the street, and the gaoler's clerk took his thirty sesterces and shut the door, and she stood in the dark alley behind the prefecture with her hand against the wall.

*Two,* she thought. *The girl, and the cook.*

She went to the baths.

The baths of Trajan at the fourth hour were the busiest place in Rome, and Antonia had not been in them in eleven years, because a woman of her condition did not go to the public baths; she had a bath in her own house, with a slave to pour the water and a bronze basin and a door that locked. She went now in a plain stola with her hair covered and a slave woman behind her and her eyes on the ground, and she went into the women's side and sat in the warm room among the wives of shopkeepers and the daughters of clerks, and she waited.

Eutychus came at the fifth hour, into the colonnade outside the cold pool, where the men walked after their exercise.

He was in a towel and sandals, and he was grey and thin and he looked, without his tunic, like an old soldier who had been left out in the rain. He sat down on the bench beside her with a grunt.

"You came," he said.

"You said you would come if I sent word."

"I said I would come if you sent word and if it was not a trap. I have had two men watching the door since the fourth hour." He looked at her. "You are in the women's colonnade."

"I am aware."

"There will be talk."

"There has been talk about me for seventeen years," Antonia said. "I have found that it is not the talk that kills you; it is the silence afterward."

Eutychus nodded slowly.

"The cook has confessed," he said.

"Yes."

"Under the hook."

"Yes."

"Then you are finished," Eutychus said, "unless the panel decides that the confession is worthless, which it may, because Pedo is cautious and because a confession from a cook is a cheap thing and every man on that panel has owned slaves and knows what a slave will say on a hook. That is your only hope. It is a thin one."

"I did not come to talk about hope."

"Then what did you come to talk about?"

Antonia took the papyrus out of her sleeve and put it on the bench between them.

Eutychus looked at it. He did not touch it.

"What is it?"

"A note from the steward of the house of the Claudii Corvi to a slave of that household, telling him the order of the deaths. The slave is dead. He was killed by my steward in an alley by the Emporium, and the note was on the body, and it says: *the old man first.*"

Eutychus picked it up.

He read it twice, and then he turned it over and looked at the back, and then he put it down on his knee and looked out at the pool, where two young men were racing each other from end to end, and the water was going everywhere, and a slave was shouting at them.

"Where is the steward now?"

"At the house on the Palatine, serving his master, who is to be consul in the spring."

"And what do you want me to do with this?"

"I want you to put it in front of the panel."

Eutychus laughed. It was a short, unhappy sound.

"You want me to destroy a consul-designate of Rome," he said, "in the third month before he takes office, on the word of a brothel-keeper and a scrap of papyrus. Do you know what happened to the last man who did a thing like that? He was a friend of mine. He was a secretary of the grain account, and he made an accusation against a man with a great name, and the man with the great name was acquitted, and my friend was found in his bath three months later with his wrists open and a note beside him saying that he could not live with the shame." He turned the papyrus over. "That note was in my friend's own hand. I saw it. It was a very good forgery."

"Then you will not do it."

"I did not say that." Eutychus was quiet for a moment. "I said that it is dangerous. But I am fifty-five years old, and I am the second most powerful man in the accounts of the empire, and I have spent my life being useful to men who despise me, and there is a particular pleasure, at my age, in doing a thing that is dangerous to somebody who deserves it." He folded the papyrus and put it in his towel. "I will put it in front of the panel. Not in my own hand; I have a man in the prefect's clerks, and the papyrus will be found in the file on the morning of the trial, having been sent in anonymously by a citizen who wishes to remain unnamed. Maro will not be able to ignore it, because it will be in the file, and Pedo will not be able to ignore it, because Maro will read it out." He looked at her. "And what do I get?"

"What do you want?"

"The house," Eutychus said.

Antonia had known. She had known since the figs.

"The house and the ground under it," he said, "and the loan discharged, and the two shops in the Argiletum, and the furniture, and the fountain. The women are yours; I do not want the women, and I would not know what to do with them. I want the building. I have a plan for it."

"What plan?"

"A bath," Eutychus said. "A small one. For the clerks of the annona and their wives. There is no decent bath between the Velabrum and the river and the men who work for me have to walk to the Esquiline." He said it without embarrassment. "I am a practical man. I have been practical all my life, and it has made me rich, and it has not made me happy, and I have found that the two are not as connected as I was led to believe."

Antonia looked at the pool. The two young men had finished their race and were hanging on the edge, laughing, and one of them had a cut on his forehead from the tiles.

"Draw up the papers," she said.

"You agree?"

"I agree."

"You are giving up everything you have built."

"I am giving up a building," Antonia said. "I have been in this city long enough to know the difference. A house is a thing that can be bought; a woman who has been hanged cannot be bought back." She stood up. "But I want one thing in return, and it is not in the papers."

"Name it."

"When this is over, whether I am acquitted or convicted or exiled, you will not touch the women. Not one of them. Not Meroe, not Prisca, not Junilla, not the girls in the kitchen. Their contracts are to be given to them, free, every one, and if any of them wishes to stay in the building and work in your bath, she stays as a free woman with wages." Antonia looked down at him. "Put it in the papers. In your own hand, sealed, with witnesses."

Eutychus looked up at her.

"You are a strange woman," he said.

"I am a woman who has been a slave," Antonia said. "It is not the same as being strange."

She went home.

Leda was in the courtyard with her lyre on her knees and her hands not moving, and she looked up when Antonia came in, and her face was the face of a girl who has been waiting three days to be told something.

"Sit down," Antonia said.

Leda sat.

"The trial is on the Ides. The cook has confessed under torture and the confession will be read out and believed by some of them." Antonia sat down on the bench opposite her. "I am going to tell you what will happen, and then I am going to ask you to do something, and you will decide, and I will not overrule you."

"All right."

"On the Ides I will be tried for the murder of a consular. The panel will hear the cook, and the physician, and the prefect's men, and then it will hear you. You are the only witness who saw a man in the passage. If you tell the truth, there is a chance, a small one, that the case will collapse and that the mark on the wrist and the papyrus will lead to the steward and to the son. If you are not there, the case will not collapse, and I will be convicted, and I will be executed or exiled, and the house will be seized, and every woman in it will be sold." She paused. "And if you are there, you will be in the room with the man who has already taken you to his house and offered you a bargain, and who has a son who loves you, and who has spent seventeen years learning how to make people do what he wants without ever raising his voice."

Leda's hands were still on the lyre.

"You want me to go," she said.

"I want you to decide." Antonia leaned forward. "There is a boat at Ostia. There is a boy who loves you and a road out of this city and a mother in Surrentum who has been looking for you for ten years. If you go tonight, you will be gone before the Ides, and I will lose, and I will not blame you, and I will not send anyone after you."

"And if I stay?"

"If you stay, you will be in a basilica in front of six hundred men, and you will have to say out loud what was done to you when you were eleven and what was done to you when you were fifteen, and there will be men in that room who will enjoy it." Antonia's voice was quiet. "I have done that. I have stood in a room and said out loud what was done to me, and I know what it costs, and I will not ask it of you."

Leda looked at her for a long time.

"You bought me," she said.

"I bought you."

"And you told me that no man would touch me until I chose."

"I did."

"And then men touched me anyway, in the street, at the door, at the dinners, and you did not stop them, because you could not stop them without losing the dinner." Leda's voice was steady. "And you told me that I was not a slave and that I would be free one day, and you kept my contract in a box, and you never once showed it to me. And I have spent six years loving you and being afraid of you and wanting you to look at me the way you looked at the fountain when it was clean."

"I know."

"Why did you buy me?"

Antonia was silent for a moment.

"Because you sang badly," she said, "and you were frightened, and you looked at me as though I were a door that might open. And because I had been sold at fourteen and nobody had opened a door for me." She looked at her hands. "And because I am a woman who has spent her life buying things, and I saw a thing that was worth more than eleven thousand sesterces, and I bought it, and I have been paying for it ever since."

Leda set the lyre down.

"I will stay," she said.

"Leda—"

"I will stay and I will tell them what I saw, and I will not be a frightened girl who is not sure, and I will not let him make me into a story." She stood up. "But not for you. I am not doing it for you. I am doing it because there was a boy in this house who was twelve years old and who was kind to me, and because I have a mother in Surrentum who has been petitioning governors for ten years, and because if I run away tonight I will spend the rest of my life being a woman who ran." She picked up the lyre. "And because I want to see his face when I say the mark."

Antonia looked at her.

"Then I will tell you one thing," she said, "and then we will not speak of it again. If the case goes against me, you will leave this city. Not with the boy; he is a Claudius and he will be married in the autumn and he will not defy his father in the end, whatever he says in the dark by a river. You will leave with the paper I give you, and you will go to Surrentum, and you will not look back."

"Antonia—"

"Say it."

"I will go to Surrentum," Leda said, "if it goes against you."

"Good."

They sat in the courtyard until the light went. The fountain ran. The bronze myrtle dripped, and above the roof the sky turned from white to gold to the colour of a bruise, and the noise of the city came over the wall as it always did, and neither of them said anything at all.

At the second hour of the night Leda went up to her room and took out the shell and the paper and put them in a bag, and then she sat on the bed with the bag in her lap.

She did not go to the Nones.

She had promised, and she did not go, and she sat on the bed all night listening to the house, and at the fourth hour she heard the sound of a horse in the street and then nothing, and at the fifth hour the sky began to lighten.

Three streets away, at the foot of the Viminal, a young man stood in a doorway with his hood up and waited for a girl who did not come.
