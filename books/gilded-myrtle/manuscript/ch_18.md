# Chapter Eighteen

## Corvus

He woke before light, and for a moment he did not know where he was.

The house was quiet in a way that it had never been quiet. There were no slaves in the corridor and no smell of bread from the kitchen; the cook had gone nine days before and the boy who had taken her place made a fire at the sixth hour and burned the porridge. The atrium, when he went down, was full of grey light and dust, and the impluvium had a skin on it, and there was a dead moth floating in the middle of the skin.

The boy who carried the water was asleep on the bench by the door with a blanket over his head.

Corvus stood over him for a moment.

He was eleven years old and he had been left behind when the others went, because nobody had told him to go, and he had been sleeping on that bench for nine days and eating whatever the boy in the kitchen gave him.

"Boy."

The boy woke up and scrambled to his feet.

"Dominus."

"Go to the kitchen and get something to eat and then go home to your mother."

"I have no mother, dominus."

"Then go to whoever you have."

The boy looked at him.

"I have a sister," he said. "In the Subura. She is married to a cooper."

"Then go to her, and tell her that you have been discharged from this house with your peculium, and that you are to say nothing about anything you have seen in it." Corvus took a coin from his purse and gave it to him. "And take this and do not tell her where it came from."

The boy took the coin and looked at it and then at him, and his face was the face of a child who has been in a great house long enough to know that a gift from a master is not always a gift.

"Dominus," he said. "Is the house going to be sold?"

"Yes."

"Will the new man want a water boy?"

"I don't know."

"Because I am good at it," the boy said. "I get up early and I do not spill."

Corvus looked at him.

"I will write you a letter," he said.

He went into the tablinum and wrote the boy a letter of recommendation, in his best hand, on the good paper, and sealed it, and gave it to him, and the boy went out through the door with the letter in his hand and did not look back.

It was the last official act of his life as a man of affairs, and it was for a water boy whose name he did not know.

His mother was in the atrium when he came back out.

She was dressed for the Forum, in a dark stola with her hair bound, and she had the box in her hands.

"You are going," she said.

"I am going."

"You will sit in the body of the court and say nothing."

"I will sit in the body of the court and I will say nothing unless I am asked."

"Gaius." She came toward him. "It is not too late. The letter is in your sleeve. Burn it in the brazier and go to the prefect this morning and tell him that you have nothing to add and that the panel should finish the case. The girl will give her evidence, and the cook has confessed, and the woman will be convicted, and it will be over by the Ides. You will not be consul, but you will not be—" she stopped.

"I will not be what?"

"You will not be the man who stood up in a basilica and destroyed a woman to save himself."

Corvus looked at her.

"That is exactly what I will be," he said, "whether I stand up or not. The only difference is whether the city knows it."

He went out.

The walk down the Palatine to the Forum took a quarter of an hour and he did it on foot with no lictor and no slave, and the streets were full of people going the same way. Nobody spoke to him. A man selling water from a cart looked at him and then looked at the ground.

He came into the Forum at the second hour and stood at the foot of the steps of the Basilica Julia.

The crowd was already there: the whole square, the steps of the temple of Castor, the roof of the portico of Gaius and Lucius, the windows of the shops along the Argiletum. A man was calling out the odds. Somewhere a woman was selling pies out of a basket, and her voice went up over the noise, flat and cheerful, *hot, hot, two for an as,* and it was the most obscene thing he had ever heard.

He went up the steps.

At the door he stopped and took the letter out of his sleeve.

It was folded twice and sealed with his own seal, and it had been written seventeen years before in the room at the top of the house, at his father's table, with a pen that was not his father's. He had read it perhaps six times in seventeen years. He knew it by heart.

*Take the purse and go. Say nothing, and you will never see my face again. Say one word, and I will see you dead.*

He turned it over in his hands.

There was a brazier by the door, for the clerks, and it was burning.

He stood in front of it for a long moment with the letter in his hand, and the crowd went on shouting behind him, and a lictor pushed past him and said, "Dominus, you cannot stand in the doorway."

Corvus put the letter back in his sleeve.

The hall filled in front of him.

They came in by the doors at the west end and stood in the aisles and along the walls and in the galleries, and by the second hour there were more people in the Basilica Julia than Corvus had ever seen under one roof, and he had been in the Circus Maximus on the day of the games.

He picked out faces. Blandus, in the second row of the senatorial benches, with his toga crooked and his eyes red. Hostilius, at the back, sweating. Eutychus, standing in the aisle with two of his clerks and a tablet, looking like a man at an auction. Vipsania Marcella in the women's gallery with a fan, and her husband beside her, and neither of them looking at anything.

And at the very back, by the door, in a plain tunic with his hood down and his arms folded, Marcus.

Corvus looked at his son for a long moment.

*He came*, he thought. *He came, and he is standing at the back where they can see him, and when the girl comes in he will look at her, and six hundred men will see a Claudius stand behind a singer.*

He turned his head and looked at the platform, at Pedo's empty chair, at the cut-away frieze, at the mosaic of the hunting scene with most of the animals missing.

The letter was against his ribs, and it weighed nothing at all, and he had never in his life carried anything so heavy.

He thought about the fact that in an hour he would either read it out or he would not, and that whichever he did, there would be no third thing.

He thought about his father, who had made every decision of his life in a room with a door, and had never once been in a basilica in his life without knowing exactly what he was going to say.

He went in and sat in the third row of the senatorial benches with the letter against his ribs, and he waited for the second hour.

The basilica was fuller than it had been the day before. There were men standing in the aisles and along the walls and in the galleries, and the women in the upper gallery had brought cushions and food, and there was a holiday air in the hall that turned his stomach. A man behind him was eating nuts. Somewhere on the far side a child was crying, and a woman was telling it to be quiet, and the child would not be quiet.

He had not slept.

He had lain on his bed in the room at the top of the house with the letter on the table beside him, and had listened to the city, and at some hour before light he had got up and gone to the window and looked out at the black shape of the Palatine and the temple of Apollo and the lights of the city below, and had thought about the fact that in nineteen years he had never once been unable to decide a thing by the morning.

At dawn his mother came to the door with a cup of water and did not speak, and he took it and drank it and gave it back, and she went away.

Marcus was in the atrium when he came down.

He had been let out of his room that morning, because there was no longer any point in keeping him in it, and he was standing by the impluvium in a clean tunic with his hands at his sides, and he was thin and there was a fading bruise on his cheekbone, and he looked at his father the way a man looks at a stranger across a room.

"I am coming to the basilica," he said.

"No."

"I am coming to the basilica," Marcus said, "and I am going to stand in the crowd where six hundred men can see me, and when the girl is brought in to give her evidence I am going to look at her, and I am going to let every man in that room see that the son of the house believes her." He did not raise his voice. "Tiro told me to do it. He said it was the only thing I could do that would help and the only thing you could not prevent."

Corvus looked at his son.

"Tiro is a murderer and a thief," he said.

"Yes," said Marcus. "He is also the only man in this house who has ever told me the truth."

They walked down the hill together, in silence, with two of the household behind them and no lictor, and the crowd in the Forum made way for them, and Corvus heard the whispering start and go along the front of the basilica like wind through wheat.

At the door Marcus stopped.

"I will sit at the back," he said.

"Marcus."

His son turned.

"Whatever happens today," Corvus said, "I want you to know that I have loved you since the hour you were born, and that I have never been able to say it, and that I am saying it now because there may not be another time."

Marcus looked at him.

"That is the first true thing you have said to me in twenty years," he said, "and it is too late, and I am sorry for both of us."

He went in.

At the second hour they brought in the girl.

She came up the aisle between two clerks, and she had been washed and dressed in a good grey stola and her hair was bound up, and she walked like a woman going to a wedding she did not want. She did not look at Corvus. She did not look at anyone. She stood on the mark and put her hands in front of her.

"The witness Leda," said Pedo. "The panel has before it the petition of Vibia Prima of Surrentum, the record of the dealer Nymphodorus of Puteoli, and the testimony of the household. The panel finds that the witness is free-born and that she is not subject to examination. She will give her evidence as a free woman."

There was a stir in the hall, and then a silence, and Corvus found that he had been holding his breath.

"Tell us what you saw," said Pedo.

She told it.

She told it in the same words she had used in the study and before the panel, and she told it slowly, so that every man in the hall could hear it, and when she came to the end she stopped and stood with her hands in front of her.

Maro rose.

"You say you saw a man in the passage with a cup. You did not see his face."

"I did not."

"You did not see his face, and you did not see the powder, and you did not see him put anything into the cup, and you cannot say that the cup he carried was the cup that the dead man drank from."

"No."

"Then what did you see?"

"I saw a man in a passage with a cup," Leda said, "and the next morning the old man was dead, and I have thought about it every night since."

"That is not evidence. That is a girl's nightmare."

"It is what I saw."

Maro turned to the panel with his hands spread. "Fathers, this is the whole of the case for the woman's innocence. A girl who cannot describe a face, a man who may have been a servant carrying a cup to a guest, and a mark on a wrist that she remembered a month after the event and only when she had been in the house of the consul-designate for a day."

Corvus's hands closed on his knees.

"There was a mark," Leda said.

"There was a mark which you did not mention to the prefect's clerk, or to the panel, or to anyone, until you had spent a day in the house of Gaius Claudius Corvus, who is sitting in the third row and whose steward has a house mark in the shape of an eight on the door of his house." Maro's voice was loud and reasonable. "Fathers, I do not say that the girl is lying. I say that she has been in a great house, and that she has been told a story, and that she has come to believe it. It is a thing that happens to witnesses. It is the reason the law does not accept a slave's evidence without examination, and it is the reason I asked that this girl be examined, and the prefect in his mercy has declined."

"I was not told a story," Leda said. "I saw the mark."

"Where?"

"On his wrist. On the inside, when he lifted his hand to the curtain."

"Which wrist?"

"The left."

"Which hand held the cup?"

The girl was silent.

"Which hand held the cup?" Maro said again, and his voice was almost gentle. "You have thought about it every night for a month. You have seen it in your sleep. Which hand held the cup?"

"The right," Leda said.

"Then he lifted the cup with his right hand and he pushed the curtain with his left, and the mark was on the inside of the left wrist." Maro turned to the panel. "It is possible. It is entirely possible. But it is also possible that a girl who has been coached has been coached a little too well, and has been given a story with a mark in it, and has not been given the whole of the story, and I would ask the panel to consider which is more likely."

Pedo looked at him for a moment.

"Does the panel have any other evidence of the mark?"

"I have," said Antonia.

She was standing at the mark on the floor with her hands at her sides, and she had not been invited to speak, and the whole hall turned.

"Domina," said Pedo. "You will speak when you are asked."

"I will speak when I am asked," said Antonia, "and I will ask the panel to ask me now, because I have a note in my sleeve which names the man with the mark, and because if it is not read out in this hall today it will be read out in a closed room in a month and the city will say that the panel had something to hide."

Pedo sat back.

"Produce the note."

Antonia produced it.

The clerk took it and carried it up to the platform, and Pedo read it, and his face did not change, and then he handed it to the man beside him, and then he said, "Read it out."

The clerk read it in the flat voice of a man reading a list of goods.

*The old man first.*

The hall was silent.

Six hundred people and not one of them moved. Somewhere a woman in the gallery dropped her fan, and the sound of it on the stone went through the whole building like a shot.

"Where did you get this?" Pedo asked.

"My steward took it from the body of a man called Callistus, who was a slave of the household of Gaius Claudius Corvus, and who carried the cup in my house, and who was killed in an alley by the Emporium nine days after the banquet." Antonia's voice carried to the back of the hall. "The man who wrote it is the steward of that household. His name is Faustus. He is in this basilica today, standing at the back, and he has been in this basilica every day, and he has a burn in the shape of an eight on the inside of his left wrist, and if the panel sends for him now he will not be able to hide it."

There was a moment of absolute quiet.

Then the hall erupted.

It took four lictors to clear a space, and it took Pedo standing up and shouting, and it took Maro hammering on the table with his fist. Corvus sat in the third row and watched his own steward being brought up the aisle between two guards.

Faustus was very pale. He walked with his head up, and when he came to the mark on the floor he stopped and stood, and he did not look at Corvus at all.

"Your name," said Pedo.

"Faustus. Freedman of Gaius Claudius Corvus."

"Hold out your arms."

Faustus held out his arms, and a guard pushed back the sleeve of his tunic, and there on the inside of the left wrist was a burn in the shape of an eight, old and white and unmistakable.

The hall made a sound like a wave breaking.

"Faustus," said Pedo. "You are accused of the murder of Gaius Vettius Proculus. You are a freedman and you may be examined. Will you answer the panel, or will you be examined?"

Faustus looked at the panel. Then he looked at Corvus, and his face did something that Corvus would see for the rest of his life: it was not hatred, and it was not fear, and it was not reproach. It was the face of a man who has been waiting for a thing for twenty years and has finally been allowed to stop waiting.

"I will answer," he said.

"Did you write this note?"

"I did."

"Did you buy poison from a woman in the Subura?"

"I did."

"Did you give it to the man Callistus?"

"I did."

"Did you tell him to put it in the cup of Gaius Vettius?"

"I did."

"Why?"

Faustus was silent for a moment.

"Because I was paid," he said.

"By whom?"

"By Publius Vettius Proculus, the son of the dead man."

The noise in the basilica was enormous.

Corvus sat very still. He was aware of men turning to look at him; he was aware of the third row of the senatorial benches, and of the faces on either side, and of the fact that his hands were flat on his knees and that he had not moved.

"Publius Vettius is in this building," said Pedo. "Bring him."

They found him in the portico, trying to leave.

He came up the aisle between four guards with his cloak torn and his hair in his eyes, and he did not fight. He looked, Corvus thought, relieved. He looked like a man who had been carrying something heavy for a year and had finally been allowed to put it down.

"Publius Vettius Proculus," said Pedo. "You are accused of the murder of your father."

"I did it," said Publius.

The hall went mad.

It took a long time to restore order. Men were standing on the benches. The women in the gallery were leaning over the rail. Somewhere at the back a man was shouting a name over and over, and it was not clear whose. Pedo stood up and shouted and the lictors pushed into the crowd, and by the time the noise had died down Publius Vettius was standing at the mark on the floor with his hands hanging at his sides and his face wet.

"Why?" Pedo asked.

"He was going to change the will," Publius said. "He had a freedwoman at the house, a woman who had been his nurse when he was a boy, and she had a son, and he was going to leave her sixty thousand sesterces and the farm at Signia, and I was going to have the house and the debts." He wiped his face with his sleeve. "I did not want him dead. I wanted him to stop. I went to him in the spring and I asked him for the money and he said no, and he said that he had been thinking about the will, and he said that a man of his age should put his affairs in order, and I knew what that meant." He looked at the panel. "I did not want him dead. I wanted him to stop."

"You had him killed."

"Yes."

"You hired a steward of the house of the Claudii Corvi to put poison in his cup at a dinner table."

"Yes."

"Did Gaius Claudius Corvus know?"

The hall went quiet.

Corvus stood up.

He did not decide to. He had not intended to. He stood up in the third row of the senatorial benches with the letter against his ribs and his hands at his sides, and every face in the basilica turned to him.

"Publius Vettius," he said. "Look at me."

Publius looked at him.

"Did I know?"

"No."

"Say it so that the court hears it."

"He did not know," Publius said, loudly. "He knew nothing. It was Faustus. Faustus came to me and said that his master would not be told and that the family would be protected, and I paid him, and I never spoke to Corvus about it, and he did not know."

"Why did your steward do it?" Pedo asked.

Corvus looked at Faustus.

"Because he was a fool," Faustus said, "and because I have served that house for twenty years and I have watched it go down and down into debt and disgrace, and because I thought that if the old man's son was finished the family would be saved, and because I wanted him dead for my own reasons, which are twenty years old and have nothing to do with any of you." He turned his head and looked at Corvus for the first time. "Dominus. I did it for the house. I have done everything for the house. I have been your father's servant and your servant and I have never once been thanked, and I do not want to be thanked now. I want to be believed. I did it for the house and you did not know, and that is the truth, and when they have finished with me I will go to the mines and I will die there and the house will go on."

The hall was silent.

Pedo sat down slowly.

"The court will adjourn," he said. "The accused Publius Vettius will be held. The accused Faustus will be held. The panel will consider the case against Antonia Cassia, and will hear the evidence of character, and will give its verdict at the ninth hour."

Corvus did not sit down.

He stood in the third row with the letter against his ribs and his heart going like a hammer, and he thought: *it is over. They have their murderer. The case is closed. She will be acquitted of the murder and convicted of the rest, and the house will be closed, and my family will survive.*

And then he thought: *she will tell them about the shrine.*

He looked across the hall at Antonia Cassia, standing at the mark on the floor with her hands at her sides, and she was looking at him.

She had not produced the stylus. It was in her sleeve; he could see the shape of it against the white wool. She was waiting.

Blandus rose at the ninth hour.

"The panel has asked whether any man in Rome will speak for the character of Antonia Cassia," he said. "I will."

He was drunk. He was fifty years old and his toga was crooked and his wife had left him that morning, and he stood in the middle of the basilica and spoke for a quarter of an hour about a woman who had given him back sixty thousand sesterces at a kitchen door and had not told anyone.

It did not save her and it was not meant to; it was meant to be said, and it was said, and when he sat down half the hall was quiet and the other half was ashamed.

Then Pedo asked the question that Corvus had been waiting for.

"Domina. Have you anything to say before the panel gives its verdict?"

Antonia looked at the panel.

"Yes," she said. "I have one thing to say, and I will say it once, and then I will not speak of it again as long as I live."

She took the stylus out of her sleeve and held it up.

"This is the pen that killed Lucius Claudius Corvus, consul, in the shrine of his own house on the Palatine, seventeen years ago," she said. "It went into his throat in my hand. I was twenty-three years old. I had been his son's slave since I was seventeen, and his since I was nineteen, and on the night he died he had me brought to the shrine and had me flogged until my back was open, and then he had me on the floor in front of the altar. His son came in and stood in the doorway and did not stop it. I reached up and took the pen from the writing box and I put it in his throat, and I have never been sorry."

The hall did not erupt. That was the terrible thing: it went absolutely still, six hundred people in a basilica holding their breath, and in the silence Corvus could hear a pigeon on the roof and the small dry sound of the stylus in her hand.

"The consul-designate has the other pen," Antonia said. "He took it from the altar before the slaves came in. He has kept it in a box in his study for seventeen years. He wrote me a letter that night, which I have kept, in which he told me to take a purse and go and to say nothing, and to say one word would be to die." She lowered the stylus. "I have kept it for seventeen years. I have kept it because I was afraid, and because I was twenty-three, and because I had killed a consul and did not know which way was up. And I am telling it now, in this basilica, because the man who is to be consul in the spring was prepared to destroy me today to keep it quiet, and because I have decided that I would rather be destroyed out loud."

She sat down.

Corvus stood in the third row of the senatorial benches with the letter in his sleeve, and the letter was a piece of paper that weighed nothing at all, and he found that he could not move.

Pedo's voice came from a long way off.

"The court is adjourned," he said. "The panel will sit again in the morning. The accused will be held in the cells. The prefect's office will take possession of the house on the Vicus Tuscus pending the verdict, and the household will remain in it and will not be sold, and if any man in this hall speaks to a magistrate or a clerk or a man of the cohorts before the morning, I will have him arrested for contempt of the court." He stood up. "And Gaius Claudius Corvus will attend me in my office at the second hour. Not in the morning. Now."

The lictors came for her.

Corvus watched them take her down the aisle, and she walked with her head up and the stylus in her hand, and as she passed the third row she turned her head and looked at him.

She did not smile. She did not look triumphant. She looked at him the way she had looked at him in the shrine seventeen years ago, across a room with a body on the floor: with the face of a woman doing sums.

And then she was gone, and the crowd was surging toward the doors, and the whole city was pouring out into the Forum with the story in its mouth.
