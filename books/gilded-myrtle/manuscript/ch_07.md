# Chapter Seven

## Tiro

He had been in the house seventeen years and in that time he had learned the shape of the quarter the way a sailor learns the shape of a hull: every crack in it, every place where the water came in.

He went out at the second hour with a purse at his belt and a knife in his sleeve, and he went first to the barber.

The barber's shop was in the Via Tuscus under a striped awning, and it had three chairs and a boy who swept the hair into the street, and it was the best place in the fourth region to hear a thing before it was true. Tiro sat in the corner with his back to the wall and let the barber do his chin, which did not need it, and listened.

"—and the prefect has put Maro on it, and Maro is a hard man—"

"—hard men are the ones who take money, because soft men cannot get close enough—"

"—they say the old man's face went black as a boot—"

"—they say the woman did it herself, with her own hands, out of spite—"

"—they say she has a box of names that would bring down half the Senate—"

Tiro paid the barber and went out. The last one was the one that mattered, and it had been said loudly, by a man who had been drinking at the ninth hour of the morning, and it had been said in the tone of a thing that had been repeated before. Somebody was spreading the box. Somebody wanted the box to be talked about, so that when the prefect came to look for it, he would find it.

He went to the Emporium.

The great warehouses along the Tiber were already busy: the cranes swinging, the porters running with sacks on their shoulders, the clerks at their tables under the colonnade with the smell of the river coming up between the stones. Tiro walked along the wharf to the fourth shed, where the goods from Africa came in, and asked for a man called Niger, and waited while a boy went to find him.

Niger came out wiping his hands. He was a big Numidian with a broken nose and a freedman's cap, and he had been in the same barracks as Tiro in the year of the games, though they had never fought each other, which was the foundation of a friendship that had lasted twenty-six years.

"You look bad," Niger said.

"I look the same."

"You look worse than the same." Niger jerked his head toward the back of the shed, and they stood among the sacks where no one could hear. "The Myrtle."

"The Myrtle."

"Is it true?"

"Some of it." Tiro looked out at the river. "An old man died at the table. Not of a fit. Something in his cup. The prefect's men are all over the house and the door is shut and the women are frightened and the domina has a lip like a plum."

"And you want to know who did it."

"I want to know who sold the thing that did it."

Niger was quiet for a moment.

"There is a woman," he said. "In the Subura. Off the Argiletum, behind the dyer's, up the stairs where the lamp is. Her name is Canthara. She is old and she is half blind and she sells rat poison and love philtres and something for the gout, and she will sell anything to anyone because she cannot see well enough to be afraid of them." He wiped his hands again. "She has been there thirty years. She sold to my wife, for the rats. She is not a wicked woman. She is a shop."

"Will she talk?"

"She will talk if you pay her and she will talk more if you frighten her, and the frightening will cost you less." Niger put a hand on his arm. "Tiro. If it is a great house, leave it. Go to the domina and tell her to pay the fine and close the door for a year and sell the house and go to Baiae. She has money. She can live anywhere."

"She will not go."

"No," said Niger. "They never do." He let go. "If you need men, I have men."

"I know."

"That is not a no."

"It is not a yes." Tiro turned up his collar. "If I need men I will send the boy with a message that says the fish is off."

The Subura at the third hour smelled of tanning and smoke and the kind of cooking that is done with whatever is left. Tiro went up the stairs behind the dyer's with his hand on the wall, because the steps were worn and the light was bad, and knocked on the door at the top.

"Who is it?"

"An old customer."

"I have no old customers. They are all dead."

"Then a new one."

The door opened a hand's breadth. Behind it there was a room with a curtain, a table, a great many jars on shelves, a smell of dried things and honey and something underneath that was like a butcher's yard in summer. The woman was very old and very small and her eyes were the colour of milk.

"Who sent you?"

"Niger, of the fourth shed."

"Ah." She opened the door. "Niger is a good man. He has a wife who beats him." She went back to her table and sat down. "What do you want? If it is for the rats, I have a paste that will kill a dog. If it is for a woman, I have a thing that will make her love you and a thing that will make her sorry, and the second is cheaper because it works quicker."

"It is not for a woman." Tiro sat down opposite her. "A man came to you nine days ago. Short hair, cut close. A dark tunic with a narrow stripe. He bought something."

The old woman's hands stopped moving among the jars.

"I do not remember," she said.

"You remember."

"I sell to a hundred men a month."

"You sell to women in this street and to the boys who work the docks, and once or twice a year a man in a good tunic comes up those stairs and you remember him for a year, because a man in a good tunic pays in silver and does not haggle and frightens you." He put a coin on the table. "I am not the prefect. I am not going to hang you. I want to know what he bought and what he said."

She looked at the coin without touching it.

"He bought white hellebore," she said. "Two drachms. And something else, which I will not name, because I did not sell it to him and he did not ask for it, and I am an old woman who has been in this street for thirty years and I know what a man is going to do with a thing before he does it."

"What did he say?"

"He said it was for his master's garden. For the moles." She laughed, and it turned into a cough. "Moles. In a garden in this city. There are no moles in the gardens of this city; there are slaves who cut the grass and a man with a stick who kills the snakes. Moles." She wiped her mouth. "He was not a gardener. He had the hands of a man who carries a tray."

"Did he give a name?"

"He gave a name that was not his. They all do." She leaned forward. "But he had a mark. On the inside of the wrist, here. A burn, small, round, the size of a pea, and it had been done twice, so it made a figure of eight. I saw it when he counted out the silver." She sat back. "That is a slave's mark. A house mark. I have seen it before, on a man who came to buy a thing for his mistress's rival, twenty years ago, and I remember it because it was a curious thing, a burn in the shape of an eight."

"Whose house?"

"I do not know. I am a poisoner in the Subura, not a herald." She pushed the coin back across the table. "Take it. I do not want to be paid for this. I want to be left alone, and I want it understood that I told you nothing, because if it is a great house, and they hear that old Canthara talked, I will be found in the Tiber with my throat open and no one will inquire."

Tiro took the coin and stood up.

"One more thing," he said. "If a man comes to you in the next month and asks whether anyone has been asking about the man with the mark, you will tell him no."

"Why would I do that?"

"Because if you tell him yes, he will kill you," Tiro said, "and I would rather you were alive."

He went down the stairs and out into the light.

He knew where the mark was from. He had known before she said it; he had known the moment she said *a burn in the shape of an eight*, and he had stood in the small room off the kitchen of the house of Gaius Claudius Corvus three times in the last month and seen the household slaves going about their business with their sleeves rolled up in the heat.

He walked for an hour without knowing where he was going, and found himself at the foot of the Aventine, at the shrine of the god who has no name, where the carters leave their garlands.

He had been a slave for the first thirty-five years of his life. He had been a gladiator for eleven of them. He had killed nine men in the arena, and the ninth had been a boy of nineteen with a net and a trident who had slipped on the sand, and Tiro had put his sword through the boy's throat because the crowd was screaming for it and because a gladiator who hesitates is a gladiator who is dead next season. He had been given his freedom at thirty-five by a magistrate who wanted to be seen to be merciful, and he had been given nothing else, and he had come to Rome with a knife and a scar and no name, and Antonia Cassia had found him in a doorway on the Vicus Tuscus in the rain and had said, *You look like a man who can keep a door*, and he had said, *I look like a man who can keep anything*, and she had laughed and taken him home.

Everything he had, he had because of her. That was the simple arithmetic of his life and he had never once needed to be reminded of it.

And he had a son.

The boy was fourteen. His name was Rufus, and he was in a smithy at Tibur, and he had been born to Tiro's wife, who had belonged to a Greek merchant and had died of a fever when the boy was six. The boy belonged to the smith, who had bought him as an apprentice with his mother, and the smith was a hard man but not a cruel one, and the boy was strong and had his mother's hands. Tiro had seen him four times in eight years, the last time in the spring, when he had walked to Tibur on his day off and stood in the street outside the smithy and watched his son work the bellows for an hour without going in, because he did not know what to say.

The papers would cost six hundred sesterces. He had two hundred and eleven in a jar under his bed.

He went back to the house at dusk.

She was in the counting room with the lamp lit and the tablets out, and she had been crying; he could tell by the way she held her face, which was the way a woman holds her face when she has been crying and has decided not to be seen doing it.

"Well?"

He stood in front of the table. "The woman in the Subura remembers a man. Short hair, a dark tunic with a narrow stripe, silver in his hand. He bought white hellebore and said it was for the moles." He paused. "She did not know his name. He had a mark on his wrist, a burn in the shape of an eight. That is a house mark, and there are forty households in Rome that use one, and she did not know which."

Antonia sat back.

"Forty households," she said.

"It is not a name."

"It is a start." She picked up the stylus. "I will send to Zosimus in the morning and have him inquire among the clerks at the prefecture; the clerks know the marks, they are always writing them down on runaway notices—"

"Domina."

She looked up.

"It will take a month to find a mark among forty households," Tiro said. "And when you find it, it will be the mark of a man who has already been paid and gone to ground, and the man who paid him will be a name in a great house that will swear the mark was stolen or the man was dismissed in the spring. I have been in this city thirty years. I know how these things go."

"Then what would you have me do?"

"I would have you take the fine and close the door and go to Baiae until the autumn."

"No."

"Domina—"

"No." She put the stylus down. "I have been in this city eighteen years, Tiro, and I have never once run, and I am not going to begin at forty because a man in a great house would find it convenient." She looked at him. "You have been with me seventeen years. You have never asked me for anything. Not a wage, not a favour, not a day. Why not?"

Tiro stood in the lamplight.

"I have everything I want," he said.

"That is not an answer."

"It is the only one I have."

She looked at him for a moment and then, because she was tired and because it was that hour of the evening when people say things, she said, "Do you have a family?"

He should have said no. He had said no for seventeen years, to the cook, to Prisca, to the women, to a magistrate in the prefecture who had asked him once for the record. It was not a lie exactly. A man who has a son he has seen four times in eight years does not have a family; he has a debt.

But she had asked, and she had asked in the voice she used when she was not asking as a mistress.

"A son," he said.

Antonia put down the stylus.

"Where?"

"Tibur. In the smithy by the gate. He is fourteen." Tiro heard his own voice, flat and unsurprised, as though he were reading out a manifest. "His mother died when he was six. She belonged to a Greek merchant and the merchant sold her and the boy together to a smith at Tibur, and the smith is not a cruel man, and the boy is strong and has the knack of the work, and he will be a good smith if he lives."

"And you see him."

"When I can."

"How often?"

"Four times in eight years."

She was quiet for a moment.

"Why so little?"

Tiro looked at the wall behind her, at the racks of tablets, at the lamp.

"Because the first time I went, he was six years old and he did not know me, and he held on to the smith's wife's skirt and would not come to me. And the second time he was eight and he knew me, and he stood in the yard and looked at me the way a dog looks at a man who has kicked it before. And the third time he was eleven and he asked me whether I was going to take him away, and I said not yet, and he said *that is what you said before.*" He stopped. "The fourth time was in the spring and I stood in the street and looked at him through the door of the shop for an hour and did not go in."

"Why?"

"Because I had nothing to say to him."

Antonia looked at him for a long moment.

"You could say: *I am sorry,*" she said.

"I have nothing to be sorry for. I have been a slave all my life and he is a slave, and I did not sell him and I did not leave him; the world did that."

"You could say it anyway."

Tiro looked at the floor.

"I could," he said.

She picked up the stylus again and turned it over.

"How much would it cost to buy him?"

"Six hundred. Gallio has said so. It will be twelve hundred by the time I have six hundred, because he is a man with a business and the boy is worth more every year."

"Then I will give you six hundred."

Tiro's head came up.

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because then he is your freedman and not mine," Tiro said, "and because you would give it to me and never mention it, and I would think about it every day for the rest of my life, and I am fifty-two years old and I do not want to spend the rest of my life being grateful." He heard how it sounded and did not take it back. "I will buy him myself. It will take four years. I have two hundred and eleven."

Antonia looked at him.

"You are a proud man for a man with a scar," she said.

"I am a proud man for a man with nothing. It is the only thing I have that nobody gave me."

She stood up and came around the table and did a thing she had not done in seventeen years: she put her hand flat on his chest, over the heart, the way a woman touches a member of her own family.

"Then go to Tibur on the next market day," she said, "and stand in the yard and say the thing you have nothing to be sorry for, and see what happens. And Tiro—" she took her hand away. "When this is over, whatever happens, if I have a house and you are in it, he will have a place. Not as a favour. As wages, for work. I will find him work."

Tiro looked at her.

"Yes, domina," he said.

He went out into the corridor and stood in the dark with his hand on the wall.

Then he went up through the house and out onto the roof and stood there for a while with his hands on the parapet.

From the roof of that house you could see the whole quarter: the roofs going down to the Velabrum and the river beyond it, and the Palatine up on the right with the temple of Apollo lit, and the great black mass of the amphitheatre away to the south-east with a light or two in it where the men were still working.

He had stood on that roof perhaps a hundred times in seventeen years. It was the only place in the city where he was above everybody.

He thought about the fact that a man may be a slave for thirty-five years and a freedman for seventeen and that the difference between the two is a piece of paper and a name, and that in the end the piece of paper is worth exactly what the man who can read it says it is worth.

Then he went down to his room and took the jar out from under the bed and counted the two hundred and eleven sesterces, and put them back.

He had told her about the mark. He had not told her whose he thought it was, and he had watched her face while he said it, and she had believed him.

He did not know, then, why he lied. He told himself it was because a name was not a proof, and because a steward's mark was not a confession, and because if he said the name out loud in that house, the domina would go to the prefect with it and the prefect would laugh at her, or worse, would go to Corvus, and Corvus would know that she had been told, and the boy in the smithy at Tibur would be the one who paid.

All of that was true. None of it was the reason.

The reason was that he had seen his son in the spring, and the boy had looked at him across the street with the flat, patient, unsurprised look of a slave who has learned that the people who are supposed to come for him do not come, and Tiro had walked back to Rome with that look in his chest like a stone.

He went out again at the ninth hour, and this time he did not go to the Subura. He went to the Emporium.

The man with the mark came down to the river every second evening to meet a boat.

Tiro watched him for three nights from the colonnade, and on the third night he followed him. The man went along the wharf past the fourth shed and the fifth, and turned up the alley between the warehouses where the lamp-oil was stored, and there was nobody there, and it was dark, and the sound of the river was loud.

Tiro took him by the back of the neck and put him against the wall.

"Don't," he said. "Don't shout. If you shout I will break your neck and leave you in the water and nobody will find you for a week."

The man was young, twenty-five or so, thin, with a face that would have been handsome if it had not been so frightened. He had a purse at his belt and a knife he did not reach for.

"Who are you?"

"That does not matter. What matters is the eight on your wrist and the hellebore you bought nine days ago in the Subura."

The man's face went slack.

"I don't know what you—"

"You do." Tiro pressed him harder against the wall. "A consular died at the table of Antonia Cassia. You bought the poison. You carried it. Somebody paid you and I want to know who."

"I can't."

"You can."

"He'll kill me."

"He will kill you anyway," Tiro said. "He will kill you because you are the only thing between him and a rope, and he is a man who does not leave things between himself and what he wants. You know this. You have known it for nine days and you have been sleeping badly."

The man began to cry. It was a horrible sound, quiet and hitching, and it went on for a while, and Tiro held him against the wall and waited, because he had learned in the arena that a man who is crying is a man who is about to talk.

"It was not for me," the man said. "I was told to put it in the cup. The old man's cup. Not the wine, because the wine was poured in front of everyone, but the cup itself, before the second course, in the passage, and then I was to go out by the kitchen and not come back."

"Who told you?"

"The steward."

"Which steward?"

The man looked at him. In the dark his eyes were two wet holes.

"I have a wife," he said. "She is in the house. If I am gone she will be sold."

"Which steward?"

"Faustus."

Tiro let go of him.

He did not decide to do it. That was the thing he would think about afterward, in the years that were left to him, which were fewer than he expected: that he did not decide, that his hand went to his sleeve and his body did the thing it had been trained to do thirty years before in a barracks in Capua, and that it was over in the time it takes to close a door.

The man made a small sound and sat down against the wall with his hands on his stomach, and looked at Tiro with an expression of enormous surprise.

"Run," Tiro said. "Run, you fool."

But the man did not run. He slid sideways into the dirt of the alley, and his hands came away from his stomach red, and Tiro stood over him and watched him die, and it took less time than he would have believed.

Then he knelt down and went through the man's belt and found a purse with eleven denarii in it and a bronze token with a bull on one side and a name on the other: CALLISTVS. And under the token, folded small, a scrap of papyrus with three words on it in a bad hand, which he could not read, and which he put in his own belt because he could not read it and because a man who cannot read a thing should not leave it on a corpse.

He dragged the body to the water and rolled it in.

He stood on the wharf afterward and washed his hands in the river, and the river went by as it always went by, black and busy and indifferent, and the lights of the boats moved on it, and somewhere a long way off a woman was singing.

He walked back up the hill and let himself in by the kitchen door, and Prisca said, "You are late," and he said, "I was at the docks," and she said, "You smell like the docks," and he went up to his room and sat on the bed in the dark with his hands in his lap.

*Faustus*, he thought.

He knew what it meant. It meant that the steward of Gaius Claudius Corvus had bought the poison that killed Gaius Vettius Proculus, and that the man who was on the panel of inquiry into the death was the master of the man who had bought it, and that either Corvus had ordered it or Corvus's house had done it without his knowledge and Corvus would destroy anyone who said so. Either way, the road from that alley went to the Palatine, and the Palatine was the tallest hill in Rome.

And in his belt there was a scrap of papyrus with three words on it, and he could not read.

He was still sitting there when there was a knock at the door.

He opened it. Faustus was standing on the landing with a lamp in his hand and a smile on his face, and behind him, at the bottom of the stairs, there were two men.

"Tiro," he said. "May I come in? I think we have something to discuss."

"You have a great deal of nerve," Tiro said, "coming into this house."

"I have a great deal of nerve and a great deal to offer." Faustus stepped past him into the room and looked around it: the bed, the chest, the knife on the table. "You killed a man tonight. A slave of the house of Claudius Corvus, in an alley by the fourth shed. He was not a good man, and he was not important, and the world is better without him, and none of that matters. What matters is that I know, and that there are two men at the bottom of the stairs who will do what I tell them, and that in a little while I am going to have to decide whether to tell them to come up."

Tiro did not move.

"There is a boy," Faustus said. "At Tibur. In the smithy of a man called Gallio. Fourteen years old. Dark hair, like his mother. He is a slave of the estate, and I keep the estate's papers, and I have his manumission here—" he tapped his sleeve— "and a place for him in a household in Gaul, where a boy who can work iron can be a freedman in six years and a citizen in ten."

"That paper is not yours to give."

"Everything is mine to give," said Faustus, "because I am the man who holds it." He set the lamp down on the chest. "Give me the scrap of papyrus you took off the body. Give me the token. Tell me what the man said, and to whom you have said it, and then go back to your bed and be a steward, and in a month, when this is over, I will put your son's papers in your hand and you will never see me again."

Tiro looked at him.

He thought about Antonia Cassia in the rain, saying *you look like a man who can keep a door.* He thought about the boy at the bellows, and the flat look across the street, and the four times in eight years.

"No," he said.

Faustus's smile did not change.

"Think about it," he said. "I will come again on the Ides."

He took his lamp and went down the stairs, and the two men went with him, and Tiro stood in the doorway of his room for a long time with his hand on the frame.

Then he went to the chest and took out the jar with the two hundred and eleven sesterces in it, and counted them, and put them back, and sat on the bed with the scrap of papyrus in his hand, turning it over and over, and thought about the fact that he was fifty-two years old and could not read.

He thought about the shrine of the crossroads, and the lamp, and the paper in Faustus's sleeve.

He thought about the fact that a man who cannot read must trust whoever can, and that he had spent his life being proud of a thing that was, in the end, only a way of not knowing.
