# Chapter Twenty-Three

## Corvus

The Senate met on the fourth day after the girl died, and Corvus stood up in the body of the house and read out his own resignation in a voice that he did not recognize.

He had written it in the night, at his father's table, in the study with the shutters closed. It was four sentences long. It said that he had been honoured beyond his deserts; that circumstances had arisen which made it impossible for him to take up the office without injury to the dignity of the Senate; that he begged leave to withdraw his name; and that he thanked the house for its forbearance. He had read it over three times and had changed nothing, because there was nothing in it that was not true and nothing in it that was not a lie.

The house listened in silence.

When he had finished, the presiding consul said, "The Senate has heard the resignation of Gaius Claudius Corvus, and accepts it, and records its thanks for his long service." And then he said the other thing, the thing that had been agreed in the antechambers before the session, in the way that such things are agreed: "The Senate will also consider, at its next sitting, the question of the conduct of Gaius Claudius Corvus in the matter of the death of his father, Lucius Claudius Corvus, consul, seventeen years ago."

One man rose.

He was old, and he had been consul in the reign of Domitian, and he had a great name and a bad temper and no money, and Corvus had known him for twenty years and had never liked him.

"Fathers," the old man said. "I did not know Lucius Claudius Corvus well, and what I knew I did not like. He was a hard man in a hard time and he did very well out of it. But I know his son, and I have watched him in this house for nineteen years, and I have never once seen him take a bribe, or protect a friend at the expense of the state, or say a thing he did not believe." He looked around the chamber. "If every man in this room were to be judged on the worst hour of his life, there would be nobody left to vote."

There was a murmur.

Another man rose, and he was young, and he had been praetor the year before, and he had ambitions.

"The house is grateful to the consular for his sentiments," he said, "but the question before us is not whether a man may be judged on the worst hour of his life. The question is whether a man who was present at the death of a consul, and who concealed it for seventeen years, and who is now to be consul himself; whether such a man may be permitted to take the seat without the house having considered the matter." He paused. "I say that he may not. I say that the house must consider it, and that it must consider it in public, and that if it does not, the city will consider it for us, in the street, with torches."

Corvus stood in the middle of the floor and listened to them both, and it occurred to him that he had spent nineteen years in that room and had never once heard a man say a thing that was entirely true, including himself.

There was no debate. There was no vote. There was a murmur of assent, which is what the Senate does instead of a vote when it wishes to be able to say afterward that nothing was decided.

Corvus went out through the doors of the Curia into the light.

The building works were going on as they always went on. A crane was swinging a block of marble through the air, and the dust was coming down over everything, and a foreman was shouting at a man in Greek. Corvus stood at the top of the steps and looked at it for a while.

Men came past him and did not stop. That was the thing he had not expected: that it would be so quick. In nineteen years he had never walked through the Forum without being stopped four or five times. He walked the whole length of the Sacred Way and up the hill and no one spoke to him at all. Two men looked at him and then looked away. One, a knight who had been a client of his father's, crossed the street.

The house was quiet when he came in.

The slaves had begun to leave. He had expected that; a great house runs on the expectation of favour, and when the favour goes the household goes with it, quietly, a few at a time, taking what they can carry. Three of the bearers had gone in the night. The cook had gone. The boy who carried the water was still there, because he was eleven and did not understand, and because nobody had told him to go.

His mother was in the atrium with her cheek still bruised, spinning.

"Marcus is gone," she said.

Corvus stopped.

"Gone where?"

"To Ostia. He went this morning, on the horse, with a bag. He came to me before he went and he stood in that doorway and he said: *I am going to Syria, and I will not write, and when I come back I will not live in this house.*" She turned the spindle. "He said it very quietly. He has your way of saying things quietly."

"Did you try to stop him?"

"No."

"Why not?"

Claudia Junia looked up.

"Because he looked at me," she said, "the way his grandfather used to look at me, when he had decided something and was waiting to see whether I would be foolish enough to argue." She set the spindle down. "I have lost the argument, Gaius. I lost it in the basilica when you stood up and told six hundred men that a girl had been sent away with a purse, and I lost it on the gallery when the rail went. There is nothing left to argue about."

Corvus stood in the atrium of his father's house.

"I have resigned the consulship," he said.

"I know. It was in the street by the second hour."

"The Senate will consider my conduct at its next sitting."

"I know that too." She picked up the wool again, and her hands were steady, and her voice was the voice she used for discussing the price of oil. "They will not try you. They will pass a resolution expressing concern, and they will ask the emperor's opinion, and the emperor will write a letter in the spring saying that the matter is best forgotten, and it will be forgotten, because there is a war coming in the east and nobody in Rome cares about a consul who died seventeen years ago." She wound the wool. "You will not be expelled. You will not be tried. You will simply be a man who was consul-designate and is not, and who lives in a house on the Palatine with his mother, and in ten years nobody will remember why."

"That is worse."

"Yes," said his mother. "That is what I have been trying to tell you for thirty years. There are worse things than disgrace. Disgrace is at least a thing that happens to a man. This is a thing that happens to a name."

She went on spinning.

He wrote three letters that afternoon and burned all three.

The first was to the emperor, and it was four paragraphs long, and it explained that the consul-designate had been present at the death of his father seventeen years before and had concealed it, and it asked for the emperor's judgment on whether such a man should hold the office. He read it over and found that it was true and that it was also a performance, and he burned it.

The second was to Marcus, at the legion in Syria, and it was one line long. He could not think of a second line. He burned it.

The third was to Antonia Cassia, at the house of the fuller's widow in the Subura, and he wrote it in the clear careful hand of a man who has been writing official letters for twenty years, and it said: *I have resigned the consulship and the Senate will consider my conduct. I am not writing to ask you for anything. I am writing because you said in the shrine that I had never once said what I meant, and I find that I would like to try, once, before I die.*

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he burned it, and went down to the kitchen and asked the boy for water, and the boy gave it to him and did not look at him, and Corvus stood in the kitchen of his father's house and drank a cup of water and thought about the fact that he had never in his life made himself a meal or swept a floor or filled a lamp.

He went to see Faustus.

The prefect had forbidden it, which meant that it cost money, and Corvus had money for another month or two. He went at the second hour of the night through the back of the basilica with a clerk who did not give his name, and down the corridor that smelled of wet stone, and the man in the third cell was no longer singing.

Faustus was in the last cell.

He was sitting on the bench with his back against the wall and his hands in his lap, and he had been beaten; there was a bruise across his face and his lip was split, and one of his eyes was closed. He looked up when the lamp came, and then he looked away.

"Dominus."

"Faustus."

"You should not be here."

"I know."

"They will say you came to silence me."

"Then they will say it." Corvus stood at the bars. "I have come to ask you a question and then I will go, and you will answer it honestly, because there is nothing left that I can do to you."

Faustus laughed, and then coughed, and the cough went on for a while.

"Ask."

"Why did you do it?"

"I told them in the basilica. I was paid."

"That is not why."

Faustus looked at him with his one open eye.

"No," he said. "That is not why." He shifted on the bench. "Do you remember the winter I came to the house? I was eleven. Your father bought me at Puteoli with a boy called Nicias and a girl whose name I never knew, and he had us brought up to the house in a cart, and it was raining, and we stood in the yard and he came out and looked at us the way a man looks at a new pair of boots." He was quiet for a moment. "He gave me to the tablinum. I was quick with figures. By the time I was twenty I was keeping the accounts of the estate, and by the time I was thirty I was running the house, and he never once said a word to me that was not an instruction or a criticism. Not once, in twenty years." He looked up. "And then one night when I was twenty-four he had me stand at the foot of his couch while he ate, and he threw the bones on the floor and told his guests that I would pick them up, and I picked them up, because I was a slave, and the guests laughed."

Corvus stood at the bars.

"I did not know that," he said.

"No," said Faustus. "You were in Germany."

"I would have—"

"You would have done nothing. You were nineteen years old and you were in Germany and your father was the consul." Faustus's voice was quiet. "I am not telling you this to make you sorry. I am telling you because you asked, and because I have been in this cell for nine days and there is nobody else to tell." He put his head back against the wall. "When Publius Vettius came to me with the money, I did not think about the old man at all. That is the thing I want you to understand. I thought about the house. I thought: if the old man dies and the son is finished, the estate comes to you, and you will be consul, and the house will go on, and everything I have done for twenty years will have been for something." He closed his eye. "And then I thought about the bones on the floor. Both things at once. That is what a man is, dominus. He is two things at once, and he tells himself the good one."

"Why did you use my household's man?"

"Because I had no one else. A steward cannot walk into a strange house and put something in a cup. I needed a man who could go into a great house and be overlooked, and Callistus was in the kitchen and he was in debt and he was stupid." Faustus opened his eye. "I told him it was a purge for an old man's stomach. That is what I told him. He did not know it was poison. He thought he was playing a trick on a guest for money, and he was proud of it, and I let him think it, and he is dead in the Tiber, and that is on me as well."

"And the boy?"

Faustus was silent for a long time.

"I did not order it," he said. "I want you to know that. I did not order it. The man I sent to watch the house was a man called Verus, who had been in the vigiles, and Verus had a partner, and the partner was a stupid violent man who had been in the army, and when the boy followed me to the Caelian and was seen at the fountain asking questions, the partner did what such men do." He looked at the floor. "I did not order it. I have been telling myself that for nine days. It is true and it is worth nothing."

Corvus put his hand on the bar.

"What do you want?" he said.

"Nothing."

"Faustus—"

"I want you to go away," said Faustus, "and be consul, or not be consul, and live in that house with your mother until you die, and I want to be sent to the mines and to die there in the first year, which I will, because I am forty-five and I have never done a day's labour in my life." He shifted on the bench. "Do not come again. Do not send money. Do not put my name on anything. I have been your father's man and your man for thirty-four years and I am finished, and the only thing I have left is that I did it for the house and not for myself, and I would like to keep that, even though it is a lie."

Corvus stood at the bars for a moment.

"I am sorry," he said.

"Yes," said Faustus. "So am I."

He turned his face to the wall, and Corvus took the lamp and went back up the corridor past the empty cells, and the clerk took him out through the back of the basilica into the street.

Corvus went up to his study and sat down at his father's table.

He stayed there for two days.

He did not eat. The boy brought him bread and water and he did not touch it. He sat at the table with the strongbox open in front of him and the stylus in his hand, and he looked at the wall, and he thought about his life.

He thought about being twelve years old and standing in the shrine while his father explained the family's ancestry to him, name by name, and the one who had thrown himself on his sword in the latrine at Ephesus, whose name was never to be spoken in the atrium. He thought about being twenty-four and standing in a doorway with a cup in his hand for three heartbeats. He thought about the girl on her knees, and the face of a woman doing sums. He thought about the letter he had written that night, in this room, at this table, with a pen that was not his father's.

*Take the purse and go. Say nothing, and you will never see my face again. Say one word, and I will see you dead.*

He had written it because he had been afraid. He had been twenty-four years old and his father was dead on the floor of the shrine and there was a slave girl with her back open standing over him, and he had not known what to do, and so he had done the thing that men in his family did: he had made an arrangement, and paid for it, and told himself afterward that it had been mercy.

He had told himself that for seventeen years.

He had told himself that he had saved her. He had given her a purse and her freedom and a letter of warning, and he had let her go, and he had never gone near her, and he had never claimed a sesterce of her fortune, and he had told himself that this was the conduct of a decent man in an impossible situation, and he had believed it, more or less, for seventeen years, in the way that a man believes a thing he has never examined.

And then she had built a house on the Vicus Tuscus and become rich, and the city had begun to talk about her, and he had heard her name in the Senate and had felt the cold thing turn over in his chest, and he had understood that he had not saved her at all. He had bought her silence with a purse and left her to make her own way in a city that eats women, and the fact that she had succeeded did not make it a kindness. It made it worse. It meant that she had done, alone and with nothing, what four hundred years of his family had not been able to do with everything.

He put the stylus down.

On the third morning a slave came to the door and said that there was a woman below who had come for the body of the girl.

Corvus went down.

She was standing in the atrium in a grey stola with her hair covered, and she looked older than she had in the basilica, and thinner, and she had the look of a woman who has been sleeping in sheds and washing in troughs.

"You are not permitted in Rome," he said.

"I am aware."

"There is a hundred-mile limit."

"There is," said Antonia Cassia, "and I am standing in your atrium, and you may inform the prefect if you wish, and he will fine me a sum I do not have and send me out again, and it will be the third time this month and I think by now he is as tired of it as I am." She looked at him. "I have come for the body. She is to be burned at Surrentum. Her mother has been written to and has sent word that she will take the ashes."

"The body is in the mortuary."

"I know. I have been to the mortuary. They will not release it to me, because I am not kin and because I am under sentence." She said it without expression. "They will release it to you. You are the head of the house in which she died."

Corvus looked at her.

"You want me to give you the body of the girl who died in my house."

"Yes."

"Why would I do that?"

"Because you have a box in your study with a pen in it," Antonia said, "and because there is a rail in your gallery that a carpenter has said was rotten, and because you are a man who has spent his life arranging things so that nothing is ever said out loud, and if you refuse me I will stand in the Forum and say it out loud until someone listens." She came toward him. "Or because you are not entirely a monster, and because you know what your mother did, and because you have not slept in three days."

The atrium was very quiet.

"Come with me," Corvus said.

He took her down through the passage behind the tablinum and into the little room at the back of the house, and the shrine was there, dark with soot, the painted gods scratched out, the altar broken at one corner, and the floor swept clean, because his mother had swept it herself seventeen years before and had never let a slave touch it since.

Antonia stood in the doorway and looked at it.

"I have not been in this room since the night," she said.

"I know."

"How many times have you?"

"Twice a year," Corvus said. "Sometimes three."

She went in and stood in front of the altar.

"Give me the pen," she said.

He took it out of his sleeve. He had carried it down from the study; he did not remember deciding to. It was wrapped in the soft leather, and he unwrapped it, and it lay in his hand, four and a half inches of bronze with a flat end and a point, and a dark stain in the groove that no amount of polishing had ever got out.

Antonia took it and turned it over.

"It is smaller than I remember," she said.

"Everything is."

She held it for a moment. Then she put it down on the broken altar.

"You kept it," she said. "I have thought about that for seventeen years. I thought you kept it as a threat, or as evidence, or because you were afraid. And then I stood in a basilica and looked at you and I understood." She turned. "You kept it because it was the only honest thing in your life."

Corvus looked at the floor.

"Yes," he said.

"Everything else in this house is a lie. The name, the ancestry, the four hundred years, the painted gods, the pit in the atrium, the way you stand and the way you speak and the way you have never once in your life said what you actually meant. It is all a story that the Claudii Corvi tell to keep the walls standing." She picked the stylus up again. "And in this room, on one night, seventeen years ago, a man died because he was a monster, and a girl killed him, and a boy stood in the doorway and did nothing, and all three of those things were true, and none of them was a story. That is why you kept it. It was the only thing you had that was real."

Corvus sat down on the edge of the altar.

"I am going to die," he said.

She did not say anything.

"I have been thinking about it for three days," he said, "and I find that the thinking is not the difficult part. The difficult part is that there is no one to tell. My son has gone to Syria and will not write. My mother is in the atrium with her wool. Faustus is in a cell under the basilica, and he will be sent to the mines, and he will die there, and he did it for me, and I cannot even go and see him because the prefect has forbidden it." He looked up. "And then you came to the door and asked for the body of a girl, and I found that there was someone to tell after all."

"Tell me, then."

He told her.

He told her about the twelve-year-old boy and the names of the consuls of the line, and about the one in Ephesus whose name was never to be spoken; and about the German war, and about the night at Zeugma; and about the fact that he had never loved his wife, who had been a good woman, and had died in childbirth with a child that would have been a daughter; and about Marcus, and the fact that he had never been able to say a single true thing to his own son; and about the consulship, which he had wanted since he was fourteen years old, and which he had wanted, he now understood, only because his father had wanted it, and because a man who has been told all his life that he is the last of a line does not have a self; he has a position.

Antonia listened.

She did not sit down and she did not interrupt and she did not comfort him, and when he had finished she said, "Is that all?"

"Yes."

"Then I will tell you something, and then I will go." She put the stylus in her sleeve. "You have spent your whole life believing that you were the last of the Claudii Corvi and that everything depended on you. It is not true. You are not the last of anything. The name will go on or it will not; your son will marry or he will not; the house will stand or it will fall; and in a hundred years there will be a man in a street in Rome who does not know that any of us existed. That is not a tragedy. It is the only mercy in the world." She looked at him. "The tragedy is that you had forty-one years and a name and a fortune and a son, and you spent them being afraid of a dead man in a room."

Corvus stood up.

"I know," he said.

"Then die if you must," said Antonia Cassia. "I am not going to talk you out of it, and I am not going to hold your hand. I have buried a boy of twelve and I am going to bury a girl of twenty-one, and I have no more comfort in me; it has all been spent." She went to the door. "Give the order for the body and I will go."

"Stay."

She stopped.

"Stay," Corvus said. "Not for me. There is no one else, and I find that I cannot do it alone. I have done everything alone for forty-one years and I am tired, and I have no right to ask you, and I am asking."

Antonia stood in the doorway for a long moment.

Then she came back into the room and sat down on the bench against the wall, and put her hands in her lap.

"Very well," she said.

He went out and came back with a cup and a small jar, and he set them on the altar, and he looked at them for a while.

"It is hemlock," he said. "I bought it in the Subura. The woman who sold it to me did not know who I was; she was old and half blind and she thought I was a gardener, and I told her it was for the moles." He laughed, once. "That is what Faustus's man told her, too. I heard it in the basilica and I thought: we are all buying poison in the Subura and telling old women it is for the moles."

"Drink it," Antonia said, "or do not. I am not going to watch you make a speech about it."

He looked at her.

"You are the hardest woman I have ever known," he said.

"I am the only woman you have ever known," said Antonia. "That is the whole of it. You have lived among the statues and the names, and I am the one real thing that ever happened to you, and you were afraid of me, and you kept a pen in a box."

Corvus poured the hemlock into the cup.

He drank it.

It was quick, or it was not; she had no way of knowing, because she did not look at him for the first part of it, and when she did look he was sitting on the floor with his back against the altar and his hands loose in his lap, and his face had gone the colour of paper.

"It is cold," he said.

"Yes."

"I did not think it would be cold."

He turned his head against the marble and looked at her.

"Antonia."

"I am here."

"I am sorry," he said. "Not for all of it. For the doorway. I am sorry that I stood in the doorway."

She looked at him for a long moment.

"I know," she said. "I have known for seventeen years."

He died a little after that, sitting against the altar of his father's house, in the room where his father had died, with the broken god on the floor and the scratched-out paintings on the wall.

Antonia sat on the bench and watched him.

She did not weep. She had wept that morning, in the mortuary, over a girl with a shell in her hand, and there was nothing left.

When it was over she got up and straightened his legs, because a man should not be found sitting, and she put his hands at his sides, and she closed his eyes with her thumb the way the women had closed the boy's eyes on the Vicus Tuscus.

Then she went up the stairs and through the house and out into the street, and she told the slave at the door that his master was dead in the shrine, and she walked down the Clivus Victoriae with the stylus in her sleeve and the sun on her face, and she did not look back.
