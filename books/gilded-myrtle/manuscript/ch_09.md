# Chapter Nine

## Felix

The thing about following a man was that you had to look as though you were not following him, and the second thing about following a man was that you had to be able to stop.

Felix had learned both from a boy called Scrofa in the Subura when he was seven, in the year before his mother sold him, and he had learned them well, and he had never had a chance to use them until now.

He went out at the second hour in his oldest tunic, with no lamp, and a crust in his belt, and he went up the hill by the Clivus Victoriae, which was the long way round, because the Palatine had two gates and the one on the Vicus Tuscus had a doorkeeper who knew him.

The house of the Claudii Corvi stood on the upper slope in the shadow of the temple of Apollo, and it was not what Felix had expected. He had expected something like the houses in the Greek novels his mistress kept in the chest in the tablinum: a portico with a hundred columns, gold on the ceiling, a fountain with a statue in it. What he found was a wall, a plain door, a lamp, and above the wall the top of a fig tree and the corner of a roof with a cracked tile. There was a smell of cooking and a sound of somebody sweeping. It was, in fact, very like the house he had grown up in, except that the sweep was a slave with good sandals.

He waited in the doorway of a fuller's shop across the street for an hour and a half, and at the fourth hour Faustus came out.

He was easy to follow. That was the first thing that surprised Felix: a man who was important in his own house walked as though the street belonged to him, and a man who walks as though the street belongs to him does not look behind him. Faustus went down the hill, and along the Sacred Way, and into the Forum, and Felix went after him at the distance Scrofa had taught him, which was the distance at which a man looks like a man and not like a person who is following you.

In the Forum Faustus stopped at the stall of a bookseller and bought nothing, and spoke to a man in a green tunic for a short time, and then went on. He crossed the square in front of the Basilica Julia, and went down toward the river, and along the wharf to the fourth shed.

Felix had never been inside the Emporium. He stood at the corner of the shed and watched Faustus go in through the small door at the side, and he waited, and after a while Faustus came out with a man Felix did not know, a big dark man with a broken nose, and they stood in the doorway and spoke for a while, and the big man shook his head twice, and Faustus gave him something small that glinted, and the big man took it and put it in his belt, and went back inside.

Then Faustus went up the hill again, to the Caelian, and stopped at a house on the slope above the Colosseum.

This one was what Felix had expected. It had a proper door with a bronze knocker in the shape of a lion, and a slave outside it with a stool, and there were cypresses in the garden behind the wall and a smell of lilies, and Faustus went in without knocking.

He was inside for a long time. Felix sat on the kerb across the street with his crust and ate it slowly, in the way of a boy who has learned to make a thing last, and watched the door, and thought about what he would tell the domina.

He would say: *He went to the Emporium and spoke to a big man with a broken nose, and the big man shook his head, and Faustus gave him money. Then he went to a house on the Caelian with a lion on the door and he is in there now.*

He would say it in order, and he would not embellish, because the domina had told him once that a boy who embellishes is a boy who cannot be trusted with anything, and he had never forgotten it, and he had never embellished since.

He was thinking about this when the door of the house on the Caelian opened and Faustus came out with another man.

This one was younger, thirty or so, narrow-faced, with a mouth that turned down at the corners, and he was angry. Felix could see it from across the street: the way he walked, the way he put his hand on Faustus's arm and then took it away, the way he stood with his weight on one foot and then the other. They spoke for a while. Then the narrow man went back inside and the door shut, and Faustus stood on the step for a moment with his hand over his mouth, and then he turned and came down the street, and Felix got up and followed him.

He followed him all the way back to the Palatine, and then he went home, and it was the fifth hour, and he was hungry, and he was very pleased with himself.

The domina listened to him in the counting room with the door shut.

He told it in order, and he did not embellish, and when he had finished she sat back and looked at him for a long time.

"The house with the lion," she said. "On the Caelian, above the Colosseum. Are you certain?"

"Yes, domina."

"Was there a name on the door?"

"There was a tablet, but it was painted over. There was a man selling water by the fountain and I asked him whose house it was, and he said it was the house of the Vettii, and that the old man had died in the city and been burned in the Campus, and that the son was selling the furniture." Felix hesitated. "He said the son had been in a good mood for a week, and then he had not been in a good mood, and now he was in a good mood again."

Antonia did not laugh.

"Anything else?"

"Faustus gave money to the big man at the Emporium. The big man shook his head first, and then took it. And when Faustus came out of the house with the lion, the man with the narrow face was angry, and he took Faustus by the arm, and then he let go, and they talked, and then he went inside and shut the door."

"You did well," Antonia said.

Felix glowed.

"You did very well," she said, "and you are not to do it again."

"But—"

"I mean it. You are not to go near that house, or the Emporium, or the Palatine, or the man Faustus, ever again. Tomorrow you will stay in the house and help Calybe, and if I hear that you have been in the street I will have Tiro beat you." She said it quietly, and she was not angry, which was worse than if she had been. "Do you understand?"

"Yes, domina."

"Say it."

"I am not to go near the house or the Emporium or the Palatine or the man Faustus ever again."

"Good." She put her hand on his head, which she did not often do, and looked at him for a moment, and then said, "Go and eat."

He went out into the courtyard and Meroe was there, and she saw his face and laughed and said, "What have you done?", and he said, "Nothing," and she said, "You look like a cat that has been in the cream," and he said, "I am not a cat," and she said, "No, you are a boy, which is worse," and gave him half a honey cake.

He ate it sitting on the wall by the kitchen door, and the sun was coming over the roof, and he thought about the bakery.

This was a thing he had never told anyone. He wanted to own a bakery. Not to work in one; to own one. He had worked out the whole of it, in the way a boy works out a thing in the dark: a shop in the Subura near the old wall, with a stone oven and a boy to work the dough and a girl to sell at the counter, and in the morning the smell of it coming out into the street so that people would come from three streets away, and there would be a bench outside, and he would sit on the bench in the afternoon with his hands clean.

The price of it was about nine thousand sesterces. He had eleven, in a crack in the wall behind his bed, and he added to it whenever he could, and he had calculated, more than once, that at the rate he was going he would have the money in about eleven thousand years.

The domina had said once, in the winter, when the house was closed and they were all sitting around the brazier, that a person could buy themselves out of anything in Rome if they were patient and saved and did not have bad luck.

He had thought about that for a long time. He had thought: *she did it.* And then he had thought: *but she is the domina, and I am a lamp boy, and the difference between us is the difference between a lamp and the sun.* And then he had thought: *she was a slave once. She said so. She said it in front of all of us.*

He had never asked her how.

He finished the honey cake and licked his fingers and went to help Calybe with the bread.

Two days later he went out again.

He would think about it afterward, in the way that a person thinks about the last hour of a life, but he did not think about it at the time; he simply did it, the way a boy does a thing, because he had been praised and because he was bored and because there was a thing in the world that was not right and he had seen it with his own eyes and he was twelve years old and believed that he was the only person in the house who was not afraid.

He went out at the ninth hour, when the kitchen was busy and nobody counted the boys. He went up the hill. He was not going to the house on the Palatine; he was going to the fountain at the corner of the Clivus, where the water sellers stood, because the man there had been friendly and had told him about the Vettii, and a boy who is bored will go and talk to a man who was friendly.

He never got there.

He was in the Via Tusculana, in the narrow part where the tall houses lean over the street and the sun does not come in until noon, when a man stepped out of a doorway in front of him and said, "Boy."

Felix stopped.

The man was not Faustus. He was bigger and older, with a broken nose and a good cloak, and Felix knew him at once, and the knowing of it went through him like cold water.

"You are the boy from the Myrtle," the man said. "The lamp boy."

"No," said Felix.

"Yes." The man took a step toward him. "You were at the Emporium. You were at the fountain on the Caelian, asking about the house with the lion. You have been a busy little boy."

Felix ran.

He was fast. He had always been fast; it was the one thing about him that everyone remarked on, and he had used it a hundred times to get out of a hundred thrashings, and he used it now. He went under the man's arm and down the street, and he heard the man's feet behind him, and he turned left into the alley by the dyer's, and left again, and he knew this quarter the way he knew his own hands.

But the man knew it too, and the man had been a soldier, and a soldier does not chase a boy down the street he runs to; a soldier goes around.

Felix came out of the alley into a little yard behind the bakery, meaning to go over the wall, and the man was standing in the yard with his arms folded.

"There you are," he said.

Felix backed against the wall.

The man came toward him, and he was not hurrying, and that was the worst thing: he was not hurrying at all, and he was breathing easily, and he looked at Felix the way a man looks at a job.

"Listen," Felix said. "I won't say anything. I won't say anything to anyone, I swear it on the gods, I swear it on my mother—"

"Your mother sold you," said the man. "I asked."

He took Felix by the throat.

It was not a quick thing. That was what the women said afterward, in the house, when they talked about it in the way that people talk about a thing they cannot bear: that it was not quick. It went on for a while. Felix kicked and clawed and got a hand into the man's face and tore something, and the man held him against the wall with one hand and pressed with the other and did not say anything at all.

At the end, Felix was thinking about bread.

That was the thing that Antonia Cassia would carry for the rest of her life, though she never knew it: that in the last of it the boy was not thinking about the gods, or his mother, or the domina, or the honey cake. He was thinking about the smell of the bakery in the morning, and about a bench outside a shop in the Subura where a man with clean hands might sit in the afternoon.

The man let go and Felix slid down the wall.

The man looked at him for a moment. Then he took the boy's purse, which had eleven sesterces in it, and he took the lamp from his belt, because a boy who has been sent on an errand carries a lamp, and he put the lamp on the ground beside the body, and he straightened his cloak, and he went out of the yard.

It was the lamp that made the story.

That was what the vigiles said, and what the prefect's clerk wrote down, and what the whole quarter believed by the second day: that the boy had been out at night with a lamp, and that he had been set upon by thieves, and that the city was full of such boys and such thieves, and that it was a wonder it did not happen more often.

The lamp was still burning when they found him.

It was Antonia who found him.

She had come out at dawn because he had not been in his bed, and she had gone up the hill with Tiro and two of the bearers, and they had asked at the fountain and at the bakery and at the fuller's, and a woman in the Via Tusculana had said that she had heard a boy shouting in the night, and had thought nothing of it, because in that street a boy shouting in the night was not a thing to think about.

They found him in the yard behind the bakery at the second hour.

He was lying on his side with his knees drawn up, and his face was swollen and dark, and there was a mark on his throat the size of a hand.

Antonia knelt down in the dirt and turned him over.

She did not scream. She had not screamed when she was fourteen and they took her out of the house of her guardian; she had not screamed in the yard at Puteoli; she had not screamed in the shrine on the Palatine with her back open. Somewhere along the way she had lost the thing that screamed, and in its place there was a cold, clear, enormously quiet place in which she could see everything.

She saw that his sandal was broken. She saw that his purse was gone. She saw that the lamp had burned itself out in the dirt and that there was a ring of black around it. She saw that his hand was curled, and in the palm of it there was a scrap of dark cloth with a thread of gold in it, torn from a cloak.

She put the scrap of cloth into her sleeve.

It was a piece of dark blue wool, good wool, with a gold thread running through the edge, and it had been torn from a cloak by the grip of a dying boy's hand.

She held it in her sleeve against her wrist all the way down the hill, and it was still there in the afternoon, and it was still there at night when she lay down on the bed in the small room by the stairs with her boots on and did not sleep.

She had bought him for eight hundred sesterces.

She had fed him for five years and taught him to say the household prayer properly and cuffed him on the back of the head perhaps a thousand times, and he had been worth, in the ledger of the city, eight hundred sesterces, and in the ledger of her own house he had been worth more than the fountain and the couches and the silver and the house itself, and there was no column in any book in Rome in which that could be written down.

Then she gathered him up, and he was lighter than she expected, and she carried him out of the yard.

Tiro took him from her at the corner of the street. She let him. They walked back down the hill through the waking city with the boy in Tiro's arms and Antonia walking in front with her head up, and the people in the street stood aside for them, and a woman selling eggs began to cry, and a man with a cart took off his hat.

At the door of the house the women were waiting.

Antonia stood on the step and looked at them.

"Nobody is to go out," she said. "Nobody is to speak to anyone. Junilla, bar the door. Prisca, bring me water and a cloth and a needle, and then bring me the boy's chest."

"Domina—"

"He will be washed and dressed and laid in the atrium tonight, and tomorrow he will be burned, and I will pay for the tomb, and there will be a stone with his name on it." Her voice did not shake. "His name was Felix. He was twelve years old. He was born in the Subura and sold at seven and he was the best of us."

She went inside.

In the afternoon she sat alone in the small room by the stairs with the boy's chest open on the floor: a wooden comb, two knucklebones, a broken strap, a stone with a hole in it, and a crack in the plaster behind the bed where eleven sesterces had been hidden in a rag.

She sat with the coins in her hand for a long time.

She went to the vigiles' station on the Aventine in the afternoon, and then to the prefecture, and then back to the vigiles' station, and it took her the whole of the day.

The station was a low building with a yard and a shed and a wall with hooks on it, and a man at a table who was writing in a ledger and did not look up when she came in.

"Antonia Cassia," she said. "Of the Vicus Tuscus. A boy of my household was killed last night in the Via Tusculana, in the yard behind the bakery."

The man turned a page.

"Name?"

"Felix. He was twelve. He was a slave of my household."

"Was he your property?"

"He was."

"Then it is a property matter." The man dipped his pen. "A slave killed by persons unknown. There were four of them last month and we found two. If we find the man who did it, he will be charged with damage to property, and you will be compensated at the value of the slave. What was he worth?"

Antonia stood in front of the table.

"Eight hundred sesterces," she said. "I bought him for eight hundred sesterces when he was seven."

"Then eight hundred sesterces, if we find him, and if he can pay, and if the court awards it." The man wrote something down. "There will be a fee for the inquiry. Two sesterces."

She paid it.

"His name was Felix," she said.

The man looked up.

"Domina," he said, "I have eleven hundred slaves in this district on my books and I write down four of them a week. I do not have the room to write their names." He went back to his ledger. "If you have a witness, bring him. If you have the body, it has been taken to the potters' field. If you want the man found, find him yourself and bring him to me with two men who will swear to it, and I will charge him."

She went to the prefecture.

She was kept waiting four hours in a corridor with a stone bench and a smell of wet wool, and at the end of it she saw a clerk, not the prefect, a young man with a clean face and a tablet, who listened to her for the length of time it takes to write a line and then said, "This is a matter for the vigiles."

"The vigiles have told me it is a matter of property."

"Then it is a matter of property."

"A boy was strangled in a yard. I have the cloth from the cloak of the man who did it, and I have the name of the man I believe ordered it, and I have a household of eleven people who will swear that men have been watching my house for a month."

The clerk looked at her.

"Domina," he said, "you are the owner of a house of ill repute on the Vicus Tuscus, and there is an inquiry before the prefect into the death of a consular in that house, and you are the principal person of interest in it. If you come to this office with a story about a dead slave and a scrap of cloth, the prefect will ask me why I did not have you removed." He put his tablet down. "I am not a fool. I know that a boy is dead and I know that it is a bad business. But I have three hundred cases on this table and four clerks, and the case with a consular in it is the one that will be read in the Senate, and the case with a slave boy in it is the one that will not." He looked at her, and for a moment he looked tired and about twenty-five. "Go home, domina. Bury the boy. Do not go to the prefect with this today, because today he will use it against you."

She went out into the Forum.

It was the ninth hour and the light was going and the shops were shutting, and the whole city was doing the thing it did every evening, which was to go home. She stood at the foot of the steps of the prefecture with the dust blowing around her ankles and the noise of it all, and she thought: *Eight hundred sesterces. That is what he was worth in the record. That is what will be written down.*

She had bought him for eight hundred sesterces when he was seven, and he had carried a lamp for five years, and he had wanted to own a bakery, and the city had put him in a ledger between a runaway mule and a stolen cloak.

Then she called Tiro and showed him the scrap of dark cloth with the gold thread.

"Faustus wears a cloak of this colour," she said. "I saw it at the basilica. It is a dark blue, with a gold thread in the edge, and it is the cloak of a man who serves a great house and wants the street to know it."

Tiro looked at the cloth. His face did not change, but his hand closed around it.

"Domina," he said. "There is a thing I have not told you."

He told her about the Subura, and the poison-seller, and the man with the mark on his wrist, and the alley by the Emporium, and the body in the water.

He did not tell her about the papyrus, or about Faustus on the stairs, or about the boy at Tibur. He did not know why. Perhaps because the telling of it would have made the rest of it real.

Antonia listened without moving.

"Then we know," she said, when he had finished. "We know who carried the poison and who paid for it and who brought it into my house. And we know that the man who paid is the son of the dead man, and the man who brought it is the steward of the man who is to judge me, and the only thing we have is a scrap of cloth and the word of a dead child."

"We have the cup."

"The cup has been handled by a hundred hands and washed by none, and a clever man will say that anything could have been in it." She stood. "We have nothing, Tiro. We are a woman and a freedman and a dead boy, and they are the Senate and the prefecture and the name of the Claudii Corvi, and in this city the strong do not need to be right."

"Then what do we do?"

Antonia looked at the small room: the bed where Leda had slept when she was fifteen, the chest, the plaster with the crack in it.

"We make them pay," she said. "Not with the law. The law is theirs. We make them pay with the only thing they value, which is what people believe." She turned. "Go to the house of Gaius Claudius Corvus. Not to the door. Find the boy who carries the water, as Felix did, and give him this."

She wrote on a tablet, quickly, and folded it.

"What does it say?"

"It says: *Your steward bought the poison and his man carried it, and the man is dead in the Tiber and my lamp boy is dead in the Via Tusculana, and I have the cloth from your steward's cloak and the word of the woman in the Subura who sold the hellebore and can name the mark on the wrist of the man who bought it. Come to me alone, tonight, or I will take it to the prefect in the morning and let the city decide what a Claudius is.*"

Tiro took the tablet.

"He will not come."

"He will come," Antonia said. "He is a man who has spent his life believing that the world can be arranged, and a man like that will always come to look at the thing that cannot be arranged."

She went out into the courtyard. The fountain was running. The bronze myrtle was dripping, and the sun was going down behind the roof, and somewhere in the house a woman was crying.

Antonia stood by the water and looked at nothing at all.

She had built this house out of nothing, with her own hands and her own wit, on the principle that everything could be counted and everything could be paid for. And now she found that there was a thing in the world that could not be counted, and that she had spent it, and that the price of a boy's life was eleven sesterces in a rag behind a bed.
At the ninth hour she went up to Leda's room.

The girl was sitting on the bed with her knees drawn up and the shell in her hand, and she had not eaten since the morning, and her face in the lamplight was the face of somebody keeping very still so as not to break.

"You knew him," Leda said.

"Yes."

"Was he your son?"

Antonia sat down on the stool.

"No," she said. "He was a boy from the Subura whose mother sold him for forty sesterces, and he came to this house with the lamp trade when he was seven, and I bought him because he was small and quick and because I was tired of carrying my own lamp." She looked at her hands. "That is the whole of it. There is nothing else. He was not my son. He was not anybody's. He was a boy who wanted to own a bakery and I did not know it until he was dead."

Leda looked at the shell.

"He used to make up the household prayer," she said. "He could not remember the words. He used to say: *little gods, don't let the roof fall on me.*"

"I know."

"I heard him say it a hundred times and I never corrected him." She closed her hand on the shell. "I could have told him the words. It would have taken a minute."

Antonia sat with her in the little room for a while, and neither of them said anything, and below them the house creaked and the fountain ran and the women moved about the kitchen, and outside the door the men of the cohort stood in the street with their sticks and waited for the morning.
