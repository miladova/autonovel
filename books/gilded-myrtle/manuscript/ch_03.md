# Chapter Three

## Leda

The men from the prefect's office came at the third hour, when the house was still in the state a house is in after a night like that: the floors half swept, the cushions still carrying the shape of men who had lain on them, a smell of wine and cold roast and the sharp green of crushed myrtle where somebody had scrubbed the garden bench.

Leda watched them from the upper gallery.

There were four. Two were soldiers of the urban cohorts in scale armour with their sticks at their belts, and they stayed in the doorway with the look of men who have been in a great many doorways. The third was a small, neat, greying man in a plain tunic with a satchel and a stylus and an expression of mild and bottomless patience, and this was the one to watch. The fourth was a big man in a good cloak who did nothing at all except stand in the middle of the atrium with his hands behind his back and look at everything, and it was his looking that made Leda's stomach go cold, because he looked at the house the way a man looks at a woman he intends to buy.

Antonia received them in the atrium. She had been up all night and she had washed and dressed and put on her rings, and she stood by the fountain with the bronze myrtle dripping behind her and did not invite them to sit.

"Telesinus," she said. "You have come early."

"Domina." The neat man inclined his head. "The prefect's duty is not to be early or late. It is to be thorough."

"You were thorough in the autumn, at the house of the widow Marcella, when her gardener died of a fall. You were thorough for nine days. Then her son made a gift to the prefect's building fund, and you became less thorough."

Telesinus wrote something on his tablets. "I shall note that you said so."

"Note whatever you like. You will note it in a room with a lamp and two clerks and it will go into a box, and in a year the box will be sold for waste."

The big man in the cloak laughed. It was a pleasant laugh, and it did not suit him.

"Antonia Cassia," he said. "I am Caius Vibius Maro, of the cohort of the watch. I am here to look at your people."

"You may look at them."

"I will do more than look. I will speak to each of them alone, and I will write down what they say, and if what they say does not agree with what the others say, I will tell the prefect so, and he will decide whether to ask the city's torturers to help them remember." He said it without heat. "That is the law. It is not my invention. A slave's word is worth nothing in a court unless it is given under torture, and so we begin with talk and end with the hook. You know this. You have lived in this city all your life."

"I have."

"Then we understand one another."

"I understand you," Antonia said. "You have not yet begun to understand me."

They took the dining room first.

Leda came down when she was called, and stood in the doorway, and saw the room in the morning light: the couches shoved back, the black floor scrubbed but not scrubbed well, a dark stain in the seam between two tiles that no one had got to. The dish of quinces was still on the side table with a fly on it. Vettius's cup was gone, and the place where it had fallen was marked with a scrap of wool.

"Your name," said Telesinus.

"Leda."

"Your owner?"

"Antonia Cassia."

"Your age?"

"Twenty-one."

"Your country?"

"I was born at Surrentum."

"Surrentum is not a country. It is a town on the bay." Telesinus wrote. "You are a singer."

"I play the lyre and I sing."

"You sang for the dead man."

"Yes."

"What did he say to you?"

Leda looked at her hands. She had a good face for this; she had learned, in six years, to keep her eyes down and her voice small, and to let men fill the silence with their own importance.

"Nothing," she said. "He asked me to sing the song about the ship. He had asked for it before."

"Did he speak to you alone, at any time in the evening?"

"No."

"Did he touch you?"

"No."

"Did you see him take anything?"

"No."

"Did you see anyone go near his cup?"

She thought of the passage behind the dining room, the narrow one that ran between the wine store and the kitchen, where the lamps were kept low because the smoke made the diners cough. She had gone out at the second course to retune, and she had stood in that passage for a moment with her back against the wall and her eyes shut, because her head ached from the smoke, and she had seen a man.

He had been ten feet away, going toward the dining room, and he had been carrying a cup.

He was not one of the house. He wore a dark tunic with a narrow stripe and his hair was cut short in the way of a man from a great household, and he moved like a man who knew where the passage went and did not want to be seen in it, and when he saw her he did not startle. He looked at her for a moment, and then he turned his shoulder and went on, and she heard the curtain move.

She had thought nothing of it. There were strangers in the house all evening. There were servants and bearers and cooks and the boys who carried the lamps and the men who came to tune the water organ, and there was wine, and there was a great deal of noise.

"Girl," said Telesinus. "Did you see anyone go near his cup?"

"No," Leda said.

The stylus went on moving. "You hesitated."

"I was thinking."

"About what?"

"About whether I had," said Leda. "There were a great many people. I was singing. When I sing I do not see the room."

"That is a pity," said Caius Vibius Maro from the doorway, "because the room sees you."

She was taken to the small room by the stairs and left there while they spoke to the others, and through the wall she could hear the voices going up and down like men in a race: Calybe's flat Bithynian defiance, Prisca's careful nothing, Felix's high gabble, Tiro's silence, which was longer than all of them put together.

She sat on the bed with her hands in her lap and looked at the wall.

The room was the same room she had been given at fifteen, when she came to this house: white plaster, a bed, a chest, a stool, a bronze mirror on a nail, a window the size of a bread board that looked onto the wall of the house next door. She had thought, when she first saw it, that it was a cell. She had come from a cell, so she knew.

*Surrentum*, she thought, because the word had been said out loud and now it would not leave her.

She could remember the smell of it: the pine on the hill behind the town, the fish, the dust of the road, the barrels in her father's yard. She could remember the yard better than her father's face. It was a small yard with a wall of rough stone and a vine over the gate, and there were amphorae stacked in rows with the painted marks on their shoulders, and in the summer the whole place smelled of wine and wasps. Her mother had a song for the wasps. She could remember the tune and not the words, and it had come to her, over the years, that a day would come when she could not remember the tune either, and that on that day Surrentum would be gone.

She had been eleven when the ship came.

She did not remember the ship. She remembered the men. She had been on the beach with her brother, collecting (she thought) something to do with a net, and there had been a boat in the shallows and men in it who spoke to them in Greek and asked whether there was a well nearby. Her brother had run. She had not run, because she was eleven and a stranger had asked her a question and she had been taught to answer.

Then the boat, and the oars, and the sea going by very fast and very close, and her brother on the beach getting smaller, and a man's hand over her mouth, and the smell of his hand, which was fish and salt and something sweet.

Four years in the yard at Puteoli. She did not think about them. She had a way of not thinking about them that was like carrying a stone in her pocket: you could forget it was there for an hour at a time, and then you put your hand in your pocket.

The dealer's name was Nymphodorus. He was a fat man with a kind voice, and he had bought her for nine hundred sesterces and fed her well and taught her nothing, and when she was fifteen he had put her on the block in his yard with forty others, on a spring morning, with a garland round her neck and her hair oiled, and the buyers had come.

There had been a fat man who smelled of cumin and wanted her for a kitchen. There had been a thin man who had asked her to open her mouth twice, which she had not understood, and then had understood later. And there had been an old man in a good cloak, standing at the back with two servants, who had looked at her for a long time with an expression she had not been able to read, and who had turned away and gone out into the street and got into his litter, and had not bid.

And there had been a woman.

She had come late, with one servant, and no litter, and she had walked along the front of the platform at a pace that made the dealer hurry to keep up with her. She had been thirty-three or so, dark, with a plain dress and good sandals and a face like a door being shut. She had looked at Leda for four heartbeats and said, "How much?"

"Twelve thousand," Nymphodorus had said.

"For a girl who has been four years in a yard? Eight."

"Twelve, domina. She sings."

"Everyone sings."

"She sings," said Nymphodorus, and had nodded at Leda, and Leda, who had understood nothing except that this was the moment, had sung the first thing that came into her head, which was the wasp song, badly, with her voice shaking.

The woman had listened all the way through. Then she had said, "Eleven thousand, and you will throw in the girl's chest and the mirror."

"Done," said Nymphodorus, too quickly.

And the woman had come up the steps of the platform and stood in front of her and said, quietly, in a voice that the dealer could not hear: "My name is Antonia Cassia. You will call me domina in the street and in the market and in front of anyone who is not of my house. When we are alone you may call me nothing at all. Do you understand?"

"Yes, domina."

"Listen to me now, because I will not say it twice. In my house you will sing, and you will be taught to sing better than you do, because that voice is worth money and money is the only thing in this city that will keep you safe. You will not be given to men. Not for a price, not for a favour, not because a consul is drunk and has asked for you and it would be easier for everyone if you went. When the day comes that you wish to go to a man, you will tell me first, and if I think you are being a fool I will tell you so, and then you will do as you please, because you will be a woman and not a jar."

"And if a man takes me anyway?"

"Then he will have to take me first," Antonia said, "and men have tried, and I am still here."

That was six years ago. Leda had believed her. She had believed her for five years, and then, in the sixth year, she had begun to notice things.

She had noticed, for instance, that the promise covered a very small piece of ground. It did not cover the men who put their hands on her wrist as she went by with the lyre. It did not cover the dinner at which a praetor's wife had said, in her hearing, *and she is very well trained*, in the tone of a woman discussing a dog. It did not cover the fact that she could not go out of the door alone, and that her contract, which Antonia had never shown her, was a piece of property that could be sold with the house, and that when Antonia died—and Antonia was thirty-nine and would die, as everyone died—Leda would be a line in an inventory, valued at whatever the executor thought fair.

It did not cover Marcus.

Nor did it cover the other women, whose arrangements were not hers and which she had spent six years failing to arrange into a single story.

Meroe, the dancer, had been bought at nineteen from a house in Alexandria that had trained her from childhood in the movements of the *kordax*, and she had a Greek contract with a fixed term and a sum named for her freedom, and she intended to buy herself out in four years and open a school for girls on the Piraeus road. She had shown Leda the contract once, proudly, and Leda had read it and seen that the term was twelve years and not eight, and had said nothing, because Meroe had been told eight by a man she trusted and it was not Leda's place to take that from her.

Prisca, who kept the linen and the keys, was forty and had been in the house since the beginning and was, in every way that mattered, the second person in it. She had a husband in Ostia, a freedman who ran a laundry, and she saw him four times a year, and she did not speak of him unless she was drunk, and when she was drunk she wept and said that he was a good man and that she had been a bad wife, though what she had done that was so bad Leda had never been able to discover.

Junilla, at the door, had been a soldier's woman on the Danube and had a knife in her sleeve and a laugh like a gate opening.

And two of the kitchen girls, and the girl who did the washing, and the girl who carried the water, none of whom had contracts and all of whom had come from somewhere in the way that a coin comes into a house, by being lost and then found.

In the winter, when the house was closed for the Saturnalia and there were no guests for nine days, they all slept in the dining room around a brazier, and ate too much, and told stories, and Meroe danced, and Calybe sang songs in Bithynian that nobody understood, and it was the only time of the year when the house felt like a house and not like a business with a fountain in the middle of it.

Leda got up and went to the chest and took out the shell.

It was a small thing, a cowrie, pink and white, with a worn place where her thumb went. Her brother had given it to her on the beach the morning the ship came, and she had had it in her hand when the men took her, and she had kept it through the yard and the block and the six years, and no one had ever thought it worth taking. She held it in her fist and put her forehead against the cold plaster of the wall.

*If I had run*, she thought. *If I had run with him.*

The door opened. It was Antonia, alone, with the lamp light behind her.

"They are going," she said. "They will come back tomorrow, and the day after, and they will talk to the cooks and the bearers and the girl who does the washing. When they talk to you again, they will ask you the same questions in a different order, and they will try to make you say something you have not said. You will say nothing. You will be stupid. You are very good at being stupid when you choose."

"I told them nothing."

"I know." Antonia came in and sat on the stool, and for a moment she looked as tired as Leda had ever seen her. "Vettius was poisoned. Not by a stopped heart. Sarpedon says the colour of the face and the foam are the marks of a thing called aconite, or of white hellebore, or of something a man can buy in the Subura for four sesterces from a woman who sells love potions and rat poison out of the same jar. It was in his cup, and his cup was his own, and his man poured it, and his man is now in the prefect's cells being asked to remember."

"Then it was his own man."

"Perhaps." Antonia looked at her for a moment. "Or perhaps somebody put it in the cup after it was poured, in a house full of strangers, in the dark, in the passage behind the dining room where the lamps are low."

Leda did not move.

"You were in that passage," Antonia said. "At the second course. With your back against the wall, with your eyes shut, because your head ached."

"I—"

"You are not the only person in this house who sees the room." Antonia's voice was quiet and entirely without accusation. "What did you see?"

Leda looked at the shell in her hand.

She thought of the man in the dark tunic, turning his shoulder. She thought of the prefect's men, and the hook, and the law that made a slave's word worth nothing until it was given in blood. She thought of Calybe, who had taught her to make the honey cakes. She thought: *if I say it, they will take me to the cells and hang me up by the wrists and ask me again, and then it will not matter what I say.*

And underneath all of it, in the place where she kept the things she did not look at, there was another thought, small and mean and hers: *if I tell her nothing, I am worth something to her. If I tell her, I am a witness, and witnesses are a liability, and I have seen what she does with liabilities.*

"There was a man," she said, "in the passage. I did not see his face. He had a cup."

Antonia was very still.

"Whose man?"

"I don't know. He was not of the house."

"Was he tall? Short? Old?"

"Young. Not a boy. His hair was short, cut close, like a soldier's or a great man's slave. He wore a dark tunic with a narrow stripe." She stopped. "That is all. I did not see his face."

Antonia sat back. In the lamplight her face had gone somewhere else, and Leda could see her thinking, the way you can see a cat thinking before it jumps.

"You will tell the prefect's man tomorrow," Antonia said. "Word for word as you have told me."

"You said I was to say nothing."

"I said you were to say nothing to them today. Tomorrow you will tell them about the man in the passage, and you will say that you did not see his face, and you will say it as though you were frightened, because you were frightened, and because a frightened girl is believed more readily than a cool one." Antonia stood. "And Leda. If they ask you whether you have ever seen that man before, in this house, on any night, you will say no."

"Have I?"

"Not to my knowledge," Antonia said. "But I want you to say no."

She went out.

Leda sat for a long time with the shell in her hand. Then she got up and put it back in the chest, and put on her mantle, and went down the back stairs to the yard, where the kitchen girls were washing the dishes from the night before and the water had gone cold in the basins, and where a boy from the baths was waiting at the gate with a folded tablet, as he did on Thursdays.

The tablet said: *The same place. The third hour after sunset. M.*

She read it twice, and then she put it into the brazier and watched it burn, and then she went back up the stairs and washed her face and sat down with her lyre and played the same twelve bars until her fingers were sore, because it was the only thing in the world that she could do to make the time go by.

The place was the portico of the Temple of Portunus, on the river side, where the old cattle market had been and where at night the only people were the beggars sleeping under the arches and the rats and, sometimes, a watchman who did not care. She went out at the third hour by the kitchen door, with a shawl over her head, and Prisca saw her go and said nothing, because Prisca had been in the house eleven years and knew which things were not her business.

Marcus was standing in the shadow of the third column with his hood up.

He was twenty and he looked, in that light, like a boy playing at being a man: the good cloak, the good boots, the sword he had brought back from Dacia that he was not legally entitled to wear in the city. When he saw her he came forward quickly, and then stopped, as though he had forgotten what he had planned to say.

"You came," he said.

"You always say that."

"I am always afraid you won't."

She let him take her hand. His was warm and dry and there was a callus on the palm from a sword hilt, and it was, she had decided some weeks ago, the only honest thing about him.

"Your father is on the panel," she said.

He let go of her hand. "How do you know?"

"Everyone knows. The prefect's man told the steward, and the steward told the cook, and the cook told the whole house by the second hour. Your father is going to inquire into the death of Gaius Vettius, in the house of Antonia Cassia, on the night that I was there."

"My father has not spoken to me about it."

"He will not."

"No." Marcus turned and looked out at the river, at the black water and the lights of the boats. "He does not speak to me about anything that matters. He speaks to me about the roof and the marriage and the family. When I came back from Dacia I wanted to tell him what it was like, and he listened for the length of a cup of wine and then he said that the seventh legion had been lucky in its officers, and that I should be grateful, and then he asked me whether I had brought back any money."

"Did you?"

"Eleven denarii and a scar." He laughed, once, without pleasure. "And a girl in a house on the Vicus Tuscus who is going to be sold or killed or both before the year is out, and who is the only person I have ever met who does not want anything from me."

Leda looked at him.

"You want something from me," she said.

"I want you."

"That is something."

"It is not money. It is not a name. It is not—" he stopped, and rubbed his face with the flat of his hand. "I have been in a room with my father this morning and he told me that I am to marry a girl whose name he does not know, for three hundred thousand sesterces, and that I am to be a consul, and that this is not a punishment but a life. And I thought: I have been in the army. I have seen men die in a ditch for a pair of boots. And now I am to spend forty years in the Senate listening to men like my father talk about the corn supply, and at the end of it I will be a bust in a corridor and a name on a list, and no one will ever know that there was a night when I stood by a river with a girl and could have got on a boat."

"Could you have got on a boat?"

"Yes."

"Where would it go?"

"Anywhere. Ostia, and then a ship. Massilia. Tarraco. There are cities where a man with a strong back and a little money can be a nobody, and a woman can be—" he stopped again.

"Can be what?"

"Free," he said.

Leda looked at the river.

She had been on a boat once. She remembered the oars and the sea going by very fast and very close and a hand over her mouth. She thought of Massilia, of Tarraco, of a city where nobody knew her, and she found that she could not see it. She could see the boat, and the water, and the port, and then nothing: a blank, like a wall.

"Your father will find you," she said.

"He will not look."

"He will look. He is a man who does not lose things." She pulled her shawl closer. "And if he finds us, he will not kill me. He will do something worse. He will take me back to the prefect and say that I am a runaway, and the prefect will send me to the mines or to the mill, and he will put you in the army on the Wall, and it will be a story that he tells for the rest of his life about his son and the whore of the Vicus Tuscus."

"You are not—"

"I am what I am." She said it without bitterness; it was simply the truth, and she had learned to hold the truth the way you hold a hot dish, at the edges. "There is another way. Your father is on the panel. He will want witnesses. If I am a witness, I am worth something to him, and a man does not throw away a thing that is worth something. I tell him what I saw, and I ask him for one thing, and he gives it to me because it costs him nothing and because he is a man who pays his debts."

"What did you see?"

"A man in a passage with a cup."

Marcus stared at her.

"That is worth a great deal," he said slowly.

"It is worth my freedom, if I am clever, and if your father is the man I think he is." She turned to go. "Do not come to the house again. Do not send tablets. If I need you, I will send word by the boy from the baths, and you will come, and you will not ask why."

"Leda."

She stopped.

"When this is over," he said, "will you come with me?"

She looked back at him, at his young face in the dark, and she felt, for a moment, a tenderness so sharp that it was almost anger. He was a good boy. He was the best thing that had happened to her in six years and he did not know the first thing about her, or about the city, or about what men did when they were refused.

"Yes," she said.

She went back through the dark streets with her shawl over her head, past the closed shops and the drinking houses and the beggars under the arches, and she came in by the kitchen door and went up the back stairs, and in the passage at the top she met Tiro.

He was standing in the dark with his arms folded, and his scarred face was not smiling.

"The third hour after sunset," he said. "The Temple of Portunus. A young man with a good cloak."

Leda's heart turned over.

"You are to tell me his name," Tiro said, "or I am to tell the domina, and she will ask you herself, and you will find that she is not as patient as I am."

"His name is Marcus."

"Marcus who?"

"Marcus Claudius Corvus."

Tiro was silent for so long that she heard the water dripping in the courtyard below and a woman laughing in the street.

"The son of the man who is inquiring into this house," he said at last. "The son of the man who will decide whether the domina is ruined or not."

"He is not his father."

"No," said Tiro. "No man is. That is the trouble with sons." He unfolded his arms. "Go to bed. Say nothing to her tonight. She has enough."

He turned and went down the stairs, and Leda stood in the passage with her hand against the wall, and above her, in the dark, the house creaked and settled like a ship, and somewhere a long way off a watchman called the hour.
