# Chapter Four

## Antonia

They burned Gaius Vettius Proculus on the fifth day, in the Campus Martius, in the family tomb by the Via Flaminia, and all of Rome came to watch.

Antonia did not go to the house of the dead. She was not welcome there and she had no wish to be. She stood instead at the top of the Argiletum, in the doorway of a bookseller's shop whose owner knew her and had gone pale at the sight of her, and she watched the procession come down out of the Caelian and turn into the Forum.

It took an hour to pass.

First the flute players, forty of them, playing the old harsh tune that is played at no other time. Then the hired mourners, the *praeficae*, women with their hair torn and their faces smeared with ash, who wailed in a rhythm that was older than the Republic and who were paid by the hour and would go on to another funeral in the afternoon. Then the wax masks of the Vettii: nine of them, in their little wooden cupboards, worn by men of the family or by hired men of the right height, each one the face of a consul or a praetor or a tribune dead two hundred years, and it was said that when a Vettius put on the mask of his ancestor he became, for a day, that man, and spoke with his voice, and the family believed it, or pretended to, which is the same thing in Rome.

Then the bier.

It was a couch of ivory and bronze with a purple cover, and on it lay what was left of Vettius, and it was carried by eight men of his own household with their hair unbound. Behind it came his son.

Publius Vettius Proculus was thirty years old, and he walked alone, and he did not weep. He had a narrow face and a mouth that turned down at the corners and a way of looking at the crowd as though he were counting it. He wore a dark toga and the mask of his father was not on his face but carried before him, because a son who delivers the laudatio does not wear the mask. He looked, Antonia thought, like a man who had slept well.

Behind him came the Senate.

There were two hundred of them at least, in their white wool, and among them she picked out the ones she knew by the way they walked: old Sura, who had been consul twice and could no longer remember his own name after dark; Hostilius, still looking guilty about the villa at Tibur; Blandus, whose toga had been turned again since the Myrtle; and, at the edge of the group, in the second rank where he always placed himself, Gaius Claudius Corvus.

She had not seen him in seventeen years.

She had prepared for it. She had spent the morning preparing for it, in the way that she prepared for everything: she had thought about what she would feel and had decided that she would feel nothing, and she had been wrong, and she knew it in the instant she picked him out of the crowd, before she had even seen his face clearly. It was the way he stood. Nobody stands like that who has not been told since childhood that the ground was made for him.

He had grown heavier. His hair was grey at the temples and cut short, and there were lines from his nose to his mouth that had not been there at twenty-four, and he walked with his hands behind his back and his head slightly forward, listening to a man beside him, and he was, she thought with a kind of cold astonishment, entirely ordinary. She had spent seventeen years being afraid of a man who looked like that.

Then he turned his head, as though someone had said her name.

Their eyes met across the width of the Argiletum, over the heads of the crowd, and he stopped walking.

It was only for a moment. The man beside him went on talking; the procession went on; the mourners wailed; and Corvus stood in the street with his face gone the colour of the plaster behind him, and then he turned away and walked on, and Antonia found that she had put her hand flat against the door frame of the bookshop and that the bookseller was asking her whether she was unwell.

"No," she said. "I am well. I am very well."

She went to the Forum.

The laudatio was delivered from the rostra, and Publius Vettius did it well. He spoke of his father's consulship, and of his father's service in Germany in the last years of the divine Domitian, and of his father's severity, and of his father's love of the old ways, and of the fact that he had died in the city he had served. He did not mention the manner of the death. He did not mention the house. He said, at the end, that his father had been a man who kept his own counsel, and that those who had known him best had known him least, and there was a murmur of approval from the Senate, and Antonia, standing at the back of the crowd with her shawl over her head like any shopkeeper's wife, thought: *He knows.*

She could not have said how she knew that he knew. It was in the way he held the tablet, and in the fact that he had not once looked at the place where the bier had stood, and in the speed with which he left the rostra when he had finished, as a man leaves a room he has robbed.

The crowd began to break up. The bier went on toward the Campus Martius with the family and the Senate behind it, and the rest of Rome went about its business, and Antonia went into the Basilica Julia.

It was the hour when the basilica was fullest. The great hall was full of the noise of the law: advocates declaiming in the corners, clerks shouting, a man weeping near the entrance where the money-changers had their tables. The air was thick with dust and the smell of wet wool. She walked along the north aisle with her head down and her heart going like a bird in a box, and she found him in the portico behind the temple of Castor, standing alone by a column with his hands behind his back, looking out at the Forum.

He did not turn.

"You should not have come," he said.

"You sent for me."

"I sent for no one."

"You stopped in the street."

"I stopped," he said, "because I thought I had seen a ghost." He turned then. In the daylight, close to, his face was harder than she remembered and his eyes were the same: pale, patient, the eyes of a man who has never once been obliged to explain himself. "You have done well for yourself."

"I have."

"I have heard the figures. They are not modest."

"They are accurate."

"Are they." He looked at her as though she were a column he was thinking of buying. "You have a house with a fountain and a Greek cook and eleven women, and you lend money to senators at rates that would make a Jew in the Transitberina blush, and you have dined with men who would not have you in their atrium. And you have kept out of my way for seventeen years, which was clever of you, and now you have put yourself in the middle of a murder inquiry and a consular is dead in your dining room, and I am on the panel that will judge you." He said it all without emphasis, the way a man reads out a list of goods. "Why did you come?"

"Because I need to know what you will do."

"You know what I will do. I will do my duty."

"Your duty." She heard her own voice go thin. "You have never done your duty in your life. You have done what was convenient and called it duty, and you have done it so well for so long that you have forgotten the difference."

His face did not change. "You are a fool to say that to me here."

"I am a fool to say anything to you anywhere. I have been a fool since I was fourteen." She stepped closer. "Vettius was poisoned. Not by me, and not by anyone in my house. His son did it."

She had the satisfaction of seeing him blink.

"That is a very large thing to say with no proof."

"I have proof enough to say it to you. The old man came to my house with his own cup because he was afraid of being poisoned, and he drank from it, and he died. His son was told before dawn, by my steward, before the prefect knew, and the first thing he asked was not how his father had died. It was whether the cup had been taken away." She watched his face. "He asked whether the cup had been taken away, Corvus. A grieving son asks for the body. He asks for the will. He does not ask about the cup."

Corvus looked out at the Forum. A flock of pigeons went up from the steps of the temple and turned in the light.

"Suppose I believed you," he said. "Suppose I went to the prefect and said: the son of a consular, a man of the Vettian *gens*, poisoned his father at a dinner table because he could not wait for an inheritance. Suppose I said it on the word of a brothel-keeper. What do you think would happen?"

"You would be laughed at."

"I would be destroyed." He turned back. "And the girl would still be dead, and your house would still be closed, and the man who did it would be consul in five years. That is the world, Antonia. It is not the world I made. It is the world that was made before I was born and will be standing when we are both dust, and I have spent my life learning to work inside it, and you have spent yours learning to sell things inside it, and neither of us can get out."

"I do not want to get out. I want to keep what I have built."

"Then give me the girl."

The noise of the basilica went on behind them, the advocates and the clerks and the man weeping by the money-changers.

"What?"

"Leda." Corvus said the name carefully. "The singer. You bought her at Puteoli six years ago for eleven thousand sesterces from a dealer called Nymphodorus. She is not yours. She was free-born, of a family at Surrentum, taken by pirates in the year of the Dacian war. There is a woman in Surrentum, a *Vibia Prima*, who has been looking for a daughter for ten years and who has petitioned three governors, and the third petition is in the archive of the prefecture and has been read by my steward." He paused. "Give her to me. Not to the court. To me. I will produce her before the panel as a free woman recovered, and the panel will have its witness, and the house of Antonia Cassia will have been shown to have bought stolen goods in good faith and to have returned them at once, and the murder of Vettius will become a matter of his own household, and you will pay a fine and close for a season and open again in the spring."

Antonia could not speak.

"You knew," she said at last.

"I have known for nine days."

"Not that. You knew—" She stopped. "You knew about the girl before the banquet. Before Vettius died. That is why you asked Faustus to find out about her. You have been looking for a way into my house since the day you heard my name in the Senate."

"I have been looking for a way to do my duty," Corvus said, "and I have found that the two are not always different."

"You will take her from me and put her in front of a panel, and she will say what you have taught her to say, and then you will have her, and I will have nothing."

"You will have your life and your house and your fortune."

"And my freedom?"

He looked at her.

The word lay between them on the stone floor of the portico, and the pigeons came down again onto the steps, and a long way off a man was selling hot sausages and calling out the price.

"You were manumitted by my hand," Corvus said. "In this city. In my father's house. Before two witnesses who are both dead. It is in the record, and the record says that you are the freedwoman of Gaius Claudius Corvus, and that when you die your property comes to me, and that if you are accused of a crime I may defend you or abandon you, and that if you are convicted I may claim the state's compensation as your patron." He said it quietly. "I have never claimed a sesterce. In seventeen years I have never once said your name in a public place. Do not tell me that I have not been generous."

"Generous." She heard the word come out of her mouth and it tasted like something spoiled. "You put me in the street with a purse and a lie and a letter telling me that if I spoke I would be dead, and you call it generosity."

"I gave you your life."

"You gave me a warning. There is a difference, and you have known it every night for seventeen years, or you would not have kept the stylus."

His face changed. It was very slight, a tightening around the eyes, and if she had not spent six years of her girlhood learning to read that face in the dark she would have missed it.

"What stylus?" he said.

"The one your father was writing with. The one I took off the altar. Do you think I did not see you take it, that night, before the slaves came in? Do you think I did not see the shape of it in your sleeve?" She smiled at him, and it was not a kind smile. "You have it still. I know you have it still. A man does not keep a thing like that for seventeen years unless he is afraid of it, and you are not afraid of me, Corvus, whatever you tell yourself. You are afraid that one morning you will wake up and find that you have become your father and that the only person who ever saw you choose not to be him is a whore on the Vicus Tuscus."

He hit her.

She had not expected it, which was strange, because she had spent six years of her life expecting it. It was not a hard blow; it was a backhanded slap with the flat of his hand, and it caught her across the mouth and knocked her against the column, and her lip split against her own teeth, and for a moment the world was white.

The portico had gone quiet. Two men by the door had turned. A clerk had stopped with his tablets in the air.

Corvus stood with his hand still raised, and his face was the face of a man who has just watched himself do something in a dream.

"Domina," he said. "I—"

"Do not." Antonia straightened. She put her fingers to her lip and looked at the blood on them, and then she looked at him, and she was surprised to find that she was not afraid. "There is the man I know."

"I am sorry."

"No, you are not. You are embarrassed, which is a smaller thing." She wiped her fingers on the edge of her shawl. "You may have the girl."

He stared at her.

"I beg your pardon?"

"You may have her. I will send her to the prefecture myself, with her purchase document and a statement, and I will say what I know about the man in the passage, and I will not ask you for anything in return." She turned to go, and then stopped, and looked back at him. "But I will tell you what I told Vettius in the garden, and what I have told every man who has ever come to my house believing that because he has a name he may take what he likes from a woman with none. Everything in this city is sold. That does not mean that everything is for sale to you."

She walked out of the portico with her lip bleeding and her heart going like a hammer, and the men by the door watched her go, and behind her she heard Corvus say her name once, quietly, in a voice she had not heard in seventeen years.

She did not turn.

She went out through the portico of Castor into the Forum, and she walked the length of it with her lip bleeding and her hands shaking and her heart going like a bird in a box, and every man she passed looked at her and then looked away.

She had thought, when she was twenty-three and had just killed a consul, that she had seen the worst thing the city could do to a woman. She had been wrong. The city had been saving it up for seventeen years, and it had turned out to be very simple: a man in a good toga, in a public place, with six hundred men within earshot, who was not afraid of her at all.

 where the tombs of the great families stood in rows along the road like houses in a street. Antonia went out through the Porta Flaminia with the crowd and stood on the grass verge with the beggars and the sellers of honey cakes, a long way off, where she could see the smoke and not the faces.

The pyre was built of pine and cypress in a square, taller than a man, with the couch set on top of it and the purple cover hanging down. The family stood at the front with their torches. The Senate stood behind them in a black mass. The hired women had stopped wailing by then, because the wailing is for the procession and not for the fire, and their throats were raw, and one of them was eating a piece of bread.

Publius Vettius put his torch to the wood.

It caught slowly, and then all at once, with a sound like a cloth being torn. The smoke went up in a column and bent sideways in the wind and came down over the crowd, and the smell of it was the smell of a city burning rubbish and something else underneath, sweeter, that made the people in front cover their faces with their mantles and the people behind push forward to see.

Antonia did not cover her face.

She stood in the smoke and watched the shape on the couch go black, and she thought about the old man in her garden with his hand on the myrtle box, saying *I have no one to talk to at night.* She had not liked him. She had not disliked him enough to want him dead. He had been a difficult old man who had wanted to buy a girl because he was lonely, and somewhere in the smoke there was a boy of thirty who had wanted his money more than he had wanted his father, and the world went on, and the fire went up, and the bakers' guild would have a new bench with his name on it.

When the crowd began to move away, she stayed.

"You came," said a voice behind her.

Publius Vettius Proculus stood with two of his father's freedmen at his shoulder and the torch still smoking in his hand. Up close his face was worse than it had been at a distance: there was a fine tremor in the muscle under his eye, and he smelled of wine, and he had been crying after all, or something like it.

"I came to pay my respects."

"To my father."

"To the dead," Antonia said. "I have no respects for your father. He was a hard man and he was rude to my cook. But he died at my table, and I do not take that lightly."

"Your table." Publius smiled. It was an unpleasant thing to watch. "You are very calm, domina, for a woman whose house is full of the prefect's men."

"I am calm because I have nothing to hide. I am also calm because I am rich, and rich women are allowed to be calm in this city for longer than poor ones."

"Not forever."

"No," she said. "Not forever."

He looked at the pyre, which had fallen in on itself and was beginning to be a heap of coals.

"My father was a fool," he said. "He had a house on the Caelian with eleven rooms and he went to the Vicus Tuscus to listen to a girl sing. He had a name that three emperors have respected and he died in a brothel, and now the whole city is telling the story and laughing, and I am the son who has to stand in the Forum and hear them." He turned the torch over in his hand. "So I will tell you what I told the prefect, and you may take it as a kindness, because it is the only one you will get from me. I do not care who killed him. I care that it was in your house. The law is the law, and a consular is dead in a whore's dining room, and somebody will be made to pay for it, and it will not be a Vettius."

"It will be me."

"It will be you," said Publius Vettius, "unless you can find me a better answer."

He threw the torch into the coals and walked away toward the litters, and the freedmen went after him, and Antonia stood in the smoke until the smell of it was in her hair and her clothes and she could not get it out for three days.

She walked home through the Forum with her lip bleeding.

It was the ninth hour and the shops were shutting. A man selling water from a cart looked at her and then looked away. Two women in good cloaks stopped talking as she passed and then began again, more quietly, when she was three feet past them.

She had been in this city for forty years and she had never once been afraid of walking through the Forum.

That was the thing the blow had taken from her, and it was not the pain and it was not the humiliation; it was the fact that a man in a public place had put his hand on her and nothing had happened. No lictor had come. No magistrate had risen. The two clerks at the door of the portico had watched, and one of them had turned away, and the man with the sausages had gone on calling out the price.

*That is what it is*, she thought. *That is what it has always been. A woman like me is not a citizen in the street. She is a thing in a good dress, and the law is a wall that is on the other side of the street, and if a man with a name puts his hand on her the wall does not move.*

She went home.

The house was quiet when she got back. The women were in the courtyard, sitting in the last of the sun: Meroe with her hair down, combing it; Prisca mending; two of the kitchen girls playing at knucklebones on the warm stone. They stopped when they saw her face.

"Domina—"

"Get me cold water and a cloth, and then get out." Antonia sat down on the bench by the fountain. "All of you. Not you, Tiro."

He came and stood in front of her while the women went, and he looked at her lip and said nothing, which was one of the reasons she had kept him for seventeen years.

"The son asked about the cup," she said.

"He did."

"Before he asked about the body."

"Before he asked about anything. He was at the door of my lady's house before the fourth hour with his hair uncombed, and he said: *is my father's cup still there?* I said I did not know. He said: *find out.*" Tiro's scar moved. "Then he asked whether the will had been sealed."

"Has it?"

"The will was sealed at the house of the Vestals this morning. The estate goes to him, with a legacy to a freedwoman whose name nobody in the house could remember, and a sum to the guild of the bakers, and nothing to the city."

"Nothing to the city." Antonia laughed, and then winced. "A consular who leaves nothing to the city. He was afraid of being forgotten and he has left his name on a bakers' guild."

"Domina." Tiro crouched down so that his face was level with hers. "What happened at the basilica?"

She told him. All of it, in order, the way she would have told a partner in a business: the offer, the girl, the stylus, the blow.

When she had finished, Tiro was quiet for a while.

"You gave him the girl," he said.

"I gave him a witness who will say what I want her to say, in front of a panel he controls, and I did it before he could take her by force and make her say something else." Antonia pressed the wet cloth to her lip. "She is free-born, Tiro. Did you know?"

"I knew she was not born to it. There was a way she held herself."

"Her mother has been petitioning governors for ten years." She looked at the fountain. "I bought a stolen child at fifteen and taught her to sing and told myself that I had saved her, and I have been paying myself that compliment for six years."

"You did save her."

"I bought her. The saving was a thing that happened afterward, and it was cheaper than the price of the girl." She stood up. "Fetch Felix."

"Domina—"

"Fetch him."

The boy came at a run, and stopped when he saw her face, and his own face did something complicated.

"You are going to the house of Gaius Claudius Corvus on the Palatine," Antonia said. "You will not go to the door. You will find a slave of his household, one of the bearers, a man called—" she thought. "No. You will find the boy who carries the water. Every great house has a boy who carries the water. You will give him this." She held out a folded tablet. "And you will wait for an answer, and you will bring it to me, and you will not open it, and you will not tell anyone, not the cook, not the girls, not Tiro."

"Not Tiro," said Felix, delighted.

"Not Tiro." She put the tablet in his hand. "Go."

When he had gone, she went to her room and unlocked the strongbox and took out the small box of myrtle wood and set it on the table and looked at it for a long time without opening it.

She knew what was in it. A letter, in a hand she had once known better than her own, on paper that had gone brown at the folds. And a stylus, bronze, with a smoothed end and a point, which he had given her in the first year, when she was seventeen and could not write her own name, and had sat with her in the little room by the stairs and guided her hand through the alphabet, and had said, *there. Now you are a person who can write.* She had kept it through everything. She had brought it to this house in her own hand.

Two styli. One to make her a person, one to make her a murderer. She had one and he had the other, and between them they made a kind of accounting, and she had spent her life in the belief that an accounting could be balanced.

She put the box back in the strongbox without opening it.

Then she went to the window and looked out at the street, where the lamps were being lit and a man was selling hot sausages and the whole vast animal of the city was settling down for the night, and she thought: *He will not keep his word. He has never kept his word, not to me, not to his father, not to himself. He will take the girl and he will close my house and he will do it kindly, because that is the worst way, and when it is done he will tell himself that he had no choice.*

*So I will need something else.*

She thought of the man in the passage, the dark tunic with the narrow stripe, the short hair. She thought of Publius Vettius asking about the cup before he asked about the body. She thought of Eutychus, who was coming in the morning with a price.

And she thought, for the first time in many years, of the house on the Palatine, and of the little room by the stairs, and of the noise the stylus had made going in.
