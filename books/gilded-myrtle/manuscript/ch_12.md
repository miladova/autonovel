# Chapter Twelve

## Antonia

On the Kalends of July, at the tenth hour, Antonia Cassia opened the door of the Gilded Myrtle and stood in it while Rome decided whether to come in.

The street was full. That was the first surprise. She had expected a few litters and a few curious neighbours and a decent number of men who had come to see a woman destroyed. What she got was a crowd: the bearers and the linkboys and the boys who sell flowers and the women from the laundry next door and a knot of young men from the Subura who had come to look at the house they had heard about, and, at the front, in a line of litters that stretched thirty feet up the Vicus Tuscus, eleven men who had been invited and perhaps twenty who had not.

She had put lamps along the whole front of the house. She had paid a man to hang garlands over the door. She had had the step scrubbed and the street swept and the fountain filled with rose petals, and she stood in the doorway in a white stola with the two pearls and a wreath of myrtle in her hair, and she smiled at every single person in that street, and she was, she thought afterward, more frightened than she had been in seventeen years.

Blandus came first, as he always did, and he came on foot, and he stopped in front of her and took both her hands.

"I said I would come," he said.

"You said you would stand up."

"That too." He looked at her, and his eyes were tired and kind and slightly drunken. "I am here, and I am not running, and if that is all I am, it is more than most."

Behind him Hostilius, sweating, with a story about his wife; Sura's son, fat and vague, with a wreath already on his head; the praetor, whose name she had never written down, in a good cloak with a bad clasp; Fannius, young and beautiful and completely penniless and delighted to be there; the knight from Spain; two men from the grain trade; a poet who had written a poem about her house that she had paid him not to publish, and who now pretended not to have written it.

Vipsania Marcella did not come. She sent a slave with a note that said she was unwell. Antonia read it on the step, in front of everyone, and folded it, and put it in her sleeve, and said, "Tell your mistress I hope she is better by the Ides," in a voice that carried.

Eutychus came at the eleventh hour, in a hired chair, with a basket.

"Figs," he said, presenting them. "The second crop. Better than the first."

"You said the first was better because it was early."

"I said that a man of my age prefers the early thing." He looked at her with his head on one side. "You have a great many people in your street, domina. There are men on the roof of the fuller's shop."

"I paid them."

"You paid them?"

"Four sesterces each, and a cup of wine. A crowd is a thing that can be bought like anything else, and a woman who is being investigated by the prefect cannot afford to be seen to be alone." She took his arm. "Come in. You are on the left couch, next to the praetor, and I want you to talk to him about the corn supply, because he is dull and it will make him feel important."

"You are using me."

"I am using everyone," Antonia said. "It is a dinner."

The dining room was full by the twelfth hour: nine men on three couches, and the lamps lit, and the garden painted on the walls, and the ceiling gilded and glowing, and the birds among the leaves. The food came in and kept coming: oysters, urchins, eggs in garum, a whole turbot on a board carried by two men, kid roasted with cumin, a boar's head, a dish of mushrooms stewed in wine, honey cakes, figs, almonds, and the Falernian, which she had bought at last, at the price of the Caecuban, from a fishmonger who now believed she knew something.

There was a moment, in the middle of the second course, when Antonia looked down the table and understood that it was going to work.

It was the noise. A room in which men are afraid is a quiet room. A room in which men are calculating is quiet too, and this room had been quiet for the first half hour, and then the wine had gone round, and Hostilius had told a joke about a Spanish governor, and Fannius had laughed too loudly, and the praetor had begun a long boring story, and the whole thing had turned, the way a room turns, into a dinner.

Blandus caught her eye and raised his cup, very slightly.

And then Corvus came.

He came late, which was deliberate, and he came alone, without a lictor or a slave, in a white toga with the narrow purple stripe, and the room did not go silent when he entered, because a room full of senators does not go silent for a man; it does something more complicated, which is to rearrange itself. Two men moved up on a couch. The praetor's story stopped in the middle of a sentence and then started again in a lower voice. Eutychus put down his cup and folded his hands.

Antonia went to meet him at the door.

"Consul-designate," she said. "You are welcome in my house."

"Domina." He took her hand. His was cold and dry, and it did not linger. "The fourth couch, on the left."

"The cushion has been kept."

She had not kept the cushion. She had bought a new one, and had it stuffed with wool, and had chosen the colour to match the wall, and she had spent an hour that afternoon deciding whether to tell him that, and had decided against it, and now she was glad, because he looked at the couch for a moment and then at her, and something went across his face that she could not read.

He took his place. He ate. He drank, a little. He talked to Hostilius about a villa at Tibur and to the praetor about a road in Campania, and he was courteous and dull and entirely at his ease, and Antonia, watching him from her stool at the head of the middle couch, thought: *He is better at this than I am. He has been doing it since he was a boy and he does it the way other men breathe.*

At the third course she had Leda sing.

It was a risk. She had thought about it for three days, and she had decided to take it, because a dinner without music is a dinner with an edge to it, and because she wanted to see what Corvus would do.

Leda came in through the curtain with her lyre and stood in the pool of light by the fountain, and she was wearing green, and she did not look at anyone.

She sang the song about the ship.

Corvus did not move at all. He sat with his cup in his hand and his eyes on the middle distance, and when it was over he applauded with the others, three times, politely, and set his cup down, and Antonia saw that his hand was not quite steady.

"Do you like music, consul-designate?" she asked.

"I know nothing about it."

"That is not what I asked."

"I like it well enough," Corvus said, "when it is not being used on me."

There was a small, delighted silence on the couch. Fannius laughed and then stopped.

"Everything in this house is being used on you," Antonia said. "That is what a dinner is. The wine is being used on you, and the fish is being used on you, and the ceiling is being used on you, and the girl is being used on you, and you know it, and you came anyway." She raised her cup. "That is either courage or appetite, and I have never been able to tell the difference in a man."

"Usually it is both," said Eutychus, "and the appetite is larger."

The room laughed. Corvus laughed too, a beat late, and lifted his cup, and the thing was done: he had been in her house for an hour and had eaten her food and laughed at her jokes and there were eleven men who would swear to it.

That was when Publius Vettius came in.

He had not been invited. He came through the curtain from the atrium with two of his father's freedmen behind him and a wreath on his head that he had plainly put on in the street, and he stopped at the end of the room and looked along the couches, and the room went quiet in the way it had not gone quiet for Corvus.

"Domina," he said. "I heard there was a dinner."

"There is always a dinner," Antonia said. "There is a couch at the end, if you have come to eat. If you have come to make a scene, there is the street."

"I have come to see the famous house." He walked along behind the couches, and men moved their feet out of his way. "My father saw it. He saw it once, and it killed him. I thought I should see it too, before it is pulled down."

"It will not be pulled down."

"Everything is pulled down eventually." He stopped at the middle couch, behind Corvus, and looked down at him. "Corvus. You are a long way from the Palatine."

"I am dining with friends."

"Are you." Publius looked around the room with his chin up, and then he did a thing that Antonia had not expected: he laughed. It was a real laugh, ugly and short. "Eleven of you. Eleven senators and knights and men of affairs, eating turbot in a whore's dining room, and not one of you will look at me." He spread his hands. "Look at me. Go on. I am the son of the man who died in this room. I am the heir. You were all at the funeral and you all said what a loss it was and now you are eating his last dinner with the woman who killed him."

"She did not kill him," said Blandus.

"Did she not?" Publius turned. "You are a brave man, Blandus. I did not know that about you. I thought you were a man who owed money to everyone in this room and had never in his life said a thing that cost him anything."

"That is enough," Corvus said.

"It is not enough. It is nowhere near enough." Publius came around the couch and stood in front of Antonia, close, and the smell of him was wine and something sour. "I know what you are doing. You are putting on a dinner so that the city will say the prefect's inquiry is a joke. I know. I have done the same thing myself, at the house of a man I wanted to ruin; you invite him to dinner and you sit him next to a consul and by the morning he is your friend and no one can touch him." He leaned closer. "It will not work with me. I have nothing to lose. My father is dead and my name is a joke and my inheritance is a house full of furniture that nobody wants, and I have been sitting in it for a month listening to the walls. Do you know what that does to a man? It makes him free."

Antonia looked up at him.

"Sit down," she said, "and have some wine."

"I do not want your wine."

"Then sit down and have some water, and stop making a fool of yourself in front of men who will remember it." She said it quietly, and something in her voice made him stop. "You are the son of a consular. You have a name that three emperors respected, and you are standing in a dining room at midnight with a wreath on your head, shouting at a woman. This is not the son your father raised. Sit down."

Publius stared at her. His mouth worked.

Then he sat down, at the end of the third couch, and somebody gave him a cup, and he drank it, and the room began, slowly and with a great deal of noise, to talk again.

It was an hour before Antonia got him alone.

She found him in the little garden behind the kitchen, standing by the myrtle box with his cup in his hand and his wreath slipping down over one ear.

"You should go home," she said.

"I am not a child."

"No. You are a man of thirty who has done a wicked thing and is discovering that it does not make him feel any better, and who has been drinking for a month because he cannot sleep." She stood in front of him. "I know what you did."

He looked at her.

"You do not."

"I know you paid a steward of the house of Corvus to have your father's cup prepared. I know the man who carried it is dead in the Tiber. I know the man who bought the poison is sitting in that room with a cup in his hand, and that he is going to be consul in the spring." She watched his face come apart. "And I know that you are going to be tried for it, unless you do something."

"Tried?" He laughed, and it cracked in the middle. "By whom? By the prefect? The prefect is Corvus's friend. The Senate will not try the son of a consular for the murder of his own father; it would be a scandal, and there is nothing in this city that men fear more than a scandal, and I have known that since I was a boy." He drank. "Nobody will try me. That is the worst of it. I did it, and nobody will try me, and I will live in that house with the furniture and the walls until I die."

"You will be tried," Antonia said, "if I am tried first."

He stopped with the cup halfway to his mouth.

"Think," she said. "The panel meets. The girl gives her evidence. Maro wants a conviction and does not much care whose, and the prefect wants the whole thing finished. If I am convicted of killing your father, the case is closed, and the man who did it is never looked for. That is what Corvus wants and it is what Maro wants and it is what you want, and it is what will happen, unless—"

"Unless what?"

"Unless I am acquitted," Antonia said. "And then the panel must look for someone else. And the first thing they will find is a steward who took money, and the second is the man who paid him."

Publius put the cup down on the rim of the myrtle box.

"You are threatening me."

"I am telling you arithmetic. You are a man who has spent a month in a house with the walls, and you have not thought it through, because men like you never think it through; you think only as far as the thing you want and then you stop, and the world goes on without you." She stepped closer. "Here is what I will do. I will not name you. Not at the panel, not in the street, not anywhere. You will keep your name and your house and your furniture and your life, and you will go home tonight and you will never come to this house again, and in the autumn you will go to your estate in Campania and stay there for a year, and when you come back the whole thing will be a bad memory that people do not mention."

"And if I do not?"

"Then I will name you," Antonia said, "in front of the prefect, with the steward's name and the poison-seller's name and the mark on the wrist of the dead man, and I will not be believed, and I will be convicted, and I will be dead within the year. But before I am dead I will have said your name in a room full of men, and there is no trial in the world that survives a thing being said out loud. You will not be convicted. You will simply be the man who was named. And you will live in that house with the walls, and every man who comes to dinner will look at you and think it, and your sons will hear it in the street."

Publius Vettius looked at her for a long moment.

Then he picked up his cup and drank the rest of it, and set it down, and took off his wreath and dropped it on the ground.

"I will go to Campania," he said.

"Go in the morning."

"I will go in the morning." He stopped at the garden door. "Domina. When you are dead—and you will be dead, whatever happens at the panel, because they have decided it—will you tell me one thing? Did you kill my father?"

"No."

He looked at her, and nodded slowly, and went out through the kitchen and into the street, and Antonia stood in the garden with her hand on the myrtle box for a long time.

She went to the Palatine at the second hour of the night.

She did not plan it. She had gone up to her room to take off the pearls and had found that she could not sit down; she had walked the length of the corridor three times and stood at the window and looked at the black ridge of the hill above the roofs, and then she had put on a dark mantle and taken a lamp and gone out by the kitchen door.

The streets were quiet. The dinner was still going on behind her; she could hear the noise of it three streets away. She went up the Clivus Victoriae with her hood up, and the lamp made a small yellow circle around her feet, and she passed the temple of Apollo and the house of Augustus and the great blank wall of the imperial palace, and she came to the house with the plain door and the fig tree.

The door was open a hand's breadth.

She stood outside it for a long time. The lamp guttered in the wind and she sheltered it with her hand, and she thought about turning around, and she did not turn around, and then she pushed the door and went in.

The atrium was dark, and it smelled the same.

That was the thing that undid her. It had been seventeen years and the house had been sold and resold and let out to tenants and used for storage and, at some point, for something worse; the floor was cracked and the paint was gone from the walls and there was rubbish in the impluvium and a smell of cats. But the shape of it was the same, and the smell was the same, which was wax and old wood and the ghost of the incense that had been burned at the shrine every morning for four hundred years.

She went through the atrium to the little room behind it.

The shrine was still there. The niche was black with soot, and the painted figures of the gods had been scratched out by somebody's knife, and the altar was broken at one corner, and there was dust on everything, but it was there.

She stood in front of it with the lamp in her hand and looked at the place on the floor where she had knelt.

"Seventeen years," said Corvus, from the doorway, "and I still cannot come into this room without expecting to see it."

She did not turn around.

"How did you know I was here?"

"I followed you. You are not difficult to follow; you walk as though the street belongs to you." He came in and stood beside her, and the two lamps made the shadows move. "I have been coming here for seventeen years. Not often. Twice a year, perhaps. The tenants think I am the owner, and I am, in a way; I bought the lease through a freedman three years after my father died, and I have kept it empty since." He looked around the room. "I do not know why."

"Yes, you do."

"Do not."

"You keep the house empty and you keep the stylus in a box and you tell yourself it is penance, and it is not penance, it is a museum. You have made a little museum of the worst night of your life and you come here twice a year and look at it and feel something, and you call that guilt, and it is not guilt. Guilt is when you do something about it." She turned to face him. "Guilt is what I have been doing for seventeen years, and I did not do anything about it either, so I am not better than you. But I have not kept a museum."

Corvus looked at the altar.

"What happened?" he said.

"You were there."

"I was there for the end of it. I want to know what happened before."

She was silent for a long time.

"I was twenty-three," she said. "I had been in this house since I was seventeen. You bought me at Puteoli for nine thousand sesterces, and you gave me to your father's household as a waiting woman, and you taught me to write, and you were kind to me for a year, and then you were not kind to me, and then you were nothing to me at all, because you had begun to be a man in the Forum and a man in the Forum cannot be seen to be kind to a slave." She held the lamp steady. "Your father took me when I was nineteen. He did not ask. He was the master of the house and I was a thing in it, and he took me in the little room by the stairs, and after that he took me when he wanted me, which was not often, because he was old and because he did not like me. He liked to hurt me. He had a strap with a name on it, and he would have the boy bring it, and he would do it himself, and he would talk while he did it, about the family, and about the Republic, and about how the world was going to the dogs."

Corvus's face in the lamplight was grey.

"I did not know," he said.

"You knew. You were in the house. There is no house in Rome where a thing like that is a secret; the slaves know, and the cook knows, and the boy who carries the water knows, and the son of the house knows, and he knows it the way a man knows there is a leak in the roof, which is to say that he knows and he has decided not to go up and look." She turned back to the shrine. "On the last night he had me brought here. To the shrine. He said it was fitting, because I had been a bad servant and the gods should see. He had the strap, and he used it until my back was open, and then he had me on the floor in front of the altar. And there was a writing box on the altar, with his pens in it, because he had been writing letters in the afternoon."

"I remember."

"You came in. You stood in the doorway. You were twenty-four years old and you had a cup in your hand and you did not move." She heard her own voice, very flat, very quiet. "I looked at you. I want you to know that. I looked at you and I thought: he will stop it. He is the son of the house and he is a man now and he will stop it. And you stood there for three heartbeats and you did nothing at all, and then your father turned his head, and I reached up and took the stylus, and I put it in his throat."

The lamp guttered. Somewhere in the empty house a shutter moved in the wind.

"I have never been sorry," Antonia said. "That is the thing I have never said to anyone. I have been sorry for a great many things in my life, and I have been afraid, and I have been ashamed, and I have never once been sorry that he died with his own pen in his throat."

Corvus put his hand over his eyes.

"I was a coward," he said.

"Yes."

"I have told myself for seventeen years that I came in at the end, that it was already done, that there was nothing I could have done." He took his hand away. "I could have stopped it. I could have said one word. He would have beaten me and he would have done it anyway, but I could have said the word, and I did not say it, and I have been not saying it ever since."

"Yes."

"And then I sent you away."

"You sent me away with a purse and a letter and a lie, and you told me that if I ever spoke of it you would see me dead, and I believed you, because I was twenty-three and I had just killed a consul and I did not know which way was up." She set the lamp down on the broken altar. "And then I built a house, and I made myself rich, and I have spent seventeen years being afraid of you in the back of my mind, and the amusing thing is that I have been afraid of the wrong thing. I thought you would come and kill me. You never came. You were not afraid of me at all. You were afraid that I would look at you the way I looked at you in this room."

Corvus was quiet for a long time.

"What do you want?" he asked at last.

"I want to live," Antonia said, "and I want my house, and I want the women in it to be safe, and I want the man who killed my boy to be punished. I have wanted those things for a month and I have got nowhere, because I have been fighting the way a woman fights, with dinners and lists and favours. I am finished with that." She picked up the lamp. "I am going to tell the truth."

"At the panel?"

"At the panel. All of it. The girl, the cup, the steward, the poison, the money, your son's messenger, the boy in the yard. And when they ask me how I know what a steward of the Claudii Corvi was doing, I will tell them that I know because I was in this house for six years, and they will ask why, and I will tell them that too."

Corvus's head came up.

"You cannot."

"I can."

"It was seventeen years ago. There is no proof. My father died of a fit, the physicians said so, the family said so, the Senate said so—"

"There is the stylus," Antonia said.

He stared at her.

"You have it in a box in your study under the letters from your brother. You have had it for seventeen years. You took it off the altar before the slaves came in and you wrapped it in a handkerchief and put it in your sleeve, and you have never been able to throw it away." She smiled at him, and there was nothing kind in it. "Bring it to the panel, Corvus. Bring it and hold it up and say: *this is the pen my father was writing with when he died, and the woman who put it in his throat is standing in front of you.* Do it, and I will hang, and the city will have its scandal, and your consulship will be gone by the evening. Or do not do it, and I will tell my story without it, and half the city will believe me and the other half will say I am a liar and a whore, and your name will be in every mouth in Rome for the rest of your life."

"You would destroy yourself to destroy me."

"I would destroy myself to be free of you," Antonia said. "You have been in my head for seventeen years. I have been careful because of you. I have been quiet because of you. I have never married and never had a child and never once in seventeen years said out loud what happened in this room, and it has been like carrying a stone in my chest, and I am forty years old and I am tired." She went to the door. "Keep your stylus. Keep your museum. I am going to go and live in the world."

She was in the atrium when he spoke.

"Antonia."

She stopped.

"I did not know about the boy," he said. "I want you to believe that. Whatever else I have done, I did not know about the boy."

She stood with her back to him for a moment.

"I believe you," she said.

And then she went out through the plain door into the street, and the lamp went with her, and behind her the house of the Claudii Corvi stood in the dark with its broken altar and its scratched-out gods.
