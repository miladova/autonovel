# Chapter Nineteen

## Antonia

They put her in the same cell, and the lamp was lit this time, and there was a stool and a jug of water, which she understood to be a courtesy offered by a man who expected her to be dead by the morning and did not want to be remembered for meanness.

She sat on the bench with the stylus in her hand.

She turned the stylus over in her fingers.

It was bronze, four and a half inches long, with a flat end for smoothing the wax and a point for writing, and it had cost perhaps two sesterces in a shop in the Argiletum seventeen years before, and a man had bought it for a girl of seventeen who could not write her own name.

She remembered the room. It was the little room by the stairs, with a bed and a chest and a stool and a window the size of a bread board, and he had sat on the stool with his knees almost touching hers and had put the tablet on her lap, and had said, *this is an A. It is the first letter of your name. No, do not hold it like a knife; hold it like a spoon.*

She had learned to write in a fortnight. She had been quick; he had said so; he had said, *you are quicker than my brother, and my brother had a tutor from Rhodes.*

And then she had learned to read, and had read everything in the house, and had found the accounts, and had understood for the first time that the money in the strongbox was not a hoard but a set of agreements, and that each agreement was a person who owed and a person who was owed, and that she was, in the accounts, a line.

She had never told him that. She had never told anyone that the first thing she read in her life was the page on which she was written down as property.

Her father had been a grain factor in the Subura, and he had hanged himself in his shop when she was eleven because he had been ruined in a lawsuit and could not face the men he owed. She remembered the shop. She remembered the smell of it, which was dust and grain and mouse, and she remembered the men who came the day after and took the sacks out one by one and wrote them down in a book.

She had been sold at fourteen by her guardian, who was a cousin, and who had taken the money and gone to Africa.

*Everything in this city is a line in a book*, she thought. *My father was a line that went to nothing and hanged himself. I was a line that was sold. The boy was eight hundred sesterces. The girl was eleven thousand. The house was fifty-one thousand, and the silver was nineteen, and the mirror was ninety.*

*And tomorrow I am going to stand up in a basilica and tell them that the lines are all wrong.*

The noise from the Forum came through the barred window in a way that was worse than noise: it was a sound with no shape to it, a roar that rose and fell and rose again, and every so often there would be a shout that was almost a word, and then the roar would take it back. She had heard that sound twice in her life. Once when she was nine, at a fire in the Subura, and once at the games, when a man had refused to die.

At the second hour of the night the bolt went back.

She thought it would be Corvus. It was not Corvus. It was a small old woman in a dark stola with a slave behind her and a guard who would not meet Antonia's eyes, and she came into the cell and stood in the middle of the floor and looked around it as though she were pricing the furniture.

"Claudia Junia," Antonia said.

"You know me."

"I have seen you once, in the atrium of your house, when I was seventeen and you looked at me the way a woman looks at a dog that has got into the kitchen." Antonia did not stand. "What do you want?"

"I want to talk to you about my son."

"He is forty-one years old."

"He is my son," Claudia Junia said, "and he has been a fool since he was a boy, and he has been a good fool, which is the worst kind, and today he stood up in a basilica and told six hundred men that a girl had been sent away from his father's house with a purse, and by tomorrow morning the whole city will have the rest of it." She sat down on the stool, which put her below Antonia, and Antonia understood that this was deliberate. "They will come for him. The prefect will report to the emperor, and the Senate will be asked to consider whether a man who was in the room when his father was killed, and who concealed it for seventeen years, is fit to be consul. He will not be consul. He may be expelled from the Senate. He may be tried."

"Yes."

"You do not care."

"I care," Antonia said. "I have cared about your son for twenty-two years, and it has cost me everything I have, and I am finished caring. That is not the same as not caring. It is worse."

The old woman looked at her.

"I wrote to you," Antonia said. "Eleven years ago, when I bought the house. I wrote to you and I said that I had not spoken and would not speak and that I wanted nothing from you, and I said it because I had a house and a name of my own and I wanted you to know that I had not come to beg." She turned the stylus over in her fingers. "You never answered."

"No."

"Did you keep the letter?"

Claudia Junia was silent for a moment.

"Yes," she said. "I kept it. I have read it perhaps once a year. I have never been able to decide whether it was a kindness or a threat."

"It was both," Antonia said. "I was thirty years old and I had just bought a house with money I had made from men, and I wanted to be forgiven by a woman who had never spoken to me, and I wanted her to be afraid of me. That is what a person is at thirty."

The old woman almost smiled.

"I have come to make you an offer," she said.

"Of course you have."

"Tomorrow the panel will give its verdict. You will be acquitted of the murder, because the murderer has confessed in open court and because the city has a new story to tell. You will be convicted of the rest, because a woman in your position is always convicted of the rest, and the prefect will fine you and close the house and perhaps send you out of Italy for a few years, because he will want the city quiet." She folded her hands. "That is what will happen if nothing is done. It will ruin you, and it will ruin my son, and it will be in the mouths of every man in Rome for a year, and in ten years it will be a joke that children tell in the street."

"And if something is done?"

"If you stand up tomorrow and say that you lied today," Claudia Junia said, "and that the story of the shrine was a story told to you by a slave who wished to injure the family, and that you have never been in the house on the Palatine in your life—" she paused— "then the prefect will be able to record that the woman's evidence was the raving of a hysteric, and there will be no report to the emperor, and my son will be consul in the spring, and I will pay you four hundred thousand sesterces and a house at Baiae, and you will leave Italy for five years and come back a rich woman with a new name."

Antonia looked at her.

"That is a great deal of money," she said.

"It is everything I have."

"And you would give it to me."

"I would give it to a woman I have hated for seventeen years," Claudia Junia said, "because it is the only thing I have that will buy my son's life, and because I am sixty-three years old and I have spent my whole life keeping this family standing, and I am not going to stop now because the price has gone up."

Antonia stood up.

She walked to the barred window and looked out at the dark, at the wall of the building opposite and the small square of sky above it, and the noise of the city came in, and there was something in the noise now that was not a roar but a rhythm.

"No," she said.

"You are a fool."

"Perhaps." Antonia turned. "I have been offered a great many things in my life by people who wanted me to be quiet, and I have taken most of them, and I have told myself each time that I was being practical. I took a purse from your son when I was twenty-three. I have taken money from senators and favours from men and I have smiled at people I despised, and I have built a house on it, and I have told myself that it was the only way a woman like me could live in a city like this." She put the stylus down on the bench. "And then a boy of twelve was strangled in a yard because he had seen something, and I found that there was a thing I would not sell. It came as a surprise to me. I am forty years old and I did not know it until this year."

"You will destroy him."

"He destroyed himself," Antonia said. "He has been destroying himself for seventeen years, in a room in a house on the Palatine, twice a year, with a pen in a box. I am only the thing he did it to."

Claudia Junia stood up.

"You will be alone," she said. "Do you understand that? Tomorrow you will be convicted, and the house will be sold, and the women will be sold or scattered, and the men who ate at your table will cross the street to avoid you, and there will be no one. A woman of your condition has no family, no patron, no name, and no friends who will stand by her when she is finished. I have been alone in a house for twenty-four years and I know what it is. You will not last five years at Baiae. You will be dead in three."

"Then I will be dead in three," Antonia said, "and I will have been myself for the whole of it."

The old woman looked at her for a long moment.

Then she turned and went out, and the guard shut the door, and the bolt went home.

It was perhaps an hour later that the light changed.

Antonia was sitting on the bench with her back against the wall and her eyes shut, and she noticed it first as a warmth on her face, and then as a flicker behind her eyelids, and she opened her eyes and the cell was full of a faint orange light that came and went.

She went to the window.

The wall of the building opposite was lit from above, and the small square of sky was the colour of a coal, and there was smoke in it, and the noise of the city had changed: it was no longer a roar but a crowd, with voices in it, and somebody was screaming.

She could not see the fire. The window looked north, and the Vicus Tuscus was to the south, and she could see only the light of it on the wall and the smoke going up over the roofs.

She stood at the bars and watched the light.

*The vigiles will be there*, she thought. *They will have the buckets. It is a stone house with a fountain in the middle of it. It will not burn.*

And then she thought: *the contracts*.

They were in the strongbox in her room: the purchase documents of eleven women and three men, the manumission records, the loan tablets, the deeds. Everything she owned was in that box and everything those women were was in that box, and if the house burned the box would burn, and a slave whose papers are burned is not free; she is a person with no evidence, which in Rome is worse.

She stood at the window until the light went down.

She watched the light on the wall until it went.

There was a small window high up, and the wall opposite was white plaster, and the light from the fire came over the roofs and moved on the plaster and then faded, and the cell got cold.

She thought about the house burning. She thought about the papers in the strongbox, and the deeds, and the purchase documents, and the manumission records, and the loan tablets with the names of senators on them, and she thought: *if the box burns, then every woman in that house is a slave again with no evidence, and every debt is unprovable, and seventeen years of accounts go up in smoke.*

And then she thought: *and if the box survives, I will have a house and no money and eleven women, and no way to feed them.*

She laughed, once, in the dark.

That was the thing she had not expected about the worst night of her life: that somewhere in the middle of it she would find something funny. She had spent seventeen years building a thing that could be written down, and a crowd from the Forum had come up the street with a torch and settled the question of what a written thing is worth.

*Nothing,* she thought. *It is worth nothing at all. A man may be owed sixty thousand sesterces on a tablet and be turned out of his house by a man in the Velabrum, and a woman may own eleven people on a tablet and be sold herself by a prefect who is tired.*

*And yet.*

*And yet Tiro went back in for it.*

She sat in the dark and thought about that for a long time.

In the morning they came for her at the second hour and took her up into the basilica, and the hall was half empty, and the men in it were quiet, and there was a smell of smoke in the air that had got into the building from the street.

Pedo read the verdict in a flat voice.

On the charge of the murder of Gaius Vettius Proculus: not guilty. The panel found that the poison had been procured by Faustus, freedman of Gaius Claudius Corvus, at the instigation of Publius Vettius Proculus, and that the woman Antonia Cassia had no part in it.

On the charge of keeping a house of ill repute: guilty. The house on the Vicus Tuscus was confiscated to the city. She was fined one hundred thousand sesterces.

On the charge of the corruption of morals: guilty. She was relegated from Rome and Italy for five years, and forbidden to come within a hundred miles of the city.

The hall made no sound at all.

"Do you wish to say anything?" Pedo asked.

"No," Antonia said.

"Then the court is dismissed." He gathered his papers, and then he looked up. "Domina. A word."

They took her into the small room behind the platform, and Pedo stood with his back to her and his hands behind his back, and for a moment he did not speak.

"The fire," he said.

"Whose house?"

"Yours." He turned. "It began in the doorway at the third hour of the night. There was a crowd in the street. The vigiles were at the granaries; there had been a fire there the week before and half the cohort was on the river. By the time they came, the roof had gone." He paused. "The women got out. All of them. The steward took them out through the kitchen and into the alley, and then he went back in."

Antonia stood very still.

"For the strongbox," she said.

"For the strongbox. He came out with it. He was on the step when the floor went, and the beam came down on him, and they brought him out into the street and he was alive for a little while." Pedo's voice was quiet. "I am told he asked for you."

"Where is he?"

"In the yard of the vigiles' station on the Aventine. They have laid him in the shed with the others. There are four of them; the fire took a woman in the house next door and her child, and a man in the street." He came around the table. "I have signed the order for your release. You will be out of the city by nightfall. That is not a thing I can change; the verdict is the verdict, and if I let you stay until the funeral I will have half the Senate asking why." He stopped in front of her. "I am sorry."

"You are not."

"I am," said Pedo, "and it is the only thing I have said today that I did not have to say. Take it or leave it."

She went out of the basilica into the Forum.

The crowd was there, but it was a different crowd now: the holiday crowd had gone home, and the people in the square were the people who always come after a thing is finished, the scavengers and the curious and the men who want to see a famous woman walk out of a court in the morning. They did not shout at her. A few of them looked at her, and one woman said something that Antonia did not hear, and a man spat on the ground as she passed, not at her, at the ground in front of her, which is what a man does when he wants to be seen doing something and does not want to be arrested for it.

She went to the Vicus Tuscus first.

The street was open. That was the first thing: that the street was open, and you could see from the corner of the Velabrum all the way to the top, because the house that had stood on the corner for seventeen years was not standing any more.

The walls were still there. That was the strange thing about a fire in a stone house: the walls stand. The west wall and the north wall were black and cracked and the roof had fallen in behind them, and through the gap where the door had been you could see the sky, and the whole of the inside was a heap of black timber and ash and broken tile.

There was a smell of wet charcoal and burnt wool and something else, sweet and foul, that she did not want to identify.

The vigiles had put a rope across the front and a notice on a stick. Two men of the cohort were sitting on the step of the fuller's shop opposite with their sticks across their knees, and one of them was eating something out of a cloth.

Antonia stood at the rope and looked in.

She could see the fountain. It was still there, in the middle of what had been the courtyard: the basin was full of ash and broken tile and the bronze myrtle tree was bent and black and the leaves were gone, but it was standing.

She had bought that fountain in the second year, from a man in the Argiletum who had taken it from a house that was being sold for debt. It had cost her four thousand sesterces, which had been a great deal of money then, and she had stood in the courtyard while they installed it and had said, *the leaves should be myrtle, because the house will be called the Myrtle*, and Tiro had said, *why not call it something without leaves*, and she had said, *because a house with a name is a house that people remember.*

"What happened?" she said.

The guard looked up.

"Fire," he said.

"I know it was a fire. Who started it?"

The guard chewed.

"A crowd came up from the Forum after the basilica yesterday," he said. "They had been at the trial. There was a man with a torch. We were on the river; there was a fire at the granaries the week before and half the cohort was down there." He wiped his mouth. "By the time we got here the roof was gone. There was nothing to do but keep it off the neighbours."

"How many dead?"

"Four. A woman in the next house and her child, and a man in the street, and a slave from this house who went back in for a box." The guard looked at the ruin. "He came out with it. He was on the step when the floor went."

Antonia stood at the rope for a long time.

"Domina," the guard said. "You cannot stand here."

"I am not standing here," she said. "I am leaving."

She walked up the street to the corner and turned and looked back once at the two black walls and the sky through them and the bent myrtle in the ash.

*Seventeen years,* she thought. *Fifty-one thousand sesterces, and eleven women, and a boy of twelve, and a cook's rolling pin, and a fountain with bronze leaves, and a box of paper, and a man on the Palatine who kept a pen.*

*And a crowd from the Forum with a torch, who did not know my name, and who went home to supper.*

She walked to the Aventine.

The vigiles' station was a low building with a yard and a shed, and the shed was open, and there were four bodies in it on boards, covered with cloth. The man at the door knew who she was; the whole city knew by then. He stood aside.

Tiro was on the third board.

They had washed him. His face was grey and there was a burn along the side of his neck and his hands were folded on his chest by somebody who had taken trouble over it. He looked fifty-two years old, which he had never looked in life.

Antonia knelt down beside him.

"You fool," she said.

The strongbox was on the floor by the door, blackened and warped, with the lock broken open. Somebody had gone through it and taken the money, and left the tablets: the deeds, the loan records, the purchase documents, the manumission papers of eleven women and three men.

She picked up the papers and held them against her chest.

"He asked for you," said the man at the door. "He said: *tell her the box is out.* And then he said something else, and I did not understand it, and he said it twice." The man rubbed his jaw. "He said: *the boy at Tibur.*"

Antonia closed her eyes.

"He has a son," she said. "At Tibur. In a smithy by the gate. He is fourteen and he is free, or he thinks he is free, and his father is on a board in a shed on the Aventine."

The man looked at the floor.

"Do you want us to send word?"

"No," Antonia said. "I will go."

She went out of the shed into the yard, and the sun was high, and the city went on beyond the wall, and somewhere a woman was selling figs.

Prisca was waiting in the street with Meroe and Junilla and the two kitchen girls and the washerwoman and the girl who carried the water. They had come up from the fire with nothing but what they stood in, and they stood in a huddle in the street with their hair down and their faces smudged, and when they saw her they did not speak.

Antonia stood in front of them.

"The house is gone," she said. "The court has taken the ground and the walls and everything that was in them. I have been fined a hundred thousand sesterces and sent out of Italy for five years, and I will be out of the city by nightfall." She looked at each of them. "But I have these."

She held up the papers.

"Your contracts. All of them. Every one of you is free from this morning, and no one in this city can sell you, and if a man tries, you will show him these, and if he does not believe you, you will go to the prefect's office and ask for the clerk who wrote the verdict and he will remember." She looked down at the papers in her hands. "Tiro went back into the house for these. He is dead. He was a thief and a murderer and a fool and he was the best man I have ever known, and he died for a box of paper."

Meroe began to cry. Then the kitchen girls. Then Prisca, who never cried, sat down on the kerb in the street and put her face in her hands.

"What will we do?" Junilla said.

"You will do what free women do," Antonia said. "You will find work, or you will come with me, and I will not lie to you: I have nothing. The fine will take the money in the chest and the house is gone and I have four hundred sesterces in my purse. But I am going to Surrentum, and there is a woman there who sells wine, and there is a town on a bay where a woman might start again, and there is a boat at Ostia that will take us for nothing if we work the passage." She put the papers in her sleeve. "I am not asking. I am telling you what I am doing, and any of you who wishes to come is welcome, and any of you who wishes to stay in Rome is free to stay, and I will give you what I have to give and I will not hold it against you."

There was a silence.

"I will come," said Prisca, from the kerb.

"And I," said Meroe.

"And I," said Junilla.

The kitchen girls said nothing, and looked at the ground, and Antonia understood that they had families in the Subura and were fifteen years old and did not want to go to the end of Italy with a ruined woman, and she said, "That is right. That is the right thing. Go home, both of you, and take the papers, and if there is trouble, send for me at Surrentum."

They went.

That afternoon Antonia went to Tibur.

She walked. There was no money for a horse and no time to find one. She walked the four hours up the Via Nomentana in the clothes she had worn in the basilica, with the papers in her sleeve and the dust on her hem, and she came to the smithy at the bottom of the town by the gate in the late afternoon, and the boy was working the bellows.

She stood in the yard until he looked up.

"You are the domina," he said. "My father's mistress."

"Yes."

"Is he coming?"

Antonia looked at him: fourteen years old, with his mother's hands, and a burn on his forearm from the sparks, and his whole life in front of him.

"No," she said. "He is not coming."

She stayed the night in Tibur. She told the boy the whole of it, sitting on the wall by the well with the goat looking at them, and she did not soften it, and at the end she gave him the paper that Faustus had given Tiro and which Tiro had put inside his tunic and which had been found on him and brought to her with his knife and his purse.

"You are free," she said. "You have been free since the Kalends of August. Your father bought it for you, and he could not read the paper, and he was afraid that it was a lie, and he gave away something he should not have given to get it. That is who he was."

The boy looked at the paper in the failing light, and turned it over, and turned it over again.

"I cannot read it," he said.

"Then I will read it to you," Antonia said. "And then I will teach you to read it yourself, if you want, and if your master will let you go, and if you will come with me to a town on a bay where there is a woman who sells wine and a great deal of work to be done."

The boy looked at her.

"Was he a good man?" he asked.

Antonia thought about it.

"He was a man who did one good thing at the end," she said, "and a great many bad ones before it, and he was sorry for the bad ones, and he did not know how to say so. That is more than most men manage."

The boy nodded slowly.

"I will come," he said.
