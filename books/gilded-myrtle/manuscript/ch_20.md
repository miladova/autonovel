# Chapter Twenty

## Tiro

After the basilica, the house belonged to the city, and the city sent a man to sit in the atrium and write down what was in it.

He came in the afternoon with a clerk and a lamp and a stool, and he sat in the atrium for four hours writing, and the women stood in the kitchen and listened to him turn the pages.

Tiro stood in the doorway and watched him.

"Two couches, citron wood," the man said to his clerk. "One table. Eleven lamps. Six curtains, Egyptian, of which two are—" he held one up— "of which two are good."

"They are all good," said Meroe, from the kitchen door.

The man looked at her and wrote something down.

"Eleven slaves," he said. "Names."

"Not today," Tiro said.

"Tomorrow, then. The prefect wants the list before the trial resumes."

"You have the house," Tiro said. "You will have the list when the trial is over and not before, and if you write down one name today I will take that stool and put it through the window."

The man looked at him for a moment, and then at the scar, and then he wrote: *slaves, to be listed.*

He went away at dusk.

The women cooked a meal in the kitchen and nobody ate it. Prisca put the plates out and Meroe sat down and then got up again, and the two kitchen girls sat at the end of the table and whispered, and Junilla went up onto the roof to look at the street and came down and said, "There are men at the corner."

"How many?"

"Six. Maybe eight. They are not doing anything. They are standing."

Tiro went out to the step and looked.

There were eight of them at the corner of the Velabrum, and they were the sort of men who stand at corners in every city: men with nothing to do and a grievance they could not name. One of them had a stick. One of them, Tiro noticed, was the man who had sold fish at the corner for eleven years and had always been civil.

He went back inside.

"Prisca," he said. "Get the money out of the strongbox and put it in your apron. All of it."

"Tiro—"

"All of it. And the papers. And then go upstairs and put on two tunics and your oldest shoes and take the chest with the boy's things down to the kitchen." He looked at the women in the kitchen. "This is what we are going to do. At the second hour of the night, or sooner if they come, we are going out by the kitchen door and up the alley and into the Clivus and then to the house of the fuller's widow in the Subura, which is nine streets away and which none of them will think of. You will walk. You will not run. You will carry one thing each and no more. If a man speaks to you, you will not answer. If a man puts a hand on you, you will scream as loud as you can and keep walking."

"And you?" said Prisca.

"I will be behind you."

"Tiro—"

"Prisca. Get the money."


They came down with their bundles and their boxes and their arguments.

Meroe would not leave her contract. It was in a wooden case with a lock and she had carried it from Alexandria to Rome and she was not going to leave it in a house that was going to be sold by the court. Prisca would not leave the household accounts. The two kitchen girls would not leave a jar of pickled fish that they had made themselves in the spring. The washerwoman had a bronze mirror that had belonged to her mother, and the girl who carried the water had a bird in a cage.

"Leave the bird," Tiro said.

"He will die."

"Then he will die. Take him out of the cage and let him go in the kitchen."

"He will come back."

"Then he will come back to a burned house and he will learn something." Tiro took the cage out of her hands and opened the door and shook it, and the bird flew up into the dark of the kitchen and sat on a beam and looked at them all with enormous offence. "There. Now he is free and he is your problem no longer. Take the mirror. Leave the fish."

They left the fish.

He got them out through the kitchen door half an hour before midnight, one at a time, into the alley, and the alley was empty, and Niger's cart was at the end of it with the tarpaulin up and one of the men sitting on the tail with a club across his knees.

Meroe went first, with her case. Then the girls. Then the washerwoman, who was sixty and could not walk fast, and whom Tiro took by the arm and half carried to the end of the alley. Then Junilla, who refused to go until the others were in, and who stood at the corner with her knife in her sleeve and her eyes on the street.

Prisca was last.

"You are coming," she said.

"I am coming."

"You are not coming."

"Prisca." He took her by the shoulders. "There is a box in her room with the papers in it. If it burns, eleven women are slaves again, and everything she has done for seventeen years is nothing. I am going to get it, and then I am going out through the kitchen and up the alley, and I will be at the fuller's widow's house before the fourth hour."

"Then I will wait for you at the corner."

"No. You will get on the cart."

"Tiro—"

"You will get on the cart," he said, "because if you do not, I will have to carry you, and I am fifty-two years old and I have a bad back, and I will drop you, and it will be undignified for both of us."

She looked at him.

"You are a terrible liar," she said.

"I know."

She kissed him on the cheek, which she had never done in eleven years, and got on the cart, and Niger's man put the tarpaulin down over her, and the cart went up the alley and turned into the Clivus and was gone.

He stood in the kitchen doorway for a moment with his hand on the frame.

Then he shut the door and put the bar across it and went to the stairs.


He stood in the kitchen and looked at the women: Meroe with her hair half braided, Junilla with her knife in her sleeve, the two girls, the washerwoman, the girl who carried the water. Six of them. Seven with Prisca. And the boy's chest, and the money, and eleven sets of papers.

He thought, quite clearly, in the way a man thinks when he has been in a burning building before: *I cannot carry all of this.*

He went upstairs and got the boy's chest.

Niger came at the ninth hour with two men and a cart.

He came to the kitchen door and knocked twice and once, which was the signal they had used in the barracks twenty-six years before, and Tiro let him in and shut the door behind him.

"I heard," Niger said.

"Who told you?"

"Everybody. It was in the Emporium by the fourth hour. They are saying the prefect is going to let her go and the crowd is going to do the job for him." He looked around the kitchen. "You have women here."

"Six."

"How many can walk?"

"All of them. None of them will want to."

Niger nodded.

"I have the cart at the end of the alley with a tarpaulin on it. Two men on it. We can take four under the tarpaulin as far as the Esquiline and then they can walk to the Subura." He put his hand on Tiro's shoulder. "You come too."

"I will come when they are out."

"Tiro."

"I will come when they are out."

Niger looked at him for a moment and then took his hand away.

"You were always like this," he said. "In the barracks. When there was a thing to be done you were the last one out of the door." He went to the back door and looked out into the alley. "If it goes bad, get onto the roof of the fuller's shop and go along the ridge to the corner. There is a builder's ladder there, and I will leave a man at the bottom of it until the fourth hour."

"Thank you."

"Do not thank me. I am doing it because you are the only man in Rome who ever gave me half his bread." Niger opened the door. "And because a woman who keeps a house of women and pays them and does not sell them is a better Christian than half the people I know, and I am not even a Christian."

He went out.

Prisca came down with the money in her apron and the papers in a cloth, and Tiro took the papers and put them inside his tunic, and then he did the thing that he would think about afterward, in the street, with the beam on him: he took the money out of her apron and put it in a bag and put the bag in her hands.

"Take it."

"You take it."

"No." He closed her fingers on it. "If they stop you, they will search you for money and they will find it and they will let you go, because a crowd wants money more than it wants a woman. If they find papers on you they will burn them, because papers are a thing a crowd does not understand and therefore hates. So you carry the money and I carry the papers, and if one of us gets out, the women are free."

Prisca looked at him.

"You are not going to get out," she said.

"I am going to get out."

"Tiro."

"Prisca." He took her by the shoulders. "I have done a bad thing this year and I have not told her and I am not going to tell her, and I am going to get every woman in this house out of it if I have to carry them one at a time, and then I am going to walk out of that door and up the alley and I am going to be at the fuller's widow's house by the fourth hour, and she will shout at me for being late, and I will sit down and eat something." He let go. "Now go and tell the women."

She went.

The crowd began to gather at the eleventh hour of the night, and by the second hour of the morning there were four hundred of them in the Vicus Tuscus.

They came from the Subura and the Velabrum and the river, and they were not a mob in the way that a mob is imagined; they were a great many people who had heard a story in a wine shop and had come to look at the house where it had happened. There were porters and water carriers and a woman with a basket of oranges and a dozen boys and a man with a performing dog. There were men who had lost money at the granaries and men who had been turned away from the door of some great house that morning. There were two men with a jar of wine and a good deal of opinions.

By the second hour they had begun to shout.

It was not about Vettius. That was the thing that Tiro understood at once, standing on the step with Junilla and two of the bearers, and it was the thing that frightened him. The shouting was not about a dead consul. It was about the woman who had killed a consul and been paid to keep quiet, and the family on the Palatine that had covered it up, and the fact that in this city a man could be murdered in his own shrine and the whole of the Senate could agree to call it a fit, and nothing would happen, and nobody would hang, and the only person who had been punished was a cook and a boy in a yard.

"They will burn us," Junilla said.

"They will not."

"Look at them."

He looked. There was a man at the front with a torch, and he was not a young man and he was not drunk, and he was holding the torch the way a man holds a tool.

"Get the women out," Tiro said.

"What?"

"Get them out. Now. The kitchen door and the alley, and up the Clivus toward the Velabrum, and do not run and do not stop for anything and do not come back." He took her by the shoulder. "Junilla. Listen to me. Prisca first; she will argue. Then Meroe. Then the girls. Count them at the top of the hill and count them again at the fountain. Do you hear me?"

"Yes."

"Go."

She went.

At the second hour, Tiro opened the door and went out onto the step.

Junilla caught at his arm. He shook her off.

There were four hundred people in the street, and the ones at the front were the ones who had been there longest, and the ones at the back were the ones who had come to see. Somebody had brought a torch. Somebody had brought a jar. There was a man with a performing dog on his shoulder, and the dog was barking, and the man was laughing.

Tiro stood on the step with his hands at his sides.

"I know some of you," he said.

The noise went down a little. He had a voice that carried; he had spent eleven years making himself heard over a crowd in an arena.

"I know you, Caius, you sell fish at the corner, and you have sold it to this house for eleven years and been paid on the day every time. I know you, mother, with the oranges, you have stood on that corner since I came to this street. I know the two of you at the back, you work the boats. I do not know the rest of you and I do not care." He took a step down. "There is nobody in this house but women. There is no money in it worth having and there is nothing in it that any of you need. The woman who owns it is not here. She is in a cell under the basilica and she will be tried in the morning, and whatever you think of her, the law will do what the law does, and it will do it whether you burn the house or not."

Somebody at the back shouted something.

"I did not hear that," Tiro said.

"He said the consul," somebody called. "He said she killed a consul and the Senate covered it up and the whole lot of you ought to be burned."

"The consul died seventeen years ago," Tiro said, "and if you want to be angry about it, go up the hill and be angry at the man who is to be consul in the spring, and see how far you get. He has a lictor. I have a door."

There was a movement in the crowd, the way there is a movement in a crowd when the front rank has decided and the back rank has not.

The fish seller looked at the ground.

"I am sorry, Tiro," he said.

"I know."

"Rent went up in the summer and there is no work on the river."

"I know."

"I am not going to be the one to do it."

"Then do not be here when it is done," Tiro said.

The fish seller went away, down the street, with his hands in his pockets. Two others went with him. The rest stayed.

Tiro stood on the step and looked at them for a moment, and then he went back inside and put the bar across and stood with his hand on it.

"That was brave," Junilla said.

"That was useless," said Tiro. "But it bought us ten minutes and eight people, and ten minutes is ten minutes."

The door went in at the third hour.

It was not a ram; it was eight men with a beam from the builder's yard at the corner, and they swung it four times, and the fourth time the bolt came out of the wood and the door fell inward and the crowd came in like water.

Tiro was standing in the atrium with a knife in his hand.

He was fifty-two years old, and he had been a gladiator for eleven years, and he had killed nine men in an arena, and he knew exactly what a crowd is. A crowd is not a man. You cannot frighten a crowd and you cannot reason with it and you cannot fight it; you can only be somewhere else.

He went up the stairs.

They were in the dining room by then, and he heard them: the crash of the couches going over, the smash of the silver, a woman's voice screaming with delight, a man shouting about the ceiling. Somebody had climbed onto the table and was hacking at the gilded coffers with a knife, and the gold was coming down in flakes.

The women were in the alley.

He could hear them through the kitchen door, and he stood at the top of the stairs and counted the voices, and he got to nine and then to eleven, and then he heard Prisca say, "Where is the boy? Where is Felix's chest?" and he remembered that the chest was still in the little room by the stairs, and the boy's knife was in it, and the stone with the hole in it, and the two knucklebones, and the rag with eleven sesterces in it that had been found in the plaster.

He went into the little room and got the chest.

He came out into the corridor with it under his arm and the smoke was already in the passage, and he went down the back stairs and out through the kitchen, and he put the chest in Prisca's arms in the alley and said, "Go. Go now."

"Tiro—"

"Go."

She went.

He stood in the alley and looked back at the house, and the smoke was coming out of the upper windows in two thick columns, and the light of the fire was on the wall of the house opposite, and the crowd was shouting inside his house.

The strongbox was in her room.

He stood there for perhaps five heartbeats and thought about it. He thought about the fire, and about the fact that a stone house with a fountain in it will not burn down in five minutes, and about the fact that the strongbox was on the second floor at the front and the fire was in the dining room at the back, and about the fact that a man who goes into a burning house for a box of paper is a fool.

And then he thought: *she will have nothing*.

Not the money. He did not care about the money. The money was in the chest and the chest was in Prisca's arms.

The papers were in the strongbox: the purchase documents of eleven women and three men, the manumission records, the loan tablets, the deeds of the two shops. He had watched her write them out over seventeen years, at that table, with that lamp. He had watched her buy women out of houses where they were beaten and put the papers in the box and say, *that is one more who cannot be sold.*

He went in.

He met a man on the stairs.

He was coming down with his arms full: two silver cups and a lamp and a folded curtain, and his face was black with soot and he was grinning like a boy. He saw Tiro and stopped.

"There is a fire," he said.

"I know."

"You should get out."

"I know." Tiro looked at the silver in his arms. "That is my mistress's."

The man looked down at the cups.

"She is not going to need it," he said.

"No," said Tiro. "She is not."

He stepped aside and let him go. He would think about that afterward; not the man, who was nothing, but the fact that he had stepped aside, that he had been in a burning house with a knife in his sleeve and a lifetime of violence in his hands and he had let a man walk past him with two silver cups because the cups did not matter and the box did.

The heat came up the stairwell in a wave.

The kitchen was full of smoke and he went through it on his hands and knees with his tunic over his mouth. The back stairs were clear. The corridor at the top was not: the fire had come through the ceiling of the dining room and into the passage, and there was a line of flame running along the top of the wall, and the smoke was rolling along the ceiling in a way that told him the roof was going.

He went under the smoke with his face a hand's breadth from the floor.
It was like crawling through a wall. The smoke had no top and no bottom; it was hot and it had a taste, and the taste was wood and pitch and the wool of the curtains and, underneath, something that he did not want to think about.

He kept his hand on the wall because the wall was the only thing that was where it had always been.

Her door was open. The room was full of a red light and there was smoke in it but the fire was not in it yet, and he got up and went to the strongbox and dragged it out from under the table.

It was heavy. It was iron-bound oak and it weighed as much as a man, and he got his arms around it and lifted it and it came off the floor by a hand's breadth and he dropped it.

He could not carry it.
He was a strong man. He had been stronger. He had carried a man out of the arena at Capua when he was twenty-two years old, and he had carried a beam at Ostia when he was forty, and it had taken two of them to lift a jar of wine off a cart in the summer, and he had done it alone because the carter was a boy.

He could not carry it now.

He stood in the middle of the room with the smoke stinging his eyes and his lungs full of it, and he thought, quite clearly, in the way that a man thinks when he is frightened: *the box or the papers.*

He took the key from the table and opened the box.

The lid came up and the smell of it came out: leather and wax and the dry dust of documents, and under that something else, which was the smell of her: the oil she used on her hair, and the cedar of the tablets, and a faint sourness that was the room.

He stood in the middle of her room with the box open in his hands and the fire coming along the corridor outside.

The money was on the top, in bags: eleven thousand sesterces in silver and gold. He left it. He lifted out the tablets and the rolls and the folded documents, and he stuffed them inside his tunic, against his skin, and there were a great many of them and they would not all fit, and he took the rest in his arms.

The ceiling of the corridor came down as he reached the door.

He went through it into a wall of heat and he could not see and he could not breathe, and he went down the back stairs on his back, sliding, with the papers against his chest and his elbows banging on the steps, and he came out into the kitchen and the kitchen was on fire.

The door was ten feet away.

He got up and ran.

He was on the step when the floor went.

It was not the beam that killed him. That was what he would have said if anyone had asked, and nobody asked. It was the beam that broke his back, and it came down across the doorway, and the whole of the front of the house came down with it, and he was thrown out into the street and the papers went everywhere.

He lay on his back in the Vicus Tuscus with the fire above him.

Somebody was pulling at him. There were faces over him, and hands, and a woman was saying something that he could not hear. The crowd had stopped shouting. The crowd had turned, the way a crowd turns, and the men who had been breaking the ceiling were coming out of the house with their arms over their heads, and somebody was shouting for water, and somewhere a long way off a bell was ringing.

Tiro looked up at the sky.

There was a great deal of smoke between him and it, and the smoke was orange at the edges, and beyond the smoke the sky was beginning to go grey.

He thought about the arena.

He thought about the boy with the net and the trident, who had slipped on the sand, and about the sound the crowd had made when he put his sword in.

He thought about his son at the bellows.

He had stood in the street outside the smithy four times in eight years and looked at the boy and gone away, and he had told himself each time that it was because he had nothing to say, and it had not been that. It had been because the boy looked at him with a face that said *you will go away again*, and because it was easier to go away than to stand there and be looked at.

He had bought the boy's freedom with a letter that was not his to give. He had stolen from the only person who had ever trusted him. He had killed a man in an alley and he had felt nothing, and then he had gone home and sat on his bed and shaken for an hour.

He was fifty-two years old. He had been a slave for thirty-five years and a free man for seventeen, and in seventeen years he had learned to read four words: his own name, her name, and *the old man first*, which he had copied out on a tablet from the papyrus, letter by letter, in the dark, so that he would know what he had given away.

He turned his head.

The strongbox was in the doorway, blackened and warped, with the lock broken. Somebody had gone through it. The bags of money were gone. The tablets were in the street, in the ash, and a woman was picking them up and putting them in a heap, and a man was standing on one of them.

"Don't," Tiro said.

The man did not hear him.

"Don't. Those are—" He could not get enough breath. "Those are people's—"

The woman looked at him. She had a broad red face and a scarf round her hair, and she was the woman who sold oranges, and she had been in the crowd an hour before, shouting.

"What?" she said.

Tiro looked at her.

"Tell her," he said. "Tell Antonia Cassia. The box is out."

"Who?"

"The box is out. Tell her—"

He stopped. The words were not coming. He lay in the street with the fire above him and the sky going grey, and he thought about the boy at the bellows, and about the fact that the paper said he was free, and that the boy could not read it, and that there was nobody now who would read it to him.

He said, "The boy at Tibur."

"What?"

"The boy at Tibur."

He said it twice. The woman with the oranges heard it and remembered it, because it was the only thing in the whole of that night that she could make any sense of, and in the morning she told the man from the vigiles, and the man from the vigiles told the prefect's clerk, and the clerk wrote it down in the margin of a report about a fire in the fourth region in which four persons died.

Then Tiro stopped saying anything at all.

He was not thinking about the arena, or the boy, or the woman with the oranges, or the papers in the street.

He was thinking about a doorway on the Vicus Tuscus in the rain, seventeen years before, and a woman in a dark cloak who had stopped and looked at him and said: *you look like a man who can keep a door.*

And he had said: *I look like a man who can keep anything.*

And she had laughed.
