# THE DIVIDED HARVEST

### A Novella in Ten Chapters — Warsaw, 1958

*Two childhood sweethearts from the village of Brzozówka, torn apart by war, meet again on opposite sides of a divided Poland: she a high-ranking cadre of the Party, he a priest nominated — at thirty-four, the youngest in modern Polish history — to a bishopric.*

*Historical note: This is a work of fiction. The Poland of 1958 — Gomułka's "Polish road to socialism," Cardinal Wyszyński two years out of internment, Warsaw rising from its ruins under the shadow of the Palace of Culture — is rendered as faithfully as the story allows. The people are invented; the world they move through was real.*

# One

## The Meeting

The fog came off the river before dawn and lay along the Poniatowski Bridge like something spilled. Zofia Krawiec watched it from the back of the Warszawa, through glass that had fogged on the inside as well, and she thought — as she did most mornings, without pleasure — that the city looked unfinished because it was. Whole streets had been drawn back in since the war in a hurry and with a good deal of noise. Others had been left as the Germans left them, and grass had come up through the rubble in the old way, unimpressed.

The driver, a boy from the transport pool named Kurek, had the radio on low. A woman was singing about a lime tree. Zofia had been awake for twenty-six hours, and the song went through her the way a train goes through a station it does not stop at.

"You can turn that off," she said.

He turned it off.

At the far end of the bridge the fog thinned and the Palace of Culture stood up out of it, grey and enormous, the spire vanishing into the low cloud. Every time she saw it she had the same thought, which she did not say to anyone: that it looked like a wedding cake ordered by a man who had never been to a wedding. She had been at the opening in 1955, on the tribune, in a blue coat, applauding. She had applauded for a long time.

They turned up Marszałkowska. The trams were not running yet. A street-cleaning cart went by, drawn by a horse, the horse's breath steaming. A workman in a cloth cap stood at a tram stop reading a newspaper folded to the size of a hand, and he looked up at the car as it passed, not with hostility, only with calculation, the way people in this city looked at cars.

"Bagatela," she said.

"Yes, comrade director."

She disliked being called that by a boy of twenty who had probably been told to call her that by someone who disliked her. But she did not correct him. She had learned in Lublin, at twenty, that correcting people is a way of spending authority you might need later. She was a member of the plenum of the Warsaw committee and had been a delegate to the Second Congress in 1954, where she had stood in a queue for forty minutes and shaken the hand of the First Secretary, and had not washed her own afterwards, which she had been ashamed of ever since, not because it was sentimental, but because it was.

The Office for Religious Affairs occupied a villa on Bagatela that had belonged to a sugar manufacturer and then to a German colonel and then, briefly, to a Soviet military mission, and had come to the Polish state in 1946 along with a great deal of other property, most of it not so well kept. The stucco was falling off the west face in sheets. Inside, the floors creaked in a particular order, so that Zofia could tell from her desk who was walking down the corridor: Brzozowski's slow flat-footed progress, the director's secretary's quick little heels, Wrona's heavy deliberate tread, which stopped outside doors before opening them, as though he were listening for something.

Her own office was on the first floor, at the corner, with two windows and a good stove and a desk that had once had a leather top and now had a sheet of plate glass. On the wall behind her there was a portrait of Gomułka, smaller than the one that had hung there in 1953, and a map of the voivodeships with pins in it. On the desk: the telephone, a brass ashtray from the sugar manufacturer's house, a glass of tea gone cold, three files, and a photograph in a cardboard frame of her and Jerzy on the steps of the registry office in 1953, both of them looking as if they were being photographed for a passport.

Hanna was already there. She was always already there. She was twenty-four and came from a village near Siedlce and had a way of standing with her hands folded that made Zofia feel like a headmistress.

"There's a car from the Curia waiting since seven," Hanna said. "They sent a letter. And the director wants you as soon as you're in."

"Which Curia?"

"Warsaw. A Monsignor... Lubieński. He says he has an appointment."

"He doesn't."

"He says he does."

Zofia took off her coat and hung it on the hook behind the door, and put her hat on the shelf, and looked at herself for a moment in the dark window, which was the only mirror in the room. Thirty-one. A narrow face, a good forehead, the mouth of a woman who has learned to keep it closed. Her hair was dark and short and set every ten days at a salon on Krucza by a woman who had once done the hair of actresses and now did the hair of wives. She looked, she thought, exactly like what she was, which was the trouble.

"Tell the monsignor," she said, "that the Office receives on Tuesdays between ten and twelve, by written application, and that today is Friday."

"Yes, comrade director."

"And then bring me the Lublin file. The new one."

Hanna went out. Zofia lit a cigarette and stood at the window with her arms crossed, watching the fog lift off the rooftops, and thought about nothing in particular, which was a skill she had acquired and valued. A pigeon walked along the gutter with the fussy, important air of a man who has been given a small responsibility.

***

Brzozowski's office smelled of tea and sickness. He had a samovar, which was ostentatious, and a rug from before the war, and a photograph of a woman in a hat on the desk, his wife, dead since 1949. He was sixty-three and looked seventy-five, and in the last six months he had begun to hold his cup with both hands, like a child.

"Sit down, Zofia. Not there, the chair is broken. There."

She sat. He poured tea without asking and pushed it across with two fingers. His hands had gone the colour of the tea.

"The terna from Lublin came in Wednesday's pouch," he said. "Krynicki wants a suffragan. He's seventy-one and he has a bad heart and he does not intend to die without arranging his own succession, which I respect. Three names. Pacewicz, Wierzbicki, and a man called Sobczyk from Zamość who is sixty and has sugar in his blood."

"And the order?"

"Wierzbicki first. Strongly first. Krynicki has written a private letter to the Cardinal, which we have not seen but which I can imagine. He calls the young man — let me find it." Brzozowski turned over a page with a slow thumb. "'A priest of uncommon gifts and uncommon courage, formed by suffering and not deformed by it.' That is the Primate's own phrase, I think. Krynicki is a better writer than he is a politician."

Zofia drank her tea. It was too hot and had a metallic taste from the samovar. "Have you read the file?"

"I have read the summary. I do not read the other thing." He meant the security material, which came separately, in a sealed envelope, from Wrona or from Wrona's people, and which Brzozowski had made a principle of not opening since 1956. "You will read it. That is the arrangement we have, and I am too tired to pretend it is a good one."

"Comrade director."

"Don't." He held up one hand. "Not at eight in the morning."

She waited.

"Here is the situation," he said. "The Primate is out. He came out of Komańcza two years ago and he has been behaving ever since like a man who spent three years in a monastery thinking about exactly what he would do when he got out, which is what he was. The Millennium is in eight years. Eight years is nothing. They are already planning it, the bishops. A great novena. Nine years of prayer for the thousandth year of the Baptism of Poland, and at the end of it, in 1966, the whole nation on its knees in front of the Black Madonna, and where will we be? Standing at the back with our hats in our hands." He drank. "The Party has decided it does not wish to be at the back."

"And this priest is part of that?"

"This priest is thirty-four years old," Brzozowski said. "He has a doctorate, he has a book that educated people actually read, he was in a Soviet camp, and he is going to be a bishop before he is thirty-five. The youngest bishop in this country since the war. Since 1918, I think, though the Curia will have a list and will argue about the fifteenth century. The papers will say so on Sunday, and for once the papers will be right. Do you know how that will look? A young man. Not a prince of the Church, not a politician in a cassock. A young man who was in the camps and came back and believes. If they make him a bishop in Lublin, in five years he will be in Warsaw or in Kraków, and in ten he will be a cardinal, and he will be the face of the Church in this country for thirty years after I am dead." He set the glass down. "That is the view from this office. The view from the fourth department is different. Wrona wants him blocked. Or owned. He has not decided, and he would like you to decide for him."

"Why me?"

Brzozowski looked at her for a while. He had the tired, courteous, absolutely merciless gaze of a man who has spent forty years reading other people's documents.

"Because you are the best officer in this building," he said, "and because you read the file before I did, and because you have not put it down since Wednesday."

She had not told him that. She did not ask how he knew. There were three copies of the summary and she had signed for one, and the registry clerk was Brzozowski's cousin's boy. In this building everything was known and nothing was said, which was, she sometimes thought, the entire achievement of the Polish People's Republic.

"The nomination requires the opinion of this Office," she said. "I will write it."

"You will write it after you have met him."

"I don't need to meet him to write an opinion on a terna."

"You need to meet him," Brzozowski said, "because Wrona is going to make you meet him, and it is better that it happens on our terms than on his. The fourth department has already prepared a proposal for the deployment of the candidate. I have not seen it. I know it exists because Wrona's major was in the building on Wednesday asking the registry for the Lublin pouch, which he has no business asking for, and because Wrona himself is coming here at eleven and has asked that you be present." He lifted the cup, found it empty, put it down. "Zofia. Look at me. Whatever he asks you in that room, remember one thing. The Church is not going to disappear. Not this year, not in twenty years. We are going to have to live with it, and it will outlive us, and the only question is whether we live with it cleverly or stupidly. I have spent eight years watching clever men be stupid about the Church. I would like, before I die, to be clever about it once."

"Are you dying?"

"Yes," he said, without any change of tone. "The doctors say a year. They said that in April, so possibly less. Do not tell anyone. Particularly not Wrona, who will start measuring the furniture."

She finished her tea. "I'll read the file properly before eleven."

"Read it properly," Brzozowski said. "And Zofia — when you meet the priest. Do not condescend to him. He will be expecting it and he is cleverer than you."

***

The file was in a cardboard folder tied with tape, and it was thicker than the summary had suggested. Zofia took it to her office, closed the door, and sat down with it under the lamp, although it was by then fully light and she could have opened the curtains.

Inside, in the order in which the registry kept such things, were: a biographical note of four pages, typed; a photograph, taken at a provincial occasion, of a thin man in a black suit standing beside a bishop, not smiling; a list of publications; a clipping from *Tygodnik Powszechny*; a report from the Lublin voivodeship office; and, in a sealed envelope stamped with the fourth department's stamp and Wrona's initials, the security material.

She read the biography first, because it was the least true and told her most about what the state thought it knew.

Wierzbicki, Tomasz Franciszek, son of Franciszek and Maria née Duda, born 6 January 1924 at Brzozówka, gmina Krasnystaw, Lublin voivodeship. Father: organist and carpenter, employed by the parish. Mother: deceased 1931. Education: village school to 1937; four classes of the gymnasium at Krasnystaw, interrupted 1939. During the occupation: no formal education; member of a clandestine youth circle; employed as organist's assistant. 1942–1944: member of the Home Army, sworn in the forest near Brzozówka, pseudonym "Dzwon." Arrested by the NKVD 9 August 1944 at Krasnystaw; interned in camp no. 445 at Ostashkov; released April 1947. Entered the diocesan seminary at Lublin, September 1948. Ordained priest 28 June 1953 by Bishop Krynicki. Curate, parish of the Conversion of St Paul, Lublin, 1953–55. From 1955, lecturer in moral theology at the Catholic University of Lublin. Author of *Głos i rana: sumienie i państwo nowoczesne* (Lublin, 1957, 3,000 copies). No wife, no children. No known membership of any illegal organisation since 1947.

Someone had underlined "no known membership" in pencil.

Zofia sat back. The lamp buzzed. Outside, a tram went past and the whole window frame rattled, which it had done since she came to this office, and which she had never reported because she liked it.

Brzozówka.

She had not said the name aloud in fourteen years. She had said it in her head perhaps twice a month, always at night, always in the same way, as a man might put his hand into his pocket and touch a stone. It was a village of four hundred and ninety souls on a tributary of the Wieprz, with a wooden church that had a bell cast in 1621, a smithy at the crossroads, a mill, two shops, and a school with one classroom and a stove that smoked. It had a market on Thursdays. In the summer the whole valley smelled of rye and diesel from the mill and, in June, of lilac, of which there was a great deal, mostly around the presbytery and the two houses opposite the church, one of them her father's.

Her father's house had been the third from the crossroads, and behind it the forge, and behind the forge the orchard, and beyond the orchard the path down to the water, and across the water, the flat meadow where the village dried its hay. In the meadow, in 1938, at the age of eleven, she had been unable to swim and had been pushed in, and a boy of fourteen had pulled her out by the collar of her dress and had been so frightened that he had shouted at her for ten minutes and then, abruptly, had sat down in the grass and said he was sorry.

She turned the page.

The security envelope contained eleven sheets. The first was a summary of the 1951 case: subject detained 3 October 1951 under article 4 of the decree of 13 June 1946, held in the Mokotów prison, Warsaw, released 2 July 1952 on the basis of a signed declaration. Attached: the declaration.

She had read the declaration before, in the summary, three lines of it, quoted, and had not slept. Now she read the whole thing, and it was four paragraphs long and written in a neat, upright hand, and it named two men.

The second sheet was the interrogation protocol of 14 November 1951. The third was a note from an officer named Szeruda, 1951, recommending that the subject "not be employed in the education of youth." The fourth was a list of the subject's visitors in Lublin, 1956–58, compiled by the voivodeship office: a great many names, mostly women, mostly students, one of them a curate, one a professor.

The fifth sheet was a translation from the German. It had been made in 1949, in Lublin, by someone whose Polish was stiff, and it carried a stamp Zofia knew from other files: *Akta niemieckie, odzyskane*. German records, recovered.

She read it twice. Then she put it down on the glass, and folded her hands on top of it, and sat very still for some time.

The document was a deposition taken on 15 June 1944, at the gendarmerie post at Krasnystaw, from a Polish girl of seventeen, name Zofia Krawiec, occupation: none, residence: Brzozówka. It recorded that the girl had been detained in the meadow east of the village on the morning of 14 June in possession of a written message, and that after questioning she had stated that a wounded Soviet soldier was concealed in the cellar beneath the smithy of Józef Krawiec, and that there were persons of Jewish origin concealed in the same dwelling.

It was two pages. Her signature was at the bottom, in ink, and below it the signature of a gendarme, and it was a good signature, a girl's signature, carefully made, with a round loop on the Z.

Zofia looked at it for a long moment. Then she got up, and went to the window, and stood there with her arms folded, and watched the street, which had filled with people going to work. A woman was carrying a goose by the neck. Two boys were kicking a tin along the gutter. A man in a good coat came out of the building opposite and stood on the step lighting a cigarette, and the match flame was invisible in the daylight, and he shook it out and went off towards the tram.

***

At eleven, Wrona came.

He came in without knocking, which was his privilege, and stood in the doorway filling it, and said, "Comrade director. You are well?"

"Colonel."

He was a large man, forty-six, in a grey suit cut for a smaller man, with a broad flat face and very small feet for his weight and the patient, almost tender manner of someone who has never in his life had to hurry. He had been in the department since 1945. In 1953 he had personally conducted the interrogation of a man Zofia had known, and the man had come out of it able to walk but not able to write his own name, and Wrona had sent him home to his mother with a travel warrant and a receipt for his watch. This was the kind of fact Zofia kept.

"Tea?" she said.

"Thank you, no. I have come from the doctor. My stomach." He sat down in the chair opposite her, which creaked. "You have read the Lublin material."

"I have read the file."

"Both files?"

"Both."

Wrona nodded slowly, as though she had confirmed something creditable. "Then we can speak as colleagues. You know that I am not a religious man. This is a defect in an officer of my department, and I have been told about it. My wife's sister is very devout, she goes to the Bernardines, she lights candles for my stomach. I tell her, Jadwiga, the candles are six złoty each, buy me a bottle of rectified spirits and I will pray myself." He laughed, a small sound, entirely without warmth or menace, like a man clearing a pipe. "But I understand them. That is my qualification. Twenty years, Zofia. I understand the Church better than the Church understands the Church."

"Go on."

"Krynicki's terna is a provocation," Wrona said. "Not because the man is a priest. Because of which priest. Wierzbicki is thirty-four. He is not from a good family, he is not from the intelligentsia, he has no manners and no connections; he is a peasant's son who plays the organ. If the Curia wanted a bishop they would put up Pacewicz, who is fifty-two, who has published nothing, who says the right things about the state and means none of them, and whom we could manage with a telephone call. They are not putting up Pacewicz. They are putting up the other one, and they are putting him up first, and they have done it in the full knowledge that he is the one man in that diocese with a file." He tapped one finger on the glass. "Why?"

"Because they think he is good."

"Because they think we will refuse him," Wrona said. "And then they will have their martyr, and they will have him cheap, without consecrating him, and the Cardinal will write a sermon about the state which obstructs the free choice of the Church, and every curate in Poland will read it in the pulpit in Lent. That is the trap. If we refuse, we lose. If we accept, we have a bishop of thirty-four who was in the AK and in a Soviet camp and who fills a church in Lublin every Sunday with students who stand in the aisles. That is the other trap." He spread his hands. "So we do not refuse him, and we do not simply accept him. We accept him, and he is ours."

"Ours."

"Not ours in the sense of a paid man. I am not a fool. He will not take money and he will not report. But there is a middle thing, comrade director, and you know it as well as I do. There is the man who signs a piece of paper on the understanding that his signature is a formality, a condition of employment, and who then spends twenty years telling himself that the paper means what he says it means. He keeps his integrity, and we keep the paper. That is not corruption. That is administration." Wrona's small eyes rested on her. "The question is how to obtain the paper."

"And you have an idea how."

"I have the only idea that will work," Wrona said. "You."

Zofia did not move. She had learned not to move at such moments; the body is a traitor and the face is worse. She reached for her cigarettes, took one, and lit it, and the match shook very slightly, and she was able to attribute that to the match.

"Explain yourself, colonel."

"You were born in Brzozówka in 1927," Wrona said. "Your father was Józef Krawiec, the smith. You were in the ZMP from 1945 and in the Party from 1947. In 1944 you were a courier for a Home Army unit in the Krasnystaw district. In June 1944 you were detained by the gendarmerie and questioned, and you made a statement, and the statement is in the file which is on your desk. On the fourteenth of June 1944 the village of Brzozówka was burned by a punitive expedition of the Krasnystaw gendarmerie and a detachment of the Wehrmacht, and thirty-one persons were killed, among them your mother, your brother, and a family of Jews whom your father was hiding. In August 1944 the man Wierzbicki was arrested by the NKVD and sent to Ostashkov. He came back in 1947 and went into the seminary. In 1949 you were questioned by a verification commission of the Lublin voivodeship committee, and you gave an account of your wartime contacts, and in that account you named him, and the sentence you used about him is in the file which is on your desk." He paused. "Would you like me to go on?"

"No."

"Then you will understand that I am not asking you to lie to him. I am asking you to speak to him. He is going to be made a bishop, and he is going to be in a position to do great harm or great good, and there is one person in this Republic who can make him understand what the harm would be, and it is not me and it is not the director and it is not the Primate of Poland. It is the girl who was pushed into the meadow." Wrona stood, with an effort. "Think about it. Not long. The Office must answer Lublin by the fifteenth of November, and I would like the paper signed before the fifteenth, because on the fifteenth there is a plenary of the episcopate and I would rather that Krynicki heard the news from us than from the newspapers."

He went to the door and then turned.

"One more thing. He does not know that you are in Warsaw. I have not told him and he has not asked. It is possible that he does not know you are alive." Wrona's face did something that was almost kind. "That will be the difficult part, comrade director. Not the paper. The face."

When he had gone she sat for a while, and then she took the German deposition out of the folder, folded it once, and put it in her handbag. She did not know why. She was a careful officer; she had never in her life removed a document from a registry. She put it in her handbag, and closed the clasp, and heard Hanna in the corridor, and said, in a voice that was perfectly level, "Hanna. The car. I am going to Miodowa."

***

The Curia was in a building that had been rebuilt after the war with the same façade and a different inside, and the inside smelled of floor polish, wet wool, and old paper. There was a nun with a bucket on the landing, and a queue of four women and one old man on a bench, and a corridor with a red runner worn to the webbing in the middle. Zofia gave her name to a young priest in a black suit too big for him, and was told to wait, and waited twenty minutes on a chair that had been designed for a person of a different shape.

At the end of the corridor there was a door with a brass plate, and beyond it the sound of a typewriter and, at intervals, a man's voice, low, saying something and then stopping. Portraits of bishops hung along the wall, most of them dead, several of them in the nineteenth century, one of them in a red cape. On the sill at the end there was a plaster Madonna with a chipped hand and a jam jar of dried flowers.

She did not think about the corridor. She had trained herself to do that: to sit in a waiting room and think about the room. The jam jar had a label on it, a shop label, *Cukry — Mąka — Herbaty*, sugared over. The floor had been laid in 1948, by the look of the boards, which had come from the east, and the polish was a cheap one, and it had gone grey in the traffic line. In the corner a radiator knocked twice and then held its peace.

The door opened.

Two men came out. The first was old, in a purple sash and a black cap, carrying a folder against his chest like a baby, and he went past her without looking at her and down the stairs, and she heard him greet the nun. The second was a man in a black cassock, and he was thin, and he had his sleeves rolled to the elbow in a way that no Warsaw priest would have done, and he was saying to someone behind him, "—then tell him Thursday, but not before eleven, because the students have the chapel until eleven," and then he turned, and stopped.

He had a long face, a high forehead, a mouth that was pleasant and held in, and grey eyes set deep. His hair was black and cut short and there was a little grey at the temple, the only thing about him that had changed, and it had changed the way frost changes a field.

He looked at her the way a man looks at a person he is certain he has seen before and cannot place, with a courteous, faintly apologetic attention, and then his face went through something she had no name for, and he took a half-step forward, and said, quietly, in a voice that had not changed at all, that was still the voice of a boy in a meadow:

"Zosia."

She stood up. She had prepared a sentence, the sentence of the Office, in the third person, formal, with her rank in it, and she never used it. What she said was:

"Father."

And the corridor went on around them, the nun's bucket, the typewriter, the radiator, and the four women and the old man on the bench, all of it continuing, in the ordinary way, as though nothing had happened at all.

# Two

## The Village

The smithy at Brzozówka stood at the crossroads where the road from Krasnystaw met the road to the mill, and it was the first thing a stranger saw of the village and the last thing he heard of it, because a forge can be heard for half a kilometre on a still day, and her father worked from five in the morning.

Zofia was nine in the summer of 1936, and she knew the sound the way she knew her own name: the ring of the hammer on the anvil, then the smaller ring of it coming back off the houses, then the bellows, then the long hiss when the iron went into the trough. She could tell from the ring whether her father was tired. When he was tired the strokes came slower and the metal answered flatly, and her mother would say, without looking up, "Your father is working too much," and go on peeling potatoes.

The forge was a low wooden shed with a tin roof and a brick hearth blackened to the colour of a storm. Inside it was always dark and always hot and it smelled of coal and hoof and burnt horn, and there were things hanging on the walls that she was not to touch: hooks, chains, a row of horseshoes in three sizes, a scythe blade with a broken tip, a horseshoe nailed over the door for luck which her father said was foolishness and kept anyway. In the corner, a bucket of water and a dipper, and a stool where she sat to watch.

Józef Krawiec was a big man with a big moustache and hands like a bundle of tools. He had been in the Polish Army in 1920 and had a scar on his left forearm from a sabre and did not talk about it. He read a newspaper when there was one — *Piast*, or *Zielony Sztandar* when the peasant party was having a dispute with itself, which was most of the time — and he had views about the government, and about the miller, and about the priest's brother, who had a motor car. He was not a man who said much to his daughter, but he had taught her to hold a hammer properly, which is to say at the end of the handle and not in the middle, and he had taught her to read the weather from the smoke and the bees, and once, when she was seven, he had lifted her onto the back of a mare that had come in to be shod and stood there with his hand on the animal's neck while she held the mane, and she had been so happy that she had not been able to speak.

Her mother, Aniela, was small and quick and devout in the way of a woman who has made an arrangement with Heaven and keeps her side of it. She went to Mass on Sundays and to the May devotions and to the Forty Hours, and she fasted, and she had a picture of the Sacred Heart in the kitchen and a palm cross behind it from Palm Sunday 1935. She sang while she worked, badly and continuously, most often "Serdeczna Matko," which she rendered in a high wavering voice that went off the note on the third line and never came back. She was the one who scrubbed the church linen, four times a year, in a tub in the yard, and hung it in the orchard to dry, and she would not let anyone walk between the sheets while they dried, because they were for God.

Piotrek was born in January 1936, in the room behind the kitchen, with the midwife and the neighbour and a great deal of hot water and noise, and afterwards Zofia was allowed in to see him, a red creature with a screwed-up face and a fist the size of a walnut, and her mother, white as the church linen, said, "She has a brother now, and she must be good, because the little one will learn everything from her." Zofia took this seriously. For the next three years she was, on the whole, good.

The village had four hundred and ninety souls, a wooden church with a bell cast in 1621 and a spire that leaned a little to the north, a school with one room, a mill with a wheel that had been mended in 1929 with iron from her father's forge, two shops, and a market on Thursdays in the square by the church, where the peasants from the hamlets came in with eggs and hens and sour cream and linen and calves, and where, in the autumn, there were carts of apples and a man who sold hot fat with onions on bread.

The bigger of the two shops belonged to Icek Frydman, who was a small, neat, worried man with a bald head and spectacles mended at the bridge with wire, and his wife Rywka, who was large and warm and gave children broken biscuits and was not supposed to. They had a daughter, Sara, born in 1934, who was three years younger than Zofia and had a doll with a china face that had come from Warsaw, the only china doll in the village, and who was permitted to sit on the step of the shop in the afternoons and not to go beyond the well. Frydman sold paraffin and salt and nails and sugar and herrings and thread and cigarettes and paper and pencils and a sweet dark bread on Fridays, and he kept the post office in the back room and was the only man in Brzozówka who read a Warsaw newspaper and could tell you what the złoty was doing.

"Pan Frydman is a Jew," her mother said once, in the tone of a fact about the weather, "and a very decent man, and you will be polite to him and not stare at his daughter."

"Why would I stare at her?"

"Because she has a china doll and you have a rag one. That is why. Staring is a kind of envy and envy is a sin, and also it is rude." Her mother took the bread out of the oven with a wooden peel. "And on Friday you will not go into the shop, because it is their Sabbath and they are busy."

"They aren't busy. They sit."

"That is their business," her mother said. "You will not go."

The school was a single room with a stove that smoked and thirty-one children in four classes, and one teacher, Pan Wróbel, who had a stiff leg from 1920 and taught everything, and who had a picture of Marshal Piłsudski on the wall next to a map of Poland on which the eastern border went a long way beyond anything Zofia had ever seen, and who once a week gave the older children a lesson in Polish history that was not in the official timetable. He was a member of the peasant party too, and he and her father would stand outside the forge in the evening and talk in low voices about the government, and then her father would say, "Enough, Panie Wróbel, the child is listening," and Pan Wróbel would say, "The child should listen. Somebody has to."

The priest was Father Antoni Zaremba, fifty-eight, the parish priest since 1921, a heavy man with a heavy breath who preached for forty minutes and had buried a third of the parish in the influenza of 1918 and had baptized the rest, and who was, in the way of village priests, both more and less than he seemed: more, because he had in fact studied at the seminary in Lublin and could read Latin and Greek and had a shelf of books in his study that no one else in Brzozówka could read, and less, because he was lazy, and fond of his dinner, and had been having a quiet, decades-long disagreement with the organist about the tempo of the processional hymn.

The organist was Franciszek Wierzbicki. He was a widower — his wife Maria had died in 1931 of something the doctor from Krasnystaw called a growth — and he was the parish carpenter as well as the organist, and he lived in the house opposite the church with his son, and he had built, with his own hands, in 1934, a new organ loft, because the old one had woodworm.

Tomasz Wierzbicki was three years and four months older than Zofia. In 1936 he was twelve and had already begun to be tall in the way that boys from that house were tall, thin, with big wrists. He served at the altar. He pumped the organ for his father during Mass, which was hard work and had to be done at the right moment, and he could read music before he could read Polish properly, or so his father said, with the particular complacency of a man praising his own son in front of others.

He was not a friendly boy. He was polite, and he was quick, and he was not friendly; he had a way of looking at adults that made them uneasy, as if he were weighing them. He had lost his mother at seven and had been thereafter much in the company of his father and of the church, and he had a habit, when he was thinking, of rubbing the knuckle of his thumb along his lower lip.

Zofia's first memory of him — as distinct from the general fact of him, which was like the fact of the church — was at the well in the autumn of 1936. She had been sent with two buckets and a yoke that was too big for her, and she could not get the second bucket up, and she sat down on the stone and began, with great fury, to cry. He came past with a saw under his arm, on an errand, and stopped, and looked at her for a moment, and then he said, "Is the rope broken?"

"No."

"Then why are you crying?"

"Because I can't."

He set down the saw, took the rope in both hands, and pulled the bucket up in about four seconds. Then he filled her other bucket from it, put the yoke on her shoulders, and adjusted it so it did not slip, and said, "It's easier if you walk fast. If you walk slow it hurts more," and picked up his saw and went on.

She did not thank him. She was nine and humiliated. But she remembered the exact place on the road where he had stopped, and the way the light came through the chestnut, and for the rest of her life, whenever she was in a strange town and saw a well or a pump, she was nine years old again and furious.

After that they were often together, in the way of children in a village, which is to say not deliberately and not constantly, but in a pattern set by errands and seasons. In spring they looked for the first mushrooms in the birch wood beyond the mill. In summer they swam in the Wieprz, at the bend where the bank was clay and the water deep enough to be frightening. In autumn they took the cows to the meadow and sat on the stone wall and he told her things he had read. In winter he pulled her on a sledge behind the mill while his father shouted at them from the presbytery garden.

He was a great reader. There was no library in Brzozówka except the priest's and the school's, and Pan Wróbel lent him books out of the school cupboard — Sienkiewicz, Orzeszkowa, a volume of Mickiewicz with a broken spine — and he read them and then told her the stories, which he did very well, with voices, and with a firm conviction that the endings should be better than they were. She could not read well herself until she was ten, and she was ashamed of it, and he never once made her feel it, which she understood, later, to have been a considerable act of character in a boy of thirteen.

The bend of the river became theirs. It was a quarter of an hour's walk below the mill, where the water turned around a stand of alders and made a flat green pool, and there was a fallen willow to sit on, and the clay bank was good for nothing and belonged to no one, which is to say it belonged to the village and the village did not care. In the summer of 1938 they decided, without any ceremony, that when they were grown they would build a house there. He drew the plan on the back of a paper bag with a carpenter's pencil: two rooms, a kitchen, a porch, a window facing the water.

"The porch is too big," she said.

"The porch is the point."

"You can't live on a porch."

"You can in summer." He drew a chimney. "And in winter we'll have a stove, and I'll make the stove myself, and it will be better than your father's, and he will be insulted."

"He'll be insulted anyway. He thinks you're a Protestant."

"He thinks everyone's a Protestant."

"He thinks the miller's a Protestant."

"The miller is a Protestant," Tomasz said. "That's why the flour's so good. God rewards thrift."

She hit him with the paper bag. He fell off the willow into the water, entirely on purpose, and came up blowing, and said, "Now the plan is ruined," and she laughed until she had to hold her side.

***

In August of that year she nearly drowned. It was the fault of a boy from the hamlet called Stach, who was fifteen and stupid, and who thought it would be funny to push her off the bank where the water was over her head. She went down and came up and went down again, and the river, which she had thought of all her life as a friendly thing, was in fact heavy and cold and completely indifferent, and she had time to think one clear thought, which was that she had been wrong about the river, and that this was an important discovery, and that it was a pity she was going to die.

Then a hand got her by the collar of her dress. Tomasz had been up at the bend cutting switches, and he dragged her to the shallows and got her onto the grass, and she was sick, and he sat there white to the lips and shouted at her for ten minutes — that it was her own fault, that she was too old to be pushed, that she had no business in the deep part with those idiots, that she had frightened him — and then he stopped, and put his face in his hands, and said, "I'm sorry."

"It's all right."

"It isn't all right. I thought you were dead." He wiped his face with his sleeve. "Don't do that again."

"I didn't do it. Stach did it."

"Don't do it again," he said, "at all," and she understood that this was the closest he could come to saying what he meant, and she said, "All right, Tomasz."

After that he taught her to swim, in the shallows, in four lessons, with immense seriousness and no patience whatsoever, and by the end of September she could cross the pool and back, and he said, "There. Now you can be pushed in whenever you like."

***

The years before the war had a shape to them, and the shape was the church's year. Advent, with the rorate Masses at six in the dark and the candles and the cold; Christmas, with the wafer and the straw under the cloth; Lent, with the Stations and her mother's fasting and the smell of vinegar in the house on Good Friday; Easter, with the fire blessed in front of the church and the procession round it three times, and the bells, and the boys with the clappers who were allowed to make a noise for once; Corpus Christi in June, which was the great day, when the whole village walked the four altars in the square, and the girls in white threw flowers, and Zofia was a flower girl from the age of seven and took it as seriously as a vocation. The priest carried the monstrance under a canopy, and the canopy was carried by four men, and her father was one of them, and Tomasz walked behind with the boys' sodality, and the choir sang "Serdeczna Matko" and her mother sang louder than anyone and off the note, and the dust came up off the road in the heat, and the whole thing was, for those years, the most beautiful thing in the world.

***

She made her First Communion in May 1936. Her dress was white and had belonged to a girl from the mill and had been altered; her shoes were borrowed; she wore a wreath of artificial flowers and a medal of the Virgin that had been her mother's. She had been prepared for a year by Father Zaremba, who was severe with the girls about the sin of vanity and had a habit of asking them questions they could not answer, and she was frightened of him in the way that one is frightened of a large tame animal. On the morning, coming down the aisle with her hands joined, she was so conscious of the eyes and the heat and the tightness of the borrowed shoes that she could not feel anything at all, and it was only afterwards, kneeling back in the pew with the taste still in her mouth, that she was suddenly, unaccountably happy, and cried, and her mother gave her a handkerchief and pretended not to notice.

***

The summer of 1939 was hot and dry and the rye came in early. There was talk in the market of the Germans, and of Gdańsk, and of the English, and it was the kind of talk that men have in a market and forget by the evening, because the rye was in and the winter had to be got ready and there was a wedding at the mill. In July there was a mission in the parish, two Redemptorists from Lublin, and the church was full every night, and a great many people went to confession who had not been in fifteen years, and her mother went twice. In August the flies were terrible. The well at the crossroads was low.

***

On the first of September the miller's boy came through the village on a bicycle at four in the afternoon, shouting, and by six there were thirty men outside the gmina office listening to the radio through the window. The Germans had crossed the border at dawn. The radio kept saying it. The Germans had crossed the border at dawn and the Polish Army was resisting and the Allies had been informed.

Her father came home at eight and sat down at the table and did not eat. Her mother put the food in front of him and he looked at it.

"Józef."

"They will come here," he said. "They came here in 1915 and they will come here now."

"Perhaps the French—"

"The French," her father said, and did not finish. He took off his cap and put it on the table, and looked at his hands. "We should bury the good tools. And the church silver, that's Father's business. And we should take the pigs up to your brother's, in case."

"Józef, the boy is three."

"I know how old the boy is."

The next week was hot and full of rumours and refugees. They came from the west in carts, families with bedding and a goat and a grandmother, and they came through Brzozówka on the Krasnystaw road and were given water at the well and went on. There was a woman with a child of Piotrek's age who had walked from Radom. There was a man who had been in the army and had lost his unit and sat on the wall for an hour and then went on. The radio said the government had left Warsaw. The radio said the Allies had declared war. The radio said nothing for a while and then said something else.

On the eleventh of September they bombed Krasnystaw. They heard it, nine kilometres away, a series of soft heavy thuds like a door slamming in another house, and then a column of smoke that stood up and bent in the wind and did not go away. Her father went in on the Thursday with the cart and came back at dark with the news: the market square had been hit, and the church had lost its roof, and the Frydmans' cousin's house was gone, and the Frydmans themselves were all right because they had been in the cellar.

On the fourteenth the Germans came through. First, in the morning, two motorcycles with sidecars, which stopped at the crossroads and let the dust settle, and the men on them looked at the forge and the well and the church and then went on towards the mill. Then in the afternoon, a column of them, on foot and in lorries, with horses, a long line of grey, going east. They did not stop. They filled the road for two hours, and the village stood at the edge of it in silence and watched them go by, and one of the soldiers, a boy with a face like a farmhand, waved at Piotrek, who was sitting on the wall, and Piotrek, who was three, waved back, and Aniela Krawiec grabbed him so hard that he cried.

That night the village did not light its lamps. They sat in the kitchen with the shutters closed and the fire banked, and her father read the newspaper from the previous Sunday as though it were a letter from a dead man. Her mother said the rosary. Tomasz came to the door at nine and said his father wanted to know whether they had heard anything, and her father said, no, nothing, come in, boy, and he came in and stood by the stove and drank a cup of milk, and Zofia sat on the bench across the room and looked at him, and he looked back once, and then at the floor.

***

The school did not open in October. The Germans had made the schoolroom a store for grain. Father Zaremba said Mass every morning and the church was fuller than it had ever been, and in November he preached a sermon in which he said that God had permitted this for the sins of Poland, and that the nation must repent, and that the nation would be restored. He said it in the same voice in which he said everything, and afterward the men stood outside in the cold and did not look at one another.

At Christmas there was no wafer from the shop, because the shop had been closed since October, and Icek Frydman had been made to paint a sign over his own door, and Rywka had been seen crying at the well, and Sara had not been out of the house for two months.

***

In February the Germans took the bell.

They came with a lorry and four men and a sergeant, and the sergeant had a paper, and Father Zaremba stood in the churchyard and read the paper and then read it again, and then he said something to the sergeant in German, and the sergeant said something back, and the priest's face went the colour of a candle. They took the bell down with ropes and a block and tackle and it fell the last two feet and made a sound that everyone in the village heard for the next forty years. It was cast in 1621. It had rung for the dead of the parish since before there was a school, before there was a mill, before any of them were born. They put it on the lorry with the ropes still on it and drove away with it, and it was the last thing Zofia saw of the village as it had been, that spring, that bell on the lorry, going off down the Krasnystaw road into the grey.

She was twelve. She stood in the churchyard with her mother and her brother and the rest of them, and Tomasz was on the other side of the crowd with his father, and she looked across at him, and he was not looking at the lorry. He was looking at her. And he did the smallest thing: he shook his head.

She did not know what he meant. She thought about it for years. In 1958, in Warsaw, in a corridor, she would still not know what he had meant.

# Three

## The Occupation

The Germans did not arrive in Brzozówka all at once. They arrived in pieces over two years, each piece small enough to be borne, and by 1942 the village had a new shape and had stopped noticing that it had one.

First came the papers. In the spring of 1940 everyone over fourteen was registered at the gmina office and given a Kennkarte, a grey fold of card with a fingerprint and a photograph, and Zofia, who was twelve, was measured and photographed with her hair pulled back and told to look at the wall. Then came the quotas: grain, potatoes, meat, milk, eggs, a list nailed to the door of the gmina office, with the name of every household and what it owed. Then came the Arbeitsamt, which took the young people for work in Germany, and the first three boys went in June and one of them wrote twice and then never again. Then came the order that the school was to be used for the storage of grain, which it already was. Then came the order that no one was to be out of doors after eight in the evening. Then came the gendarmes, four of them, at the post in Krasnystaw, who came through the village on Thursdays in a lorry and sometimes on foot and sometimes not at all, and whose names, once learned, were never forgotten: Kliemann, who was fat and had a pleasant face and had killed a man in the market at Krasnystaw with the butt of a rifle for not taking off his cap; Bauer, who was thin and silent and collected watches; a Ukrainian from Lublin they called Iwan; and a young one, Vogel, who was nineteen and had been a bank clerk and who gave sweets to children and once wept in the street, and who was the worst of them in the end.

The village did what villages do. It lied about its grain. It hid its pigs in the birch wood and its best tools under the floor of the barn. It sent its boys to the forest when the lorries came. It bribed the Polish policeman, who was a man from the next parish and who took the bribes and passed on what he could. It kept its mouth shut, and it kept its accounts in its head, and it survived, in the way that a stone survives a river, by not being soluble.

And it went on teaching its children.

Pan Wróbel had been dismissed from the school in November 1939 for having been a member of the peasant party and for having a son in Vilnius. He stayed in the village, because he had nowhere else, and in January 1940 he began to teach again, four evenings a week, in the kitchen of Franciszek Wierzbicki's house, to eleven children between the ages of nine and seventeen.

It was dangerous. The penalty for clandestine teaching was not always death, but it was camp, and everyone knew it, and the parents who sent their children knew it and sent them anyway, because a Pole without Polish is a peasant and a peasant without letters is a serf and this had been understood in Brzozówka for a hundred years. The lessons were held after dark with the shutters closed and a lamp turned low and a lookout in the yard. The book was one book, a history of Poland, in three volumes, which Pan Wróbel had wrapped in oilcloth and buried in the priest's garden in October 1939 and dug up again in the spring. The children learned the dates by singing them, and they learned the poetry by heart, and when the lookout knocked twice on the window frame, the book went into the space behind the stove and the eleven children sat in silence with their hands folded while Pan Wróbel told them, in a level voice, about the beet harvest.

Zofia went to those lessons for three years. She learned the history of her country in a kitchen, by lamplight, in a house that smelled of wood shavings and cabbage, sitting on a bench beside Tomasz Wierzbicki, who had already read the book twice and who corrected Pan Wróbel when he got a date wrong, which Pan Wróbel permitted, because the boy was right.

***

In the autumn of 1942 the Germans took the Frydmans away.

There had been a ghetto in Krasnystaw since the winter of 1940, and the Jews of the district had been moved into it, and the shop in Brzozówka had been closed and its windows broken and its stock taken by the Kreishauptmann's office and sold, and Icek Frydman had been seen in the market at Krasnystaw that winter with a yellow star on his coat, selling cigarettes from a tray. Then in October 1942 the lorries came to the ghetto at night, and in the morning there were no Jews in Krasnystaw, or very few, and the ones who remained were in the camp at Trawniki, and the ones who remained after that were nowhere.

But Frydman had got out. In October of 1942, in the middle of a night of rain, he had come to the door of the smithy with his wife and his daughter and a bundle, and her father had opened the door in his shirt and looked at them for a long moment, and then he had said, "Come in, Icek," and closed the door behind them, and that was the whole of the discussion.

They lived for twenty months in a space under the floor of the barn, which Józef Krawiec had dug out himself over four nights, with a hatch under the straw and a pipe for air that came up behind the pigsty. They were not to make a sound during the day. They were not to come out except at night, and not every night, and only into the barn. Rywka Frydman, who had been a large woman, became thin, and then became something else, something that sat in the dark with its hands in its lap. Sara, who had been eight, was nine, and then ten; she had her doll with the china face, and she had a piece of chalk and a board, and Zofia taught her the alphabet from a primer she had stolen from the school storeroom, in the dark, by touch, whispering, and Sara learned to read in the summer of 1943, which is to say she learned to read in a hole in the ground under a barn in occupied Poland at the age of nine, and she was very proud of it, and she could not tell anyone.

Her father never spoke of it. Not once, in all the years after. He carried the hay down to them and carried the slops up and put his ear to the ground when there were footsteps in the yard, and he did it for twenty months, and he did not mention it, and if anyone had asked him why he would have said that a man does not let his neighbour be taken out of his house, and he would have thought the question stupid.

The war came into the village in other ways. In November 1942 the Germans began the expulsions in the Zamość region, which was sixty kilometres east, and the roads filled with people. They came through Brzozówka in wagons and on foot, families from villages that no longer existed, with their bedding and their pots and whatever they had been able to carry, and the Germans put them in transit camps and then in trains, and the trains went west, and some of the people were Poles from good farms who had been there since the fourteenth century and were now being moved to make room for German settlers from Bessarabia and the Baltic.

Some of them did not go on. They stopped in Brzozówka, in the barns and the school storeroom and the church porch, and the village fed them, which it could not afford, and hid some of them, which it could not afford at all. There were children among them who had been separated from their parents, and there was a great deal of talk about the children, because the Germans were taking the children too, not for labour but for something else, and no one knew what, and everyone knew.

***

Zofia was fifteen in the winter of 1942. She was tall for her age and strong from the yoke and the water and the hay, and she had begun to be looked at by men, which she disliked and used. She had stopped going to the secret lessons in the autumn, because Pan Wróbel had been arrested in September and sent to Majdanek, and had died there in January, and there was no one to teach. She could read now, well, and she read everything in the priest's study that he would lend her, which was not much, and everything in the Wierzbickis' house, which was more. She had a notebook in which she wrote, in a careful hand, the things she did not say: the dates from Pan Wróbel's history, the poems, the names of the dead.

Tomasz was eighteen in the winter of 1942 and was no longer in the village most nights.

She did not know at first. He was there in the daytime, in the carpenter's shop, planing boards, or in the church, pumping the organ, or at the forge, helping her father with a hinge. And then he would be gone, and his father would say he had gone to Krasnystaw for an order, and Zofia would see the old man's hands, and the way he did not look at her.

***

In March she found out. She had gone up to the church to sweep the aisle — Father Zaremba paid her four złoty a month and a loaf — and Tomasz was in the organ loft with the little door open, and he had taken off his shirt, and there was a bandage around his ribs, badly done, and blood coming through it in a line the width of a finger.

He heard her on the stair and turned, and for a moment his face was not his face.

"Go down."

"You're bleeding."

"It's a scratch. Go down, Zosia."

She came up the last three steps, and he did not stop her, and she looked at the bandage, which had been put on by someone who did not know how, and she said, "Sit on the bench. I'll do it. I've done my father's hands."

"Zofia."

"Sit on the bench."

He sat. She fetched water from the jug by the organ and a clean cloth from the chest where the vestments were kept, and she unwound the bandage, and the wound underneath was a groove along the lowest rib, about four inches long, not deep, and beginning, she thought, to be angry at the edges. She cleaned it. He held the bench with both hands and did not make a sound, and she put on the salve from the tin in the vestment chest, and bound it with a strip of the linen that was kept for the altar, which was a sin, and which she did not mention to him, and which he did not mention either.

When she had finished he said, "You won't tell your father."

"I won't tell my father."

"You won't tell anyone."

"I know what to do, Tomasz."

He looked at her. He had grown into his face at last that winter; he was going to be a handsome man, and she had noticed it in the way one notices a change in the weather, without deciding anything. There was a bruise coming up along his jaw.

"Since when?" she said.

"Last spring."

"The forest?"

He did not answer, which was an answer.

"Since last spring," she said, "and you never said."

"I couldn't."

"You could have. Not the names. Just — that you were. I thought you were going to Krasnystaw for orders." Her voice had gone thin. "I thought you were — I don't know what I thought."

"Zosia."

"You could have told me that much."

He was silent for a moment. Then he said, "Do you know what they do to a man's family? If he's taken and they find out who knew? They take the family. Not the man, he's in the forest, they can't get him. The family." He looked at his hands. "My father is sixty. Yours has the frydmans under the barn." He said it flatly, without emphasis, and she understood that he was telling her the most dangerous thing he knew in order to explain himself, and that he was trusting her with it, and that it was the only apology he had.

"All right," she said.

"All right?"

"All right." She put the stopper in the salve. "But you will eat. You're thin. You'll come to the house on Thursday and my mother will feed you and she will think it's for the church."

He laughed — one breath of it, and then he winced and put a hand to his ribs. "You're a terrible girl, Zofia Krawiec."

"I know," she said. "Lie down for ten minutes and then go down the back way. There's a hole in the fence by the orchard."

In the summer of 1943 she became a courier.

It was Tomasz's doing, and she never knew whether to be grateful. A unit in the forest needed people who could carry a message and not be looked at twice: girls of fifteen and sixteen, boys too young for the army, old women with baskets. Zofia was sixteen, she could walk fifteen kilometres and back in a day, she had a good memory, and she had a face that people trusted, which she had already begun to understand was a resource.

Her first message was a folded paper in the false bottom of a basket of eggs, from the mill to a farm beyond the birch wood. Her second was spoken: eleven words, which she repeated to herself all the way there and all the way back, and which she could still recite in 1958, though she had long since forgotten what they meant. Her third was a warning.

She was not brave, she said afterward, when people asked, and people did ask, in the years when it was still possible to ask. She was frightened the whole time. She was frightened in a way that made her hands cold and her hearing unnaturally good, and she went on doing it because doing nothing was worse, and because there was a boy in the forest who would know.

***

The summer of 1943 was the last good summer of her life, and she knew it while it was happening, which is a rare and terrible thing.

The rye was high that year. The Germans were busy in the east; the front had moved and the troops had gone with it, and the gendarmes were fewer, and there was a kind of breathing space in the valley, and in that space the ordinary life of the village went on with a strange intensity, as if it knew it had been given a reprieve and meant to use it. The hay was cut. The bees swarmed and were caught. There was a wedding at the mill in July and they danced, and Zofia danced with Tomasz, and the band was two fiddles and a drum, and her mother cried and pretended it was the onions.

It happened in August, at the river, on a hot night after the Assumption.

They had walked down to the bend, as they had a thousand times, and the water was low and warm and the alders made a dark place, and the moon was up. He had been telling her something about the unit, nothing important, an argument about a horse, and then he stopped talking, and she knew, in the way that one knows, that the evening had turned, and she sat very still.

"Zosia."

"Mm."

"I'm going to say something and you mustn't laugh."

"I won't laugh."

"I've thought about it for a long time. Since — I don't know. Since the well."

"The well?"

"The bucket. When you were nine." He was looking at the water. "I thought, that girl is going to be somebody. And then I thought, I would like to be there when she is. And then it was years later and I was thinking it still."

She could not speak. Her heart was going in a way that was not entirely pleasant.

"Say something," he said.

"I can't."

"Say anything. Insult me. That would be better than—"

She kissed him.

It was a bad kiss, the first one; she had no idea what she was doing, and neither, she discovered, did he, and there was a moment when their noses were in the way and she started to laugh against his mouth, and then it stopped being funny. Afterward they sat on the fallen willow, holding hands, and neither of them said anything for a long time, and the river went past, and a heron got up out of the reeds and went away downstream, and she thought: I will remember this. I will remember the exact temperature of the air. And she did, for the rest of her life.

"There's a war on," he said at last.

"I know."

"I might be killed."

"Don't."

"I have to say it. I might be killed, and you might—"

"Tomasz." She turned and took his face in her hands, which she had never done, which she would not do again for fifteen years. "I know there's a war on. I know you might be killed. I am not a child. But if you say it again I will push you in the river, and you will deserve it, and the fish will eat you, and I will tell everyone you were a Protestant."

He laughed, and then he was quiet, and then he said, "When it's over."

"When it's over."

"The house at the bend. Two rooms and a porch."

"And a stove better than my father's."

"A stove better than your father's," he said gravely, "and he will be insulted, and I will invite him to dinner, and he will come, and he will say the stove is not bad, which from him is a compliment, and then we will be all right."

She put her head on his shoulder and looked at the water. "What will you do? After. For work."

He was silent a moment. Then he said, "I think I'm going to be a priest."

She sat up. "What?"

"I've thought about it since I was a boy. My father knows. I've not — I've never said it to anyone else." He turned to look at her, and his face in the moonlight was anxious and very young. "It doesn't change anything. It doesn't change this. I don't know how to explain it. It's not that I don't want—" He stopped. "I want you. I have wanted you since the well. But there is also this other thing, and it has been there longer, and I don't know what to do with it, and I thought you should know before—"

"You thought I should know," she said, "before I kissed you?"

"Before I asked you to wait for me."

She looked at him for a long moment. The heron had gone. Somewhere up at the mill a dog was barking, and then it stopped.

"Then I'll wait," she said.

***

He made her the ring in October. It was a bicycle spoke, cut and filed and bent round a mandrel in his father's shop, and it was not beautiful, and it left a green mark on her finger, and she wore it on a string around her neck because a ring on the hand would be seen. He gave it to her behind the church after the rosary, in the dark, with the smell of the cold coming off the fields.

"It's not gold," he said.

"It's better than gold. Gold is soft."

"That's not how it works."

"That's how it works for me," she said, and put it in her blouse, against her skin, and felt it cold and then warm.

The winter of 1943 was hard. The Germans took the potatoes and then took the pigs and then took the men for labour, thirty-one from the gmina in January, and one of them was the miller's son, who was twenty and had been at the wedding. The partisans in the forest were being hunted; there were sweeps in December and January, and twice Tomasz did not come home for three weeks, and his father played the organ at Mass with his face like a wall. The front was coming. Everyone said so. The Reds were at the Dnieper, and then the Bug, and it was said they would be in Lublin by the summer, and what would happen then no one knew, though everyone had an opinion.

***

In April 1944 the Germans put up a poster in the market square at Krasnystaw. It was printed in Polish and German, and it announced that the population of the district was to be resettled for reasons of military necessity, and that anyone who sheltered partisans or Jews would be punished with death, and that the punishment would be carried out on the whole village.

Her father read it and folded his hands behind his back and looked at it for a while, and then he said, "They will come here."

"When?"

"When they come." He turned away. "We should dig another place. Under the orchard. Not for us — for them. If they come at night the barn is the first place they'll look."

They dug it over three nights, in the dark, with the soil going into the pigsty so that the ground would not show. It was a hole four paces by three, lined with boards, with a hatch under the woodpile and a pipe for air, and it took them six nights in the end, and on the last night her father stood at the edge of it with the spade and said, "If I am taken, you will take them to Pani Zaleska at the mill. She will not refuse. She owes me."

"She won't be asked."

"She will be asked," he said, "and she will not refuse. You will go at night, by the river, not the road."

"Tata."

"Say it back to me."

"At night, by the river, to Pani Zaleska at the mill. She will not refuse."

"Good." He put the spade over his shoulder. "And Zofia. If they take me, you will not come after me, and you will not go to the gendarmes, and you will not do anything at all. You will stay in the house with your mother and the boy. Do you understand?"

"Yes."

"You are lying," her father said, without heat, "but say it anyway."

In May the front came closer and the nights were full of aircraft going east, a low continuous sound like a river in the sky. Tomasz came home for four days in the middle of the month, thinner, with a cough, and on the last evening they walked down to the bend and sat on the willow and did not talk much. The water was high. The alders were in leaf.

"In June," he said, "the unit is going to move. There's a mobilization. After that — I don't know. Maybe it will be over. They say the Red Army will be in Lublin by July."

"And then?"

"And then the war will be over and I will come back and I will go to the seminary and you will wait." He took her hand. "Unless you've changed your mind."

"I haven't changed my mind."

"You might. You're seventeen."

"I'm seventeen and I have made a ring out of a bicycle spoke and I am not going to change my mind." She held up her hand with the string of it around her neck, and he caught the ring and turned it in his fingers. "When?"

"August. September. The latest." He put the ring back. "Zosia, if I don't come back—"

"Then I'll come and find you. I'll walk to wherever you are."

"You won't know where I am."

"I'll find out. I'm a courier," she said. "I'm very good."

He laughed. Then he said, quietly, "I know you are."

They went up the path at midnight with their arms around each other, and at the orchard gate he kissed her and said, "In August," and she said, "In August," and watched him go down the road between the two rows of lilacs, which were in flower, and which filled the whole valley with their smell, so that for the rest of her life she could not smell lilac without being twenty again, and seventeen, and standing at a gate in the dark, believing a thing that was not true.

## The Fire

On the ninth of June 1944 the air was so still that the smoke from the mill went straight up, and the swallows flew low, and the old women said there would be a storm.

There was no storm. There was, instead, a wounded Russian in the forge cellar.

They brought him on the night of the tenth, three men from the forest and Tomasz, on a cart with the boards taken out and straw in the bottom, and he was unconscious and had been for two days, and the wound in his thigh had gone the colour of a plum and smelled of something sweet. He was perhaps twenty-five. He had a name, which was Dmitri, and a photograph in his tunic of a woman with a wide face, and a hole in the left thigh where a shell fragment had gone in and had not come out.

"He'll die," her father said, when they had him on the floor of the forge with the lamp turned low.

"He might not," Tomasz said.

"Look at it. He'll die by Friday and then we'll have a German in the yard asking what the smell is." Her father knelt down with a grunt and put his hand on the man's forehead. "And if he doesn't die, he'll talk in his fever, and the whole village will hear it, and then we'll all be in the ground. Where did you get him?"

"From the swamp. There was a group of them, four, and two were dead, and the others went east. He was in the reeds."

"And you carried him here."

"On the cart. Yes."

Her father looked up at him. "You're a fool, Tomasz Wierzbicki."

"I know."

"Get me hot water and the good knife and all the clean linen in the house, and tell your father to pray, because I can't do both." He rolled up his sleeves. "And then go away. You're not to be seen here."

They kept him four days. The fragment came out on the first night, and her father sewed the wound and packed it with a paste of honey and rye flour that his own mother had used, and Zofia held the lamp and then held the man's shoulders while he thrashed, and on the third day the fever broke and he opened his eyes and said something in Russian, and began to cry, and her father gave him water and said, "There, there, soldier," in Polish, and the man fell asleep again.

On the twelfth the Frydmans moved from the barn to the new hole under the orchard, because her father did not want them in the same building as a Russian who might shout in his sleep. It took an hour, in the dark. Rywka Frydman had to be carried. Sara walked between Zofia and her father with the doll held against her chest, and she was ten now, and thin as a stick, and she had not been out of doors in daylight since 1942. When they got to the orchard she stopped and looked up at the sky, which was full of stars, and she said, "Oh," very quietly, and stood there, and Zofia had to make her move.

***

On the morning of the fourteenth Zofia was sent with a message to the mill.

It was a stupid errand and she knew it was stupid. The message was in a loaf, and the loaf was in a basket, and the basket had eggs on top of it, and she went by the field path because the road had a checkpoint. The message was from the unit to Pani Zaleska, and it was about flour, and it was written in pencil on a piece of paper the size of a hand, and it was in Tomasz's writing, which she did not know until she was already in the meadow and had stopped to check that the bread was not broken, and saw the shape of his letters, and smiled.

The patrol came out of the birch wood.

There were three of them and one was Vogel, the young one, and one was the Ukrainian they called Iwan, and she did not know the third. They had a dog. She ran, which was the worst thing she could have done, and she got perhaps forty metres before Iwan caught her by the hair and put her on the ground.

They found the paper. Of course they found it. They took her basket apart with the butts of their rifles and shook out the eggs and the loaf onto the grass, and Vogel picked up the paper and read it and laughed, and said something to Iwan in German, and Iwan hit her across the face with the back of his hand, twice, and then they took her to Krasnystaw in the lorry.

The gendarmerie post was in a house on the Lublin road that had belonged to a doctor. There was a yard with a pump. There was a room at the back with a table and two chairs and a strip of carpet, and on the wall a picture of the Führer that had been hung too high, and a bucket in the corner, and a window with the shutters closed and a bar across them, and a wasp working at the window all the time she was in there.

Kliemann came at noon. He was fat and had a pleasant face and he brought a bottle of beer with him and set it on the table and did not offer her any.

"You are Zofia Krawiec," he said in Polish, which he spoke well and with enjoyment. "From Brzozówka. Your father is the smith. Yes?"

"Yes."

"You are seventeen. My God, you are a child." He sat down opposite her and folded his hands on the table. "Do you know what happens to children who carry messages for bandits?"

She said nothing.

"They are shot," Kliemann said pleasantly, "and their families are shot, and their village is burned. This is not my idea. This is the order of the Reich. I am a policeman, I carry out orders." He took a drink of the beer. "But it is a hot day and I am not a monster. You are going to tell me where the bandits are, and then you will go home to your mother, and we will forget the eggs."

She said nothing.

He put the paper on the table and turned it round with one finger. "This is a boy's hand. Look at it. A boy, not a man. Someone from your village, yes? Someone you know. Someone you like, perhaps." He smiled. "You are seventeen. There is always someone."

She looked at the paper. The flour. The signature was a mark, not a name, but the letters were his, and she thought: they will find him. They will go to the mill and Pani Zaleska will tell them nothing, and they will go to the village and they will ask, and someone will say, the organist's boy, he writes like that, everyone knows he writes like that, and they will take his father. And she thought: if I say nothing they will shoot me, and then they will take his father anyway.

Kliemann waited. He was patient in a way that was worse than violence, because it suggested that he had done this many times and had never yet needed to hurry.

At four in the afternoon Vogel came in and spoke to him in German, quickly, and Kliemann's face changed, and he said something back, and Vogel went out.

"There is a wounded man in your village," Kliemann said. "A Russian. My sergeant has been watching your father's forge since Tuesday. Also there are Jews." He leaned forward. "Now, girl. I am going to be honest with you, because I have no time. In one hour I am going to send a company to Brzozówka, and they are going to search the village, house by house, and if they find a Russian in your father's cellar, or Jews, or weapons, then the village will be burned, and the men will be shot, and the women will be sent to the camp, and you will have caused it. If you tell me now, this afternoon, then I will send my sergeant and four men and we will take the Russian and the Jews and it will be a police matter, and the village will be spared, and you will go home." He spread his hands. "That is the whole of it. One hour."

She was seventeen. She had been hit twice in the face and her left eye was closing, and she had been in that room for five hours with a wasp, and she was so frightened that she could taste it, a metallic taste at the back of her mouth, and she was thinking not in sentences but in pictures: her mother at the tub with the church linen; Piotrek asleep; her father's hands.

"The forge," she said.

Kliemann did not move. "What about the forge?"

"Under the forge. There's a cellar. The coal cellar." Her voice came out steady, which afterwards seemed to her the most terrible thing of all. "He's there. The Russian. He's been there since Monday."

Kliemann wrote it down. He wrote slowly, in a small neat hand, and while he wrote the wasp went up and down the window.

"And the Jews?"

"I don't know about any Jews."

"Girl."

"I don't know about any Jews." She heard her own voice go up at the end, like a question. "There are no Jews in Brzozówka. They took them in forty-two."

Kliemann looked at her for a while. Then he shrugged, and wrote something else, and blotted it, and said, "Wait here."

She waited two hours. The wasp died or went away. At six a policeman brought her a piece of bread and a cup of water and did not speak. At half past six Kliemann came back with the paper and put it in front of her, and it was two pages, written in German, and he read it aloud to her in Polish, translating as he went, and it said that she had been detained in the meadow with a message, and that she had stated that a wounded Soviet soldier was concealed in the cellar beneath the smithy of Józef Krawiec, and that there were persons of Jewish origin concealed in the same dwelling, and that she had signed it of her own free will.

"That last part isn't true," she said.

"It is true now," Kliemann said. "Sign."

She signed. Her hand was shaking, and the Z came out with a round loop, careful, a girl's signature, and she looked at it after she had done it and thought, that is my hand, that is my hand on this paper, and then Kliemann took the paper away and put it in a folder and said, "Go home."

"Am I—"

"Go home," he said, and held the door. "Walk. Do not run. If you run, my men will shoot you, and I will have to fill out another paper."

She walked out through the yard, past the pump, past the lorry, and the sentry at the gate looked at her and did not move, and she went down the Lublin road with her arms at her sides, and the sun was going down behind the town and the sky over the rooftops was the colour of a peach.

She walked for half a kilometre. Then she heard the shooting, a long way off, in the direction of the village, and she began to run.

The first thing she saw was the smoke, and it was not the colour she expected. She had expected black. It was white and grey, a great slow column going up and bending east, and under it, as she came over the rise by the birch wood, the village was burning in a line, from the mill end, house by house, in the still air, and she could hear the fire, which was a sound like canvas tearing, and men shouting, and a horse screaming, and she could not see any people at all.

***

She came down the hill into the smoke.

The mill was gone. The Frydmans' shop was gone. The school was burning. The church was not burning; they had not fired the church, and the wooden spire stood up out of the smoke with the bell gone, and the door was open, and there was a man on the steps with his hands over his head.

She ran to the crossroads. The forge was burning, and her father's house behind it was burning, and the roof had fallen in at one end, and there were flames coming out of the windows in a way that was almost quiet, almost domestic, like a stove on a winter evening.

"Matka!"

Nobody answered.

She got as far as the gate. The heat came off the house like a wall and she could not go further, and she stood at the gate and shouted, and her voice went into the fire and came back to her, and then a man caught her round the waist from behind and carried her backwards, and it was Antoni Szeliga, the deputy sołtys, whose face was black with soot, and he was shouting something she could not hear.

"The orchard!" he was shouting. "Zofia! The orchard!"

She did not understand. Then she did.

She broke away from him and ran along the fence to the orchard, and the orchard was not burning, and the woodpile was there, and the hatch under it was open.

The hole was empty.

There was a chair knocked over at the bottom of the ladder. There was Sara's doll on the earth floor, face up, the china face cracked across one cheek. There was a book. There was the smell.

She stood at the edge of the hole and looked down at it for a long time. Then she climbed down and picked up the doll and the book, and climbed out, and sat down on the grass under the apple tree with the doll in her lap, and that is where they found her, half an hour later, when the fire had come down to embers and the Germans had gone.

They had taken her father. They had taken him alive, with his hands tied, and put him in the lorry with four other men and the young man from the mill, and driven away. Her mother and Piotrek had been in the house. The neighbours had got them out, or had got out what there was. The Frydmans had been taken out of the orchard and shot at the edge of the birch wood with the Russian, and they were lying there in a row, four of them, and someone had put a coat over Rywka's face, and Sara was between her mother and her father with her hand out, and Zofia, when she saw it, made a sound that she had not known she could make, and Antoni Szeliga took her by the shoulders and turned her round and held her against his chest while she screamed into his coat.

Thirty-one people died at Brzozówka on the fourteenth of June 1944. Eleven houses burned, and the mill, and the school, and the shop, and the forge, and the barns with the winter grain in them. The church stood. It stood for another year, and then in 1945 a shell from the front took the roof, and in 1957 the parish rebuilt it in brick, with the money of the parish and a grant from the Office for Religious Affairs which was signed, in the end, on a Tuesday, by a deputy director who had never been to Brzozówka and who did not know, when she signed it, that she had.

***

Tomasz came at dawn.

He came up the road from the forest with two others and his rifle on his back, and he stopped at the edge of the village and looked at it, and then he came on, and he found her sitting by the church wall with her hands in her lap.

"Zosia."

She looked up at him. He was grey with fatigue and his eyes were red, and he had come fifteen kilometres on foot, and she could see that he had been running.

"Your mother?" he said.

She shook her head.

"And Piotrek?"

She shook her head.

He knelt down in front of her. "Your father?"

"They took him. To Lublin, they said. To the castle."

He was silent. Then he said, "The frydmans?"

She could not say it. She looked towards the birch wood and he followed her look, and he got up, and stood there for a moment with his hand over his mouth, and then he went off towards the trees, and she watched him go, and she thought: now. Tell him now. Tell him now, before he gets there.

He came back in twenty minutes. His face was different. He sat down on the ground beside her with his back against the wall, and neither of them spoke for a long time.

"The Russian too," he said at last.

"Yes."

"I brought him here." He put his hands over his face. "I brought him here."

"Tomasz—"

"I brought him here," he said, from behind his hands, "and they found him, and they burned the village. That's what happened. That's what happened, isn't it."

She looked at him. The sun was up over the birch wood and the smoke had thinned to a haze, and somewhere behind them a woman was keening over a body, a long rising note that went on and on and then stopped.

"Yes," she said. "That's what happened."

She meant, at that moment, to tell him everything. She had it in her mouth. She had the exact words. But the words were: *I told them where he was. Kliemann asked me and I told him and they burned the village anyway,* and every time she got to the end of that sentence, in her mind, there was nothing on the other side of it — no face that she could bear to see, no life after it — and so she said what she said, and then it was too late, and then it was years too late, and then it was 1958.

***

They buried them on the sixteenth, in the churchyard, in one grave for the ones who had no family, and separate graves for the Frydmans, because Father Zaremba said that they were of the old covenant and should be buried by their own law, and there was no one to do it, so he did it himself, with his own hands, in his vestments, reading the Hebrew he did not know from a book he borrowed from a doctor in Krasnystaw, and he was sick afterwards, and he did it anyway.

The village looked for someone to blame. It looked, first, at the partisans, because the partisans had brought the Russian to the forge, and the men in the forest were not popular that summer; then it looked at the miller, because the miller was a Protestant and had a cousin in the Gestapo, which was not true; then it looked at Antoni Szeliga, who had been seen on the road at Krasnystaw on the twelfth talking to the gendarmes about a permit for the mill.

Szeliga had been talking to the gendarmes about a permit for the mill. He said so, at the meeting in the church porch, in front of eighty people, and he said it again, and nobody believed him, and a man called Bednarek hit him in the face and he did not hit back, and after that no one in Brzozówka spoke to Antoni Szeliga again for nine years, and his wife would not sit beside him in church, and in March 1953 he hanged himself in his barn.

Zofia was in the church porch at that meeting. She was seventeen. She stood at the back with her hands folded and let a man be condemned for what she had done, and she did not say a word, and afterwards she went home to the house that was not there any more and lay down on the floor of the neighbour's kitchen and slept for fourteen hours.

***

On the twenty-second of July the radio said that a Polish Committee of National Liberation had been established at Chełm, and that the Red Army had crossed the Bug. On the twenty-fourth it said that Lublin was free. On the twenty-third the Soviet tanks came through Brzozówka on the Krasnystaw road, and the village stood at the edge of the road and watched them, and this time nobody waved.

On the twenty-fifth a lorry came up from Krasnystaw with four men in it, in Polish uniform with the eagle on their caps but not the eagle that anyone in Brzozówka recognized, and one of them got down, and he was a man of forty in a captain's tunic with the left sleeve pinned up, and he walked into the middle of the village and stood in the ashes of the forge and looked around him and said, "Where is Józef Krawiec?"

"He was taken," said someone. "In June."

The man turned. He had a broad, ugly, clever face, and grey eyes, and he looked at the burned houses one after another as though he were counting them.

"And the family?"

"His wife and son are dead. In the churchyard." She was standing ten paces away, and she said it herself, in a voice that surprised her. "I'm his daughter."

The man looked at her for a long time. Then he came over and took off his cap, which nobody had done, and said, "I knew your father. In 1937, at Krasnystaw, when they broke up the strike at the mill. He gave me a horse." He put his cap back on. "I am Wiktor Sroka. I am with the Polish Army, and also with the Party, and I have come to see what can be done here."

"There's nothing here," Zofia said.

"There is always something," said Wiktor Sroka. "That is the first thing you learn."

He came back in August, and in September, and by October she was working for him. Not for the Party, at first — she was seventeen and did not know what the Party was, except that her father had hated it and that the men who belonged to it had come in the lorries and had not been afraid. She worked for Sroka because he was the only person in the district who talked about the future as if it were a real place, and because he had lost an arm at the Ebro fighting for something he could explain, and because he looked at her without pity, which after the summer was a relief.

"You are carrying something," he said to her once, in October, in the office they had made in the mill that had been rebuilt as a cooperative. "I can see it. You walk like a woman carrying a bucket."

"I'm not carrying anything."

"Everyone in this district is carrying something. The ones who say they are not are carrying the most." He lit a cigarette, one-handed, with a matchbox held under his chin. "I will tell you a thing, Zofia Krawiec, and you will not believe it for ten years. Guilt is a bourgeois luxury. It is the most expensive thing in Poland and it does no one any good. What happened in June happened because of the Germans and because of the war and because this country is a road between two empires that both like to march down it. If you want to do something about it, then do something. Build. Teach. Join. But do not sit in a corner with your bucket."

She looked at him. "And if it was partly my fault?"

"Then it was partly your fault," Sroka said, "and you will do something anyway, and in twenty years you will look back and see that the something you did was worth more than the fault. Or you will not. But you will not know until you have done it." He put the matchbox in his pocket. "There is a school at Krasnystaw. We need someone to teach the children to read, because the Germans did not, and the nuns are too frightened, and the peasants are too busy. You can read. Pan Wróbel taught you. Go and teach."

She went.

***

In August, on the ninth, Tomasz was arrested at Krasnystaw.

She did not know for three years. She was at a meeting in Chełm that day, learning how to organize a cooperative, and when she came back they told her that the organist's son had been taken by the NKVD with eleven others from the district and put on a train, and that the charge was service in the Home Army, and that they had been sent east, and that no one knew where.

She went to Sroka. She stood in front of his desk and said, "Get him out."

Sroka looked at her for a while. "Who?"

"Tomasz Wierzbicki. The organist's son. You know who."

"I know who he is." Sroka put down his pen. "I cannot get him out. Nobody can get him out. He was in the AK, and the AK shot at the Red Army in Vilnius, and the NKVD does not ask my opinion about such things." He paused. "Why does it matter to you?"

She opened her mouth, and then closed it.

"Ah," said Sroka. And then, more quietly, "Zofia. Listen to me. The war is not over. It is going to go on for a long time, in this country, in another form. There will be people on one side and people on the other, and you will have to choose, and the choosing will cost you things you cannot imagine now. Do not choose with your heart. The heart is a bad commissar. Choose with your head, and then make your heart follow, and in ten years you will find that it has."

She stood there. Outside, the mill wheel turned, and someone was singing in the yard.

"I don't know what that means," she said.

"You will," said Wiktor Sroka.

# Five

## The Building

They put her in a room on the ground floor with a table and six chairs and a portrait of the Cardinal Primate, and a young priest brought a glass of water she did not touch.

"Please sit down, Father."

"I'll stand, thank you." He was looking at her with a kind of careful astonishment, as though she were a piece of furniture that had been reported lost. "Zosia."

"Comrade Director," she said. "Or Pani Dyrektor, if you prefer. In this building."

"Of course." He inclined his head. "Pani Dyrektor."

"Sit down."

He sat. He folded his hands on the table. He was thirty-four and looked it, and he was tired, and there was a small blue mark at the corner of his mouth where he had cut himself shaving. His cassock was good wool and had been turned once at the cuffs. His shoes were polished and had been resoled at the toe.

"You're in the Office," he said.

"I am deputy director."

"I know. I have known for two years." He said it without any particular weight and watched what it did to her. "The grant for the church at Brzozówka. It came through your office in 1956, and again in 1957 for the roof. Your signature is on the second one. I have looked at it a good many times."

She had known this and had arranged not to know it. The application had come up through the ordinary channel, from the Lublin voivodeship office, with a recommendation, and she had read it and had seen the parish name and had signed it because there was no reason not to sign it, and because to refuse to sign it would have been a fact that someone might notice, and because a roof is a roof.

"You might have written," she said.

"To what address?"

There was a knock and the young priest put his head in and said that Monsignor Lubieński was waiting, and Tomasz said, without looking away from her, "Tell the monsignor that I am with the Office and will come when I am finished," and the door closed.

"The nomination," Zofia said. She opened her folder. She had brought the summary, not the file, and the summary was four pages and she knew it by heart. "His Excellency Bishop Krynicki has submitted a terna to the Council of State, through this Office, in accordance with the understanding of 1956. The Office is required to give its opinion within thirty days. It is my task to prepare that opinion. In order to do so I am directed to speak with the candidate."

"Directed by whom?"

"By my director."

"And your director is directed by whom?"

"The Presidium."

"Which is directed by the Politburo, which is directed by Moscow, which is directed by—" He stopped himself, and made a small apologetic gesture with one hand. "I'm sorry. That was a speech. I have been giving a great many speeches lately, and I find they come more easily than they used to, which frightens me."

"It's a chain of authority," Zofia said. "Every institution has one. Yours has one. I have read your file, so I know about yours."

His eyes were steady. "Then you know that I have been obedient, and that obedience has cost me, and that I am not inclined to be lectured about chains."

She closed the folder. "Father. I am going to speak to you as one official to another, and then I am going to speak to you as myself, and I would be grateful if you would let me do the first before the second."

He sat back. "Go on."

"The Office has no objection in principle to your nomination." She heard her own voice, flat and competent, and was relieved by it. "The Bishop's choice is the Bishop's choice. But this Office has a legitimate interest in the public order, and there are three matters on which it requires assurances before it will transmit a favourable opinion."

"Three."

"First. The Millennium preparations. The episcopate has announced a program of pastoral work leading to 1966. This Office has no wish to interfere with prayer. It does have an interest in ensuring that the program is not used for political ends, and in particular that no bishop is deployed in a manner that will bring the Church into conflict with the state. The Curia will be asked to give a written undertaking that the candidate will take no part in the Millennium program without the prior knowledge of this Office."

He did not move.

"Second. The question of religious instruction in the schools. You have spoken on this subject in Lublin, twice, in April and in June, and both sermons were reported. You are aware that the matter is under review. The Office requires an undertaking that the candidate will not speak publicly on the question of catechism in the schools while the review is pending."

"And third?"

Zofia looked at the folder. "Third. The candidate will make himself available for an informative conversation with the fourth department of the Security Service, at a time and place of its choosing, and will confirm in writing that the conversation took place and that he has no matters to conceal."

The room was quiet. Somewhere in the building a door banged and a woman laughed, and the laugh went up the stairwell and faded.

"Ah," said Tomasz.

"That is the position."

"That is not a position. That is a purchase." He said it mildly. "The first is a gag. The second is a gag. The third is a rope." He leaned forward. "Pani Dyrektor. Let us be accurate with each other, since we are being official. In 1952 I signed a declaration in the Mokotów prison, and I named two men, and one of them lost his post and one of them was in Rakowiecka for a year. I have spent seven years since then being a priest, which is what I am, and doing it as well as I know how, and I have not signed anything else. I have not informed. I have not reported. Not once. And now you come to me with a paper that says: confirm in writing that you have no matters to conceal." He shook his head slowly. "Do you know what that paper is? It is the beginning. That is all. The first one is always a formality. The second one is a name. The third one is a friend."

"Nobody is asking you for names."

"Not yet."

"Father—"

"You said you would speak to me as yourself," he said. "I would like that now. Because I have not seen you since I was twenty and you were seventeen, and in that time I have thought about you every day, and I would like to know what happened to you, and instead you are reading me a list." His voice had not risen, but something in it had changed, and the change was worse than shouting. "What happened to you, Zosia?"

She stood up. She went to the window, which looked out on the yard and the wall and a plane tree with three leaves left on it, and she stood there with her back to him and her arms folded.

"The village was burned," she said. "My mother and my brother were killed. My father was taken to Lublin and then to Majdanek and he came out in August and he was never the same. You were taken by the NKVD and I did not know for three years whether you were alive. I was seventeen years old and I had nothing and no one and a man came with a lorry and told me that the future was a real place and that I could help build it. So I did. I have been building it for fourteen years. Whatever you think of the building, I helped put it up, and I am not ashamed of it, and I am not going to be lectured about it by a priest who spent the war in the forest and the peace in a seminary."

She turned round.

He was looking at her with an expression she could not read at all.

"That is a good answer," he said. "It is almost true. It is true in the way that a wall is true — the bricks are real, and the wall is in the wrong place." He stood. "I'll take the first two."

"What?"

"The first two conditions. I will give the Curia my word that I will not use the Millennium for politics, and I will not speak on the schools while the review is pending. I will keep my word; I always keep my word; it is my only accomplishment. The third I will not do." He picked up his hat from the table. "And I would like you to write that in your opinion. Write: the candidate declines. He declines in the following words. 'I have signed one paper in my life and I will not sign another.' You may quote me."

"That will end your nomination."

"Then it will end." He put on his hat and took it off again, a habit of his father's. "Pani Dyrektor. May I say one more thing, not as a candidate?"

"Yes."

"You have read my file. I have read yours." He said it evenly. "Not the Security Service file. The other one. The one that everybody in this country carries, the one that is written on their faces. I have been looking at yours for six years, from a distance, in the newspapers, at the Congress of the Polish-Soviet Friendship Society in 1955, where you sat in the fourth row." He paused. "You are not a happy woman."

"Don't."

"I am not trying to wound you."

"You are doing it very well for a man who isn't trying."

"I know," he said. "I'm sorry."

They stood there. The plane tree moved in the wind, and one of the three leaves came off it and went down the length of the window and out of sight.

"They will send me again," she said. "To ask."

"I know."

"And you will refuse again."

"I will refuse again." He turned to go, and then stopped with his hand on the door. "Zosia. The church at Brzozówka. The roof. Did you know it was for the roof?"

"Yes."

"Thank you for the roof."

"Go away, Tomasz."

He went. She stood at the window for a long time, and then she sat down at the table and wrote, in her small careful hand, on the back of the summary: *Declines third condition. Quotes: I have signed one paper in my life and I will not sign another. Advises opinion will end nomination. — 4:20 p.m.* And then she sat looking at it, and then she tore it into four pieces and put them in her handbag, because Hanna would type it and Hanna would file it and Wrona would read it before the ink was dry.

***

She left the office at seven. She did not take the car. She walked up Bagatela to the corner and then, without deciding to, turned into the park.

Łazienki in November is a place for people who have nowhere to be. The trees were bare and the water was the colour of lead, and there were three old men on a bench with a newspaper between them, and a woman with a pram, and a boy throwing stones at the ducks, and a couple standing close together under the monument to Chopin with their backs to the path.

She walked down to the water and stood at the rail.

"Pani Dyrektor."

She turned. He was five paces behind her, hat in his hand, and he had been following her, or he had come here too, and she found that she did not care which.

"You followed me."

"I followed you," he said. "I am sorry. It was not dignified. I stood at the gate for ten minutes deciding, and then I decided badly."

"You should go home, Father."

"I have a room at the seminary and a bed and a lamp and a book, and I have been going home to it for five years, and I find tonight that I do not want to." He came and stood beside her at the rail, at a proper distance, and looked at the water. "May I walk with you? Only walk. I will not ask you anything."

"All right."

They walked. Past the water, up the avenue, under the bare trees, with the lamps just coming on and the gravel loud under their feet. It was cold enough to see breath. He put his hat on, then took it off, then put it on again.

"Ostashkov," he said at last. "You will want to know. Everyone wants to know, and no one asks, and I have got into the habit of telling people so that they do not have to ask."

"You don't have to."

"It's easier than not telling." He put his hands in his pockets. "It was a monastery, before. On an island in the Seliger, a wall and a church and a lot of buildings, and they put us in the buildings. There were about five thousand of us in the end, Poles, mostly officers, but also a good many boys like me, and some civilians. We had a roll call twice a day in all weathers. The food was a bowl of soup and two hundred grams of bread, and in the winter the bread came frozen and you thawed it under your arm. The worst was the first winter, forty-four to forty-five. I was twenty. I had never been ill in my life." He paused. "I got scurvy. My gums bled and my teeth loosened, and I could not eat the bread because my mouth was sores, and a man from Poznań taught me to chew pine needles, which was vile, and which saved me, and which is why I can tell you the taste of a pine needle in the same way I can tell you the taste of an apple."

They came to the Belweder and turned back. The guards at the gate watched them go by without interest.

"There was a priest in the camp," he said. "A Jesuit from Kraków, Father Bąk, and he was sixty and he did not last the first winter, but before he died he heard confessions in the latrine, because it was the only place where a man could speak without being heard, and I served his Mass on a suitcase with a tin of sardines for a paten. He had a stole that he kept inside his shirt, and once a week he put it on and we stood around him in the cold and he said the words, and it was illegal, and if they had found it he would have been shot, and he did it anyway, and it was the only real thing that happened to me in three years." He glanced at her. "I have never told anyone that."

"Why me?"

"Because you are the only person I know who will not be impressed by it," he said.

She stopped walking. They were on the path by the water again, and the lamps were reflected in it in long broken lines.

"I wrote about you," she said.

He was silent.

"In 1949. There was a commission, a verification commission, for the voivodeship committee, and they asked me about my wartime contacts, and they had a list of names, and I was asked about everyone I had known in the Home Army. And I told them the truth, which was that I had known you, and that you had been in the unit, and I said—" She stopped. "I said that you were clever and that you should not be allowed to teach young people."

He looked at the water.

"It was in the file," she said. "It went with you to Mokotów in 1951. That is what I do now, Tomasz. I write sentences that go into files, and men read them in rooms, and other men go and knock on doors. I have been doing it for nine years, and I am very good at it, and I have never once been asked to look at the face of the man in the file until today."

He was quiet for so long that she began to be afraid.

"Say something," she said.

"When they showed me the deposition," he said, "in 1951, the German one — they showed me yours first. The commission paper. The officer put it on the table and said, 'A comrade from your village has given us a full account of you.' And I read it, and I saw the sentence about teaching young people, and it was not signed, it was a summary, and I did not know whose it was. I thought it was Szeliga. Or the miller." He turned to her. "And then they showed me the German, and I knew."

She could not speak.

"I have known since 1951," he said. "Both things. Since 1951."

The wind came off the water. Somewhere across the park a tram went along the street beyond the trees.

"And you never—" She stopped. "You never wrote. You never told anyone. You could have destroyed me. Six years ago, when the thaw came, you could have written one letter to *Tygodnik* and I would have been finished."

"Yes," he said.

"Why?"

He put his hat on, and this time he left it on.

"Because I am a priest," he said, "and because you are not the only person in this story who has signed a paper." He turned and began to walk, slowly, and she went with him, because there was nothing else to do. "In the prison they put a statement in front of me at four in the morning, after nine days, and it named two men I had studied with, and one of them was a good man and a fool and the other was neither, and I signed it, and I have been told since that the good man lost his post and the other was in Rakowiecka for eleven months, and I do not know to this day what became of him." He looked at her. "So you see. We are the same. We are two people who signed, and who have spent ten years being very careful about what we do with our hands. I have no stones to throw. I have never had any stones. That is the whole of it."

They had come to the gate on Agrykola. The street beyond was full of people going home, and the lights of a tram, and a man selling something hot from a brazier with a good red glow.

"Will you come again?" he said.

"I'll be sent."

"That is not what I asked."

She looked at him. In the lamplight he was a thin man in a black coat with his hat in his hand again and a cut on his lip, and she was thirty-one years old and the deputy director of an office of the Polish state, and she had not been this frightened since she was seventeen.

"Yes," she said. "I'll come."

***

She walked home. It was three kilometres and she did it in forty minutes and she did not feel the cold. At the flat in the MDM the light was on in the kitchen and Jerzy was at the table with his papers and a glass of tea, and he looked up and said, "You're late. There's cabbage."

"I'm not hungry."

"You're never hungry. Sit down and have some cabbage." He pushed a chair out with his foot. He was a big, slow, kind man with a bald patch and spectacles, and he had been at the Planning Commission since 1950, and he loved her in a way that she had accepted at the time as sufficient and had never examined since. "How was the Curia?"

"Difficult."

"They're all difficult." He turned a page. "There's a thing in the paper about the film. Wajda. *Popiół i diament*. It's at the Palladium. Everyone at the Commission has seen it and they all say it's a masterpiece and nobody will tell me what it's about."

"It's about the Home Army."

"Ah," said Jerzy. "Then they won't tell you what it's about." He took off his spectacles and rubbed his eyes. "Zofia. Are you all right?"

"Yes."

"You've been standing in the hall for a minute and a half."

She came and sat down and ate the cabbage, which was very good, and he told her about a dispute over the allocation of cement, and she listened and asked two questions, and at half past ten she went into the bedroom and took the tin of buttons down from the shelf.

It was a good tin, from before the war, with a picture of a Dutch girl on the lid. Inside were buttons, a thimble, a brass key, and a ring made from a bicycle spoke, which left a green mark on the finger and which she had not looked at for four years. She took it out and held it in her palm.

Then she put it back, and put the tin on the shelf, and lay down beside her husband in the dark and listened to him breathe, and did not sleep until three.

# Six

## The Thaw

The circular came down from the Department of Propaganda on the fourth of November and it was three pages long and Hanna had typed it four times because the first three had been corrected in three different hands.

Zofia read it standing up. It concerned the conduct of religious instruction in the schools of the voivodeship, and it said, in the language that such documents used, that in view of the forthcoming review of the concordat-adjacent arrangements, no priest was to be admitted to a school building for the purposes of instruction without the written consent of the presidium of the appropriate people's council, and that the consent was to be issued on a form, and that the form was attached.

"Who drafted this?"

"The department. It came with a note from Comrade Rykowski."

"Rykowski is a secretary of the Warsaw committee. He does not draft circulars about catechism."

"He signed the note," Hanna said. "The circular is from the department."

Zofia turned to the last page. The form had eleven questions, and question nine asked the applicant to state whether he had ever been a member of an organization hostile to the People's Republic, and question ten asked him to list the names of persons who could vouch for his political attitude.

"Who is the addressee?"

"All voivodeship offices. And the curias, through the voivodeship offices."

"So it will be in the papers by Sunday."

"It will be in the curias by Thursday," Hanna said, "and in the papers by Sunday, yes."

Zofia looked at her. Hanna was standing with her hands folded, in the grey wool dress she wore on Mondays and Thursdays, with her hair in a roll, and her face was perfectly composed, and Zofia, who had learned to read her secretary the way she read a document, saw that she was angry.

"You think it's stupid."

"I think it is a mistake," Hanna said.

"Say why."

Hanna's hands did not move. "Because it will not stop anything. The priests will hold the lessons in the sacristy, and the children will go, and the parents will send them, and it will be in the papers, and everyone will say the state is persecuting the Church, and the ones who write the form will be the ones who are blamed." She paused. "And because question ten is a list of names. People will write down their neighbours."

"Yes," Zofia said. "They will."

"I am to make twenty copies."

"Make twenty copies, and add a covering note from this office saying that the form is to be administered by the voivodeship offices and not by this office, and that no copy is to be retained in a parish file." Zofia handed the circular back. "If we must do it, we will do it in a way that does not look like an invitation to denounce someone."

Hanna took it. At the door she said, "Pani Dyrektor," and hesitated.

"Yes?"

"My brother is at the seminary at Włocławek."

"I know."

"He will have to fill in a form like this in four years, if he is ordained."

Zofia looked at her for a moment. "Then go home tonight and tell him to learn to write very short answers," she said, "and to keep his own list in his head, and never to write it down. And Hanna. Do not tell anyone in this building that your brother is at Włocławek."

Hanna's face did not change, but something behind it did, and she said, "No, Pani Dyrektor," and went out.

***

Brzozowski collapsed on the tenth, in his office, at about eleven in the morning, and it was Hanna who found him, and it was Zofia who rode with him in the ambulance, holding his hand, which was the first time she had touched him in four years.

He was operated on that night at the clinic on Skaryszewska and he came out of it alive and full of tubes and very angry, and on the third day he sent for her.

"You are acting director," he said. His voice had gone to a whisper with a whistle in it. "The minister will sign the paper tomorrow. It is in my drawer, the second one, with the red seal, and you will not open it until Monday because I want to be dead before it is read."

"You are not going to die."

"I am going to die in about five weeks, and I have made my peace with it in the sense that I am furious and there is nothing I can do." He motioned at the chair. "Sit down. Have they come to you yet?"

"Wrona came on Thursday."

"What did he want?"

"He wants me to obtain a signature. He has a text. It is a cooperation declaration, in the ordinary form, and he has left the date blank so that it can be filled in by the candidate's own hand."

Brzozowski closed his eyes. "And what did you say?"

"I said that I would consider it."

"Good. Consider it slowly." He opened his eyes. "Zofia. I am going to tell you something and then I am going to be sick, which is what I do now, and you will please not help me, because the nurse is a Communist and enjoys it." He shifted in the bed. "In 1949 I was on a commission. Not the one that examined you. Another one. It concerned a man in the ministry, a lawyer, a very good lawyer, my friend since 1932, and the question was whether he had concealed his membership of the PPS before the war, which he had not concealed, because everyone knew. The commission asked me to write a note. I wrote a note. I wrote that he was politically unreliable and that his attitude to the Party was formal. It was a sentence of eleven words and it cost him his career and probably his life, because he was arrested in 1951 and I do not know where he died." He stopped and breathed for a moment. "I did it because I was frightened. Not of prison. Of being a man who was not asked again. That is the real fear in this building, Zofia. Not the cell. The corridor with the doors that do not open for you."

The nurse came in with a tray and Brzozowski waved her away.

"So," he said. "You will now ask me why I am telling you this."

"To tell me not to do it."

"No," said Brzozowski. "To tell you that you will do it, or you will not, and that whichever you do you will be a person who did it, and that this is not a matter of a clean conscience at the end. There is no clean conscience. There are only the things you can live inside." He closed his eyes. "I have lived inside mine for nine years and it has been a small house. I would not recommend it. But I have lived."

***

On the eleventh there was a meeting at the Central Committee, in a room on the fourth floor with a long table and green baize and a portrait of Gomułka at one end, and eleven men and two women, and Zofia was one of the two.

The secretary who chaired it was called Tkaczuk, and he was fifty-one and had been a plumber in Łódź and had spent the war in the Soviet Union and had come back in 1945 in a uniform, and he was not stupid. He had a way of listening with his head down and his hands flat on the table that made everyone speak more carefully.

"The Lublin terna," he said. "The Office has had it since October. Comrade Krawiec, you have the file."

"I have the file."

"Summarize."

She summarized. She gave them the three names, and the Bishop's preference, and the age and the record of the candidate, and the state of the opinion, and she did it in four minutes, without adjectives, in the way that Brzozowski had taught her.

"And the fourth department?"

"Colonel Wrona has submitted a proposal. It is that the candidate be accepted, subject to a declaration of cooperation."

There was a movement along the table, not much.

"Which he will not sign," said Tkaczuk.

"He has refused, in conversation, to sign what was proposed to him. He has offered other assurances."

"Which are worth nothing." Tkaczuk raised his head. "Comrade. Let me tell you what the situation is, because the Office has a habit of seeing this as a legal question and it is not a legal question. In 1956 we made an agreement with the Church. We made it because it was necessary and because the country was in a state that required it, and I do not repudiate it. But the agreement was not a conversion. The Church has used the last two years as a breathing space, and it has used it well, and now we are in a position where a bishop of thirty-four with a book and a camp record is being put forward as the future of Polish Catholicism, and we are being asked to consent to it, and the question is whether we are governing this country or whether we are managing its applause." He looked down the table. "The comrades of the fourth department take the view that the candidate can be neutralized. I have some sympathy with that. Comrade Krawiec, do you?"

"I think the candidate can be refused," Zofia said, "and that if he is refused, the Church will have a martyr who costs nothing to maintain and who will be preaching in twenty years, and the Primate will write a pastoral letter, and it will be read in every church in Poland in Lent, and it will say that the state obstructs the free election of bishops. I think that is a worse outcome than a bishop."

"And if he is accepted and does not sign?"

"Then we have a bishop of thirty-four who owes us nothing and who knows that he beat us." She paused. "But he has given his word on two matters, and I believe he will keep it, because it is his only achievement."

Tkaczuk looked at her for a long moment. Then he said, "The fourth department's view is that his word can be obtained. Not on two matters. On the paper."

"Colonel Wrona's view."

"Colonel Wrona's view, which has the confidence of the appropriate comrades." Tkaczuk's voice was perfectly even. "You will work with him. The opinion is due on the fifteenth. I would like the matter resolved before the episcopate meets, and I would like the resolution to be one that we do not have to explain in six months." He closed his folder. "One more thing, comrade. You are acting director of the Office now. That is a considerable responsibility. There are comrades who have said that the Office has been too soft for too long, and that its leadership has confused the art of the possible with the habit of the comfortable." He looked up. "I do not agree with them. But they exist. I mention it because you should know it."

"I know it," Zofia said.

***

Wrona came on the fourteenth with a folder and a box of chocolates, which was a thing he did, and put the chocolates on the desk and said they were for Hanna.

"You are looking thin, comrade director. Acting director. My congratulations, by the way, though I understand the circumstances are not happy."

"My director is dying."

"All the more reason." He sat down. "I have been thinking about our conversation. I have decided to be frank with you, which I find is usually a mistake, but I am forty-six and my stomach is worse and I have begun to be in a hurry." He put the folder on the desk. "This is your material. Not the German. The other one."

She opened it. There were six pages, typed, and on the fourth page there was a paragraph in her own handwriting, and a signature at the bottom of the page, and the date, 11 October 1949.

She had known it existed. She had not seen it since the day she wrote it.

"Read the sentence," Wrona said.

She read it. It was in her hand and it was perfectly legible. *Wierzbicki is gifted and ought not to be permitted to teach youth; he is the kind of man who will be believed.*

"You were twenty-two," Wrona said. "You were a good girl. You were asked a question and you answered it honestly, which is more than most people did, and it went into a file, as things do." He shifted his weight. "I am not here to blackmail you, comrade. I want to be clear about that, because I have a reputation and it is deserved but it is not always deserved. If I wanted to destroy you I would not need this. I would need three other things, and I have them, and I have not used them and I do not intend to. This is not a threat. This is a briefing."

"Then brief me."

"The man Sroka," Wrona said. "Your mentor. I have his file. In 1953 he was shot, and in 1951 you gave evidence at his hearing, and your evidence was — how shall I put it — correct. It was correct and it was not generous, and it was the evidence of a person who wished to survive." He held up a hand. "I am not criticizing. I have given evidence myself, in similar circumstances, and mine was worse. But here is the thing, comrade. Sroka was rehabilitated in April. Did you know? The Military Court reviewed the case and quashed the sentence, and there will be a paragraph in *Trybuna Ludu* in a week or two, and there are people who will want to know who gave evidence against him. Some of those people are in this building. Some of them are in the Central Committee. You are acting director of an office that is about to make a decision about a bishop, and there is a document in a file with your name on it, and the document is in the hand of a twenty-two-year-old girl who was frightened, and the girl is now a woman who is being asked to be the state's conscience about the Church." He sat back. "That is the situation. I have not created it. I am describing it."

She put the page down.

"What do you want, colonel?"

"I want the signature," Wrona said. "I have wanted it since October. But I will tell you honestly that I no longer think it is the most important thing in this room." He leaned forward. "Listen to me. If you write an opinion that approves this man without conditions, and he is consecrated in December, and in February he gives an interview to the Italian press, or he writes a pastoral letter about the schools, or he stands up in the cathedral and says the state is godless — and he will, comrade, because they always do, they cannot help it — then the question in the Central Committee will not be 'why did the bishop do it.' The question will be 'who approved him.' And the answer will be a woman with a file and a dead mentor and a village in her past, and there will be a commission, and the commission will read the German deposition, and the men around this table will discover, all at once and with great regret, that the Office has been run for four years by a person who has a personal history with the candidate." He spread his hands. "I am offering you a way to be a colleague. That is all. There is a paper. It says: I have explained the situation to the candidate and he has agreed to cooperate. It does not require his signature. It requires yours."

She looked at him.

"You would forge it," she said. "You would accept my note and put his signature on the declaration underneath."

"I would accept your note," Wrona said, "and the rest is administration."

"No."

"No?"

"No." She closed the folder. "I will not write that note. If he signs, he signs in his own hand, in front of me, and the text is the text he has seen, and it goes into the file as it was signed, with the date and the place and the names of the witnesses. If he does not sign, then he does not sign, and the opinion is mine and it will say what I think."

Wrona sat quite still. Then he smiled, and it was the first smile she had seen from him that seemed to cost him something.

"Ah," he said. "There she is."

"Colonel—"

"I have been waiting four weeks for that sentence." He stood up, and took his hat, and looked down at her. "I will make you a proposal, comrade director, and it is a real one. It is the fourteenth today. The opinion is due on the fifteenth. I will ask the department to extend the deadline by ten days, on the ground that the candidate is to be interviewed again. In those ten days you will go to him, and you will put the matter to him yourself — not as a demand, as a question. You will ask him whether he will sign. And then you will tell me what he says, and I will accept it, and I will not ask you to write any note." He put on his hat. "That is the offer. It is the only one I have. If you refuse it, I will go to the minister on Monday with the German deposition and the Sroka hearing, and I will not enjoy it, and neither will you."

"Why me?" she said. "Why does it have to be me?"

Wrona was at the door. He turned.

"Because he will say yes to you," he said. "That is the whole of it. He would say no to me, and he would say no to Tkaczuk, and he would say no to the Cardinal himself. But he will say yes to you, because you are the only person in this Republic who has ever been loved by him, and a man will do for that what he will not do for God." He opened the door. "You have ten days. Use them badly or well."

She sat for a long time after he had gone.

Then she got up and went to the window and stood there with her arms folded, looking at the Palace of Culture, which was grey and enormous against a grey sky, and she thought about 1946, when she was nineteen and believed that she was building something, and about the particular day in June of that year when she had stood in the market square at Krasnystaw with a ballot box in her hands.

***

They had given her a school at Krasnystaw in the autumn of 1944 and she had taught thirty-one children in a room with a stove and no glass in one window, and she had been happy in a way that she had not expected ever to be happy again. The children were wild and hungry and quick, and half of them had lost someone, and they learned to read in eight months, and one of them, a boy called Adam, had copied out the whole of the alphabet on the back of a German poster, and she had kept it for years.

In 1945 Sroka had asked her to join the Union of Polish Youth and she had joined, and in 1947 the Party, and she had done it for reasons that had seemed to her at the time to be entirely practical. The village had been burned. The old world had been a world in which a girl could be taken out of a meadow and put in a room with a wasp and a man with a bottle of beer, and it had ended, and the people who had ended it were the ones with the lorries. She had seen the Red Army come through, and she had seen the Polish Committee of National Liberation hand out land to peasants who had never owned any, including eleven families in her own district, and she had seen her father, who had come back from Majdanek in August 1944 weighing forty-nine kilograms, stand at the window of the cooperative office and say, "They are giving away the mills," in a voice of complete bewilderment.

He had not been wrong. In the referendum of June 1946, she had stood in the square at Krasnystaw with a ballot box, and the question had been whether the Senate should be abolished, and the answer had been yes, and everyone in the district knew that the answer had been helped. In January 1947 she had been an usher at a polling station, and she had watched men in militia uniform go into the room where the count was made, and she had seen the numbers change on the board, and she had said nothing. She had been nineteen. She had told herself that the other side would have done the same, and that the peasants had voted for the peasant party anyway, and that the election had produced a government which had given the village a school and a clinic and a road.

Her father lived until 1953. He never joined the cooperative and he never spoke at a meeting again, and after 1951 he kept a pig in the orchard in defiance of the delivery quota and paid the fine each year out of the money for shoeing horses. When she came home he was civil and silent, and he asked her once, in 1950, whether she had seen the organist's boy, and she said that he had been taken to Russia and had not come back, and her father nodded and looked at the fire and said, "He was a good boy," and that was the last time they spoke about anything that mattered. He died in the spring of 1953, in the village, of a stroke, in the eleven days between the arrest of Wiktor Sroka's wife and the opening of the Party school she was due to attend in Warsaw, and she was at a conference in Katowice when the telegram came, and she did not go to the funeral, and she has never been able to tell anyone, including herself, whether that was because the conference could not be left or because she could not face the village.

In 1947 she had sat for two days in a courtroom in Lublin, at the back, at the trial of eleven men from the underground who had gone on fighting after the war because nobody had told them to stop. She had listened to the prosecutor call them fascist bandits and hirelings of London, and she had believed perhaps a third of it, and had said nothing about the other two thirds, and two of the eleven were hanged in the following spring. She had been twenty. She had gone home afterwards and eaten supper.

And in 1949, when the Party was being cleaned, she had been asked about her past, and she had sat in a room in Lublin with three men and a stenographer, and one of them had asked her, "And this Wierzbicki. The organist's son. What was he?"

She had said: "He was in the Home Army."

And then, after a moment, because the silence in the room had a shape, and because she was twenty-two, and because the war had been over for four years and she had not seen him for five and he was somewhere in Russia and might be dead, she had said the other thing. She had said it in the belief, which she could still remember with perfect clarity, that she was telling the truth in a way that would protect him: that he was gifted, that he was not a thug, that he was exactly the kind of priest who would be listened to, and that for that reason he should not be given young people. She had thought she was describing a danger accurately. She had thought that accuracy was a virtue.

The stenographer had typed it. The chairman had said, "Thank you, comrade."

She had walked out into the street in Lublin and bought a bun and eaten it, and it was a Thursday, and the sun was out, and she had not thought about it again for two years.

# Seven

## The File

The file came to her office at six in the evening in the hands of a corporal with a travel warrant and a sealed envelope, and on the envelope, in Wrona's small neat hand: *For the acting director. To be read before the interview on the 20th. Return by hand.*

She signed for it. The corporal saluted and went. Hanna had gone home at five; the building was empty except for the night watchman and a light on the second floor where someone in the legal section was working late. Zofia put the folder in her briefcase, put on her coat and her hat, and walked home, and the whole way she was aware of the weight of it against her hip, like a stone in a pocket.

Jerzy was out. There was a note on the kitchen table in his big slanted hand: *Commission until late. There is barley soup. Do not wait. J.*

She made tea and did not eat the soup. She put the folder on the table under the lamp and untied the tape.

***

The first document she had seen before: the German deposition, two pages, translated in 1949 by someone whose Polish was stiff. She read it once, quickly, and put it aside.

The second she had not seen. It was in German, with a translation, and it was headed *Sonderaktion Brzozówka, 14.6.1944, Bericht*, and it was two and a half pages and it was the most terrible thing she had ever read, because it was dull.

> At 05.40 the detachment, consisting of 2 officers, 22 men of Gendarmerie Krasnystaw and 40 men of the Wehrmacht company stationed at Krasnystaw, moved out...
>
> The village was cordoned at 06.25. The population was assembled in the square before the church. Search of the buildings was carried out by four groups...
>
> In the cellar beneath the smithy was found one wounded Soviet soldier, who was shot while attempting to escape. In the orchard of the property of Józef Krawiec were found four persons of Jewish origin, namely two men and two women, together with one female child aged approximately ten years, who were shot in accordance with the standing order...
>
> The buildings were fired at 07.50. The village was burned to the extent of 11 dwelling houses, 9 barns, the mill, the school, and the shop. The church was spared...
>
> Losses to our side: none. Ammunition expended: 340 rounds.
>
> Remarks: The population is hostile. The action was carried out as a reprisal for the harbouring of bandits and Jews, in accordance with the order of the Kreishauptmann of 12.4.1944.

Thirty-one persons. She had known the number for fourteen years. It was in the report, in a table, with a breakdown: men 14, women 11, children 6. One of the children was eight years old and was called Piotr Krawiec. One of the women was thirty-six and was called Aniela Krawiec.

She turned the page. There was a list of property destroyed, with values in złoty.

She got up and went into the kitchen and stood at the sink with her hands on the edge of it, and ran the water, and stood there until the water was hot, and then she washed the cup she had used, and dried it, and put it away, and came back and sat down.

The third document was the protocol of the interrogation of 14 November 1951. It was nine pages, typed, with the questions numbered and the answers taken down by a stenographer and signed at the bottom of each page by the interrogator and by the subject. The subject's signature was the same upright hand as the declaration, and it had not shaken at all.

> **Q:** You were a member of the Home Army from 1942.
> **A:** Yes.
> **Q:** Under what pseudonym?
> **A:** Dzwon.
> **Q:** Your duties?
> **A:** Courier. Then liaison to the district command. I carried orders and I carried money and twice I carried a cipher book.
> **Q:** You were armed?
> **A:** I was given a pistol in 1943.
> **Q:** Did you use it?
> **A:** No.
> **Q:** Did you take part in any action against the German occupation forces?
> **A:** Yes. Twice, in a supporting role. In April 1943 at Izbica I was on the road with two others and we were to warn of the approach of a patrol. In July 1944 near Krasnystaw I was in a group that destroyed a telephone line.
> **Q:** Did you take part in any action against the Polish People's Army or against the Red Army?
> **A:** No.
> **Q:** You are aware that the Home Army was a terrorist organization in the service of the London government?
> **A:** I am aware that is the official description.
> **Q:** Do you accept it?
> **A:** No.

She read that answer three times. It was not brave — it was worse than brave. It was the answer of a man who had decided, somewhere in the first hour, to say exactly what was true and to let them do what they would with it, and who had kept to that decision for nine days.

The protocol went on. They asked him about the seminary, about the professors, about a discussion circle in Lublin, about a priest who had preached a sermon at Zamość. They asked him whether he had read certain books. They asked him whether he had ever spoken against the collective farms. And then, on page six:

> **Q:** Do you know this document?
> **A:** I have not seen it before.
> **Q:** Read it.
> *(The subject was given a document of one page, typewritten, dated 11 October 1949, and was permitted to read it.)*
> **Q:** Do you know the author of this document?
> **A:** No.
> **Q:** Look at it again. You are mentioned by name.
> **A:** I can read my own name.
> **Q:** Who wrote it?
> **A:** I don't know.
> **Q:** You have a good idea.
> **A:** I have no idea.
> **Q:** A comrade from your village has given us a full account of you. Do you understand what that means?
> **A:** It means someone from my village has given you a full account of me.
> **Q:** Her name.
> **A:** I don't know.
> **Q:** Her name, Wierzbicki.
> **A:** I don't know.
>
> *(The subject was silent for a period of approximately two minutes. He was then shown a second document, being a translation from the German, and was permitted to read it. After reading it the subject asked for water, which was given. He was then asked whether he wished to make a statement. He stated that he did not. The interrogation was concluded at 04.20.)*

She put the page down. Then she picked it up again and read the last paragraph twice, and then a third time.

He had not given her name. In a room in Mokotów at four in the morning, with nine days behind him, he had sat and said "I don't know" four times, and they had written it down, and it had gone into the file, and the file had come to her in a sealed envelope fourteen years later, and she was reading the only act of love anyone had ever performed for her that she had not known about.

***

The declaration was the next document, and she had read it before, but she read it again with attention.

> I, Tomasz Wierzbicki, son of Franciszek, born 6 January 1924 at Brzozówka, priest of the diocese of Lublin, declare as follows. In the years 1942 to 1944 I was a member of an armed organization described as the Home Army, and I did not inform the authorities of the Polish People's Republic of this fact. In the years 1948 to 1951, in the course of my studies and afterwards, I took part in conversations in which the policy of the state towards the Church was criticized, and I did not report these conversations. Among the persons who took part in them were Henryk Zaleski, then a student of the seminary at Lublin, and Michał Bortnowski, then a curate at Zamość. I make this declaration freely, without duress, and I regret my conduct.
>
> *T. Wierzbicki, Mokotów, 28 June 1952*

*Freely, without duress.* The phrase was in every such declaration; she had seen it forty times; it was a formula, like the *etc.* at the end of a prayer.

There was a note in the file, added in 1956 in a different hand: *Zaleski H. — removed from seminary list 1952; employed as clerk, Municipal Board, Lublin; no further interest. Bortnowski M. — released Rakowiecka 1953; died 1956 pulmonary; no further interest.*

*No further interest.*

She made more tea. It was a quarter past nine. The flat was very quiet; the refrigerator, which was a Polish model and had been a great prize in 1957, hummed in the kitchen like a large insect. She could hear a neighbour's radio through the wall, the dance music programme, and someone laughing.

The last document in the folder was not a document. It was an envelope, unsealed, containing one sheet of prison paper, folded twice, with the censor's stamp on the back and a note in pencil: *Not dispatched. Withheld 14.11.51. Szeruda.*

She unfolded it.

The hand was the same upright hand, but smaller, and pressed hard into the paper, and in one place the pen had gone through.

> 13 November 1951
>
> Zosia,
>
> They told me today that you are alive. I did not ask and they did not say it kindly; they said it as a fact about a document. I have thought for four years that you were dead in the summer of 1944, and now I know that you are not, and I find that I am glad, and then I find that I do not know what else I am.
>
> I am in a cell with four men. There is a window with the upper pane painted white, so that we see the light but not the sky. I have a blanket and a bowl and a spoon, and I am allowed to walk in the yard for twenty minutes on alternate days. It is not the worst place I have slept. The camp at Ostashkov was worse, and I have thought about that a good deal, and I have decided that this is because at Ostashkov I was twenty and believed I would be released, and here I am twenty-seven and have stopped believing things of that kind, and a man who has stopped believing is much harder to frighten.
>
> They showed me a paper today with your sentence on it, and then a German paper, and then they asked me your name, and I said I did not know it.
>
> I want to write to you about this and I find that I cannot do it in order. So I will write it as it comes.
>
> I am not angry with you. I have tried for six hours to be angry with you and it will not hold. I know what was in the room with you when you wrote that sentence, because I know you, and because I was in a similar room in 1944 with a German in it, and I know what a person will say in such a room in order to be allowed to go on living. You wrote that I was gifted and should not teach the young. It is a hard sentence and it is not a lie. I am gifted and I do teach the young, and I have thought since that if I had been the officer reading it I would have written the same.
>
> The German paper is another thing. I am not going to write about the German paper tonight. I have read it twice and I will read it again in the morning when I am steadier. I want you to know that I have known since this afternoon that you were the one who was taken in the meadow, and that I have spent the afternoon thinking not about what you said but about what was done to you before you said it, and that I am sorrier for that than I can put down.
>
> I do not know what you have become. They say you are in the Party and that you are doing well, and I find that I believe it, because you were always the cleverest person in the village and the most stubborn and you could not bear to be left out of anything. I hope you have a house with a warm stove. I hope someone makes you laugh. You had a good laugh once and it came out of you all at once, like a door banging, and it used to make my father smile, and he did not smile much after 1939.
>
> If you are alive, and if you ever read this — you will not, they will take it, they take everything — I want you to know one thing, and then I will stop, because my hand is bad and the light is going.
>
> I have not stopped thinking of you as the person I knew. Not because I am forgiving. I am not, particularly. It is because it is the truth, and the truth is the only thing I have in this cell, and I am not going to give it up to make myself feel better. You were seventeen and you stood at a gate in the dark and you said *in August,* and you meant it, and I have never once believed that the girl at the gate was lying. Whatever else has happened since, that girl existed, and I was there, and I am the only person left who was there.
>
> The house at the bend. Two rooms and a porch. I have thought about the porch a great deal in the last four years. It is a stupid thing to think about in a cell, and I have thought about it more than I have thought about God, which I will have to confess to someone eventually.
>
> Do not answer this. I am not certain that I could bear it, and in any case they will not let it out. I am writing it because a man must put something down somewhere, and because if I am shot on Thursday I would like there to have been one piece of paper in the world with the truth on it.
>
> I will pray for you by name at Mass on Sunday, if they let me say Mass, which they do not.
>
> T.

***

She sat with it in her hands for a long time.

Then she folded it along its folds, and put it back in the envelope, and put the envelope in her handbag, and cleared the table, and washed the cup, and turned off the lamp in the kitchen, and stood in the dark of the hall with her hand on the wall.

She did not cry. She had not cried since the sixteenth of June 1944, at the graveside, when Antoni Szeliga had held her against his coat; she had stopped then, and it had closed over, and there had been nothing since but a pressure, like water behind a dam, and she had built her life on the far side of it and had never once looked over.

***

At half past eleven Jerzy came home. He came in quietly, as he always did, and put his coat on the hook, and she heard him in the kitchen, and then he came into the front room and found her sitting in the armchair with her shoes off and the light off.

"You're sitting in the dark."

"Yes."

He turned on the small lamp by the sofa, and looked at her, and then at the briefcase on the floor, and then at her face.

"Zofia."

"It's a file."

"It's the priest," Jerzy said.

She looked up at him.

He was standing there in his shirtsleeves with his spectacles in his hand, a big tired man of forty-three, and he was not angry and he was not surprised, and she understood in that moment that she had been living with a man for five years and had not been paying attention.

"How long have you known?"

"Since 1953." He sat down on the arm of the sofa, at a little distance, and turned his spectacles over in his fingers. "You talk in your sleep. You have talked in your sleep since we were married. It isn't much — a name, sometimes, and once a sentence about a river. I worked it out." He put the spectacles on. "I didn't mind. I want you to know that. I thought: she has a thing in her past and it is not my business and she will tell me if she wants to. And then a year went by, and then two, and then it got to be the kind of thing that cannot be said, and now it is 1958." He looked at her. "Is he going to be a bishop?"

"Yes."

"And you have to decide about it."

"Yes."

Jerzy nodded slowly. He looked at the window, which was black, and at the reflection of the two of them in it.

"I would like to ask you one thing," he said, "and then I will go to bed and you can do what you have to do. Do you love him?"

She sat there. The refrigerator hummed. A tram went by at the end of the street, and the window frames rattled, and were still.

"I don't know," she said. "I don't know what that word means any more. I loved him when I was seventeen and I have spent fourteen years making sure I never found out what became of that. And now there is a file on the table with a letter in it that he wrote in a cell in 1951 and was not allowed to send, and in it he says he is not angry with me, and I find that I would rather he had been." She looked at her husband. "Does that answer your question?"

"Not really," Jerzy said. "But it's an honest answer, and I've had worse ones from the Commission." He stood up, and then bent and kissed the top of her head, which he had not done in a long time, and said, "Come to bed at a reasonable hour. You have a decision to make and you will make it badly if you are tired."

He went out. At the door he stopped.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I think you should see him. Not for the Office. For the other reason." And then he went in and closed the door, and she heard him washing, and then the bed creaking, and then nothing.

***

She sat up until four.

***

At half past four she got up and put on her coat and her hat and her good gloves, and took the letter out of her handbag and put it in the inside pocket of her coat, and went out.

The streets were empty except for a cart and a man hosing down the pavement outside the dairy. It was cold and clear, and the stars were going out one by one from the east, and the first tram of the day went past with its lights on and two men in it, and she walked up Marszałkowska past the Palace of Culture, which was still lit at the top, and turned into Świętokrzyska, and kept walking.

She did not know where she was going until she got there.

St Anne's on Krakowskie Przedmieście had a low door and a baroque façade and a notice board with the times of Masses. The door was open. Inside it was dark and cold, and there was a woman in a headscarf kneeling in the third row, and a verger moving along the side aisle with a long pole, putting out the candles one by one, and the smell of cold stone and wet wool and last night's incense.

Zofia went down the side aisle and sat in the last row and put her hands in her lap.

She did not pray. She had not prayed since 1944, and she had discovered in the intervening years that the cessation of prayer is not a decision but an atrophy, like the loss of a language. She sat in the cold and looked at the altar and at the small red lamp, and she thought about the sentence in the report — *the church was spared* — and about the fact that all the houses around it had burned, and about the fact that she had never in fourteen years asked herself why the church had been spared, and that she was asking it now.

The verger came to the last candle and put it out. The church got darker. The woman in the headscarf crossed herself and went out.

Zofia sat there until the first Mass began at six, and a priest came out in green vestments with two boys, and there were eleven people in the church. She did not go up. She listened to the Latin, which she had known by heart at nine, and found that she could still follow it, and that it did not mean anything at all.

At half past six she went out into the grey morning and bought a bun from a woman at the corner and ate it standing up in the cold, and then she went to the office, where Hanna was already at her desk, and said, "Hanna. Send a letter to the Curia. I am to see the candidate on the twentieth at eleven, here, and I am to see him alone."

# Eight

## The Confession

He came to Bagatela on the twentieth at eleven, in the rain, with his hat dripping, and Hanna took his coat and hung it by the stove, and he thanked her by name, which surprised her, and she went out pink.

"Sit down, Father."

"I'll stand. I've been sitting in a train since six." He stood in the middle of the office with his hands behind his back, and looked at the portrait of Gomułka, and at the map with the pins, and at the brass ashtray, and then at her. "You've moved the desk."

"The light."

"It was better before." He turned to the window. "You can see the Palace from here. Do you look at it?"

"Every day."

"It's a terrible building."

"Yes."

"I like it," he said.

She laughed, once, before she could stop herself, and then put her hand over her mouth, and he smiled at her with his whole face, and for a moment the office was a room in a village in 1940 and there was a book behind the stove.

Then he said, "The twentieth. You asked for the twentieth. I have come for the answer to the question you did not ask me on the seventh."

She opened the drawer and took out the paper. It was one page, typed, with a space at the bottom for a signature and a date and a line for the witness.

"That's it?" he said. "That's the famous paper?"

"That is it."

He took it and read it standing, in the light from the window, and it took him less than a minute, and when he had finished he put it down on the desk very carefully, as though it were something that might break.

"It's shorter than I expected."

"It always is."

"It says that I undertake to furnish information to the organs of the Security Service concerning persons and matters coming to my knowledge in the exercise of my office, and that I will maintain the confidentiality of this cooperation." He looked up. "It does not say the word priest anywhere. I notice that. It is written for a factory foreman."

"Yes."

"And if I sign it, I am a bishop in December."

"If you sign it, the Office's opinion will be favourable and the nomination will go forward. I will not pretend that the rest is certain, but that is what my opinion is worth."

"And what is my opinion worth?" He picked it up again. "They will have this in a file in a room, and in ten years there will be a man at that desk—" he nodded at her chair — "who is not you, and he will open the file and he will find this paper with my signature, and he will know what I am. And then he will telephone me about something, and I will do what he asks, because he will not have to threaten me. He will only have to mention the paper." He put it down. "That is how it works, isn't it. It is not the first signature that costs. It is the second, which is cheaper."

"Yes," she said. "That is how it works."

"You are very honest with me."

"I have decided to be. It is a recent decision and I am not sure it will last."

He walked to the window and stood with his back to her, looking out at the yard and the wall and the plane tree, which was bare now.

"Zofia. I want to ask you something, and I want you to answer as the official, not as yourself."

"Ask."

"If I refuse — if I go back to Lublin and I am not made a bishop, and Pacewicz is made a bishop instead, and he is a decent dull man who will do nothing for twenty years and will sign whatever is put in front of him — what happens to you?"

She was silent.

"Answer the question."

"I will be removed from this office," she said, "within a month. There will be a commission. The commission will read a German deposition and a note I wrote in 1949 and a hearing at which I gave evidence against a man who has just been rehabilitated. I will not be expelled from the Party, because expelling me would require an explanation, and explanations are expensive. I will be transferred to something small — a museum, perhaps, or a publishing house, or the cultural section of a voivodeship council in a town where there is nothing. My husband will keep his post, because his post has nothing to do with me. In three years no one will remember my name."

He turned round.

"And you knew that when you refused Wrona's note."

"Yes."

"Then why did you refuse it?"

"Because it was a lie," she said, "and because I am tired of writing sentences that are not true, and because —" She stopped.

"Because?"

"Because you said in a cell in 1951 that you would like there to have been one piece of paper in the world with the truth on it," she said, "and I have been thinking about that for eight days, and I find that I would like the same thing, and I have left it very late."

He came back to the desk. He stood there looking at the paper, and then he picked up the pen from the tray, and unscrewed the cap, and she felt her heart turn over.

"No," he said. "I'm sorry. I am not going to sign it in front of you as a way of being dramatic. I am not going to sign it at all." He put the pen down. "But I want you to know that I stood here for a moment and considered it, and the reason I considered it was not the bishopric. It was you. It was the thought that if I signed it, you would keep your office, and you have built your whole life on that office, and I know what it costs a person to lose the thing they have built." He looked at her. "That is the only reason I have. I want it on the record, since we are being honest."

"Thank you."

"It isn't a kindness. It's a confession." He put on his hat. "I will be at St Anne's until six. If the Office requires anything further, it can write to the Curia, and the Curia will lose the letter, and in the spring I will be a curate in a parish in the country and this will all be a story that someone tells at a table." He went to the door, and then turned. "Zosia. Come to the church. Not to the Office. To the church."

"Why?"

"Because you have something to say and you have been carrying it for fourteen years, and you are not going to say it in an office with a portrait of Gomułka on the wall."

She sat for a long time after he had gone. Then she rang for Hanna and told her to cancel the afternoon, and went home, and slept for two hours in the middle of the day, which she had not done since 1952.

***

She went at half past five.

The queue for confessions at St Anne's was eight people long and mostly women, and it moved slowly, and she stood at the end of it in her grey coat with her hands folded, and the church was cold and smelled of wet stone and candle wax, and the candles on the stand by the pillar burned with small steady flames that leaned when the door opened.

The confessional was a wooden box on the north side with a curtain and a grille and a little shelf for a hand. She had not been in one since she was seventeen. She knelt down in the dark on the other side of the grille and put her hands on the shelf, and she could hear him breathing, and the rustle of the stole, and beyond the curtain the low murmur of the church.

"Bless me, Father, for I have sinned."

Her voice came out wrong. He was silent for a moment.

"Zofia."

"Yes."

"Say the rest."

"It has been fourteen years since my last confession." She heard herself say it, and it was such an ordinary sentence, and it was the most extraordinary thing she had said in her adult life. "I do not remember the formula. I have not been to communion since 1944."

"Then we will do it badly together, and it will still count." He moved behind the grille. "How long has it been since you were last at Mass? Not confession. Mass."

"June 1944. Except that I sat at the back of a church three weeks ago at six in the morning and did not go up."

"Fourteen years." A pause. "That is a long time to be hungry and not know it."

"I'm not hungry."

"You are in a church at half past five in the evening in November with your hands on a shelf."

She looked at her hands. They were shaking. She put them in her lap.

"I don't know where to start."

"Start with the worst thing," he said. "It is usually at the top, and people spend a great deal of time at the bottom."

She sat in the dark for a moment. Then she began.

"I was taken on the fourteenth of June 1944 in the meadow east of Brzozówka. There was a patrol, three men, and they found a message in my basket, and they took me to the post at Krasnystaw and put me in a room at the back with a table and two chairs and a wasp on the window. The gendarme was called Kliemann. He was fat and pleasant and he had a bottle of beer and he did not offer me any. He asked me where the partisans were and I said nothing. He hit me twice, or the Ukrainian did, and then he told me that at six o'clock he was going to send a company to the village and burn it, and that if I told him where the wounded Russian was, he would send four men instead and it would be a police matter and the village would be spared." She stopped. "And I told him. I told him the cellar under the forge. I gave him my father's cellar and the man in it, and I signed a paper, and it said that there were Jews in the house as well."

"Did you say that?"

"I don't know." She said it flatly, looking at the grille. "That is the truth, Father, and it is the worst thing I have. I have gone over it for fourteen years and I cannot hear my own voice. I remember the wasp. I remember the table. I remember him writing, and the pen going slowly, and I remember thinking that if I said the right thing he would stop writing. I do not remember saying the word *Jews.* It is in the paper. I signed the paper. That is all I know."

The church was quiet. Somewhere a door opened and closed.

"They were in the orchard by then," she said. "My father had moved them the day before, because of the Russian. They were in the hole under the woodpile, and they had been there two days, and Sara had come out at night and looked at the stars and said *oh.* And they found them anyway, because the paper said so, or because the gendarmes searched the orchard anyway, because they searched everything. It does not matter which. They were shot at the edge of the birch wood with the Russian, five of them, and my mother and my brother were in the house, and my father was taken to Lublin, and the village burned. And I have never said this to a living person. Not to my husband. Not to anyone. I let a man be blamed for it — Antoni Szeliga, the deputy sołtys, who was innocent, and who hanged himself in 1953, and whose daughter still lives at Krasnystaw and does not know my name."

"Go on."

"I went to work for the Party in 1945. I told myself it was for the school and the land reform. It was for the school and the land reform, partly. It was also because I could not bear to be a person who had done what I had done and had nothing to show for it, and because a man told me that guilt was a bourgeois luxury and I believed him because I wanted to." She looked at the grille. "His name was Wiktor Sroka. He was a good man in the way that a hammer is a good thing, and in 1951 I gave evidence at his hearing, and my evidence was correct and it was not generous, and in 1953 they shot him, and in April of this year they quashed the sentence. And there is a document with my name on it in a file, and the file will be read, and I have known that for seven years and I have never once written to his wife."

"Anything else?"

"In 1949 a commission asked me about my wartime contacts and I gave them a sentence about a man I loved. It is in his file. It went with him to Mokotów. I wrote that he was gifted and should not be permitted to teach youth."

"Why?"

"Because I was twenty-two and frightened and because it was true," she said, "and because the truth, said in a certain room, in a certain voice, is the most dangerous thing in the world, and I have spent nine years learning how to say true things in that voice. I have signed circulars that put questions to parish priests about their politics. I have written opinions on men's lives. I have sat in this city and been promoted twice." She stopped. "And on the fourteenth of June 1944 I said a sentence in a room to save my own life, and thirty-one people died, and the sentence I said in 1949 was the same sentence. It was the same sentence, Father. It was the same sentence with better grammar."

Neither of them spoke for a while.

"Zofia," he said at last, "I am going to do something now that will seem to you like a betrayal, and I want you to listen to the whole of it before you say anything."

"All right."

"I cannot absolve you."

She sat very still.

"It is not because of the size of what you have confessed. If it were only the size I would absolve you and give you a penance and send you out, and you would go home and be a person who had been to confession, and it would do you no harm and some good. But there is a decree, from 1949, of the Holy Office, which our bishops have accepted in this country, and it says that a Catholic who knowingly and freely joins the Communist Party, or who defends or spreads its doctrine, may not be admitted to the sacraments unless he renounces it. You are not merely a member. You are an officer of the state whose function is to administer the Church. I cannot give you absolution while you are that, and if I did, I would be giving you a piece of paper that means nothing, and you and I both know exactly what a signature that means nothing costs, because we have both signed one."

She looked at the grille. Her hands were cold.

"So the answer is no."

"The answer is: not yet, and not like this." His voice was low and steady. "I am not refusing you. I am telling you the truth in a box in the dark, which is the only place where it is any use. If I told you that you could have both — the office and the absolution, the Party card and the Host — I would be a liar in a stole, and there is nothing lower than that." A pause. "And I will tell you something else, which is not doctrine. I forgave you in 1951, in a cell, before I had finished reading the German paper. I have forgiven you every morning since. It does not stay done — that is the thing nobody tells you about forgiveness, it is not an event, it is a labour — and I have done it in the dark in a room in Lublin for seven years, and I have never once been able to tell you, and I have wanted to, and now I have."

She put her hand over her mouth.

"That is the man's forgiveness," he said. "It is worth what it is worth. The other thing — the thing in the sacrament — is not mine to give on my own terms, and I will not pretend it is, because the pretending is the whole disease of this country." He shifted. "Do you understand what I am saying to you?"

"I understand," she said, "that I have to choose."

"You have always had to choose. You have been choosing since 1944. What is new is that someone has finally told you the price." He was quiet for a moment. "Would you like to make an act of contrition? You may. It does not require me."

"I don't remember it."

"Say the first line and stop."

She sat in the dark. She could hear the candles guttering beyond the curtain, and someone's shoes on the stone, and her own breathing.

"Boże mój," she said, "żałuję za grzechy moje—"

Her voice broke on the last word. She stopped, and sat there, and did not go on, and after a while she said, in a perfectly ordinary voice, "That is all I have."

"That is enough for tonight."

She got up. Her knees hurt. She pushed the curtain aside and stood in the aisle, and the church was darker than it had been, and the candles had burned down, and there were three people left, all of them old, and they were all praying.

He came out of the confessional a moment later and stood beside her, and he had taken off the stole and folded it over his arm, and he was a thin man in a black cassock with a bad shave, and he looked tired and about ten years older than he had at eleven that morning.

"Walk with me to the sacristy," he said. "I have to lock up."

***

They went down the side aisle together. The sacristy was a small room with a cupboard and a sink and a table with a clean cloth, and a smell of starch and old incense, and a window with a bar.

He put the stole in the cupboard and turned.

"Ask me the question," he said.

"What question?"

"The one you came to ask. You did not come here for the sacrament. You could have gone to any priest in Warsaw for that and you would have got a kinder answer and a shorter one." He folded his arms. "Ask me."

She stood in the middle of the sacristy with her gloves in her hands.

"If I asked you to sign," she said, "would you?"

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he said, "Yes."

She closed her eyes.

"I would sign it," he said, "and I would do it badly, and I would hate myself in the morning, and I would do it, and I would be a bishop in December, and every time I stood up in that cathedral I would know that I was there because a woman asked me at six o'clock in a sacristy on a Tuesday in November." He picked up his hat from the table. "That is what you have, Zofia. That is the whole of my offering. It is not much and it is not clean and it is yours."

She opened her eyes.

"Then I will never ask," she said.

"Zofia—"

"Never. Not tonight and not in ten days and not in ten years." She put on her gloves, one and then the other, and she was surprised to find that her hands were steady. "You said I had to choose. This is the choice. I am not going to buy a bishop, and I am not going to buy an absolution, and I am not going to spend the rest of my life being a woman who did a thing for a man because he loved her." She looked at him. "I have done that once. It cost thirty-one people. I am not doing it again."

He stood there with his hat in his hands.

"You will lose your office."

"Yes."

"You will lose more than that. They will not leave you alone. Wrona will not leave you alone."

"I know."

"And you will have nothing to show for it. Not even me." His voice was not quite steady. "I will be a curate in a parish in the country, and you will be in a room somewhere being asked questions, and there will be nothing between us but a letter that was never sent."

"There will be a piece of paper in the world with the truth on it," she said. "You said you wanted one. I am going to write one."

She went to the door of the sacristy, and then stopped, and turned round.

"Tomasz. The penance."

"I didn't give you one."

"Give me one."

He thought for a moment.

"Three Hail Marys," he said, "and go to Brzozówka, and stand in the place where the house was, and stay there for an hour. That is not a penance, it is a prescription, and I am giving it to you because I am a priest and I cannot help it." He put on his hat. "And find Antoni Szeliga's daughter and tell her. That is the penance. The rest is advice."

She went out through the church, past the last three old women, and down the steps into Krakowskie Przedmieście, where it had begun to snow, the first snow of the year, small hard flakes coming sideways off the river.

# Nine

## The Choice

She wrote it at the kitchen table between eleven at night and four in the morning, on the twenty-first of November, with the lamp turned low and a pot of tea going cold at her elbow, and she wrote it in the flat, neutral, bloodless language of the Office, because that was the language that would be read.

The first document took her two hours.

> **URZĄD DO SPRAW WYZNAŃ**
> **Opinia w sprawie kandydatury ks. Tomasza Wierzbickiego na biskupa sufragana diecezji lubelskiej**
>
> The Office has examined the terna submitted by His Excellency the Bishop of Lublin on 8 October 1958, together with the documentation supplied by the Curia and the material held by this Office.
>
> The candidate, Tomasz Wierzbicki, born 6 January 1924 at Brzozówka, priest of the diocese of Lublin, is thirty-four years of age. He was a member of the Home Army in the years 1942–1944, was interned in the USSR 1944–1947, completed his studies at the diocesan seminary at Lublin, was ordained in 1953, and since 1955 has lectured in moral theology at the Catholic University of Lublin. He is the author of one book and eleven articles. He has no criminal record and no pending proceedings.
>
> In the course of the examination the candidate was interviewed by this Office on 7 November and 20 November 1958.
>
> The Office records the following findings.
>
> 1. The candidate has given a written undertaking, in the presence of the undersigned, that he will take no part in the pastoral programme preparatory to 1966 without the prior knowledge of this Office, and that he will not speak publicly on the question of religious instruction in the schools while the review of that question is pending. The undertaking is appended.
>
> 2. The candidate declines to enter into any arrangement with the organs of the Security Service, whether by declaration, by undertaking, or by informal cooperation. He was invited to do so on 20 November 1958 and refused. He stated that he had signed one such document in 1952 and would not sign another.
>
> 3. The material held by the fourth department concerning the candidate consists of (a) his service in the Home Army, being a matter of record and closed; (b) a statement made by him in 1952 under interrogation, the circumstances of which are set out in the file; and (c) a deposition made in 1944 by a person then aged seventeen, at present an officer of this Office, which is set out in full in the annexe to this opinion and which the undersigned has read.
>
> In respect of (c) the undersigned makes the following statement, which is not customary in an opinion of this Office and which she makes in her own name. The deposition in question was obtained by the German gendarmerie at Krasnystaw on 15 June 1944 from a girl of seventeen who had been detained, struck, and threatened with death, and it was used that same day as the basis of a punitive expedition against the village of Brzozówka in which thirty-one persons were killed. The use made of the deposition was not its author's intention. It was, nevertheless, its consequence. The author of the deposition has been an officer of this Office since 1954 and its acting director since 6 November 1958. She has neither concealed nor disclosed this matter before today. She discloses it now.
>
> **OPINION.** In the view of the undersigned the candidature should be approved without conditions other than those recorded at 1 above. The refusal at 2 is not, in the view of the undersigned, a ground of objection.
>
> The reasons are as follows. The candidate is a man of ability and of known integrity, and his exclusion would be attributed by the Church in Poland, and by opinion abroad, to the fact of his wartime service and to his refusal to cooperate with the Security Service, and would be used to represent the state as an enemy of the Church's freedom. His promotion, by contrast, places in a position of authority a man who has given his word on two matters and who will keep it. It is the experience of this Office that a bishop who has refused to sign is more reliable than a bishop who has signed.
>
> The undersigned wishes to add one observation of a general nature. This Office has for four years received files upon men and has been asked to form conclusions as to their fitness. In the course of that work the undersigned has read a great many sentences written about other people, and has written a number herself. She has come to the view that an institution which requires a man to sign a paper in order to be permitted to serve his country will in the end be served only by men who can sign papers, and that this is a description of an institution which has already begun to fail.
>
> *Zofia Krawiec, acting director*
> *Warsaw, 22 November 1958*

The second document took her forty minutes, and it was much shorter, and she wrote it twice, and the first version she burned in the sink.

> To the Voivodeship Committee of the Polish United Workers' Party, Warsaw
>
> I resign my membership of the Party.
>
> I do so because I can no longer be a member honestly. In 1944, under interrogation by the German gendarmerie, I gave information which led to the destruction of my village and the deaths of thirty-one persons, among them my mother and my brother. In 1949 I gave evidence to a verification commission concerning a man against whom I had no complaint. In 1951 I gave evidence at the hearing of Wiktor Sroka which was correct in fact and which I knew would be used against him. In each case I did what I did in order to survive, and in each case I survived, and in each case the state recorded my survival in a file and rewarded me for it.
>
> I have been asked many times in the last ten years whether I am loyal. I have always answered yes, and the answer was true, and it was true because I had arranged my life so that loyalty and survival were the same thing. They are not the same thing. I have understood this late.
>
> I ask for no hearing and I make no complaint against any person. The fault is mine and it is not a fault of the system, though the system was glad of it.
>
> *Zofia Krawiec*
> *Warsaw, 22 November 1958*

She took both documents to the office at eight in the morning, and typed the fair copies herself, because Hanna would have cried, and because she wanted the letters to be her own.

Then she put on her coat and went to the clinic on Skaryszewska.

***

Brzozowski was much worse. He had been moved to a side room and there was a screen around the bed and a smell in the room that she recognized from 1944 and did not name. His face had gone the colour of the wall. But his eyes were the same, and when she came in he said, "Ah. The acting director. You have the look of a woman who has done something irrevocable."

"I have."

"Good. Sit down and don't tell me about it until I have had my morphine." He waited while the nurse came and went. Then he said, "Now."

She gave him the opinion. He read it slowly, holding it up at an angle, and when he came to the paragraph about the fourth department he stopped and read it again, and when he came to the annexe he read it all the way through, and then he put the pages down on the blanket and lay back and looked at the ceiling for a while.

"Who else has seen this?"

"No one."

"You have not sent it to the minister."

"Not yet."

"Then it is not a document. It is a letter." He held out his hand. "Give me a pen."

"Comrade director—"

"Pen." She gave him the pen from her bag. He turned the last page over and wrote at the bottom, in a hand that had gone large and shaky and was still unmistakably his: *Seen. Concur. M. Brzozowski, director. 22.XI.58.* And then he lay back and closed his eyes.

"That is the whole of my achievement," he said. "Eleven years in this office and one signature at the end of it, in a hospital, on a document that will be filed and forgotten." He opened his eyes. "Do not look like that. I am not being bitter. I have been thinking about it since Thursday and I have decided that it is not a bad achievement. Do you know why?"

"Why?"

"Because it is the only signature I have ever put on a paper that did not cost somebody something." He fumbled for her hand and found it. "You will be destroyed for this, Zofia. Not immediately and not completely, because they will want to do it quietly, but you will be destroyed. And in twenty years, when the file is opened by some boy with a diploma, he will find this opinion and he will find my name at the bottom of it and he will think: there were two of them. That is not nothing. Two is not nothing."

"One," she said. "You were dying."

"I was dying in April. I have been dying since April and I have been signing things since April, and this is the only one I remember." He let go of her hand. "Go on. Send it. Send it today, by messenger, with a receipt, and send a copy to the minister's secretary personally, and keep a third copy in your bag, and if anyone asks you for the file, tell them it has been sent and give them a receipt number. Bureaucracy is a wall, Zofia. It is a stupid wall built by stupid men, but it is a wall, and today you are on the right side of it."

She sent it at eleven, by messenger, with a receipt; and a copy to the minister's secretary, personally; and a third copy she folded into her handbag, next to the letter that had never been sent.

Brzozowski died on the twenty-fourth, at about four in the morning, of the thing that had been eating him since April. There were nine people at the funeral, and Zofia was one of them, and the priest who said the Mass was old and had known him before the war and said in the sermon that Mieczysław Brzozowski had been a just man, which was true, and a patriot, which was also true, and that he had served the state according to his conscience, which was the most that could be said for anyone in that cemetery, and which was said in a tone that made Zofia look up.

***

Wrona came on the twenty-fifth.

He came into her office without knocking, as he always did, and stood in the doorway, and he did not sit down. He had a folder under his arm and his face was different: not angry. Something worse. He looked, she thought, like a man who has been told that a building he designed has fallen down and killed a child.

"You sent it."

"I sent it on the twenty-second."

"Without consulting me."

"Colonel, I am the acting director of this Office. The opinion of this Office is mine to send."

"You are the acting director of this Office," Wrona said, "until Monday." He put the folder on the desk. "It was prepared on the fourth of November. I want you to look at it. It is a draft order transferring you to the department of culture of the Presidium of the Voivodeship Council at Olsztyn, with effect from the first of December, at a salary of two thousand one hundred. It was prepared on the fourth. Before you refused me. Before the church. Before any of it." He tapped it with one finger. "There is a machine in this building that makes these papers, comrade. It does not care what you decide. It was always going to make this paper. The only question was the date."

She looked at the folder without opening it.

"Olstyn is a long way."

"It is a long way, and there is nothing there for a woman like you, and your husband will not go, because the Commission will not release him, and in a year you will be living in one room and going to the cinema alone on Sundays." Wrona's voice was quiet. "I want to be clear that I did not want this. I want to be clear that I offered you a way out on the fourteenth, and it was a real way out, and you refused it in a manner that I have thought about a good deal since."

"I know."

"You wrote an opinion that quotes my department by name and accuses yourself of a war crime in an official document and then says the state is failing." He shook his head slowly. "Do you know what the minister said when he read it? He read it twice and he put it down and he said, 'Who is she?' And I said, 'She is the best officer in the Office.' And he said, 'Then why has she done this?' And I said, 'Because she is the best officer in the Office.'"

"Colonel."

"I am not finished." He came forward and put both hands on the back of the chair. "There will be a commission on Friday. It will be composed of three persons, one of whom will be Comrade Tkaczuk, who likes you, and one of whom will be a man from the Control Commission whose name I do not propose to tell you, and one of whom will be me. It will examine your conduct in the matter of the Wierzbicki candidature and in the matter of the opinion of 22 November. You will be asked questions. You will answer them." He straightened. "And I will tell you now what I am going to do, so that you are not surprised. I am going to ask you one question and I am going to ask it first, before the others, because I want the answer on the record. I am going to ask you whether you have any personal connection with the candidate. And you will answer, and it will be the truth, because you have decided to be a woman who tells the truth, and after that the commission will do what commissions do."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because I am a Catholic," said Wrona, "in the way that a man is a Catholic who has not been to confession since 1939 and who gives money to the Bernardines because his wife makes him. And because I have read your annexe eleven times, and because I have a daughter of seventeen, and because on the fourteenth of November I offered you a way out and you refused it in a manner I have thought about a good deal." He picked up his folder. "And because when they have finished with you, comrade, there will be nothing left of you but the opinion, and I want you to know that I will keep the opinion. I have made a copy. It is in my own file, in my own hand, and when I am dead my successor will find it, and he will read it, and he will wonder what kind of woman wrote it. That is the best I can do. It is not much."

He went to the door.

"Colonel Wrona."

He stopped.

"The draft order. Was it really prepared on the fourth?"

He looked at her for a moment.

"No," he said. "It was prepared on the twenty-third, at nine in the morning, by a clerk in the personnel section, on my instruction, in one hour, because I was angry." He opened the door. "But you believed it, and that is the whole art of the thing, and I am sorry for it."

***

The commission met on Friday the twenty-eighth in a room on the third floor with a table and four chairs and a portrait of Gomułka and a window that gave on the yard.

There were three of them, as Wrona had said. Tkaczuk sat in the middle with his hands flat on the table. The man from the Control Commission was called Wojdyła and was thin and had a notebook and did not look up. Wrona sat at the end with his folder and said nothing for the first twenty minutes.

"Comrade Krawiec," Tkaczuk said. "You are the acting director of the Office for Religious Affairs. On the twenty-second of November you transmitted to the minister an opinion in the matter of the candidature of Tomasz Wierzbicki which contained a statement concerning yourself. Is that correct?"

"It is correct."

"You were not required to make that statement."

"No."

"Why did you make it?"

"Because it was true, and because the opinion would have been worthless without it."

Tkaczuk looked at the papers in front of him. "The opinion recommends approval of the candidature. The fourth department has submitted that this recommendation is unsound, on the ground that the candidate has refused cooperation and that a bishop of thirty-four with his record will be a focus of opposition." He looked up. "Colonel Wrona. Do you wish to put your question now?"

Wrona opened his folder.

"Comrade Krawiec. Have you any personal connection with the candidate?"

She sat with her hands in her lap.

"Yes."

"Kindly describe it."

"I was born in the village of Brzozówka in 1927. The candidate was born there in 1924. We were children together. When I was seventeen and he was twenty we were in love, and we intended to marry, and we were separated by the events of 1944 and did not meet again until the seventh of November this year, in the corridor of the Curia on Miodowa Street."

The room was quiet. Wojdyła wrote something down and then stopped writing and looked at her.

"Was your opinion influenced by this connection?"

"Yes," she said. "It was influenced by it, and it would be a lie to say otherwise. It was influenced in this way: that when I sat in a room with the candidate I saw a man and not a file, and it occurred to me that I had been making decisions about files for nine years without once seeing a man, and that this was not a defect in my method but a defect in the institution, and that the institution had been relying on it." She looked at Tkaczuk. "I do not ask the commission to believe that my opinion is correct. I ask it to consider that an opinion written by an officer who has a personal interest, and who discloses it in the document itself, is worth more than an opinion written by an officer who has a personal interest and conceals it, which is the ordinary practice of this Office and of every office in this city."

Tkaczuk looked at her for a long moment.

"That is a good answer," he said. "It is a better answer than the question deserved." He closed the folder. "Comrade Krawiec, this commission is not a tribunal. It has no power to punish. It has power to recommend, and it will recommend that you be released from your functions in this Office with effect from the first of December, and that the matter of your Party membership be referred to the voivodeship committee, which will do what it will do. I want to say, before we finish, that I have read your opinion, and that I disagree with the last paragraph of it, and that I have not been able to stop thinking about the last paragraph of it since Tuesday." He stood up. "You may go."

She stood. At the door, Wrona said, without looking up, "Comrade."

She turned.

"The candidate was approved this morning," he said. "The Council of State approved the terna at ten. He will be consecrated on the eighth of December at Lublin. The announcement will be in the papers on Saturday." He turned a page. "I thought you would want to know before you read it in the street."

She went out and down the stairs and out of the building into the yard, where it had begun to snow again, and she stood by the wall with her hand against it and laughed, once, out loud, and a clerk coming out of the side door looked at her and went away quickly.

Jerzy was at the table when she got home, and there was a bottle of vodka on it, which had not happened in years, and two glasses.

"You heard," she said.

"Hanna telephoned the Commission. She said, and I quote, 'Tell Pan Madej that the Pani Dyrektor has finished and is coming home and will need something hot.'" He poured two glasses. "Sit down."

She sat down. He pushed a glass across to her.

"I have been thinking all afternoon," Jerzy said, "about what I would say, and I have prepared a speech, and I am not going to make it, because it is a speech about my own feelings and it is not the moment." He raised his glass. "So: to the bishop. May he be a good one."

"Jerzy—"

"Drink it. It's from before the war." She drank. He drank. He put the glass down and took off his spectacles and rubbed the bridge of his nose. "Now the other thing. I am not going to Olsztyn."

"I know."

"I am not saying it as a reproach. I am saying it because you must not spend the next five years thinking that I failed you. I have a post here, and I am forty-three, and if I leave it I will not get another, and I have no family and no money and no Polish except the Polish of a man who reads cement reports. I would be a burden to you and I would hate it and in three years I would hate you." He put his spectacles on. "But I want you to understand what I am offering, because it is not nothing, and I have thought about it all afternoon. I am offering you this flat, and my name, and every second Friday, and a place to come back to. It is not what a husband ought to offer. It is what I have."

She looked at him. He was a good man, and she had married him in 1953 in a registry office on a Thursday because he was kind and steady and because she was twenty-six and tired and because a woman in her position was expected to have a husband, and she had never once, in five years, looked at him properly.

"That's more than most people have," she said.

"It is not a marriage."

"No," she said. "It is something else. I don't know what it's called." She reached across the table and took his hand. "I'll take it."

They sat there until midnight, and drank the whole bottle, and he told her about the cement dispute in more detail than she had ever heard, and she laughed until her ribs hurt, and at some point she began to cry, and he pretended not to notice, which was the kindest thing he had ever done for her.

On the sixth of December the announcement appeared in *Trybuna Ludu*, in four lines, between a report on the steel target and a notice about the price of coal:

> By decision of the Council of State, the Reverend Tomasz Wierzbicki has been appointed auxiliary bishop of the diocese of Lublin. The consecration will take place in the cathedral church at Lublin on 8 December.

She read it standing at the kiosk in the snow with her hands bare, and then she folded the paper and put it under her arm and went home and took the tin of buttons down from the shelf.

She held the ring in her palm for a while. Then she put it back, and closed the lid, and put the tin in her suitcase, on top of the letter that had never been sent, and went out to the station to buy a ticket for Krasnystaw.

# Ten

## What Remains

The train to Krasnystaw left Lublin at 6.40 in the morning and took two hours and eleven minutes, and she had a compartment to herself for the first hour.

She had bought a second-class ticket and a paper and a bun, and she sat by the window with her coat on and watched the country come up out of the dark. It was flat and white and the snow lay in the furrows, and there were crows on the telegraph wires, and here and there a farm with a light in the window and smoke going up straight. In 1946 she had travelled this line four times a month in a cattle truck with a propaganda gramophone and a box of leaflets, and had been happy, and had believed that the country was being pulled out of the nineteenth century by its collar.

They came into Krasnystaw at ten to nine. The station was small and had been rebuilt, and there was a new concrete platform and a man sweeping the snow off it with a besom, and a woman selling hot tea from a samovar on a table.

She had not been in the town since 1953. It had grown. There were three new blocks on the Lublin road with balconies, and the square had been paved, and the church had a new roof of copper that had gone green, and there was a cinema called the *Iskra* where the old synagogue had been, and she stood in front of it for a moment and then went on.

The clinic was in a low building behind the hospital, and she asked at the desk for Maria Szeliga, and the woman said, "Second door, the dressing station," and she went down the corridor and stood in the doorway.

Maria Szeliga was nineteen years old and had her father's heavy jaw and her mother's hands, and she was changing a dressing on an old man's leg, and she did it competently and without tenderness, and she said, "Sit down, Panie, it will be a minute." Then she looked up.

"You are not a patient."

"No."

"Then wait in the corridor."

"I'll wait here."

Maria Szeliga finished the dressing, and helped the old man with his trouser leg, and saw him to the door, and then turned round and folded her arms.

"Well?"

"My name is Zofia Krawiec."

The name did nothing. Maria waited.

"I was born at Brzozówka. My father was Józef Krawiec, the smith."

Still nothing, and then something — a flicker, the smallest movement of the eyes, an old story heard at a table and half forgotten.

"Krawiec. The one who was taken."

"Yes."

"And your mother and the little boy were killed."

"Yes."

Maria Szeliga looked at her for a moment. "I remember my father talking about your father. They were in the peasant party together." She picked up a kidney dish and put it down. "What do you want? If it is about a pension, it is the office on the first floor and they will tell you it is not their business."

"It is not about a pension." Zofia put her handbag on the chair and stood with her hands at her sides. "On the fourteenth of June 1944 I was detained by the gendarmerie at Krasnystaw. I was seventeen. They questioned me in a room in the post on the Lublin road for five hours, and at the end of it I told them where a wounded Soviet soldier was hidden in my father's cellar. On the same day the village was burned as a reprisal. Thirty-one people were killed." She paused. "In the autumn there was a meeting in the church porch, and the village decided that your father had informed, because he had been seen talking to the gendarmes at Krasnystaw on the twelfth. He had been talking to them about a permit for the mill. I was at that meeting. I was standing at the back. I said nothing."

The room was quiet. Outside in the corridor someone dropped a metal tray and swore.

Maria Szeliga did not move for a long moment. Then she sat down on the stool by the dressing table, slowly, as if her legs had been taken away, and looked at the floor.

"You said nothing," she said.

"I said nothing."

"For five years."

"Fourteen."

"And now you have come to tell me."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Zofia had thought about this on the train for two hours and eleven minutes, and had prepared several answers, and discarded all of them, and in the end she said the true one.

"Because I am about to stop being a person who can be useful to you," she said. "On the first of December I was removed from my post. On the fourth I was excluded from the Party. In January I will be a clerk in a museum at Olsztyn with a salary of two thousand one hundred złoty and no influence over anything at all. If I had come to you in 1950 I could have had your father's name cleared in the records of the district, and I could have got you a flat and a telephone and a place for your son at the school in Lublin." She stopped. "I did not come in 1950. I came now, when I have nothing to give you, and I have come because a priest told me to and because I could not do it while I still had something to lose, and I know exactly how that sounds."

Maria Szeliga looked up.

"It sounds," she said, "like the truth."

"Yes."

"It also sounds like a coward."

"Yes," Zofia said. "It is."

Maria stood up. She was not crying. She went to the window and stood with her back to the room, and her shoulders were rigid, and she stood there for what seemed to Zofia a very long time.

"He hanged himself in the barn," she said at last, to the window. "In March 1953. I was fourteen. I found him. He had been in the barn since the morning and I came home from school and I found him, and I got the ladder and I cut him down, and he was still warm, and I sat on the floor of that barn with him for an hour before I went for anybody, because I did not want them to see him like that." She turned. "That is what you did not say at the meeting. That is what your silence was worth. Not a hangman. A ladder and a fourteen-year-old girl on the floor of a barn."

Zofia did not say anything. There was nothing to say.

"I am not going to forgive you," Maria said. "I want to be clear about it. I am not going to say that I understand, and I am not going to say that it was the war, and I am not going to say that you were seventeen, because I have heard all of those things about my father from people in this town for five years and they have never once made the barn any smaller. You will go away and you will have told me and you will feel that you have done something, and I will be here, and in a week I will still be here." She wiped her hands on her apron, once, hard. "Do you have children?"

"No."

"Then you will not know what it is to have a child find that."

"No," Zofia said. "I won't."

Maria Szeliga stood there. Then she said, in a different voice, quieter and much worse, "What was my father like? Before. In 1944. I was three."

Zofia looked at her.

"He was the deputy sołtys," she said. "He had a grey coat with the collar turned up and he was always the first man at a fire. He carried me out of the way when the village burned. He picked me up round the waist and carried me backwards and he was shouting at me that the orchard was on fire, and I did not understand him, and he carried me anyway, and then he held me against his coat while I screamed, and he did not let go, and he was the only person in Brzozówka who did anything at all that day." She stopped. "And then the village took it away from him, and I let them, and I am the only person alive who could have stopped it, and I did not."

Maria Szeliga nodded slowly. She looked at the floor.

"Say his name," she said.

"Antoni Szeliga."

"Again."

"Antoni Szeliga. Deputy sołtys of Brzozówka. He carried me out of the fire and he was innocent, and the village was wrong, and I was wrong, and I have known it every day for fourteen years."

Maria turned back to the window.

"Go away now," she said. "I have a dressing at eleven."

Zofia picked up her handbag. At the door she said, "If you ever want—"

"No," Maria said. "I will not want. That is the thing you have to understand. There is nothing you can do for me now, and that is the whole of your punishment, and I did not arrange it, and I am not sorry." She turned. "But I will tell you one thing, and then you will go. For five years I have thought that the man who did it was a German, or a Jew, or a partisan, or the government. And now I know it was a girl of seventeen in a room with a wasp. And I find that it is easier. Do you know why? Because a German is weather. A girl of seventeen is a person, and a person can be told, and now I have told you, and now it is yours and not mine." She picked up the kidney dish. "Goodbye, Pani Krawiec."

Zofia went out into the snow.

She walked to Brzozówka. It was nine kilometres and there was a bus at two but she did not wait for it. The road went out of the town past the sugar factory and the new housing and then between fields, and the snow had stopped and the sky had cleared and the light was the hard white light of a Polish December. She walked for two hours and met no one but a man with a horse and a cart of wood, who nodded.

***

The village had been rebuilt, and it had not.

There were houses where the houses had been, but fewer, and they were smaller, and they were brick and concrete instead of wood and thatch. The mill was gone and there was a cooperative shed in its place with a tractor standing outside it under a tarpaulin. The shop was a shop again and it was closed at midday. The school had been rebuilt in 1951 and had two classrooms and a plaque on the wall. The crossroads was paved. There was a bus shelter of grey concrete with a bench in it and a poster for a film.

And there was the church.

It was brick, and it was not beautiful, and it had been built in 1957 on the footprint of the old one with the money of the parish and a grant from the Office for Religious Affairs, and the roof was the roof she had signed for. The spire was new and did not lean. There was no bell in it. There was a place for a bell, and a beam, and no bell, and a notice board by the door with the times of Masses and a list of the dead of the parish from 1939 to 1945, thirty-nine names, and near the bottom of the second column, in letters cut small to make them fit: *KRAWIEC ANIELA · KRAWIEC PIOTR · FRYDMAN ICEK · FRYDMAN RYWKA · FRYDMAN SARA.*

She stood in front of the board for a long time.

Then she went to the place where the house had been.

It was a piece of ground between two new houses, about twenty paces by fifteen, and it had not been built on. There was a pear tree at the bottom of it, old and crooked and still standing, and there was a low wall of the old foundation coming up out of the snow, and that was all. The forge was gone entirely. There was a hollow in the ground where the cellar had been, filled with snow and the dead stalks of nettles. Somebody had put a fence around the plot, a good fence, and there was a gate, and the gate was not locked.

She went in and stood in the middle of it.

It was one o'clock. She stood there for an hour, and the cold came up through her shoes and into her legs, and she did not move. She thought about the kitchen, and where the table had been, and the stove, and the picture of the Sacred Heart, and the palm cross behind it from Palm Sunday 1935. She thought about her mother singing "Serdeczna Matko" and going off the note on the third line. She thought about Piotrek, who would have been twenty-two in 1958, and who would have been called up in 1957, and who might have been a smith, or a teacher, or a man in a cooperative with a wife and two children, and who was eight years old forever in a table in a German report.

She thought about the boy who had pulled her out of the water and shouted at her for ten minutes and then said he was sorry.

At two o'clock she turned round and went out and closed the gate behind her.

***

The parish priest was called Father Kulesza and he was fifty and had been at Brzozówka since 1949, and he was in the sacristy when she knocked, and he was expecting, that week, a great many things, but not her.

"Pani Krawiec? From the Office?"

"Not any more."

"Ah." He was a practical man with a red face. "I heard something. There is always something." He wiped his hands on a cloth. "You will stay for the Mass tomorrow? His Excellency is coming. The new bishop. He is from this parish, you know. He was born here."

"I know."

"He is coming to say his first Mass in the parish. It is a great honour for us. We have no bell, unfortunately, so we cannot ring it, but the children will sing." He looked at her. "Did you know him? You are from here."

"Yes," she said. "I knew him."

***

On the eighth of December the consecration was held at Lublin.

She had decided on the train and again in the room at the inn at Krasnystaw, lying awake with the stove ticking, that she would not go. She was not a Catholic in good standing, or a friend, or an official, or anything at all; she was a woman of thirty-one with a suitcase who had been excluded from the Party four days earlier, and there was no place for her in that cathedral that would not require an explanation. She decided this four times between Friday and Monday. Then on Monday morning she got up and put on her good suit and took the 6.10 to Lublin and stood at the back with her hat in her hands.

It was full. There were three hundred priests and nine bishops and a great many nuns and the seminarians in the front, and the whole of the nave was full of people standing, and there were people standing in the side aisles and in the porch and out on the steps in the snow, and the singing was enormous. She stood against the pillar by the door where the light came in from the west window, and she could see the high altar and the throne and the bishops in their copes.

It took three hours.

She saw the procession come in, and the bishops, and a great press of priests in black, and she looked for the Primate among the mitres and could not tell which of them he was. She saw the candidate come in last, in a white alb with his hands joined, and he looked very young and very pale and entirely composed, and he did not look at the congregation, and she found that she was glad.

She heard the Litany of the Saints, which she remembered from the Forty Hours in 1938, and the psalms, and the long Latin. She saw the laying on of hands, when the bishops came one by one and put their hands on his head in silence, and the cathedral was so quiet that she could hear a woman near her breathing. She saw the book of the Gospels held open over his head by two priests while the consecrating bishop said the prayer. She saw the anointing of his hands with chrism, and the linen cloths wound round them, and the ring put on his finger, and the mitre, and the crosier put into his hand, and she heard the acclamation when he was led to the throne and the organ went up and the whole cathedral sang.

She did not cry. She stood against the pillar with her hat in her hands and her coat over her arm, and she watched a man she had known since he was twelve be made a bishop in the cathedral of Lublin, and she thought: I have signed a great many papers, and one of them was this.

Afterwards she waited until the church was empty. The sacristan came and looked at her and she said, "A moment," and he went away and came back ten minutes later and said, "Pani, we are closing," and she said, "Yes," and went out.

***

The tenth of December was a Wednesday and cold, and the Mass at Brzozówka was at eleven.

There were four hundred people in a church built for three hundred, and the children's choir sang, and the parish had put up a platform outside for the ones who could not get in, and there was a microphone, which did not work, and Father Kulesza announced that His Excellency would preach, and His Excellency preached for nineteen minutes on the text *Nolite timere, pusillus grex*, and he preached well, in the way that a man preaches who has been doing it for five years and has something to say, and twice he stopped and looked at the church, and once he said, in the middle of a sentence about the dead of the parish, "I was baptized in the old church on this ground, and I served at that altar as a boy, and the men who built this one are in the churchyard, and some of them were not old men when they died."

Then it was over and there was a great deal of noise.

She waited outside by the wall of the churchyard where the dead were, in her grey coat, with her hands in her pockets, and the crowd came out and stood about in the snow in groups, and the children ran, and the old women crowded round the bishop and kissed his ring, and he was patient with them, and bent his head, and said something to each one.

He saw her over the heads of the crowd. She was twenty paces away, beyond the wall, and she did not move.

He finished with the old women. He spoke to Father Kulesza. Then he came through the gate in his black coat and his hat and the ring on his finger, and he stood in front of her.

"Pani Dyrektor."

"Your Excellency."

"Don't."

"Congratulations," she said. "It was a good sermon. The microphone was broken but it was a good sermon."

"Nineteen minutes. Kulesza told me twelve. I could see him at the side counting." He looked at her. "You were in Lublin. I saw you at the pillar."

"You couldn't have. I was at the back."

"The light from the west window was behind you and you were the only person in that church not wearing a hat," he said. "I have known where you were standing since ten past eleven on Sunday." He put his hands in his pockets. "Walk with me. Not through the village. Down to the water."

They went by the back lane, past the cooperative shed, along the path that went behind the orchard, and the snow was deep and unbroken and they walked in it single file for part of the way and side by side where the path widened. The river was low and dark between white banks, and the alders were there, and the fallen willow was gone, and the pool was frozen at the edges.

They stood on the bank.

"It's smaller," she said.

"Everything is smaller."

"No. It is actually smaller. They straightened the channel in 1952. There was a cooperative works project." She looked at the water. "I read the file."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. It is a fact about a river." She put her hands deeper in her pockets. "Tomasz. I have to tell you what happened, and then I have to go and get the four o'clock bus, and I would like to do it in that order."

"All right."

"I went to Maria Szeliga at Krasnystaw on Sunday. I told her. She did not forgive me. I did not expect her to and I did not ask her to. I told her what her father did on the fourteenth of June and I said his name out loud and she made me say it twice." She looked at him. "I stood in the place where the house was for an hour. It was the coldest hour of my life and it was not long enough. And on the first of December I was removed from my post, and on the fourth I was excluded from the Party, and in January I am going to Olsztyn to catalogue agricultural machinery in a museum with a leaking roof, and I have a room in the hotel there and Jerzy is staying in Warsaw because he will not leave the Commission, and that is all of it, and there is nothing else."

He was quiet for a moment.

"Nothing else?"

"Nothing else."

"The Party," he said. "You resigned before they excluded you."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because I wanted it to be a decision and not a punishment," she said. "It is the only thing I have ever done that was not a punishment."

He looked at the water. Then he said, "I have a ring on my finger that was put there by a bishop in front of three thousand people, and it means that I am the successor of the apostles, and that I am to be the shepherd of a diocese, and that I may not marry, and that I answer to God for the souls in my care." He turned his hand over. "And in my breast pocket there is a breviary, and inside the breviary there is nothing at all, because I have not yet put anything in it, and I am telling you this because I want you to give me something to put in it."

She looked at him.

"The ring," he said.

She took it out. It was on a string, and she had worn it since October 1943, and it had left a green mark on her skin for fifteen years, and she had it in her hand.

"It's not gold," she said.

"It's better than gold. Gold is soft."

She laughed, once, and it came out of her all at once, like a door banging, and then she put the ring in his hand and closed his fingers over it, and he held it for a moment, and then put it in his breast pocket.

"There," he said.

"That was not a sacrament."

"No," he said. "It was a piece of wire. But I will carry it until I die, and when I die someone will find it in my breviary and will not know what it is, and that is exactly right."

They stood there. The light was going. Up in the village a dog barked and stopped, and someone called a child in.

"Tomasz. I want to go to confession."

He looked at her.

"Now?"

"Now. Before the bus." She met his eyes. "You said not yet. On the twenty-first of November you said not yet, and not like this. I am no longer a member of the Party. I am no longer an officer of the state. I am a woman of thirty-one with a suitcase and a post at Olsztyn, and I have no power over anyone, and I cannot do anything for you or against you, and I have come to the end of what I can give up." She said it steadily. "So. Not yet has become now. Or it has not, and you will tell me, and I will get the bus."

He looked at her for a long moment.

"Come to the church," he said.

They went back up the lane in the dark with the snow squeaking under their feet, and the church was empty and cold, and Father Kulesza had banked the stove in the sacristy and gone to his dinner. There was one candle burning at the side altar and the red lamp.

He put on the stole in the sacristy and sat down in the confessional, and she knelt on the other side of the grille in the dark, and it was much colder than St Anne's had been.

"Bless me, Father, for I have sinned. It has been nineteen days since my last confession."

"Go on."

"I have already told you everything," she said. "I am not going to tell it again. I told it on the twenty-first of November and I have not done anything since except go to a woman in Krasnystaw and stand in a field. So I will say it short. I was seventeen and I was frightened and I gave a man a cellar, and thirty-one people died, and I have carried it for fourteen years and made a career out of it. I named a man in 1949 and it went into a file. I gave evidence against a man in 1951 and he was shot. I let an innocent man be destroyed in 1944 and he hanged himself in a barn in 1953 while I was in Warsaw being promoted." She paused. "And I have been proud. That is the one I have only just found. I have been proud for fourteen years, Father, of being a woman who carried a heavy thing well. I have been proud of my own guilt. It has been the last thing I owned and I have polished it."

The grille was silent for a moment.

"Anything else?"

"I let my husband love me for five years without looking at him."

"Anything else?"

"No."

"Then make your act of contrition."

She folded her hands on the shelf.

"Boże mój, żałuję za grzechy moje z całego serca, ponieważ zasłużyłem na karę od Ciebie—" She stopped. "I don't believe in God."

"I know."

"You said to say it anyway."

"Say it anyway."

She said it. All of it, in Polish, the whole formula, slowly, and it took a long time, and her voice was not steady and it did not matter, and at the end she said, "Amen."

He said the words in Latin, quietly, with his hand raised, and she heard them through the grille, and they were the oldest words she knew, older than the Party, older than the state, older than the report with the table in it.

"Ego te absolvo a peccatis tuis, in nomine Patris, et Filii, et Spiritus Sancti. Amen."

"Amen," she said.

She came out of the confessional and stood in the aisle, and he came out after her and took off the stole and folded it, and they stood in the empty church in the dark with one candle burning.

"For the penance," he said, "I want you to eat something. You are thin and you have been thin since October and I have been looking at it."

"That is not a penance, that is a prescription."

"I am a priest and I cannot help it." He put the stole in his pocket. "And I will write to you at Olsztyn. Once a month. Not about anything. About the diocese, and the roof, and the bell we do not have. You will not answer, and I will go on writing, and in ten years there will be a hundred and twenty letters, and somebody will read them after I am dead and will think that the Bishop of Lublin had a very dull friend."

"I will answer," she said.

"Then it will be two hundred and forty letters."

They went out together onto the steps. It had begun to snow again, small hard flakes out of the east, and the village was dark except for three windows and the lamp over the church door, and the bus would come at four and it was ten to.

"Zofia."

"Mm."

"I am going to tell you something and I do not want you to answer it." He stood with his hands in his pockets and the snow on his shoulders. "In 1944 I was twenty years old and I was in the forest, and a man came up from the village on the fourteenth of June and told us that Brzozówka was burning and that the Germans were in it, and my commander said we could not go, because we had eleven men and two machine guns and there was a company in the village with armoured cars, and that if we went we would be killed and the village would burn anyway. And I obeyed him. I obeyed him, and I stayed in the forest, and I sat under a tree all night and listened to it, and I have never told anyone that either." He looked at her. "So. Two people who signed. Two people who obeyed. We have been very careful with our hands for fourteen years and it has not saved one single person. I have decided that this is not a reason to stop. I have decided that it is the only reason to go on."

She looked at him.

"The bus," she said.

"I know."

"Will you bless me? Not as a priest. As a man who is standing in the snow."

He raised his hand and made the sign of the cross over her, and said nothing at all.

She walked down the path to the road with her suitcase, and the snow was coming harder, and she turned at the gate — the same gate, or a gate in the same place, at the end of the lane where the lilacs had been — and he was still standing on the steps under the lamp with his hands in his pockets.

"Tomasz."

"Yes."

"Plant lilacs," she said. "At the new presbytery. There aren't any."

"I will plant lilacs," he said.

The bus came at four o'clock and it was a new bus, with a diesel engine that smelled of the future, and there were nine people in it and a woman with a basket of eggs and a boy asleep against the window. Zofia Krawiec got on and put her suitcase on the rack and sat down at the back, and the bus turned in the yard and went out onto the Krasnystaw road, and the village went past the window, the church and the school and the cooperative shed and the plot of ground with the pear tree in it, and then the fields, and then nothing but snow and the dark and, a long way off, the lights of a train going east.

She sat with her hands in her lap. The ring was gone from her neck and there was a pale mark where it had been, and it would fade in a month, and she found that she did not want it to.

The bus went on through the dark towards Krasnystaw, and the snow came down over the fields, and somewhere behind her a man was telling his neighbour about the price of pigs, and the engine made its noise, and the road went on, and it did not care.

What remained was this: a woman on a bus; a bishop standing on the steps of a village church until the bus had gone; a ring of galvanised wire in a breviary; thirty-nine names on a board; a field where a house had been; a pear tree; and a river that had been straightened in 1952 and was still, in the bend that was left of it, deep enough to drown in.
