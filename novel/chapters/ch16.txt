A Funeral Across the River

Vibius dies in the hour before dawn, when the city's poor do most of their important business to avoid troubling the light. The guild pays for the pyre, as the guild pays for everything the trade has first made necessary. Prima washes the body with the guildswomen, who are kind in the competent way of women who have washed everything the world produces, and lays in the shroud a needle — not his, hers; her first, blunted now to the colour of river water — because a fuller goes out of Rome in the garment he made of his life, and she was, whatever the books in whatever hands may say, his making in every way the gods inspect.

The night before is measured in honey. Prima heats it at the hours the watch changes because the watch, passing the end of the street with its buckets, is the only clock the house can afford that works outdoors. First watch: he drinks, and jokes, in the whisper the room has agreed to call his voice this week, that the guild physician should be charged by the spoonful since the medicine is mostly her labour with thyme in it. Second watch: he asks for the bench by the frames, is refused by three women in council, and accepts the refusal the way he accepts weather in his trade, as a finding. Third watch: the cough takes up its residence documents and shows them to the room one by one, and Prima is up with the cup and the cloth and the other cloth, counting breaths with her mouth open the way she counts everything she is afraid of finishing.

By the fourth watch the street knows. Streets know these things in Transtiberim the way houses know them on the Palatine: through the plumbing, in its night register. A light in a fuller's window at an hour when fullers economise lamp oil is a sentence anybody passing can decline. The baker's mother arrives without soup, which is how the neighbourhood understands that the situation has passed the soup stage and entered government. She takes the fire from Prima without asking, as generals relieve exhausted subordinates, and sends Titus — flour still in his eyebrows from a night bake undertaken because tomorrow's bread does not stop for anybody's century — to fetch the guild women in an order she has clearly been rehearsing since the Saturnalia.

They come as the plural always comes: with shawls, basins, spare linen, opinions about water temperature, and a silence that is not the opposite of talk but its container. The eldest of them, the vats woman who has lately sold a great deal of thread to a Greek, puts her hand for one moment on Prima's shoulder — where a stripe would be in a family that promotes its daughters by sewing — and says, “Count with us, girl. It's work. Work's the ride.” And Prima, who has counted stitches, stairs by proxy, breaths, second columns and her father's sayings, understands that the washing of a dead fuller by the women of his trade is also a count: so many cloths, so much water no worse used, a body made ready with the competence the world has always demanded of women it declines to register.

Her father, laid out, looks less grey. The trade in him relaxes out of him like dye out of an unrinsed garment, and what is left is the man under the fuller, the one her mother saw at an island market counting a porter's load wrong and deciding a life on the correction. She washes his hands last. They have carried other people's clothing through urine and earth and out the other side clean for forty years, and they lie now with the nails trimmed by his daughter and the thumbs quiet, like tools set down correctly after use. She lays the needle in beside them, out of a bookkeeping no guild keeps: his making, her instrument; her making, his workmanship. The women see it and say nothing. In this neighbourhood a signature in a shroud is nobody's business except the fire's.

Then there is the house to be governed, because death in two rooms is also an administrative event. The guild chest sends its contribution with a clerk whose cough apologises ahead of him up the street. The pyre place outside the walls is spoken for, at the poor men's hour, with wood whose price Mother Livia negotiates down by a method involving the words *the bakery knows your family*. Titus's father, an oven of a man who speaks in loaves, sends bread for the mourners and stays away himself, from a delicacy the street understands: too many father-shaped men in a room spoil the proportions of grief. By the time the winter light gets its one bar into the yard, the household has become what Vibius made it for thirty years it will not have him — ordered, ferociously, like a thing being held steady for a woman who is about to have to walk behind it through the rain holding nobody.

The funeral winds through Transtiberim in the rain. It rains in Rome for funerals of every class, out of fairness or habit. The baker's family walks in the position of almost-relatives, which the baker's mother discharges like a magistracy. Marcus, who has heard of the death the way he hears everything, through the plumbing, comes to stand hatless at the procession's edge in a plain cloak; he does not approach; Daphne's return of a necklace has taught him at least the outline of the lesson that grief is not an audience. Barbatus walks the whole route in the neighbourhood's ranks where he has no legal place and an entirely unchallenged one, carrying, when the bearers tire, the bier's front pole without being asked, in the manner of men who express condolence by weight.

They take him out by the yard gate because the front door of a fuller's house is for customers' garments, and Vibius, whatever else the books in whatever hands may say, was never his own customer. The bearers are guild men in their festival cloaks gone dark at the shoulders with rain. Six of them carry in rotation; three more walk the rotation empty-handed, spelling, the way reliefs are worked at the vats when a heavy piece comes through. Barbatus falls in at the first corner without a word to anybody, between a tanner's apprentice and a widower from the street of the rope-makers, and his great weather of a body does to the procession what ballast does to a ship in Marcus's current philosophy: nobody praises it, everything arrives.

Prima walks behind the bier in the place the neighbourhood has silently surveyed for her. Not veiled like a senator's daughter — veils across the river are working cloth, and hers has her own stitching in the hem, an early piece, pre-Caelian prices. She does not wail. There are women for wailing in the guild, two of them, with the professional lungs of an older economy, and they perform the grief of the street at the correct volume while Prima performs hers by competence: she counts. She counts the turnings where the bearers change, because at each turning the pole must be passed like a relay baton in a richer people's game. She counts doorways with somebody standing in them in the posture of respect — tradespeople taking one step out of the wet, a hand lifted, a word for the deceased filed under his trade rather than his name: *a clean fuller, that one; a man who paid on the day; Claudia's husband; the girl's father*.

The rain collaborates with the route's low places. At the lane by the Temple the water is over the stepping stones, and the front bearers slow, and it is there that Barbatus takes the pole's weight whole for the length of the puddle, one shoulder under the front corner like a man holding up a piece of roof in a story. Nobody applauds. That is how you can tell it is not the arena. Titus is at Prima's elbow by then — not holding it, exactly; the neighbourhood has strong views about elbows and betrothals, even at funerals, especially at funerals — but present at the angle where a slip would become his business. He has left the ovens in his brother's charge and brought, of all the useless and necessary things, the dry end of his own cloak turned outward against the rain's angle, so that the girl walking beside him gets marginally less weather than the rest of Transtiberim. It is a small, complete act of government. Prima notices. Mother Livia, two rows back in the magistracy of almost-relatives, notices Prima notice, and revises nothing aloud.

At the bridge end, where the route turns for the road outside the walls, a senator's son is standing hatless in a plain cloak that is failing to be plain in the way of expensive things in poor streets. The procession notices him at the speed Servilia hears things in another constituency. The wailing women lower their key by a professional fraction. Marcus does not step forward, does not offer the condolence coin, does not make the small, fatal gesture by which his class buys a place in other people's photographs. He stands in the rain with water finding the places in his education where the tutors skipped chapters, and he looks at the bier, and then — once, and for less time than a held note in a music room — at the girl walking behind it counting doorways, and something in his face does the thing faces do in his family at moments of unminuted honesty: nothing, thoroughly, at cost. Prima does not see him. She is counting. Barbatus sees him, files him, and moves his shoulder six inches so that his shadow falls between the heir of the Cornelii and the fuller's procession for the length of the bridge turning, which in the day-book of no house whatsoever is entered as *manners, further education of*.

Outside the walls the pyre ground smokes in the wet like the city's memory of all its other mornings. There are cheaper fires further off, and one grand one, two streets of cypress away, where an equestrian family has hired flutes and is getting its money's worth out of the acoustics. The guild's plot is in between, as guild things are: respectable, rained on, carefully measured. They lay Vibius with his needle in the shroud and a coin from the chest and a piece of unbleached cloth the women have argued about briefly and included unanimously. The priest the guild keeps for such work says the words at a pace suitable to wet wood.

Prima does not look away when the fire takes. She has heated honey on small fires for the dying all winter; she understands tending as a form of loyalty that does not require the patient's cooperation. While the flames negotiate with the rain in the language both have spoken since Romulus, she counts the guildsmen filing past with their contributions and condolences, and her mouth is open on the numbers, and Titus's cloak is at its angle, and the baker's mother is crying in the place nobody can itemise, which as it turns out is behind the wailing women, professionally camouflaged. Somebody says Claudia's name then, the way names surface at pyres, and somebody else — with wine already in them though it is not yet the first hour by any respectable sundial — says *she'd be proud of the keeping*, and the old vats woman looks hard at the speaker and says, “Drink at home,” in a voice that rearranges the conversation's seating. But the word *keeping* has been said aloud beside a fire, in the hearing of the great world, and gone up with the smoke where the city's stories live until they are brought down again by somebody's dinner.

Antonia does not come. She sends Eirene, who has buried people from every century of her own life and knows how to be present as furniture: steady, unremarked, holding what needs holding. Eirene comes back across the bridge in the evening with wet hems and a report in her filing voice. “She did her father's washing proud. The baker boy kept hold of her elbow at the pyre like a man steadying a ladder — not taking the climb off her, just holding the ladder. I liked him for it. The mother cried where nobody could itemise it.”

“And Prima?”

“Counted the guildsmen as they came past the pyre.” Eirene wrings her hem into the courtyard drain with strong wrists. “Under her breath. Mouth open on the numbers. Antonia, my love, you cannot wait out of delicacy much longer. Grief makes people honest at retail. Somebody at those vats is telling that girl, tonight, with wine in them, the story of the hennaed woman. It is what funerals are for, among the poor: the redistribution of the truth.”

“Retail honesty,” Antonia says. “Is that the phrase of the season across the river, or have you coined it on the bridge?”

“I have buried people since before you could price a room, Antonia. The coinage accrues.” Eirene accepts, without being offered it, the chair in Antonia's office that is never offered to anyone: the one with its back to the accounts. She is in her outdoor shawl, damp through one shoulder, and she looks, suddenly, terribly, like the girl from the Subura house who learned to keep one mystery and no illusions in a corridor where the mirrors were held by other people's daughters. “There was wine at the edge of that crowd by the first hour. There's always wine at the edge. Wine is how the poor file their evidence — out loud, to strangers, in the certainty somebody's clerk is listening. Tonight the hennaed woman is a story in the vats with a beginning and a middle and a place where a girl of nineteen is doing the sum on her way home in the rain. Either she hears it from us tomorrow in a room with a door and a needle to hold, or she hears it in pieces for a year and assembles it the way Rome assembles its columns: the top of it out of sight, the carving done by anonymous hands.”

Antonia has been arranging tax receipts for two hours. She now discovers that she has arranged them, with the unerring instinct of her hands when they do not ask her permission, into the shape of the year itself: inspections here, physician's account there — it has been that kind of winter. She sets the stylus down in exact alignment with the tablet's edge, an act of government she no longer bothers to pretend is influence over anything larger than the province of the desk.

“How does one tell it well?” she asks, and it is not a question she has asked any living person since Baiae. She hears it in the room like a request for the small room at the Greek's. “In the order of the evening, Eirene, the evening is twenty-one years long and most of it is stairs.”

“Then we begin with the stairs and not with the committee.” Eirene leans forward, elbows on knees, in the posture women in this city assume for the governance of the unspeakable, having assumed it for childbeds, deaths, betrothals and the reading of men's letters since letters were invented to be left open on dressing tables. “You tell it as work. It's the only language that girl won't suspect of being a chair she's meant to faint in. She has a trade, a counting voice and a father in the ground across the river as of this forenoon. If you go at her with 'my child' in the first sentence you'll lose her to the doorway, and I don't blame any daughter of Claudia's for using doorways correctly; she comes by it in several genealogies. Tell the work. The mirror, the stairs, the man, the money, the report. Then the thread. Then what you propose, which I trust, as your junior partner in catastrophe, is *nothing, at her prices, with the paperwork available*.”

Antonia looks at her for a long time in the lamp. “You have rehearsed this on the bridge too.”

“I rehearsed it in the Subura, love. We all did our versions of that speech in that house. Yours is the only one that has ever got to be delivered in premises the speaker owns.” Eirene rises and takes the damp out of the room with her, the way she always takes the weather: personally, and onward to where it is needed. At the door she stops with her hand on it and doesn't turn round, because in this friendship there is a whole economy of not-turning-round that has served since they were both other men's property, and says, “Bring soup. Whatever the small rooms in bankers' offices do without it, our rooms needn't. Tell it in a working room, with stuff on the table for her hands. If we've learned anything from nineteen years of her across the river existing at full rate, it's that the hands confess first. Give hers something true to hold.”

Antonia looks up from the tax receipts, which she has been arranging for two hours in an order comprehensible only to the recently bereaved. “Tomorrow,” she says. “Here. Not at the vats. I will send Barbatus with a commission urgent enough to be respectable.”

“And if she's already been told, badly?”

“Then I will tell her again, well. It will not be the first thing in this city I have had to launder.”

The morning offering is a small government too, and this morning it has a full agenda. There is rain in the compluvium's basin to be heard over; there is Cornelia at the far door of the shrine room with her hands correctly folded and her attention visibly elsewhere, a position perfected at family sacrifices since she was nine and the household discovered she could do her scales silently with her eyes open; there is Marcus, who is not there. His absence is entered, by the steward, as *the young master is at the baths*. It is not yet the hour for baths. Nobody corrects the entry. In this family the day-book's fictions have always been load-bearing, and Lentulus, pouring the offering with his father's hands at the ends of his own sleeves, finds he cannot remember whether he taught the boy that trick or merely inherited the building where it's kept.

“Marcus is across the river,” Servilia says, after the words have been said and the cup set down and the slave who carries the tray has been dismissed by a glance that functions, in her management, as a door closing. She says it exactly once, the way she drops sentences about other people's climates, and does not repeat it for the record. Cornelia, at the door, hears *across the river* arrive at the altitude where, in this house, geography is a euphemism with citizenship.

Her father looks at his wife. Then at the empty, correct space where his son should be standing in the order of the offertory procession. Then — the sequence is given here in the order of the morning, not the order of his questions — he says, “In the rain.”

“In a plain cloak, Onesimus believes. He forgot to see it professionally in three languages.” Servilia adjusts nothing about her own appearance; she never does when intelligence is being exchanged; fidgeting is for people who trade in coins of smaller denomination. “He will stand at the procession's edge, from what the plumbing tells me, and get wet in a way his tutors did not cover. It is not the worst education this family has arranged for an heir. It is merely the only one we did not.”

Cornelia says — because in this family, this year, the daughters have started saying things in doorways in a way that has the steward keeping a fourth set of books — “Should someone stand for us, Father?”

It is, as questions go in a shrine room, a very Cornelian one, which is to say that it displaces the emotional work onto representation. Lentulus looks at his daughter — sixteen, registered in every book the city keeps for girls of his order, dowered in figures her mother handles like weather — and he hears the clause he drafted at dawn with a barber waiting go past again, on its way to the courts, dressed in a sixteen-year-old's grammar. *Standing for us* would be exactly the scene his dead father taxed him to avoid imposing, the tax in the new currency paid by the wrong people at a pyre.

“Barbatus is standing for us,” he says, and discovers he believes it: a former gladiator in a neighbourhood procession, carrying a front pole by weight in the rain, is this family's entire effective foreign policy in Transtiberim at present, and better than most of what the Senate would have sent. “Your brother is standing for himself. It is a new arrangement. We will see how it files.”

“Marcus, standing for himself,” Cornelia says, tasting their father's new coinage for counterfeit. “That's the Greek song too, isn't it? The one for people who'd know.” She bows correctly to the shrine, to her parents, to the Lares' whole department of household arrangements, and goes to her lesson, and is heard through two doors to begin the exercise the second way.

It is after this — after the acoustics have decided what may be said where, after the slaves have performed their drilled withdrawal, after the morning has been properly constituted as a theatre with only the principals left on stage — that Servilia delivers, in the tone for provincial appointments, her intelligence about the other side of the river, and about what she has already done there with her brother's steward and a sum to a guild, unattributed.

Lentulus hears of the fuller’s death from Servilia, who tells him at the morning offering in the same tone she uses for provincial appointments, because in their house all intelligence now travels at that altitude, above the weather. “Your other — the girl across the river is burying her father today. Her father,” Servilia says, correcting the sentence as a general corrects a map. “The man who raised her. I have sent, through my brother's steward, a sum to the fullers' guild for widows and orphans of the trade, unattributed. It will reach her as the world reaches people like her: late, and through institutions, and smelling faintly of somebody else's conscience.”

Lentulus pours the offering. His hand — the household watches his hands at the shrine the way clients watch a banker's — does its work without visible cost. “You might have told me you meant to send it.”

“You might have told me, at any point in nineteen years, that you had a daughter. We are even, husband, in the great Cornelian ledger. We have both been settling your youth at a distance and charging the house for the mileage.” She sets the cup in its ring. “I knew Baiae. I did not know she lived. Your father charged my ignorance as the price of my cooperation, and I paid it — I want that in whatever minutes you are keeping now, in whichever of your rooms. I married a settlement, Lentulus. I made it work the way rain makes a roof work: by finding every hole for nineteen years. Do not now make the girl into one.”

It is the longest speech of their marriage. The slaves have withdrawn at its first sentence with the drilled instincts of a household that knows which weather is for indoors. Lentulus stands at the little shrine with the Lares gleaming dully in their niche — household gods, custodians of exactly this: the sanctity of a family's arrangements — and understands that he has been forgiven nothing and condemned of very little, which in Servilia's bookkeeping is the state called *marriage*.

“What would you have me do?” he asks.

Servilia does not answer in abstractions. Abstractions are for men composing, and she has a century to settle before the clients' hour is over. She tells him the route as if she were issuing kitchen orders in another house across the city — for such, at this point, it is hard among their household gods to distinguish her logistics from a certain custodianship of stairs and dresses: Soter to the guild, not Onesimus, because a family's steward leaves footprints in other people's paperwork; the money to arrive as three things, never one, because one sum is a story and three are administration; the first already sent, unattributed, for widows and orphans — the general fund from which her individual case will be watered without a single name being carried separately like a stone in a sling.

“The second is the bequest you and — your creditor — drafted in a banker's other room.” She says *your creditor* the way men of her husband's class say *the Greek*: a function, a ministry, a force of weather in a veil. It is, Lentulus understands with the part of him a dead man named forwarding address, the most respectful word available in this house for the woman his father sold, and Servilia, who is precise about such denominations as registers, knows exactly what it costs her to use it. It costs her nothing she does not choose to spend. That too is in the ledger now.

“The third,” she continues, “is not money at all. It is your continued absence, maintained at the standard of a public work. I have had the house's instructions about Marcus amended: he is not to be prevented at the garden gate, he is to be *witnessed* at it, which has always been our system with heirs and aqueducts. If he stands in rain across rivers of his own motion, the house will let the day-book say the baths. A boy who is learning manners at the full rate should not be interrupted by his family's bookkeeping arriving behind him in a carrying chair.”

Lentulus looks at the Lares, who have supervised three centuries of this family's arrangements and never once asked to see the paperwork. “You have it all drafted.”

“I have had it drafted since the oysters, husband. I merely lacked, until your father chose better through you, an administration that would file it.” She adjusts her veil for the household's public morning — the veil is state architecture, and on her it works. “There is a further matter. The girl's *telling*, which is not ours to schedule — attend to this, as it is the interesting clause — will happen in a house where the women are registered, the tax is paid quarterly, and the mistress counts faster than any of us. When it has happened, there will be consequences in the plumbing. A fuller’s daughter does not learn she is a Cornelius's by-blow in a linen room without the news taking the waters to every bath in Rome by the new moon, attenuated, adorned, and useless for anything except making the Avidian contract a document we wish to have signed in fair weather. Therefore the contract is to be brought forward. My brother will write today. The figures are ready to be weather.”

It lands, as all her briefings land, leaving him in command of the ground she has chosen. What surprises him — surprises: at forty-five, after this of all winters, the senses still report novelties like provinces applying for annexation — is that he is not diminished by it. He married a settlement and it has become, in the rain, in the finding of every hole, something his father's Latin lacks a dative for. In the doorway, re-entering the house's public constitution, he stops his wife with the one piece of intelligence at his own altitude: “She said the person wronged was not me. The creditor. In the banker's room.”

“Then we are three in agreement,” Servilia says, “the dead, the creditor, and the wife. History will call it the Senate. Come to breakfast, Lentulus. Marcus will be home for it, wet through, with the news that everybody buried a fuller very decently in the rain, and I wish to see whether your son can deliver it in the order of the morning.”

He does come home for it, which the steward enters as *the young master returned, weather*, a form of words whose elasticity this house is coming to rely upon the way fleets rely on ballast. He has changed out of the plain cloak into dry wool that costs conspicuously more, and his hair is doing the thing young men's hair does after rain when it is innocent of barbers, and at the threshold of the dining room he performs — before slaves, ancestors by proxy, a father lately unsealed and a mother in full sail — the small miracle his winter's expensive, unminuted education has been arranging without his permission: he says nothing about himself at all.

“A fuller was buried very decently across the river,” he says. “The guild were out in strength. The baker held the ladder, is how I'd file it. Barbatus carried.” He takes the cup his mother has positioned within prophecy's reach and drinks it, slowly, the way a woman on the Caelian once instructed him in front of witnesses. “There was a girl counting the guildsmen past the pyre. Under her breath. Her mouth was open on the numbers. I didn't —” Marcus stops, in the middle of his own morning, like a sentence coming right at the second count. “I stood at the edge. It seemed the available manners.”

Cornelia, across the table, looks up from an egg she has been conducting like a small province. She looks at her brother, and then at her father, and then, because she is Servilia's daughter and this is the season of daughters in doorways, she looks at nobody at all and says, “Define *edge*,” at a volume a music master would call indicative.

Her father does not rebuke her. The form for it is in fair copy somewhere in the house, yellowing. He pours for his son instead — watered, at the rate for mornings when a man has been across a river in other people's weather — and says, in the first book's hand, upright and dull: “It was correctly observed, Marcus. The house records it.” And the clerk at the side table, who has been writing *who came, what they asked, what it will cost when it comes back* since Marcus was in a smaller chair than this one, writes nothing down about the funeral across the river, which in a new system that nobody has yet voted on, in a household learning its future in another woman's grammar, is the most serious form of record.

“The dowry, through the guild, as agreed with —” she does not say the name in the shrine room; names, like knives, are put away in her house after use “— as agreed. Then stay away from the girl's life with the same talent you have employed for nineteen years, but this time as a kindness she can spend. And Lentulus—” Servilia turns at the door with her veil arranged and her war over for the morning “—when Cornelia marries the Avidius boy, which she will, you will stand there like a man who has given a daughter, not settled a debt. We owe the register that much acting. It is the one tax your class still pays in full.”
