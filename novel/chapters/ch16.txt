A Funeral Across the River
Vibius dies in the hour before dawn, when the city's poor do most of their important business to avoid troubling the light. The guild pays for the pyre, as the guild pays for everything the trade has first made necessary. Prima washes the body with the guildswomen, who are kind in the competent way of women who have washed everything the world produces, and lays in the shroud a needle — not his, hers; her first, blunted now to the colour of river water — because a fuller goes out of Rome in the garment he made of his life, and she was, whatever the books in whatever hands may say, his making in every way the gods inspect.

The funeral winds through Transtiberim in the rain. It rains in Rome for funerals of every class, out of fairness or habit. The baker's family walks in the position of almost-relatives, which the baker's mother discharges like a magistracy. Marcus, who has heard of the death the way he hears everything, through the plumbing, comes to stand hatless at the procession's edge in a plain cloak; he does not approach; Daphne's return of a necklace has taught him at least the outline of the lesson that grief is not an audience. Barbatus walks the whole route in the neighbourhood's ranks where he has no legal place and an entirely unchallenged one, carrying, when the bearers tire, the bier's front pole without being asked, in the manner of men who express condolence by weight.

Antonia does not come. She sends Eirene, who has buried people from every century of her own life and knows how to be present as furniture: steady, unremarked, holding what needs holding. Eirene comes back across the bridge in the evening with wet hems and a report in her filing voice. “She did her father's washing proud. The baker boy kept hold of her elbow at the pyre like a man steadying a ladder — not taking the climb off her, just holding the ladder. I liked him for it. The mother cried where nobody could itemise it.”

“And Prima?”

“Counted the guildsmen as they came past the pyre.” Eirene wrings her hem into the courtyard drain with strong wrists. “Under her breath. Mouth open on the numbers. Antonia, my love, you cannot wait out of delicacy much longer. Grief makes people honest at retail. Somebody at those vats is telling that girl, tonight, with wine in them, the story of the hennaed woman. It is what funerals are for, among the poor: the redistribution of the truth.”

Antonia looks up from the tax receipts, which she has been arranging for two hours in an order comprehensible only to the recently bereaved. “Tomorrow,” she says. “Here. Not at the vats. I will send Barbatus with a commission urgent enough to be respectable.”

“And if she's already been told, badly?”

“Then I will tell her again, well. It will not be the first thing in this city I have had to launder.”

Lentulus hears of the fuller’s death from Servilia, who tells him at the morning offering in the same tone she uses for provincial appointments, because in their house all intelligence now travels at that altitude, above the weather. “Your other — the girl across the river is burying her father today. Her father,” Servilia says, correcting the sentence as a general corrects a map. “The man who raised her. I have sent, through my brother's steward, a sum to the fullers' guild for widows and orphans of the trade, unattributed. It will reach her as the world reaches people like her: late, and through institutions, and smelling faintly of somebody else's conscience.”

Lentulus pours the offering. His hand — the household watches his hands at the shrine the way clients watch a banker's — does its work without visible cost. “You might have told me you meant to send it.”

“You might have told me, at any point in nineteen years, that you had a daughter. We are even, husband, in the great Cornelian ledger. We have both been settling your youth at a distance and charging the house for the mileage.” She sets the cup in its ring. “I knew Baiae. I did not know she lived. Your father charged my ignorance as the price of my cooperation, and I paid it — I want that in whatever minutes you are keeping now, in whichever of your rooms. I married a settlement, Lentulus. I made it work the way rain makes a roof work: by finding every hole for nineteen years. Do not now make the girl into one.”

It is the longest speech of their marriage. The slaves have withdrawn at its first sentence with the drilled instincts of a household that knows which weather is for indoors. Lentulus stands at the little shrine with the Lares gleaming dully in their niche — household gods, custodians of exactly this: the sanctity of a family's arrangements — and understands that he has been forgiven nothing and condemned of very little, which in Servilia's bookkeeping is the state called *marriage*.

“What would you have me do?” he asks.

“The dowry, through the guild, as agreed with —” she does not say the name in the shrine room; names, like knives, are put away in her house after use “— as agreed. Then stay away from the girl's life with the same talent you have employed for nineteen years, but this time as a kindness she can spend. And Lentulus—” Servilia turns at the door with her veil arranged and her war over for the morning “—when Cornelia marries the Avidius boy, which she will, you will stand there like a man who has given a daughter, not settled a debt. We owe the register that much acting. It is the one tax your class still pays in full.”
