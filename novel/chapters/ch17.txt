In the Order of the Evening

She tells her in the small room off the courtyard where the linen is kept, because it is where Prima's hands will know what they are for if the rest of her forgets. There is a commission on the table — spring hangings, genuinely — and Eirene within call, and the door not quite shut, which in Antonia's house has always been a policy about conversations: nothing true here should require a lock.

She has had the room prepared the way she prepares for a difficult guest: by removing everything that could be made to testify about the hostess. No Greeks' strongboxes, by proxy or metaphor. No paper. Diomedes has been instructed — and has instructed the house, in the hand nobody sees — that the small room off the courtyard is engaged for a linen consultation of unreasonable importance, with soup. The soup is Ammonius' morning stock clarified to a transparency he considers a moral position. It is to be brought by Eirene, without ceremony, at whatever point in the proceedings a door opening and shutting would be a form of punctuation rather than an escape route. After thirty years, Antonia trusts Eirene's punctuation over most men's prose.

Barbatus went across the river at the second hour with the commission in its official form: *spring hangings, urgent, the mistress will see the needlewoman herself about the return of the pattern*. He delivered it standing in the fuller's yard among the frames, in his door-keeper's Latin, and then — because a man who carried a front pole in the rain has earned one uncommissioned sentence in that street for the rest of his natural life — he said to Prima, as to a recruit he was walking to a first posting: “Eat before. House'll feed you anyway, whatever the paperwork says. Eat before all the same. It's a rule with information.”

Antonia has rehearsed, of course. Antonia rehearses the way ships rehearse fire: not because she expects the drill to survive smoke, but so that her hands will know where the buckets are when the smoke makes philosophers of everyone else. There have been versions in the night office with Diomedes' practice tablet smoothed between paragraphs. In one version she begins with Baiae, in the belief that a bad flight of stairs honestly described might carry the rest; in another she begins with Claudia, the counting woman, because any story that gives Prima's mother-in-Transtiberim the first word is a story that cannot be accused in that girl's hearing of arriving to re-audit her. In a third version, which she burned in no fire because she drafts in wax and not in feelings, she began with *I am sorry*, and Eirene, consulted over the lamps like a consul over a war, eliminated it on procedural grounds: “Sorry is a door other people hold open, love. If you lead with it, she'll spend the hour trying to shut it for your comfort, and then we've spent her, on the first morning, exactly like everybody else with a name.”

So: work first. Soup as required. The spring linen on the table not as a pretext but as a true bill: there *is* a border; houses need borders; a length of cloth can hold twenty years the way a table holds a dinner, by not requiring anybody to look directly at anybody else until the fish course of the truth is ready. Antonia dresses for the occasion in the clothes she wears for the tax office and for no one else's theatre — plain, at full price, hair done by her own hands with a hairdresser's daughter's refusal to sit idle in that particular chair for any woman living — and at the third hour, with the house performing an afternoon it has been paid to make unremarkable, she hears Barbatus' step in the courtyard and another, lighter, under it, and the future arrives in mourning at her father's rates, carrying a work-box.

Prima comes in mourning, which in Transtiberim means her best dress gone dark at the edges and her hair done by the baker's mother into an arrangement of great respectability and no mercy. She has her work-box. She has, Antonia sees at once, been told something badly already — not the thing itself, perhaps, but its approach: the averted vat-women, an old woman's funeral wine loosening the phrase *the money stopped*, the way a room changes its temperature when a girl who was *theirs in every way the gods inspect* enters it. Grief has made the neighbourhood honest at retail.

It was at the pyre that the temperature of rooms began to change for Prima, though at the time she filed it under smoke. A man from the tanners' benches, with wine already in him, told his neighbour that Claudia Vibii *kept that one close as thread on a spool, and small wonder, given the money in the sleeve of that winter* — and then met the old vats woman's eye and found, suddenly, an urgent interest in the weather. At the guild meal afterwards, which is eaten standing because grief in a small property has nowhere to sit down, an aunt by guild addressed her as *Vibius' girl* with a stress on the name that Prima's ear, trained by bakers' mothers and music she has only overheard through doors, registered as new music. On the way home from the walls, with Titus's cloak no longer required for rain and therefore looming even larger as a declaration, Mother Livia said out of her own weather: “Whatever telling there's to be, girl, you're to hear it indoors, sitting, from somebody who keeps a house. Not at the vats. I won't have my future family informed out of a jar.”

Prima has been told, since she was old enough to hold a needle, that she takes things *in*, the way linen takes a dye. It is not always meant as praise. She has taken in, in seven days: a funeral, a guild chest's exact contribution, a father's place at the fire filled by his own absence in the posture of a small, complete government, a sentence about money stopping, an old woman's advice to *sign yours*, a blank space in her dead mother's leaves under a word she is still learning to decline. This morning Barbatus stood in the yard with a commission urgent enough to be respectable and a face she now knows how to read better than a pattern, and she put on her best dress gone dark at the edges and checked the spring linen's count one more time — right, at the second attempt — and walked behind him across the island bridge in air that smelled of wet stone and other people's breakfasts.

“Is it about the count being off by three flowers?” she asked him, at the bridge's crown, because it was the least expensive of the questions queuing in her.

“It's about the mistress wanting the room with the good light and the woman who works best in it to be in it at the same time,” Barbatus said. “Beyond that my instructions are a tactician's. Eat before, I said, and you haven't, because young people file wisdom under Other People's.” He produced, from somewhere in the weather of him, half a festival loaf of the bakery's third-best work — campaign rations, in this city, often arrive as baked goods — and they shared it walking, like soldiers in a ballad neither party would be caught singing. River under them: lights in pieces. Rome ahead of them: the mistress of the known world, as the inscription programme says, going about its morning in columns nobody at ground level can read whole.

She knew, by the Caelian gate, what the commission's real name was. Girls who hold other people's news steady for a living develop, at nineteen, a customs officer's sense of a sentence being smuggled. She did not know its terms, its order, its author, or its price. But the doorkeeper's silence had a soup-shaped hole in it, and the thread Greek had a face that went with a certain absence in a guild book's columns, and her father's whole love arranged like tools on a bench had been saying, for a month of evenings, the one word in his accounts that was never at the front.

*Keeping*, Prima thinks, with her work-box held correctly and her chin not yet doing anything she has decided about, on the threshold of the linen room where a rich woman is waiting with her hands flat on spring cloth. Let it be work, at least. Let it be told in a working room, with stuff on the table for her hands, at prices she can audit. She goes in as she was raised to go into other people's houses: as though she, and not the house, is the person who will be charged for any breakages.

“You sent for me about spring,” Prima says.

“I sent for you.” Antonia sets her hands on the linen, flat, the way she tested the courtyard wall for damp on a January morning when all the year's catastrophes were still correctly unscheduled. “Prima. I am going to tell you my life. Not because you asked, and not in the order of your questions, because your questions will be better afterwards. Interrupt anyway. It is a family failing.”

She tells it in the order of the evening. The house on the Quirinal. The mirror held at seventeen. A line of Greek about a woman at a loom, and a word said before it could be stopped. A young man's laugh. Teaching, and what houses call teaching when one party cannot leave them. A muster morning, a contract read upside down — *with her increase* — stairs in Baiae, a midwife's hennaed nails, money in a sleeve, a report: *a girl, not strong*. Standing up anyway, because the alternative arithmetic did not permit standing. Freedom in a will, loans to women, two tables, a house, a name assembled from good materials with no one's permission. And then a hanging with a border of small red flowers, and a needlewoman counting with her mouth open, and thread bought across the river by a Greek who kept his face like a book.

Prima listens the way she sews: entirely, with her whole posture given over to not dropping the work. At *the money stopped* her hands go still in her lap. At *not strong* she makes a sound that she converts, with a nineteen-year-old's ferocious governance of herself, into breath. When Antonia stops, the room contains linen, light through a high window, and two women at a table with twenty years laid on it like a length of cloth awaiting the shears.

It is Eirene who breaks the first silence the room constructs around itself, with soup, as punctuation. She sets two bowls down without asking anybody's cosmology and withdraws to the door that is not quite shut, where she will be within call and outside the record, an arrangement she has been perfecting since the Subura. The soup steams between them. Prima, out of habits stronger than history, apologises for eating in the house — her father's old foreign policy, arriving late to its own repeal — and then catches the apology in mid-air like dropped crockery and looks, for the first time since *not strong*, directly at the woman across the linen.

“He's a week buried,” she says. “His foreign policy can renegotiate.”

It is Antonia's cadence. It is Claudia's office. It is entirely Prima's, arrived at by needle and ladder in a fuller's house, and it goes through Antonia the way the laugh in the dining room changed key that night last month: lastingly. She picks up her own spoon, because policy in this house is modelled, never announced.

“Did they tell her?” Prima says. No one in the room needs the pronoun declined. “My mother. When she — when the money came and the arrangements. Did anyone tell her the whole invoice, or was she in your position, with the report?”

The question is better afterwards, as advertised, and it is the first one: not *who am I*, which is the Senate's version, but *was my other mother also lied to in the upright hand*. Antonia answers it at the level the household can bear, which is Diomedes' three columns, smoothed for telling: the steward who settled things at distance, the sums that arrived like weather through other people's hands, the absence of any paper in a laundress's name that a counting woman could have audited. “I believe she was told a child whose people could not be named in the neighbourhood. I believe she made a better story out of it than the one she was sold, the way she made everything she was given to keep. By the second winter, a tanner's widow says, your father would have taken you for nothing. By then the money was just the tax the arrangement paid to look respectable in a guild book.”

Prima eats a spoonful of soup with the air of a person conducting an interview with it. “She signed her leaves with stairs,” she says. “Under *Kept*, there's a space with a mark beside it. I found it the night before—” She does not say *the funeral*, because in this room the words have work to do and she is allocating them like thread. “I thought the count wasn't finished. Father said a completed book is a dead one.”

“Then we are agreed on the form of the record,” Antonia says quietly. “This conversation is not a completion. It is a column added where you can check the carry.” She does not reach for the girl across the soup. Doorways, offers, hands — the whole Antonian system of not holding: everything in her has been trained, at rates other people set, not to make an advance the other party must process as a cost. Instead she puts, into the work space between them, the one document she has allowed into the room: nothing. The empty table, the spring linen, two bowls. “Ask me the ones that are left, Prima. In any order. It is the last time this week anybody in Rome will tell you that in a room where you outrank the furniture.”

Prima looks, briefly, like laughing, and does not. It goes into her hands instead, which are finding — under the table, where confidences in her family have always been expressed — the exact place on her own wrist where a goddess of the river was said, for nineteen years of baths and needlework and guild inspectors' thumbs, to keep her hold so that a kept thing should not be lost.

“The wrist,” Prima says at last. She turns her left hand over. The pale mark, the half-closed eye. “My mother — my *mother* in Transtiberim — used to say it was where the goddess of the river kept her thumb on me so I wouldn't be lost. She said every kept thing has a thumbprint.” She looks up. “She knew, then. All my life she was telling me in that way. And Father. *Keeping especially.* That was — he said that to me, a month ago. I thought it was the fever being fond.”

“It was your father being accurate. It is the family's other failing.” Antonia's voice performs its duties. Her hands remain flat on the linen, holding the cloth of the world in place by main housekeeping. “I will tell you his name too, because you are owed the whole invoice. But understand what names are in this city: his will open no door for you that your needle hasn't, and it may close several your betrothal needs. That is not my shame speaking, or his — though we both keep accounts in that currency — it is the tax office. Everything is the tax office, in the end. I have paid it for twenty years to be let alone. I propose to go on paying it for you.”

“Is it—” Prima stops. She looks toward the courtyard, where Marcus's laugh, some evenings ago, arrived ahead of him like a herald. She does the arithmetic of faces. Dark brows. A mouth open on a count. A senator's son who shared, for an instant at a doorway, something she had filed under Rome manufacturing girls. “Oh,” Prima says, with a seamstress's bleak admiration for a well-hidden join. “The committee.”

“The committee.”

“And the boy who corrected — whom I corrected. In your dining room. About his sister.”

“Your brother,” Antonia says. “Half, by the law's arithmetic. Wholly, by the arithmetic your mother kept. When you told him not to talk about his sister that way — I want it in the record, since no clerk will put it there — you were the only person in Rome that evening speaking of the Cornelii as a family. Including the Cornelii.”

“Say his name, then,” Prima says, in the voice she uses for second columns. “Since I'm owed the whole invoice. I would like it with all the names in. It's my trade. I check hems by what they say about how a garment was treated when nobody was looking.”

“Gnaeus Cornelius Lentulus.” Antonia gives it the way she gives her own name at bankers': level, without interest charged on the saying. “A former consul — *suffect*, as the family minutes concede — senator, committee's late chairman, father of Marcus who is learning manners and of Cornelia who is shortly to be allied with, and, for a quarter now, my debtor in olives at a Greek's office. He was twenty-four and I was seventeen and his father's house was Rome, in the form Rome took for girls who held mirrors. He asked one question afterwards. He accepted one answer, because it was the one that let him keep his inheritance of himself. That is the whole of my willingness to draft his portion in this room. He may attend for his own cross-examination in a year that is not this girl's betrothal winter.”

Prima turns the name over in the silence of a woman inspecting foreign coin for milling. “It was on the posters. The registry. I bought a copy of the text off a scribe by the island bridge, half price for the one extract.” She says it with a seamstress's appreciation of a discount where she has found one. “Mother Livia says it's a strange son-in-law present from a father — a law where a woman's *no* exists in the books. My mother —” The word trips, is caught, is set down correctly by the person who owns all its uses now. “My mother in Transtiberim would have filed it under her leaves. *Kept: refusals.*”

“It will do more work there than his name will do you.” Antonia lays out the rest of the invoice without gilt: the acknowledgement that will not occur, in public, in her lifetime or Cornelia's marriage negotiations; the money that will, through guild and bequest, in forms a baker's mother can inspect like hems and find respectable; the conditions, which are Antonia's whole religion in this room, first service and second. “Nothing arrives from the Palatine with a name on it. Nothing is asked of you — not correspondence, not appearances in doorways, not a procession at anybody's pyre. If you want, in time, a letter, you will say so to me, and I will see it in his own hand, costed. If you want never, then never is what's registered, in every sense the Senate has lately been taught.”

“My wedding's in the guild book for after the mourning,” Prima says, “and the oven share is in my tools column, which Mother Livia has recited to two priests. Can your money arrive without making my marriage look bought? Because it *wasn't*.” There is a colour now in the first person that Antonia recognises out of a Subura mirror. “Titus held the ladder before there was any of this. I want that at the front of the book, Antonia —” The name, used back, does a small revolution in the room's government. She hears it too and does not flinch from the expenditure. “— I want my life that I had this morning to stay mine, with extra. Not replaced. *Arrived*, like the guild says. In the household I already keep.”

“Then keep it as household money,” Antonia says. “A widow's portion for the house, guild money for the pyre — that part is done, at their rates, not mine — and the bequest enlarged on the tools heading, as a dead Baiae lady's remembrance of your mother's stairs. Anybody inspecting will find a world in which women pay women at long distances and men write it down as weather. It has the advantage of being true in every particular the guild would check.” She opens her hands flat on the linen, palms up, an act of government over another province now: the display of emptiness. “And I am entered nowhere in it, Prima, except in the commission for spring hangings, by the piece, thread separate. That entry you can show your mother-in-law until the vintage.”

Prima is crying now, in the way of her trade and her river: without sound, achieved at the eyes, wiped with the back of a wrist that has work to do. “I don't know what to call you.”

“Antonia will do. I assembled it myself. It has held heavier.” She moves then — slowly, in the order of the evening; nothing true here requires a lock, but some things require asking — and takes the girl's left hand in hers and turns it, palm up, thumb resting, for one breath, beside the pale mark rather than on it. “Your mother in Transtiberim counted stairs so they wouldn't count her. She counted you, Prima. Every day of your life, at her own rates, and the count came right. I am not arriving to re-audit her. I am arriving, late — the family opens things late — to say that the report was false, the money was a crime filed as kindness, and the daughter lived and sews better than anyone in Rome. The rest is dowry and spring hangings, and will keep until it doesn't.”

Outside, the house performs its afternoon: the cook arguing with Alexandria by way of saffron, Barbatus telling the porter a story whose preamble lengthens with each telling, a dove on the tiles with its soft stupid music. Prima picks up, because her hands require it and so, she understands, does every woman in her blood whichever riverbank filed them, the spring linen, and begins the border.

“Red flowers again,” she says, when she can. “You'd think a fine house would vary it.”

“A fine house repeats what works,” Antonia says, “and calls it taste. Show me the stitch that doesn't remember itself. I never learned. Hairdressers' daughters; we only learned to hold the mirror for other people's repairs.”

The stitch that doesn't remember itself is taught, as all the house's real business is taught, by showing once and then watching at the correct distance. Prima takes a torn length from the mending basket — not the spring linen, *not the spring linen*, Eirene says from the door, arriving with more water and the exact air of a woman who has buried people from every century of her life for the privilege of saying so — and shows, with her mouth shut now and her whole attention in the quarter inch of damaged cloth, how a thread can be taken across a tear at an angle that lets the warp forgive it. Antonia follows on a second piece. Her hands, which can count coin in the dark and test plaster for treason, are beginners' hands here. It does not distress her. Nothing has distressed her in this room since the soup, which is how she knows, in Diomedes' hand behind her ribs, that she is paying for it all elsewhere and at compound rates.

“Looser,” Prima says.

“I am accused of many tightnesses in this city. You may record the first." The stitch goes in at the forgiving angle and holds. “Your mother taught you.”

“My mother taught me everything except this one, which the guild women did after, out of spite, they said, for the copyists at the Forum." Prima ties off, bites, inspects. “She said a seam is an argument for things being one thing that have been two. She said don't let the argument show its workings unless the workings are the point.” She looks up from the unremarkable scar in the cloth with a young woman's first, unafraid directness since the work-box threshold. “Is it the point today, Antonia? The workings?”

“Today the workings are yours,” Antonia says. She gives her cloth back across the table for inspection, customer to tradeswoman, and watches the customs officer's thumb pass over a knot of her own making with a fairness that goes through her like the Latin in a tax receipt. “What Rome sees will be a spring hanging. What you know about how it was held together is your property in the older sense: the kind no steward can hold against breakages.”

Eirene, from the door, makes the sound cash makes being recognised in a foreign country. She deposits the water and, on her way out, straightens Prima's mourning shawl at the shoulder with a hand that has straightened, in its time, a generation of girls in other people's houses before their great evenings. She does not say *there, love*, because she has never needed to say the words at their market price. Prima bears it. Later, walking home with Barbatus' lantern again, she will file the touch in the second column, under the general heading of women in this city who appear to have been keeping her accounts from various banks all her life without any of them, until today, presenting a bill.

What is said in the working hour is mostly of no account in the first book: the price of red dye since the Spanish boats, whether a banker can be trusted to itemise thread for an oven, how Daphne's return of the glass necklace has been entered in the street's books and what its current exchange rate is in Marcus. At the last of these Prima gives, quite suddenly and without the sound being recruited, the young, unregistered laugh of the girl at the ladder, and Antonia hears herself at seventeen over a scansion corrected, in a house that no longer stands, and does not put the cup down anywhere at all because there is no cup; there is a needle, going in and coming out. It is enough. Houses have been founded on less in this city and called it taste.

At the hour's end Prima rolls the work face inward and puts on her shawl at the angle her hair was done for respectability — and says, at the door that was never quite shut, the sentence that will go into no book anywhere except the fourth: “Next Thursday I'll come about the return of the border. If the count comes right before that, mistress— Antonia. I may have questions with names in them.”

“You may have questions at this house at your own prices.” Antonia opens the door herself, which in this building is a public act. “Barbatus will walk you to the crossing. Eat before next time, at whichever rates your street agrees with mine. It is a rule with information.”

“I had bread with my escort,” Prima says, with a seamstress's bleak satisfaction at a join well hidden. “Campaign rations. It is apparently a family system.”

That night, across the river, the house measures it by honey no longer required. The pot is scrubbed and put away in its place; the bench by the frames has been given to the drying; a place at the fire has gone the way of chairs in greater houses after deaths — respected, unoccupied, discussed by nobody at any price the room can afford. Mother Livia brings bread this time instead of soup, because the neighbourhood has moved by its calendars into the provisions phase of mourning, where people are fed in advance of needing anything. She looks at Prima's face with the baker's thumb of her whole personality resting, lightly, along the edge of it, and says: “The Caelian money come with instructions?”

“Spring hangings,” Prima says. “By the piece. Thread separate.”

“And other customs business.” It is not a question. Ministries, in the generation above Prima's betrothal, don't always require one. “I told you: you'd hear it indoors, sitting, from somebody who keeps a house. The vats haven't run this family since my own marriage and they won't start over a girl with honest hems.” She cuts the bread with a knife that has raised four sons and a business and sets the largest piece where Vibius's would have been, not out of piety — Mother Livia's pieties all have uses — but so that Titus, coming in with the night bake in his hair, will take it without ceremony and the place will be a place again by means of appetite, which is how most successions are managed among the poor.

Titus takes it. He looks at his betrothed over it with the expression of a man steadying a ladder in a new building. “You're counting something that isn't stitches,” he says.

“Questions with names in them. Due Thursday.” She puts her work-box down in the place it lives and does not open it. Inside it, under the needles, a dead woman's leaves continue not to be a book. “Titus. If a bequest came to this house through the guild — an old Baiae lady, my mother's mistress, dead convenient and well-disposed on paper — and it enlarged the tools column and put your family's oven partly in my name — would your mother inspect it as bought?”

Mother Livia, by the fire, makes a sound that in another woman might be the beginning of a career in prophecy. Titus, who has grown up between ovens and second columns, says with the caution of a man named in whichever document this is training to be: “I'd want to know the dead lady's name could be read aloud.”

“It can. That's the bit that took the hours.” Prima sits down in the household's new seating, at a place that after today exists because she is taking it. “Everything else is like the seam I showed you: you can feel it hold. The workings are mine. My *father's*,” she says — and the room goes through the small re-rigging it has been practising all winter around that word, sails adjusted, nobody overboard — “my father knew who held this house when the money stopped. We're not being *changed*, Titus. We're being kept, at better rates. It is apparently the family system on every bank there is.”

Later, when the bakery people have gone into the street's ordinary night and the frames drip their last into the yard, she opens the leaves and looks for a long time at the space under *Kept*, with its little unfinished mark like a stitch counted twice. She does not write her name in. Not yet. A completed book, her father said, out of her mother's whole unminuted philosophy, is a dead one. Instead she takes the spring linen from its roll and, in the border where no copyist would find it and no aedile's form has a column to tax it, works one flower wrong by a single petal — an extra petal, gone back into the wool and nearly invisible, the stitch that does remember itself, if you know the room and the rates at which she has been paid — and goes to bed in a world altered at every point and altered in no particular the tax office will be able to measure by Thursday.

They work an hour. It is not a reconciliation, there being nothing to reconcile except the past, which does not attend meetings. It is the thing itself, which Rome, for all its Latin, has only short words for: kin, at a table, with work between them so their hands could be honest while their faces caught up.
