# Before Frost

*Warsaw, 22–23 October 1943*

For forty-eight hours after walking out of Aleja Szucha Sylwia operated like a machine driven off flywheel momentum alone—resecting gangrenous bowel on Thursday morning, draining two pleural empyemas Thursday night, lecturing six pale underground medical girls down in the boiler coal-cellar at six a.m. Friday on femoral triangle tourniquet points without pausing once for breath—because stop moving for ten minutes and her brain started multiplying artillery densities east of Kremenchug.

At four o'clock on Friday afternoon the twenty-second of October, when thick yellow river-fog rolled up out of the Vistula valley smelling of coal-barge soot and rotting willow leaves, old Walenty laid afternoon post beside the ground-floor ward register.

Beneath two municipal laundry invoices lay an unsealed buff penny-postcard stamped at *Warszawa 1* main post office two hours earlier, addressed in cramped Polish shopkeeper script to *Wielmożna Pani Dr. med. S. Kotarska, Szpital Dzieciątka Jezus, Paw. III*:

*Szanowna Pani Doktor — Oprawki rogowe po naprawie zawiasu gotowe do odbioru przed likwidacją warsztatu w piątek dnia 22 b.m. wyłącznie między godz. 19 a 19.30. Zakład Optyczny, ul. Tamka 41, III p. prawa oficyna.*

*Horn-rimmed frames after hinge repair ready for collection prior to workshop liquidation Friday 22nd inst. strictly between 19:00 and 19:30 hours. Optical Workshop, ulica Tamka 41, third floor, right courtyard wing.*

Sylwia stood motionless under the dim corridor bulb holding six square inches of cheap buff cardboard until her thumb dented the margin.

Not Królewska Sixteen—*Wohnung 4* would be stripped to bare Judge Silberstein parquet under Kroll's binoculars tonight. **Tamka Forty-One**, plunging steep down the cobblestoned Powiśle escarpment below the Chopin Conservatory straight toward the black Vistula riverfront and the iron arches of the **Średnicowy Railway Bridge**, where eastbound military trains loaded at *Warszawa Gdańska* and *Wschodnia* rumbled night and day toward Brest-Litovsk and the Dnieper!

And *between 19:00 and 19:30 hours*—thirty minutes before eight-o'clock curfew locked every street in Warsaw shut until five-thirty Saturday morning! Nobody scheduled a seven-fifteen appointment forty minutes' walk from Lindleya expecting anyone to walk home before dawn.

At six-fifteen Sylwia signed night emergency coverage over to Doktor Jankowski (*"My sister's boy on Filtrowa has high acetone—if curfews hold me past eight I'll walk straight into morning scrub at six"*), changed into a clean cream linen blouse beneath her heavy dark-grey winter coat without jewels or scent save scrubbed hospital soap, tucked one small oiled-linen surgical packet inside her deep coat pocket, and walked east down Jerozolimskie and Nowy Świat into thickening river fog.

Down Tamka's steep wet granite cobbles streetlamps burned only as dim blue blackout slits fifty yards apart; fog muffled tram-bells uphill into cotton wool, while ahead through damp gloom boomed the hollow rhythmic iron thunder—*ka-klang, ka-klang, ka-klang*—of heavy flatcars rolling east across the Vistula viaduct toward Praga.

Passing beneath the towering brick railway-viaduct arch off Kruczkowskiego three minutes short of seven, Sylwia had to pause ten seconds at the kerb while two mounted *Feldgendarmerie* motorcycles cleared cross-traffic below. Directly thirty feet overhead along the elevated steel girders, an eastbound Wehrmacht *Marschbataillon* replacement transport ground temporarily to a halt behind red signals—steam hissing white and scalding down off coupling-pipes into yellow Powiśle fog, forty chained wooden boxcars smelling of wet straw, gun-oil, and horse-dung creaking bumper to bumper above the street.

Looking up involuntarily for two seconds through drifting river mist, Sylwia saw eight or ten bareheaded German boys no older than seventeen or eighteen—smooth-chinned Saxon and Westphalian conscripts drafted straight out of schoolrooms since Stalingrad—leaning forearms over the waist-high wooden ventilation bar of a boxcar directly above the streetlamp slit, staring silently down without singing a note into dark, hostile Warsaw cobbles before whistle-blasts shrieked forward, couplings crashed iron on iron, and forty cars of grey wool rolled rumbling east across black water toward the Dnieper cauldron.

Number Forty-One was a tall, soot-blackened eighteen-eighties tenement smelling of river damp, cabbage soup, and coal dust; its iron courtyard gate stood unlatched before eight. Sylwia slipped quietly past dark ground-floor workshops up the right rear wooden staircase—worn pine treads groaning faint beneath her shoes—past silent first and second landings until at the top of the third flight, beneath a slanted attic transom, a door bearing a peeled tin plate (*J. Grabowski — Introligatornia*, Bookbindery) stood half an inch ajar off its latch.

Dim yellow flame wavered upward, lighting slanted mansard rafters, a grime-frosted iron skylight trembling softly overhead to distant railway-bridge vibrations, two heavy grey army greatcoats spread layered over three dry straw-filled sacking palliasses in the corner, and **Ludwig himself**.

"I looked up divisional combat-group casualty tables from Kremenchug on Stabsarzt Vesper's own casualty circulars yesterday morning! Walk into Cracow Military Court voluntarily Wednesday morning forty-eight hours *ahead* of Himmler's warrant—demand immediate frontline combat transfer under Wehrmacht *Ehrengericht* jurisdiction in exchange for sealing Warsaw's municipal medical files—and Field Marshal Keitel's own red stamp slams the iron door shut in Reinhart Kroll's bleeding teeth!"

"Every single hour my heart has beaten past twelve-fifty-one a.m. on the ninth of February belonged to your two hands anyway, Sylwia," Ludwig said rawly, releasing her wrists to frame both sides of her fierce, pale face between warm palms, thumbs sweeping hard across her cheekbones. "Did you really think I survived your operating table just to sit alive behind ink-pots while somebody broke those hands in a basement? Besides—" one crooked, reckless flash of twenty-two-year-old Luisenstraße arrogance cut across his mouth inches above hers—"Soviet artillery hasn't met anybody yet who survived you twice."

Sylwia didn't answer with words—she dragged his head down by both wool lapels and bit his lower lip until he groaned deep in his chest, kicking the heavy winter coat off her shoulders onto bare pine boards while eight o'clock curfew bells tolled muffled through Vistula fog outside and locked all Warsaw shut around their attic for nine unbroken hours!

Cold air inside the unheated Powiśle garret stood barely five degrees above freezing—every exhaled breath rising faint white in tallow candlelight until two bodies wrapped naked between coarse wool army greatcoats turned three straw palliasses into a furnace.

Nothing hurried tonight: no waiting ambulance below stairs, no string quartet tuning Schubert twenty feet beyond velvet curtains, no red darkroom timer ticking toward evening rounds. Kneeling upright over him on grey wool blankets while an eastbound munitions train thundered slow across the iron Vistula bridge four hundred yards away—*ka-klang, ka-klang*, vibrating dry dust motes golden through tallow light and rattling the frosted skylight overhead—Sylwia unbuttoned his coarse army undershirt and stripped it over his cropped dark head, laying both bare palms flat across his warm, lean-muscled ribs and sternum.

At quarter to five on Saturday morning a cold greyness paler than milk seeped faintly down through frosted skylight glass; far off across black water a yard locomotive shrieked twice at *Warszawa Wschodnia*, coupling flatcars for tonight's twenty-three-hundred departure toward Brest and Kremenchug.

They dressed silently by touch and dim grey dawn-filter—buttoning linen, buckling cold leather, smoothing wool without switching on matches so zero light showed down Tamka's courtyard well before curfew lifted at half-past five.

When Ludwig buckled his heavy brown pistol-belt round his field-grey waist, he reached inside his inner breast pocket first and drew out a flat, hand-sewn chamois-leather pouch four inches square, pressing it warm and heavy into Sylwia's bare hands.

"Don't argue—listen thirty seconds," Ludwig said quietly, holding her hands closed around the chamois packet. "Inside are twenty British gold sovereigns from my father's Kiel sea-chest—gold buys peasant carts when Reichsmarks and złoty turn to wallpaper—plus two stamped International Red Cross medical-repatriation transit blanks routed via Vienna to **Sanatorium Hochzirl above Innsbruck**, where Friedrich von Halden's uncle directs Alpine tuberculosis surgery three hours from the Swiss wire. Wait—I know you won't leave Lindleya while Warsaw stands!" He cut across her instant breath before she spoke. "Keep it cemented behind loose brick in your attic flue for next summer. Because by July or August next year the Red Army will sit on the east bank of that river out there—and if Warsaw rises between two millstones while Hitler orders every brick leveled to mud, take Staś, take Wanda, put Halina's name on Blank Two, and get out west to Innsbruck."

"Only if you walk up Innsbruck's mountain road behind us," Sylwia said fiercely under her breath, slipping the flat chamois pouch deep inside her inner coat-lining pocket—and drawing out her own five-inch oiled-linen packet in trade.

Unwrapping the oiled cloth between them in dim grey skylight shadow gleamed zero ribbon, zero photograph, zero lock of hair that could betray identity to Russian or German searchers:
