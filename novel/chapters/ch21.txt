A Wedding in the Morning
Cornelia marries young Avidius on an auspicious morning in the early summer, with the whole expensive machinery of a Roman wedding making its noise around her: flutes, torches at noon, nuts thrown at futures, the bride's saffron veil worn — Servilia's last act of generalship — like a banner rather than a shroud. The groom winces twice, once at the contract's Latin and once, gloriously, at his own relief. Marcus behaves. This is minuted by no one and noticed by everyone: the heir of the Cornelii is sober, attentive, kind to old women; the baths will take a week to find a sinister explanation, the truth — that he has been reading his own letter for a month and learning the cost of language — being unsuited to plumbing.

Antonia receives an account of it from three directions, as she receives everything: the door-book (guests of both houses dine on the Caelian that week and talk, as men do, in the order of the courses rather than the questions), the Greek (the dowry's settlement has released, through agents, a significance of payment toward Campanian paper), and Eirene, who has a cousin in service at the Acilii and no shame anywhere in her system.

“She sang at her own wedding breakfast,” Eirene reports. “One song. The Greek one. Her mother, they say, looked at the ceiling as if consulting an upstairs neighbour, and let her. And the Avidius boy cried where the flutes could cover it, which the cousin says is the most patrician thing she's ever seen a man do.”

“Let her,” Antonia says, tasting a sauce. “That is Servilia's whole art, according to Diomedes, who reveres her like a rival firm: knowing which instructions to be disobeyed in.” She looks across the kitchen to where Prima, installing the summer hangings, is standing on a stool with pins in her mouth, engaged in not falling off the year’s whole metaphor. “Prima. When you marry your baker — guild, mourning, and your future mother-in-law's calendar permitting — you will not have flutes at noon. The neighbours would report the household to itself.”

“I shall have bread,” Prima says around the pins, “at all hours. It compounds better.”

“You will have,” Antonia says, with the air of a woman filing something between quarters, “whatever this house can send across a river that smells of respectability and keeps like money. Which is to say: also bread. I am rich, Prima, not imaginative. The imagination in the family has always been on the stairs side.”

The kitchen laughs — the whole kitchen, the pastry cook from Alexandria laughing in Greek at a Roman widow-inventing joke — and Prima, descending from the stool into the world's ordinary level, looks around at the big stove, the arguing empire of it, with an expression Antonia can read as easily as a tax receipt: *this, too, is a house where women keep things standing; we merely charge admission to the keeping, which in Rome is called every name except honest.*

Servilia, in the week after the wedding, does the thing no committee drafted: she calls on the Caelian. Not at dining hour. At the dead middle of the afternoon, veiled in the style women of her order wear to temples and assignations of state, with one slave who waits in the street with the chair and the family talent, bred over generations of service, for being furniture. Barbatus announces her by no name at all, in the doorway, in the voice of a man reporting weather of an imperial size: “A lady by appointment with nobody, mistress.”

They sit in the small dining room, sized — Antonia decides in mid-breath — to nobody's self-regard at all: two chairs, the house's best water, a bowl of the pastry cook's least argumentative cakes. Two women of forty in a city that has arranged their lives as a diptych: the one kept by the family like its standard in battle, the other sold by it like surplus baggage after one. Neither has ever been allowed, by the minuting habits of Rome, to be the other's contemporary. In a room, at a table, with cakes, the fact becomes sayable, the way things in this family do, by not being said.

“You gave her the clause,” Servilia says, without preamble, because preamble is for men who schedule. “My husband. For your women to cite over their contracts.”

“He gave the Senate a sentence. The house has adopted it at the usual rates.”

“My daughter sang Greek at her wedding.” Servilia selects a cake, inspects it like a minor treaty, eats it. “The whole city will decide I have lost authority. I find I have gained a daughter-in-law's family who pay musicians properly. It is the best exchange I have made since marrying the Cornelii, which the gods may file under their own irony.” She looks, then, directly — it is said in the Palatine house that Servilia's direct look has ended more this year campaigns than the prefect's office. “The girl across the river. Is she—” A search, through registers: *provided, settled,* the whole dowry of un-saying. Servilia, who has run out of quarter in which to file things, finishes the sentence like a woman, at last, in her own hand. “Is she happy in her arrangement, Antonia? Baking and hems?”

“She is stitching a life at her own prices,” Antonia says. “With a man who holds ladders. She knows what knowing is worth here and spends it in her own street. That is as close to happiness as my people file, Servilia.”

“Your people.” Servilia considers the cakes. “And mine. My son has written her a letter.”

“I advised keeping it with her tools. Evidence of what men cost, in their own handwriting.”

“It will be Greek and terrible.”

“I said so too.” Antonia, to her own extreme inconvenience, feels the sentence arrive that she has not minuted and cannot invoice. “He meant it well. In our families —” she stops. Re-files. “That is new. That can stay.”

Servilia rises. The visit has lasted exactly the length of a temple call; the slave in the street will report a temple call. At the door she looks back across the small dining room, the plain good furniture of it, the house beyond beginning its evening the way a ship begins a tide. “We were both bought, you know,” she says, conversationally, as one quartermaster to another. “The currencies differed. I believe that is what my husband's clause is about, draftsman though he was of everything else. Do not let the law students explain it to you. They will make it smaller.”

“Come again when the committee is forgotten,” Antonia says. “The cakes improve by quarter. It is our whole fiscal policy.”

“I do not make social calls I cannot describe to my household gods.” Servilia arranges her veil with hands expert and, for the first time in Antonia's observation of them at shrine-distance, not quite certain of the fold. “But I may send for the recipe. Rome will call that the beginning of the end, and for once the baths will be right at their usual rate of accuracy.”
