A Wedding in the Morning

Cornelia marries young Avidius on an auspicious morning in the early summer, with the whole expensive machinery of a Roman wedding making its noise around her: flutes, torches at noon, nuts thrown at futures, the bride's saffron veil worn — Servilia's last act of generalship — like a banner rather than a shroud. The groom winces twice, once at the contract's Latin and once, gloriously, at his own relief. Marcus behaves. This is minuted by no one and noticed by everyone: the heir of the Cornelii is sober, attentive, kind to old women; the baths will take a week to find a sinister explanation, the truth — that he has been reading his own letter for a month and learning the cost of language — being unsuited to plumbing.

The morning begins long before the auspicious hour, in rooms where women have been awake since the less sacred one. Cornelia’s dress hangs ready like a small state awaiting inauguration. Her hair must be parted and bound in the ancient style; her veil must lie over it; her girdle must be tied by hands that can pretend symbolism is primarily a matter of knots. The old nurse cries while doing competent work. Servilia, whose capacity for emotion in an emergency is usually expressed through seating plans, discards two arrangements of flowers and then restores the first with the air of a general conceding that the frontier has views.

Cornelia sits still under ministrations. “Do brides always feel like a room being prepared for other people’s furniture?” she asks.

“They are not encouraged to file reports,” her mother says.

“I am filing this one in the box.”

“At the front.”

The box—papers, copies, schedule of her music master’s fee, the new plain notebook she has begun to imitate from some influence her father affects not to identify—has already been sent ahead under her maid’s personal guard. It matters to Cornelia obscurely that the box will enter the Avidian house before she does. A person should have an advance party.

Downstairs Marcus is being made presentable by a household that has stopped expecting catastrophe from him and therefore finds his cooperation faintly suspicious. Onesimus removes an imaginary thread from his shoulder. “Master Marcus is early,” he observes.

“I am practising instalments.”

“Of punctuality, sir?”

“Of being a brother.” The word is out before he can amend its scope. Onesimus, whose diplomacy has survived the administration of three generations, treats the singular as perfectly adequate. “In this house, practice is commonly mistaken for a plot. Bear it.”

Lentulus receives the Avidian party in the atrium beneath the masks. There are formal words and hands and documents, torches even at the wrong time of day, because tradition is a landlord that expects rent regardless of light. Avidius the elder stands with a harbour-man’s intolerance for ceremony that cannot be weighed. Avidius the younger is pale in a way that flatters neither his tailor nor his self-image.

When Cornelia comes in, saffron-veiled, the younger man’s relief is so undisguised that the flute players pause in sympathy with it. It is possible to see at once that she is not entering a romance manufactured for ballads. She is entering a negotiation in which one party has already learned not to look surprised when she speaks. In Rome, this is a door. Many marriages have less furniture.

The contract Latin proceeds. Somebody’s child throws nuts too early and is removed by an aunt with battlefield efficiency. Marcus stands where a brother should, gives the expected answers with the expected face, and privately amends the official seating by thinking of another woman across a river who has corrected him in a dining room. He does not confuse the sisters. That would be cheap. He holds the two kinds of good fortune in separate hands: one veiled at noon among ancestors, the other, probably at this minute, naming a price for cloth with her mouth full of pins.

At the wedding breakfast Cornelia asks—not silently, not through a steward, not by making the request sound as if it had originated in furniture—whether she may sing one song before the formal music begins. It takes the room a second to understand that a bride has used a verb in the first person. Servilia looks at the ceiling. There is, on the ceiling, a fresco concerning agricultural abundance. She studies it as if it had been painted by the household gods for emergency consultation.

“One,” she says.

Cornelia sings the Greek song, not too fast, with the turn in the middle verse her master no longer corrects. Young Avidius cries when the flutes can cover it. Marcus does not; he has become too well educated this winter to waste the moment on an easily misfiled grimace. Lentulus hears his declared daughter take breath where she chooses and understands, among torch smoke and marriage nuts, that exits can be forms of arrival when they are not conducted by stewards.

Antonia receives an account of it from three directions, as she receives everything: the door-book (guests of both houses dine on the Caelian that week and talk, as men do, in the order of the courses rather than the questions), the Greek (the dowry's settlement has released, through agents, a significance of payment toward Campanian paper), and Eirene, who has a cousin in service at the Acilii and no shame anywhere in her system.

The door-book version arrives in fragments at dinner. A client of the Avidii, full of wedding food and maritime equity, reports that the Cornelian bride has a voice; a cousin of somebody says the Greek song was “a compliment to the shipping interest”; a man who has been wrong about every public event since the dedication announces that Servilia has lost control of her daughter. The house records the first two accounts under Amusements and leaves the third to natural decay.

The Greek’s version arrives on paper and is therefore more dangerous. Diomedes reads it in the office and gives Antonia the shape without the seals: dowry funds moving out of the Palatine, certain Campanian obligations eased, an Avidian settlement entering Cornelia’s name through channels a hostile accountant would find unusually well lit. There has been, in addition, a payment to a music master described so blamelessly that any future husband will have to be a philosopher to unpick it.

“Servilia,” Diomedes says, with professional awe, “has taught the harbour to put money where her daughter can reach it and call the arrangement respect for art.”

“Art is an excellent cloak. It has pockets.”

“Also, madam, a modest sum has passed from the Campanian paper toward our side of the current obligations. Nothing addressed. Nothing named. The olive affair remains in its columns like a well-trained citizen.”

Antonia looks at the figures without touching them. There was a time—seventeen, nineteen, Baiae, Naples—when money arrived in her vicinity only in sleeves, reports, threats or stories other people could deny by breakfast. Now sums travel across the city trailing clerks. It is not justice, which would require a different empire. It is at least weather that has learned to sign.

“Enter it as received.”

“It is entered.”

“Without a metaphor, Diomedes.”

“The third book has no metaphors before dinner.”

From the Acilii, by Eirene’s cousinly intelligence service, comes the version with knives in it: which aunt approved of the song and which required smelling salts; whether Marcus’s sobriety is seen as reform or as an ominous novelty; whether young Avidius looked at his bride like a bill of lading or like a person. Eirene delivers the report while cutting up a chicken for stock, a process that lends all social intelligence a useful sense of proportion.

“The boy looked as if he’d been handed weather and asked to govern it kindly,” she says. “Could be worse. I have seen bridegrooms look like tax collectors discovering arrears.”

“And Cornelia?”

“Like a singer who has read the contract.”

Antonia files that, privately, in the book with no formal headings. A girl who reads the contract. It is the difference between generations compressed into one domestic verb.

“Would Servilia approve of your cousin?”

“She approves of all information. She disapproves of being seen to receive it. There are management styles worse, love.”

The three accounts settle, as accounts do, into a picture none of them individually owned: a wedding accomplished, a troublesome young woman exported into an allied house with papers of her own, a son behaving at a rate that has destabilised bathhouse economics, a father discovering that being present in the present tense has administrative consequences.

“She sang at her own wedding breakfast,” Eirene reports. “One song. The Greek one. Her mother, they say, looked at the ceiling as if consulting an upstairs neighbour, and let her. And the Avidius boy cried where the flutes could cover it, which the cousin says is the most patrician thing she's ever seen a man do.”

“Let her,” Antonia says, tasting a sauce. “That is Servilia's whole art, according to Diomedes, who reveres her like a rival firm: knowing which instructions to be disobeyed in.” She looks across the kitchen to where Prima, installing the summer hangings, is standing on a stool with pins in her mouth, engaged in not falling off the year’s whole metaphor. “Prima. When you marry your baker — guild, mourning, and your future mother-in-law's calendar permitting — you will not have flutes at noon. The neighbours would report the household to itself.”

“I shall have bread,” Prima says around the pins, “at all hours. It compounds better.”

“You will have,” Antonia says, with the air of a woman filing something between quarters, “whatever this house can send across a river that smells of respectability and keeps like money. Which is to say: also bread. I am rich, Prima, not imaginative. The imagination in the family has always been on the stairs side.”

The kitchen laughs — the whole kitchen, the pastry cook from Alexandria laughing in Greek at a Roman widow-inventing joke — and Prima, descending from the stool into the world's ordinary level, looks around at the big stove, the arguing empire of it, with an expression Antonia can read as easily as a tax receipt: *this, too, is a house where women keep things standing; we merely charge admission to the keeping, which in Rome is called every name except honest.*

After the laughter settles back into saucepans, Prima climbs the stool again. The summer hangings are lighter than the spring set, less red in the border, intended for rooms where heat makes men imagine that their opinions have become thinner too. Barbatus stands beneath her without being asked and takes pins one at a time from a cushion. It is absurd and touching, a former gladiator inducted into the auxiliary forces of interior decoration.

“Did she wear flowers?” Prima asks suddenly.

The kitchen knows which *she*. Eirene says, “Saffron veil, flowers according to rank, a knot a ship’s chaplain could preach from.”

“And she chose the song?”

“Chose it, sang it, survived the ceiling.”

Prima works for a while. “I do not want to sing at mine.”

“Nobody has proposed it,” Antonia says.

“I know. I am registering objections before the committee develops.”

Daphne, arranging figs into a pattern no guest will appreciate before ruining, says, “You can have me at a discount.”

“The neighbours would think Titus had allied with Alexandria.”

“Your mother-in-law would inspect the alliance and find the thread separate.”

It draws a laugh from Prima that Antonia is learning to let pass without making it into ancestry. Not every sound must be filed as an inheritance. Some sounds are just a girl being funny before lunch.

Still, the wedding on the Palatine has thrown its shadow across the room in the way a large public event can remeasure smaller private ones. Prima’s own marriage is approaching in the guild calendar—after mourning, after contracts, after Mother Livia has inspected every object that may legally move between houses. There will be food at sane hours, a procession without a vehicular empire, witnesses who work with cloth and fire. Yet suddenly people in this kitchen are speaking to her as if Cornelia’s saffron veil has issued a challenge in couture.

“I do not want a bigger wedding,” Prima says, as if someone had accused her of ambition.

“Good,” says Eirene. “Big weddings are mostly other people eating your future in public.”

“I want the papers at my table.”

“They will be.” Antonia speaks before caution can make it legalistic. “Your papers, your witnesses, your mother-in-law’s calendar at the correct weight.”

“And no letters arriving in Greek.” She says it lightly, then sees the room take it too seriously. “That was a joke.”

Daphne says, “In this house jokes are minuted as early drafts.”

Prima descends and inspects the hanging from floor level. One corner falls wrong. She goes up the stool again, Barbatus shifting below her without touching her ankle or making a drama of gods. Her competence is a relief from symbolism, and Antonia stands among sauce steam and copper pans thinking how much of a life arrears of love expresses itself as: somebody competent, in the middle height of a room, being handed pins at the exact moment they are needed.

“Titus holds ladders,” Daphne says, meditatively, “and Barbatus hands pins. We are becoming a support organisation.”

“Revolution,” Eirene says, “at catering prices.”

Large events always generate small taxes. By the end of wedding week, three separate clients have asked Antonia whether Greek music is now to be the fashion at respectable houses, which is Rome’s way of discovering whether it should take offence before or after paying. Daphne’s price for teaching a banker’s niece one discreet song doubles without a meeting being held. The niece, it transpires, wants only to annoy a stepmother whose ears are so patrician they require a veil. Daphne takes the commission and declines the bonus for secrecy. “Secrecy is a surcharge that always comes back wearing somebody else’s face,” she says, and wonders aloud when she learned to talk like the house.

Antonia could tell her. It happens around the time a woman sees her name correctly entered under her own money and discovers that charm does not have to report to a committee.

That evening Diomedes presents the wedding in balance-sheet form: not the money—money has already had its audience—but the shifts of risk. Marcus is less likely now to be used as a casual weapon by men who want his father’s attention. Cornelia has an allied roof, her own schedule, and a bridegroom whose family understands cargo insurance. The Caelian house has neither gatecrashed its safety nor appeared at the wedding in any form more material than a border’s maker’s knife-work perhaps known to three people and God.

“Prima’s position?” Antonia asks.

“Guild: rising. Bakery: entered. Household: hers. Correspondents: under discipline.” Diomedes closes the tablet. “In the third book, madam, she is no longer represented as an omission.”

Antonia looks out at the courtyard where the overwatered roses are doing their best with ill-applied love. “What is she represented as?”

“A tradeswoman with columns.”

It is, from Diomedes, a coronation.

Across the river that evening, Prima gives her own account of the invisible wedding to the only audience licensed for the full emotional edition: nobody. She works on the tunic she has already altered twice, lets out a seam by a fraction and decides Titus is either expanding under bakery government or being measured each week by hope.

Mother Livia arrives with two loaves and a list. “The guild will do the tables,” she says. “Your uncle by guild has a courtyard if it rains. The baker women will not be put under the fullers in seating, whatever the registrar’s sense of poetry. A wedding is a town, girl. It needs streets.”

“What if I want a village?”

“Then half the town will stand outside acting offended. Better feed them indoors where we can supervise their gratitude.” She looks at the tunic. “You have let him out again.”

“He breathes at work.”

“So does an oven, and we don’t marry it.” But she folds the garment with the tenderness she reserves for money and children. “He is a good size in his sentences, my son. Says what he knows. Leaves room around what he doesn’t. That is rarer than a veil.”

Prima thinks of a saffron veil at noon and torches inexplicable to weather. She thinks of another young woman singing in Greek because she has chosen to, in a room of ancestors who once gave those choices to stewards. Their kinship still has no social word, but at certain hours it has a practical shape: both of them being prepared for lives negotiated by women who have read the schedules.

“I hope her husband pays the music master on time,” she says.

Mother Livia, who knows by pulse when a sentence has another family standing behind it, asks no names. “If he doesn’t, she can sing at him until his accounts improve. Music is a tool. Like a good hem. Not decorative in the right hands.”

Titus comes in with flour to the elbow and news that the courtyard can indeed be borrowed if the fullers lend two benches. He sees the list, the loaves, his tunic and Prima’s face in one efficient sweep. “Am I entering negotiations?”

“You are entering a town,” his mother says. “Mind the streets.”

He looks at Prima. “I can hold a ladder.”

“There may be putting up of garlands,” she says. “We are considering your qualifications.”

“At last, a wedding arranged around my talents.”

It goes into her new strip of accounts that night, not among liabilities: *Ladders, reliable. Bread, several kinds. Song, unregistered, elsewhere.*

On the seventh day after the wedding, a parcel arrives at the Caelian from the Avidian house. It is not addressed to Antonia. That would be an incident, and the harbour families pride themselves on cargo that does not leap from the manifest. It is addressed, through the Greek’s outer office, to the tradeswoman who made the summer hangings in which a customer of the Avidii has lately admired the finishing. There is an order for two lengths, measurements to follow, payment by the piece. The hand on the attached note is a Secretary’s.

Diomedes presents it in the linen room with the expression of a man laying a treaty before an allied power.

Prima reads it twice: once for the commission, once for the join. “Whose thought was this?”

“A shipping family has many clerks.”

“Diomedes.”

“The thought has no return address. That is its charm and its limitation.”

Antonia watches her decide. On a different Tuesday, in a worse world, this might be patronage disguised as lace. On this one, the measurements are real, the price is market rate with a tendency, and the customer has seen the work because the work was good enough to be noticed in rooms where women’s labour ordinarily becomes scenery.

“I’ll send an estimate,” Prima says. “Thread extra, because their rooms are larger and it will matter.”

“An exact reply.”

“If it is her idea—” She stops before the family vocabulary rushes in and upholsters the sentence. “If it is anyone’s idea who cannot sign it, the estimate will still be an answer.”

She writes it herself, in her firm new hand: materials, time, conditions for hanging, no work accepted on mourning days still owed to her father. At the bottom, not in the main invoice, she adds: *The red can be reduced for summer if the room takes strong light.*

It is a professional sentence. It is also, across a city of veils and boards, one young woman telling another—hypothetically, deniably, at market rate—that she understands rooms and what glare does to colour.

Antonia does not inform her that Cornelia’s newlywed home faces, according to the schedule Marcus described in an unguarded moment to Daphne, west into the exact weather described. There are messages women are allowed to send without mothers annotating the postage.

“Seal it,” she says.

Prima presses the seal with the side of her thimble, because she has no family signet and does not intend to acquire a personality at a metalworker’s. The mark comes out indistinct and entirely adequate: a circle, a pressure, an answer.

Servilia, in the week after the wedding, does the thing no committee drafted: she calls on the Caelian. Not at dining hour. At the dead middle of the afternoon, veiled in the style women of her order wear to temples and assignations of state, with one slave who waits in the street with the chair and the family talent, bred over generations of service, for being furniture. Barbatus announces her by no name at all, in the doorway, in the voice of a man reporting weather of an imperial size: “A lady by appointment with nobody, mistress.”

She has considered the visit since the contract signing, and considered is the correct Servilian verb: weighed, routed, stripped of accidental witnesses. She does not tell Lentulus. He would formulate a policy about it and then spend the afternoon obeying the policy in a room full of masks. She does not tell Marcus, whose new integrity is still young enough to be startled into literature. She tells Onesimus that she is paying a call connected with the pastry at the Acilii, which is true in the sense that Rome is full of pastry and false in every operational detail.

The chair goes not directly to the Caelian but by a route that takes in a shrine where she is known to leave offerings. She makes an offering. Management, to Servilia, includes not lying to gods she may later need at short notice. The slave who waits has belonged to her side of the marriage for twenty-six years and has reached a stage of discretion that borders on geology.

At the Caelian door Barbatus looks at the veiled lady, at the chair, at the hour, and reaches a series of conclusions without altering his posture. “House is between services,” he says.

“So am I.”

Something in the voice travels downward through his training like a familiar signal in an unfamiliar camp. He does not say a name. He opens the door by the six inches necessary for revelation to enter without procession, and announces her in a formula that should be inscribed over the public records of the empire.

Inside, the house is not as Servilia expected. She has known brothels by reputation, dinner clubs by marriage and this establishment principally as a problem of columns. In the dead afternoon it smells of linen and stock and floor oil. A girl is practising scales somewhere without theatrical intent. Furniture is better than it needs to be and plainer than rumour has supplied. Eirene passes through the courtyard with an armful of napkins, sees the visitor, and becomes, by instantaneous professional decision, entirely elsewhere.

“Your porter is well trained,” Servilia says, when Antonia has come down with a working woman’s lack of fanfare.

“He was trained by arenas. We merely provide doors.”

“My household could use his taxonomy. I am *a lady by appointment with nobody* at least twice a week.”

It is as close as either comes to an introduction. Servilia removes neither veil nor dignity at once. Antonia does not ask her to. She indicates the small dining room, where afternoon light falls in a shape later useful to painters and presently useful to women who need to see one another without being blinded by symbolism.

Before sitting, Servilia looks at the hangings: summer issue, red reduced, the border falling correctly now. “The needlewoman from across the river,” she says.

“Vibia Prima.”

“Yes.” The name is received, not repeated. It may be the first concession of the visit.

“Cakes,” Antonia says. “Water. The house can provide wine, but negotiations have been known to improve without it.”

“Water,” Servilia says. “My husband has developed an interest in it as governance. I should hate to be underinformed.”

They sit in the small dining room, sized — Antonia decides in mid-breath — to nobody's self-regard at all: two chairs, the house's best water, a bowl of the pastry cook's least argumentative cakes. Two women of forty in a city that has arranged their lives as a diptych: the one kept by the family like its standard in battle, the other sold by it like surplus baggage after one. Neither has ever been allowed, by the minuting habits of Rome, to be the other's contemporary. In a room, at a table, with cakes, the fact becomes sayable, the way things in this family do, by not being said.

“You gave her the clause,” Servilia says, without preamble, because preamble is for men who schedule. “My husband. For your women to cite over their contracts.”

“He gave the Senate a sentence. The house has adopted it at the usual rates.”

“My daughter sang Greek at her wedding.” Servilia selects a cake, inspects it like a minor treaty, eats it. “The whole city will decide I have lost authority. I find I have gained a daughter-in-law's family who pay musicians properly. It is the best exchange I have made since marrying the Cornelii, which the gods may file under their own irony.” She looks, then, directly — it is said in the Palatine house that Servilia's direct look has ended more this year campaigns than the prefect's office. “The girl across the river. Is she—” A search, through registers: *provided, settled,* the whole dowry of un-saying. Servilia, who has run out of quarter in which to file things, finishes the sentence like a woman, at last, in her own hand. “Is she happy in her arrangement, Antonia? Baking and hems?”

“She is stitching a life at her own prices,” Antonia says. “With a man who holds ladders. She knows what knowing is worth here and spends it in her own street. That is as close to happiness as my people file, Servilia.”

There is a pause then in which both women become aware of being forty—not old by the standards of their households, not young by the standards of their memories. Servilia’s hands are unadorned by afternoon policy except for one ring that could buy a boat if boats were sold by women who understood rings. Antonia’s carry a lifetime of work disguised as management: ink near one nail, a small burn from supervising a sauce at too close a range, no rings at all when she is about her own house.

“You did not come for an audit,” Antonia says.

“I have auditors. Half my family now qualifies.” Servilia takes water. “I came because a woman of my order is eventually required to look at the part of the map the roads were built around.”

“An expedition.”

“Do not let it go to your provincial importance.”

“Madam, I am an institution now. Rome writes to me by appointment.”

The smallest alteration occurs around Servilia’s mouth—not a smile, an adjustment to light. “You would have enjoyed certain rooms.”

“I was not invited to enjoy them.”

“No.” The admission is unadorned. Servilia does not arrange it into an apology, which would be an insult to both their intelligence. “Your exclusion was part of my marriage settlement. I did not draft that clause. I lived well under it.”

Antonia feels the sentence settle where so many others have failed to land. It does not open a compensation schedule. It does something more useful and less theatrical: it gives the room two adults in it.

“Would you have married him knowing everything?” she asks, because the afternoon has already broken several laws whose jurisdiction neither woman respects.

Servilia considers. She is not a woman who answers hypothetical questions from the surface. “I married the Cornelii. He was included as resident management. Had I known the child lived, I would have required a better settlement for the child—and a worse one for my father-in-law. Whether that is morality or pricing, I leave to priests and the Greek.”

“He would charge for the distinction.”

“All the best authorities do.”

From elsewhere in the building comes the muted beginning of dinner: crockery, water, Ammonius discovering a philosophical objection to mint. Servilia listens as to an allied province through a wall. “My daughter has your clause in her music schedule, by the way. Not the words. The structure. Fees in her name. Refusal preserved in accountancy. My husband believes it is his influence.”

“Men require a pedigree for ideas.”

“Hers comes from several houses.” Servilia looks towards the hanging with the corrected corner. “When I tell her—about the river—it will not be as a scandal. I shall tell her as family information that arrives in the wrong century, like most of our property.”

“That is your business.”

“Yes. I am informing you as a courtesy, not consulting you as an annex.”

Antonia nods. Courtesy, between these two, is the only form of diplomatic relations currently solvent. “Prima decides what she receives from your house. One letter at a time. No choir.”

“My son has been instructed that sincerity is not a ram.”

“Then there has been progress in the siege department.”

This time the adjustment around Servilia’s mouth does qualify. “We are,” she says, “a modern family. We communicate by withholding correctly.”

“Your people.” Servilia considers the cakes. “And mine. My son has written her a letter.”

“I advised keeping it with her tools. Evidence of what men cost, in their own handwriting.”

“It will be Greek and terrible.”

“I said so too.” Antonia, to her own extreme inconvenience, feels the sentence arrive that she has not minuted and cannot invoice. “He meant it well. In our families —” she stops. Re-files. “That is new. That can stay.”

“He meant it well,” Servilia repeats, testing the phrase as if it were a coin her family used to issue without adequate silver. “My husband meant nothing for thirty years, which gave his intentions an excellent credit rating. Marcus is overcorrecting. He will require supervision until meaning and manners converge.”

“Prima will supervise by not being impressed.”

“That is a form of instruction our tutors never mastered.” Servilia looks down at the table, an unusually honest direction for her. “There is another matter. The wedding settlement has released a small amount which my husband wanted routed—let us not soil the afternoon with the Greek’s exact architecture—to the girl’s household.”

“No.”

Antonia says it without heat, and consequently it fills the room. Servilia does not look startled. She looks, if anything, reassured that the frontier is defended by someone who knows its geography.

“I expected opposition.”

“Then your husband expected a refusal and wanted it delivered by a better diplomat.”

“He wanted to be told whether a further contribution would be accuracy or interference.”

“Prima has been provided under forms she can show her guild without lying at it. Any additional money now begins to look like your house purchasing a son’s correspondence rights, or a father’s relief at post-wedding prices.”

Servilia inclines her head. “Interference.”

“With excellent table manners.”

“Those are the expensive kind.” She sets down her cup in an exact circle of damp left by some earlier conference. “The amount will remain where it is, in case need ever drafts a better argument. I shall tell my husband a refusal is not an interruption.”

“He is learning the phrase at several frontiers.”

“He learned it here.” Servilia rises a fraction, then sits again, which for her constitutes an earthquake. “One more discourtesy. When Cornelia asks, one day, whether her father was—” Words present themselves in all registers: wicked, weak, young, Roman. She dismisses the parade. “What will your house say?”

Antonia considers the other woman across the cakes, neither ally nor defendant. “My house will say your husband wrote a clause and that women made it work. If she comes to me herself, she will have more than our party line. Until then she has enough houses speaking from behind curtains.”

Servilia nods, once. It has the weight of a treaty clause nobody will ever sign. “A woman should know which questions are hers in her own handwriting.”

“Did Prima say that?”

“No. I have some original sentences.”

“I am relieved. The family was becoming a quotation bureau.”

Servilia rises. The visit has lasted exactly the length of a temple call; the slave in the street will report a temple call. At the door she looks back across the small dining room, the plain good furniture of it, the house beyond beginning its evening the way a ship begins a tide. “We were both bought, you know,” she says, conversationally, as one quartermaster to another. “The currencies differed. I believe that is what my husband's clause is about, draftsman though he was of everything else. Do not let the law students explain it to you. They will make it smaller.”

“Come again when the committee is forgotten,” Antonia says. “The cakes improve by quarter. It is our whole fiscal policy.”

“I do not make social calls I cannot describe to my household gods.” Servilia arranges her veil with hands expert and, for the first time in Antonia's observation of them at shrine-distance, not quite certain of the fold. “But I may send for the recipe. Rome will call that the beginning of the end, and for once the baths will be right at their usual rate of accuracy.”
