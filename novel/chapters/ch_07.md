# One-Handed Razor

*Warsaw, 19–26 March 1943*

On the morning of St. Joseph's Day Sylwia pushed Room Fourteen's door open six minutes before seven without knocking, expecting to catch Mekelburg brewing illegal spirit-tea on the radiator, and stopped two paces inside the threshold.

The bed was empty and stripped neatly back to air the mattress ticking.

Over by the tall frosted window, standing barefoot on the cold scrubbed pine boards in grey Wehrmacht officer's riding-breeches with his grey elastic braces hanging unlooped down either thigh, Ludwig was shaving his own throat right-handed in a six-inch square of tarnished mirror hung from the window latch.

Mekelburg wasn't even in the room—gone down to the kitchen pump with the slop pail. Without calico turns binding his left arm across his ribs—because the bastard had taken scissors to her Velpeau sling himself sometime after dawn, leaving the cut linen heap folded tidily atop the washstand—his left forearm hung free at forty degrees flexion, fingers pale and slightly puffy from five weeks' immobilization, flexing slowly open and closed, open and closed in stubborn isometric counts while his right hand drew five inches of hollow-ground Solingen steel upward from Adam's apple to jaw-point with long, whistling scrapes through cold lye-soap lather.

Seen upright for the first time since lying stretched out bloody on canvas poles, he took far too much air out of the narrow white room. Six feet one even barefoot, waist lean inside high-cut wool breeches left unfastened at the top button so a flat line of dark blond hair disappeared down below his navel, back muscles shifting taut and asymmetrical across his shoulder blades every time he raised his right elbow: clean smooth rhomboid and latissimus on the right, angry puckered purple-red star pulling inward beneath a palm-sized square of adhesive gauze on the left.

In the six-inch mirror glass his winter-green eyes met hers without breaking the upward glide of the razor across his carotid pulse.

"Close the door, *Kotarska*," he said calmly around a corner of soap lather. "Draft freezes the soap before the blade gets there."

Sylwia kicked the oak door shut behind her heel and walked straight across the floorboards in cold fury, grabbing the cut calico heap off the washstand. "Who gave you permission to cut off a Velpeau immobilization sling before week six?"

"Immobilize a resected clavicle and traumatized brachial plexus past day thirty-eight," Ludwig said, rinsing lather and dark gold stubble off the Solingen blade in a tin mug of lukewarm water before wiping his chin dry on a rough hand-towel, "and fibrous adhesions glue the subdeltoid bursa solid to the coracoid process for life. Von Halden mobilized dog shoulders on day twenty-one; Lexer mobilized cavalrymen on day twenty-eight. Sit on your hands six weeks for Stabsarzt Vesper's textbook and you leave Lindleya with a frozen wing that won't reach a tunic pocket."

He turned round to face her fully, folding the straight razor shut into its black horn scales one-handed against his thigh.

Even furious, her surgeon's eye involuntarily registered the truth of it: standing upright, his left shoulder girdle sagged seven millimeters lower and slightly forward where the missing two centimeters of middle collarbone let pectoralis pull the acromion inward, yet his wrist drop wasn't there, his interossei hadn't wasted into claw-hollows between the knuckles, and blood flow down the arm was warm pink rather than cyanotic violet.

"Sit on the stool," she snapped, pointing at Mekelburg's wooden chair. "Before you faint onto cold pine and split your skull open for an encore."

"My hemoglobin was sixty-eight percent Sahli on Tuesday's ear-prick," he said mildly, sitting down anyway because standing twenty minutes *had* put a fine bead of sweat at his temple hairline, though he would cheerfully have chewed glass before admitting dizziness to her. "And your Jewish girl downstairs counts red cells cleaner than Vesper's orderlies."

Sylwia's fingers did not pause or twitch by half a millimeter as she peeled back the square of adhesive gauze over his left infraclavicular hollow. Zero change in breath, zero glance toward the floor, zero defensive denial—because denying a phrase tossed like a lead sinker into dark water only told the fisherman how deep the bottom sat.

"Panna Kwiatkowska's father kept the parish apothecary opposite the collegiate church in Łowicz for thirty years," Sylwia said in flat, bored German, dabbing warm boric solution around the contracting granulation rim with cotton forceps. "If she looks dark to northern eyes, blame Masovian Tatars—or buy thicker spectacles, *Herr Oberstleutnant*. Lift your left elbow forward ten degrees. Stop the instant you feel traction behind the collarbone."

Ludwig watched her face from eight inches below while obeying—lifting the pale left humerus five degrees, eight, twelve, until a sharp cord of scar-fascia pulled taut directly across the deep pectoral bridge over her vascular repair and his jaw tightened once.

Up close in clear March daylight, the wound had shrunk from a palm-wide crater down to an oval shallow trough no wider than two thumbs laid side by side: clean firm crimson granulations flush with surrounding skin, pearly blue-white epithelial islands already creeping inward three millimeters from every margin like thin ice forming round a pond. No rubber Carrel tubes remained; nothing drained now except scant clear amber serum.

Whether Docent Mirecki prayed for it or Marek Wolski cursed it, biology did not take bribes: inside fourteen days—by the end of the first week of April at the absolute outside—even the most shameless surgical liar in Europe wouldn't be able to tell Stabsarzt Vesper this man risked arterial blowout in a staff car.

"Don't worry about Panna Kwiatkowska's baptismal font on my account," Ludwig murmured directly beneath Sylwia's chin as she laid a thin Vaseline-gauze square over the pink epithelial rim so dry cotton wouldn't rip newborn skin cells off tomorrow. "Any bacteriologist who keeps eight thousand typhus lice alive under my floorboards keeps SS inspection clerks out of this wing better than barbed wire. Just tell her—if you happen to pass her on the stairs—that cheap Silesian peroxide turns hair brass-orange at the tips when steamed over agar pots, whereas chamomile decoction keeps ash-brown plausible."

Sylwia pressed two strips of zinc-oxide adhesive plaster down hard across his bare clavicle skin—harder than strictly necessary—before looking straight down into his cool, unblinking eyes.

"Why aren't you cutting chests on Luisenstraße, Ludwig?"

The question came out starker than she had meant to ask it—stripped suddenly of titles, war-rank, and fencing masks, dropped straight into the pale morning light between washbasin and unmade bed while her raw fingers still rested half an inch from the purple star her own knife had carved into his chest.

For three seconds he looked up at her without answering. A shaft of watery March sun broke briefly between courtyard clouds, striking through frosted glass across the sharp crescent scar beneath his right cheekbone and turning his grey-green eyes transparent as bottle glass.

"Because by May nineteen-thirty-three," Ludwig said quietly, "Geheimrat von Halden was opening faculty meetings with his right arm stuck out at forty-five degrees so thirty-year-old Dozenten in SA riding boots wouldn't audit his wife's grandmother from Frankfurt, and Kleist—remember fat Kleist from Table Seven who couldn't find the thoracic duct in a horse?—was appointed provisional leader of the National Socialist Lecturers' League for Anatomical Sciences."

Sylwia stared down at him. "So to escape brownshirts in lecture halls you put on a soldier's coat for the same regime?"

A cold, humorless edge cut across his mouth. "In nineteen-thirty-three the hundred-thousand-man Reichswehr still shot brownshirts who tried climbing barracks fences—until thirty-four taught generals how cheaply honor buys paint. And when Margarethe von Dewitz-Schoeler's lawyers informed me that spring that my late father's naval pension died with my university enrollment unless I signed her chemical-trust proxy papers, an army medical-reserve transfer into Bendlerstraße paid rent without kissing Kleist's boots." His gaze rose steady and pitiless to hers. "Once you're inside the machine, *Kotarska*, nobody asks at the door whether you climbed in to steer or merely to stay out of the mud. One day you wake up in Minsk or Warsaw wiping blood off oilcloth, and every exit door behind you bricked up five years ago."

No plea for sympathy softened a syllable of it—not *I hated it*, not *I was trapped*, not *forgive my uniform*, none of the rotten green-*Kennkarte* whining Stefan Bielski served with goose-neck turnip soup on Filtrowa. Just cold iron fact laid bare on scrubbed pine: *this is what I am now, and here we stand.*

Out in the corridor heavy boots clumped past the sentry bench—Mekelburg coming back from the kitchen carrying two steaming tin buckets.

Sylwia stepped back half a pace from the wooden chair before the latch clicked, tossing him a clean hospital linen shirt off the foot rail so he wouldn't sit half-naked when Bronka brought morning gruel.

"Put your arm back in a light triangular neck-sling past noon," she said briskly, voice all starch and cold water again. "Fifteen minutes passive pendulum swings morning and evening—zero weight-bearing, zero chin-ups on doorframes, or you'll pull clavicle callus crooked and press bone callus straight down onto your brachial plexus."

Ludwig caught the linen shirt right-handed in mid-air, burying one corner against his chest as he slid his stiff left arm cautiously down the sleeve without asking help.

"And you?" he asked casually as his head emerged through the collar slit, hair rumpled gold-ash across his forehead. "Nobody waiting in Poznań with a brass nameplate and four fat children after ten years?"

"Warsaw had plenty of shrapnel without importing husbands from Poznań," Sylwia said at the door, hand on the brass knob. "Button your cuff before Bronka comes in—she dislikes bony Prussians before breakfast."

* * *

Four days later, at noon on Tuesday the twenty-third of March, rain fell cold and vertical through the roofless brick shell behind the bombed Warsaw Philharmonic on ulica Jasna, dripping off twisted iron balcony girders into weeds browned by last autumn's frost.

Marek did not take off his wet hat, and he did not step close between the broken marble columns where prewar concertgoers used to smoke during Chopin intermissions. Even from six paces Sylwia saw the pinched grey line round his nostrils that meant an operation had gone sideways.

"What happened on Chłodna on the tenth?" she asked before he spoke.

"Nothing happened from Saski Square," Marek said flatly. "My two girls stood in the sour-rye queue opposite Number Twenty-Two from six-forty-five in the morning with a Leica hidden inside a hollowed cabbage loaf. At seven-ten—twenty minutes after shutter-opening—a dark-grey Adler Trumpf sedan with civilian Warsaw plates that match Aleja Szucha's motor-pool parked thirty meters west by Waliców corner. Two men in loden coats sat inside smoking until six p.m. Neither Kaminski's Łódź optician nor a single courier from *Abwehrstelle* came within two blocks all day."

Cold rain trickled off Sylwia's umbrella rim onto her shoe-cap.

Think fast, clean, without panic. On February twenty-eighth at two-fifteen a.m., burning at forty degrees point four, Ludwig had gasped: *Chłodna twenty-two—not to the optician before the tenth—if Kroll has the clock-hand, the whole net burns as far as Spiska.* On March eighth at four p.m., behind stacked stove grates off Krochmalna, Sylwia had given *Chłodna twenty-two* and *the tenth of March* to Marek Wolski—nobody else on God's earth—and thirty-nine hours later Reinhart Kroll's Gestapo surveillance car sat parked at Waliców waiting for someone to walk into the optician's shop!

Two possibilities existed in physical space, and both turned her blood to ice water.

Possibility One: Kroll *already* owned the optician's address from whatever courier died at the Ochota water-tower on February ninth—which meant Ludwig's delirium fear (*wenn Kroll den Uhrzeiger hat*) had already come true weeks ago, and Abwehr had abandoned the drop independently.

Possibility Two: someone sitting inside Warsaw District *Kontrwywiad*—either above Marek, beside Marek, or *Marek himself*—had read Marek's Tuesday evening report on *Chłodna 22* and passed it across town to Aleja Szucha before Wednesday dawn!

And thank Christ—thank ten years of Berlin paranoia carved into her bones—she had kept *der Uhrzeiger* and *Spiska* locked behind her own teeth on Krochmalna!

Now Marek was staring straight across wet brick rubble at her with the exact mirror-image suspicion hardening his narrow grey eyes.

"You told me Schoeler muttered that address in forty-degree delirium," Marek said quietly.

"Forty point four, before pus drainage," Sylwia said without moving an eyelash. "With his corporal snoring six feet away."

"Are you certain he was delirious, Sylwia?" Marek stepped two paces closer over broken plaster, voice dropping dry and lethal. "An *Abwehr* counter-espionage chief lies in your pavilion three weeks, recognizes an old Berlin rival walking round Polish wards, pretends forty-degree babble, and drops a burned address straight into your lap to see whether Polish underground watchers turn up opposite Number Twenty-Two inside forty-eight hours—while tipping Szucha himself, or watching Szucha's existing stakeout catch our girls with a camera in a bread loaf!"

"My girls weren't caught?" she asked sharply.

"No, because Anka spotted the Adler's tire tracks from Pawiak vans inside five minutes and bought two kilos of turnips instead of clicking a shutter." Marek's jaw worked once under wet skin. "Upstairs wants to know every single word Schoeler has spoken to you since nine February—Berlin, Warsaw, Latin, or Greek. And the day Vesper removes the military police guard from your corridor or signs his transfer paper back to Saski Square, *Kontrwywiad*'s liquidation squad takes his car at the corner of Koszykowa and Chałubińskiego."

Wind gusted rain sideways through a blown-out arched window overhead, spattering dark drops across Sylwia's wool sleeve. Sixty meters south of Lindleya gate—shoot up a Wehrmacht *Oberstleutnant*'s staff car thirty seconds after Pavilion Three discharged him, and before the brass shell casings stopped rolling in the gutter, Reinhart Kroll would line Docent Mirecki, Bronka, Walenty, Halina, and thirty random hostages from Ward B up against Pavilion Three's courtyard brick and machine-gun every last one of them!

"Shoot him inside five blocks of Lindleya," Sylwia said, voice so cold and level Marek stopped breathing mid-sentence, "and you murder seventy Polish patients, three underground surgeons, and Warsaw's clandestine typhus vaccine cellar in ten minutes flat. Tell *Upstairs* if they want dead Germans, shoot them on Królewska or Aleje Ujazdowskie—not across my hospital gate."

"Since when do surgeons draw street maps for execution squads?" Marek asked softly.

"Since engineers started parking bread-loaf cameras outside burned addresses and blaming thermometers when Aleja Szucha beats them to the kerb," she snapped back, turning on her heel before he could reach for her elbow. "Don't come to Koszykowa this week, Marek. Vesper's inspecting surgical stock inventories Thursday and Friday, and my stairs aren't clean."

* * *

On Friday afternoon the twenty-sixth of March, spring wind blew sudden warm dust up Marszałkowska for three hours, drying pavement puddles white with winter road-salt before gunshots tore the five o'clock sky open north toward the Old Town edge.

At Lindleya, three kilometers southwest of the Arsenal arch at Długa and Bielańska, nobody heard the first Sten bursts or grenade concussions directly—city tram rumbling drowned anything lighter than artillery past Saski Garden. What Pavilion Three heard at five-forty-eight was every *Polizei* klaxon, SS riot-truck horn, and street corner *szczekaczka* megaphone across downtown Warsaw erupting inside the same ninety seconds like kicked hornets.

Sylwia was in Theater Two closing the peritoneum on a fifty-year-old seamstress's perforated duodenal ulcer when Bronka pushed her masked head round the scrub-room doorframe, eyes hard as black buttons above white gauze.

"Big shootout up by Nalewki or Bielańska ten minutes ago," Bronka muttered low in Polish while Mirecki tied skin sutures beside Sylwia. "Bread boy who just slipped in the kitchen alley says boys with grenades blew open the big grey prison truck coming from Aleja Szucha to Pawiak—killed four or five Gestapo guards in the street and carried twenty prisoners off in lorries! Half the city north of Senatorska's crawling with armoured cars already, and checkpoints are dropping barriers across Marszałkowska!"

Mirecki's needle-holder clicked sharply against the toothed tissue forceps in his hand; one grey drop of sweat rolled straight off his eyebrow onto his gauze mask. Everybody in Warsaw knew what happened within twelve hours when four Gestapo men died on open asphalt: Pawiak cellars emptied into execution trucks, hospitals raided bed by bed from Wola to Praga for fresh bullet wounds, any unregistered bandage under fifty years old dragged out onto stretchers with bayonets.

"Finish the skin clips, panie docencie," Sylwia said without raising her voice, stripping blood-streaked rubber gloves off inside-out into the talc basin—one of the two precious pairs saved for open bellies—and listening to boots running already outside the east-wing guard post downstairs as *Abwehr* telephone bells shrilled behind the sentry desk. "If German patrols seal Chałubińskiego before seven, any boy hit near Bielańska who drove south before the cordons closed has nowhere left to go except Lindleya or private flats."

She was barely down the back service stairs to check the courtyard coal-alley gate when dusk thickened into indigo sleet-clouds and two dull iron taps—*two, pause, three*—sounded against the zinc-lined mortuary cart-door facing Nowogrodzka back lane, forty yards away from the main Lindleya gatehouse where Wehrmacht MPs smoked.

Not Walenty's knock. *Kedyw* medical emergency code, unused since November's railway sabotage arrests.

Sylwia didn't call Mirecki—Mirecki's grey lips would drop dead of coronary spasm on the spot—motioning only to Bronka heaving laundry sacks by the boiler chute. Together they slid the rusted mortuary bolt back three inches into darkness smelling of wet coke and panic.

Backed tight against the brick arch with headlights blacked out sat a municipal street-sweeping horse wagon heaped high with stinking gutter-sweepings and wet straw. Down under the straw, held quiet by a white-faced girl courier no older than nineteen whose camel coat was soaked black from waist to hem with somebody else's arterial and venous blood, lay two boys in mud-caked civilian windbreakers.

"Wola safe-flat's cut off by gendarmes on Towarowa," the girl gasped, teeth chattering so hard she bit her own tongue bloody. "Świętego Ducha's surrounded already—we came down Emilii Plater five minutes ahead of the motorcycles. One's femoral—tourniquet on since five-forty—the little one took a machine-pistol round low right ribs coming over a wall on Długa—"

"Don't bring them up the main ramp—there are two Wehrmacht MPs forty paces left behind the glass swing doors!" Sylwia hissed, climbing straight onto the wagon hub to shove two fingers inside the collar of the smaller boy under the wet straw.

Barely eighteen—freckled nose, soft chestnut hair matted with street filth, breathing shallow bubbling red foam at forty breaths a minute, right lower hemithorax dull to percussion and abdomen board-hard underneath: right costophrenic angle through-and-through, nicking lung edge and tearing liver dome! Leave him in a horse wagon thirty minutes more and he died in straw; carry him upstairs to Polish Ward B and the first midnight Gestapo bed-check murdered the entire hospital.

"Plaster cellar under Theater Two," Sylwia decided in three heartbeats, grabbing the boy's wet armpits herself while Bronka—built like an ox from Wola butcher's hooks—hoisted both his mud-caked boots together under one massive forearm. "Coal-chute steps! Girl—leave the second boy under straw ninety seconds till we clear the steps, then drive your horse straight out Koszykowa gate as if you dropped slops and burn that coat before eight!"

Down six narrow brick steps behind the anatomical ash-bins they dragged first the chest-shot freckled boy ("Leszek" painted on a torn cardboard tag inside his pocket? no, zero papers at all, pockets turned cleanly empty per Kedyw combat standing orders, just a lead St. Christopher medal on dirty string round his throat) and ninety seconds later the older boy ("Sowa," twenty-two, teeth locked over a knotted handkerchief, lucky clean through-and-through lateral thigh track missing the femoral artery by a finger's breadth).

The plaster-of-Paris storage cellar beneath Theater Two had no windows at all—twelve feet by nine, stacked ceiling-high with paper drums of gypsum powder, Thomas splints, and rusty Balkan traction frames, forty horizontal feet down the brick foundation corridor from Halina's locked typhus lab and directly *underneath* the floorboards of the ground-floor German annex! Overhead, muffled through twelve inches of oak joist and slag-fill, Sylwia could actually hear heavy German jackboots pacing briskly back and forth outside Room Fourteen as alarm dispatches crackled in from Saski Square.

For two hours by one hooded electric trouble-lamp hung low inside a zinc bucket so not a splinter of yellow light leaked under the iron door, Sylwia and Bronka worked on their knees across two trestle-planks laid over gypsum drums.

Sowa took ten minutes: release tourniquet, verify intact femoral pulse, irrigate track with rivanol, pack open, splint knee straight, gag him gently with dry gauze and twenty milligrams of morphine so he couldn't groan through ceiling joists.

Leszek took ninety minutes of quiet hell under trickled local procaine along the ninth rib bed and light open-drop chloroform dripped by Bronka ten drops at a time onto folded gauze while Sylwia resected four centimeters of ninth costal cartilage, evacuated six hundred milliliters of dark liquid liver-blood out of the subphrenic space with soup ladles boiled in the sterilizer upstairs, packed a thick Viuchnevsky bolster of hemostatic catgut and oxidized gauze tight into the bleeding four-centimeter stellate rent on the dome of his right hepatic lobe—liver parenchyme tore like wet cheese if you pulled silk unbolstered—closed the diaphragm defect so blood wouldn't drown his right lung, and brought a rubber Pezzer drain out between his ribs into a water-seal pickle jar on the floor.

By nine-thirty the boy was breathing twenty-six times a minute instead of forty, pulse weak at one-eighteen but holding, pale freckled cheek resting cold against rough canvas while half a liter of Type O universal blood—drawn ten minutes earlier straight out of Bronka's own left median cubital vein into sodium-citrate bottle without cross-match fuss because Bronka bled like a prize heifer twice a month for dying kids and ate horse-liver afterward to make it back—dripped dark crimson down rubber tubing into his ankle saphenous cut-down.

Sylwia knelt back on her heels on the gypsum-dusted bricks, wiping sweat off her forehead with a blood-stiffened forearm. Every muscle across her lower spine burned; she hadn't eaten since breakfast gruel at seven.

Upstairs, two floors seemed suddenly to vibrate at once as heavy motor engines—not one ambulance, three or four heavy six-wheeled lorries—growled to a halt along both Lindleya and Nowogrodzka curbstones outside.

Above their heads in the ground-floor vestibule, iron-shod boots slammed across tiles—ten, twelve pairs—followed not by Stabsarzt Vesper's bluster, by a polite, quiet Saxon voice Sylwia knew in her marrow even muffled through mortar.

Bronka froze with her thumb still pressed over the citrate cotton wad in her own elbow crease, gold eyetooth glinting dull in the bucket-shaded light.

"Matko Przenajświętsza," the matron breathed without sound. "White gloves. And dogs."
