# Six

## The Thaw

The circular came down from the Department of Propaganda on the fourth of November and it was three pages long and Hanna had typed it four times because the first three had been corrected in three different hands.

Zofia read it standing up. It concerned the conduct of religious instruction in the schools of the voivodeship, and it said, in the language that such documents used, that in view of the forthcoming review of the concordat-adjacent arrangements, no priest was to be admitted to a school building for the purposes of instruction without the written consent of the presidium of the appropriate people's council, and that the consent was to be issued on a form, and that the form was attached.

"Who drafted this?"

"The department. It came with a note from Comrade Rykowski."

"Rykowski is a secretary of the Warsaw committee. He does not draft circulars about catechism."

"He signed the note," Hanna said. "The circular is from the department."

Zofia turned to the last page. The form had eleven questions, and question nine asked the applicant to state whether he had ever been a member of an organization hostile to the People's Republic, and question ten asked him to list the names of persons who could vouch for his political attitude.

"Who is the addressee?"

"All voivodeship offices. And the curias, through the voivodeship offices."

"So it will be in the papers by Sunday."

"It will be in the curias by Thursday," Hanna said, "and in the papers by Sunday, yes."

Zofia looked at her. Hanna was standing with her hands folded, in the grey wool dress she wore on Mondays and Thursdays, with her hair in a roll, and her face was perfectly composed, and Zofia, who had learned to read her secretary the way she read a document, saw that she was angry.

"You think it's stupid."

"I think it is a mistake," Hanna said.

"Say why."

Hanna's hands did not move. "Because it will not stop anything. The priests will hold the lessons in the sacristy, and the children will go, and the parents will send them, and it will be in the papers, and everyone will say the state is persecuting the Church, and the ones who write the form will be the ones who are blamed." She paused. "And because question ten is a list of names. People will write down their neighbours."

"Yes," Zofia said. "They will."

"I am to make twenty copies."

"Make twenty copies, and add a covering note from this office saying that the form is to be administered by the voivodeship offices and not by this office, and that no copy is to be retained in a parish file." Zofia handed the circular back. "If we must do it, we will do it in a way that does not look like an invitation to denounce someone."

Hanna took it. At the door she said, "Pani Dyrektor," and hesitated.

"Yes?"

"My brother is at the seminary at Włocławek."

"I know."

"He will have to fill in a form like this in four years, if he is ordained."

Zofia looked at her for a moment. "Then go home tonight and tell him to learn to write very short answers," she said, "and to keep his own list in his head, and never to write it down. And Hanna. Do not tell anyone in this building that your brother is at Włocławek."

Hanna's face did not change, but something behind it did, and she said, "No, Pani Dyrektor," and went out.

Brzozowski collapsed on the tenth, in his office, at about eleven in the morning, and it was Hanna who found him, and it was Zofia who rode with him in the ambulance, holding his hand, which was the first time she had touched him in four years.

He was operated on that night at the clinic on Skaryszewska and he came out of it alive and full of tubes and very angry, and on the third day he sent for her.

"You are acting director," he said. His voice had gone to a whisper with a whistle in it. "The minister will sign the paper tomorrow. It is in my drawer, the second one, with the red seal, and you will not open it until Monday because I want to be dead before it is read."

"You are not going to die."

"I am going to die in about five weeks, and I have made my peace with it in the sense that I am furious and there is nothing I can do." He motioned at the chair. "Sit down. Have they come to you yet?"

"Wrona came on Thursday."

"What did he want?"

"He wants me to obtain a signature. He has a text. It is a cooperation declaration, in the ordinary form, and he has left the date blank so that it can be filled in by the candidate's own hand."

Brzozowski closed his eyes. "And what did you say?"

"I said that I would consider it."

"Good. Consider it slowly." He opened his eyes. "Zofia. I am going to tell you something and then I am going to be sick, which is what I do now, and you will please not help me, because the nurse is a Communist and enjoys it." He shifted in the bed. "In 1949 I was on a commission. Not the one that examined you. Another one. It concerned a man in the ministry, a lawyer, a very good lawyer, my friend since 1932, and the question was whether he had concealed his membership of the PPS before the war, which he had not concealed, because everyone knew. The commission asked me to write a note. I wrote a note. I wrote that he was politically unreliable and that his attitude to the Party was formal. It was a sentence of eleven words and it cost him his career and probably his life, because he was arrested in 1951 and I do not know where he died." He stopped and breathed for a moment. "I did it because I was frightened. Not of prison. Of being a man who was not asked again. That is the real fear in this building, Zofia. Not the cell. The corridor with the doors that do not open for you."

The nurse came in with a tray and Brzozowski waved her away.

"So," he said. "You will now ask me why I am telling you this."

"To tell me not to do it."

"No," said Brzozowski. "To tell you that you will do it, or you will not, and that whichever you do you will be a person who did it, and that this is not a matter of a clean conscience at the end. There is no clean conscience. There are only the things you can live inside." He closed his eyes. "I have lived inside mine for nine years and it has been a small house. I would not recommend it. But I have lived."

On the eleventh there was a meeting at the Central Committee, in a room on the fourth floor with a long table and green baize and a portrait of Gomułka at one end, and eleven men and two women, and Zofia was one of the two.

The secretary who chaired it was called Tkaczuk, and he was fifty-one and had been a plumber in Łódź and had spent the war in the Soviet Union and had come back in 1945 in a uniform, and he was not stupid. He had a way of listening with his head down and his hands flat on the table that made everyone speak more carefully.

"The Lublin terna," he said. "The Office has had it since October. Comrade Krawiec, you have the file."

"I have the file."

"Summarize."

She summarized. She gave them the three names, and the Bishop's preference, and the age and the record of the candidate, and the state of the opinion, and she did it in four minutes, without adjectives, in the way that Brzozowski had taught her.

"And the fourth department?"

"Colonel Wrona has submitted a proposal. It is that the candidate be accepted, subject to a declaration of cooperation."

There was a movement along the table, not much.

"Which he will not sign," said Tkaczuk.

"He has refused, in conversation, to sign what was proposed to him. He has offered other assurances."

"Which are worth nothing." Tkaczuk raised his head. "Comrade. Let me tell you what the situation is, because the Office has a habit of seeing this as a legal question and it is not a legal question. In 1956 we made an agreement with the Church. We made it because it was necessary and because the country was in a state that required it, and I do not repudiate it. But the agreement was not a conversion. The Church has used the last two years as a breathing space, and it has used it well, and now we are in a position where a bishop of thirty-four with a book and a camp record is being put forward as the future of Polish Catholicism, and we are being asked to consent to it, and the question is whether we are governing this country or whether we are managing its applause." He looked down the table. "The comrades of the fourth department take the view that the candidate can be neutralized. I have some sympathy with that. Comrade Krawiec, do you?"

"I think the candidate can be refused," Zofia said, "and that if he is refused, the Church will have a martyr who costs nothing to maintain and who will be preaching in twenty years, and the Primate will write a pastoral letter, and it will be read in every church in Poland in Lent, and it will say that the state obstructs the free election of bishops. I think that is a worse outcome than a bishop."

"And if he is accepted and does not sign?"

"Then we have a bishop of thirty-four who owes us nothing and who knows that he beat us." She paused. "But he has given his word on two matters, and I believe he will keep it, because it is his only achievement."

Tkaczuk looked at her for a long moment. Then he said, "The fourth department's view is that his word can be obtained. Not on two matters. On the paper."

"Colonel Wrona's view."

"Colonel Wrona's view, which has the confidence of the appropriate comrades." Tkaczuk's voice was perfectly even. "You will work with him. The opinion is due on the fifteenth. I would like the matter resolved before the episcopate meets, and I would like the resolution to be one that we do not have to explain in six months." He closed his folder. "One more thing, comrade. You are acting director of the Office now. That is a considerable responsibility. There are comrades who have said that the Office has been too soft for too long, and that its leadership has confused the art of the possible with the habit of the comfortable." He looked up. "I do not agree with them. But they exist. I mention it because you should know it."

"I know it," Zofia said.

Wrona came on the fourteenth with a folder and a box of chocolates, which was a thing he did, and put the chocolates on the desk and said they were for Hanna.

"You are looking thin, comrade director. Acting director. My congratulations, by the way, though I understand the circumstances are not happy."

"My director is dying."

"All the more reason." He sat down. "I have been thinking about our conversation. I have decided to be frank with you, which I find is usually a mistake, but I am forty-six and my stomach is worse and I have begun to be in a hurry." He put the folder on the desk. "This is your material. Not the German. The other one."

She opened it. There were six pages, typed, and on the fourth page there was a paragraph in her own handwriting, and a signature at the bottom of the page, and the date, 11 October 1949.

She had known it existed. She had not seen it since the day she wrote it.

"Read the sentence," Wrona said.

She read it. It was in her hand and it was perfectly legible. *Wierzbicki is gifted and ought not to be permitted to teach youth; he is the kind of man who will be believed.*

"You were twenty-two," Wrona said. "You were a good girl. You were asked a question and you answered it honestly, which is more than most people did, and it went into a file, as things do." He shifted his weight. "I am not here to blackmail you, comrade. I want to be clear about that, because I have a reputation and it is deserved but it is not always deserved. If I wanted to destroy you I would not need this. I would need three other things, and I have them, and I have not used them and I do not intend to. This is not a threat. This is a briefing."

"Then brief me."

"The man Sroka," Wrona said. "Your mentor. I have his file. In 1953 he was shot, and in 1951 you gave evidence at his hearing, and your evidence was — how shall I put it — correct. It was correct and it was not generous, and it was the evidence of a person who wished to survive." He held up a hand. "I am not criticizing. I have given evidence myself, in similar circumstances, and mine was worse. But here is the thing, comrade. Sroka was rehabilitated in April. Did you know? The Military Court reviewed the case and quashed the sentence, and there will be a paragraph in *Trybuna Ludu* in a week or two, and there are people who will want to know who gave evidence against him. Some of those people are in this building. Some of them are in the Central Committee. You are acting director of an office that is about to make a decision about a bishop, and there is a document in a file with your name on it, and the document is in the hand of a twenty-two-year-old girl who was frightened, and the girl is now a woman who is being asked to be the state's conscience about the Church." He sat back. "That is the situation. I have not created it. I am describing it."

She put the page down.

"What do you want, colonel?"

"I want the signature," Wrona said. "I have wanted it since October. But I will tell you honestly that I no longer think it is the most important thing in this room." He leaned forward. "Listen to me. If you write an opinion that approves this man without conditions, and he is consecrated in December, and in February he gives an interview to the Italian press, or he writes a pastoral letter about the schools, or he stands up in the cathedral and says the state is godless — and he will, comrade, because they always do, they cannot help it — then the question in the Central Committee will not be 'why did the bishop do it.' The question will be 'who approved him.' And the answer will be a woman with a file and a dead mentor and a village in her past, and there will be a commission, and the commission will read the German deposition, and the men around this table will discover, all at once and with great regret, that the Office has been run for four years by a person who has a personal history with the candidate." He spread his hands. "I am offering you a way to be a colleague. That is all. There is a paper. It says: I have explained the situation to the candidate and he has agreed to cooperate. It does not require his signature. It requires yours."

She looked at him.

"You would forge it," she said. "You would accept my note and put his signature on the declaration underneath."

"I would accept your note," Wrona said, "and the rest is administration."

"No."

"No?"

"No." She closed the folder. "I will not write that note. If he signs, he signs in his own hand, in front of me, and the text is the text he has seen, and it goes into the file as it was signed, with the date and the place and the names of the witnesses. If he does not sign, then he does not sign, and the opinion is mine and it will say what I think."

Wrona sat quite still. Then he smiled, and it was the first smile she had seen from him that seemed to cost him something.

"Ah," he said. "There she is."

"Colonel—"

"I have been waiting four weeks for that sentence." He stood up, and took his hat, and looked down at her. "I will make you a proposal, comrade director, and it is a real one. It is the fourteenth today. The opinion is due on the fifteenth. I will ask the department to extend the deadline by ten days, on the ground that the candidate is to be interviewed again. In those ten days you will go to him, and you will put the matter to him yourself — not as a demand, as a question. You will ask him whether he will sign. And then you will tell me what he says, and I will accept it, and I will not ask you to write any note." He put on his hat. "That is the offer. It is the only one I have. If you refuse it, I will go to the minister on Monday with the German deposition and the Sroka hearing, and I will not enjoy it, and neither will you."

"Why me?" she said. "Why does it have to be me?"

Wrona was at the door. He turned.

"Because he will say yes to you," he said. "That is the whole of it. He would say no to me, and he would say no to Tkaczuk, and he would say no to the Cardinal himself. But he will say yes to you, because you are the only person in this Republic who has ever been loved by him, and a man will do for that what he will not do for God." He opened the door. "You have ten days. Use them badly or well."

She sat for a long time after he had gone.

Then she got up and went to the window and stood there with her arms folded, looking at the Palace of Culture, which was grey and enormous against a grey sky, and she thought about 1946, when she was nineteen and believed that she was building something, and about the particular day in June of that year when she had stood in the market square at Krasnystaw with a ballot box in her hands.

They had given her a school at Krasnystaw in the autumn of 1944 and she had taught thirty-one children in a room with a stove and no glass in one window, and she had been happy in a way that she had not expected ever to be happy again. The children were wild and hungry and quick, and half of them had lost someone, and they learned to read in eight months, and one of them, a boy called Adam, had copied out the whole of the alphabet on the back of a German poster, and she had kept it for years.

In 1945 Sroka had asked her to join the Union of Polish Youth and she had joined, and in 1947 the Party, and she had done it for reasons that had seemed to her at the time to be entirely practical. The village had been burned. The old world had been a world in which a girl could be taken out of a meadow and put in a room with a wasp and a man with a bottle of beer, and it had ended, and the people who had ended it were the ones with the lorries. She had seen the Red Army come through, and she had seen the Polish Committee of National Liberation hand out land to peasants who had never owned any, including eleven families in her own district, and she had seen her father, who had come back from Majdanek in August 1944 weighing forty-nine kilograms, stand at the window of the cooperative office and say, "They are giving away the mills," in a voice of complete bewilderment.

He had not been wrong. In the referendum of June 1946, she had stood in the square at Krasnystaw with a ballot box, and the question had been whether the Senate should be abolished, and the answer had been yes, and everyone in the district knew that the answer had been helped. In January 1947 she had been an usher at a polling station, and she had watched men in militia uniform go into the room where the count was made, and she had seen the numbers change on the board, and she had said nothing. She had been nineteen. She had told herself that the other side would have done the same, and that the peasants had voted for the peasant party anyway, and that the election had produced a government which had given the village a school and a clinic and a road.

Her father lived until 1953. He never joined the cooperative and he never spoke at a meeting again, and after 1951 he kept a pig in the orchard in defiance of the delivery quota and paid the fine each year out of the money for shoeing horses. When she came home he was civil and silent, and he asked her once, in 1950, whether she had seen the organist's boy, and she said that he had been taken to Russia and had not come back, and her father nodded and looked at the fire and said, "He was a good boy," and that was the last time they spoke about anything that mattered. He died in the spring of 1953, in the village, of a stroke, in the eleven days between the arrest of Wiktor Sroka's wife and the opening of the Party school she was due to attend in Warsaw, and she was at a conference in Katowice when the telegram came, and she did not go to the funeral, and she has never been able to tell anyone, including herself, whether that was because the conference could not be left or because she could not face the village.

In 1947 she had sat for two days in a courtroom in Lublin, at the back, at the trial of eleven men from the underground who had gone on fighting after the war because nobody had told them to stop. She had listened to the prosecutor call them fascist bandits and hirelings of London, and she had believed perhaps a third of it, and had said nothing about the other two thirds, and two of the eleven were hanged in the following spring. She had been twenty. She had gone home afterwards and eaten supper.

And in 1949, when the Party was being cleaned, she had been asked about her past, and she had sat in a room in Lublin with three men and a stenographer, and one of them had asked her, "And this Wierzbicki. The organist's son. What was he?"

She had said: "He was in the Home Army."

And then, after a moment, because the silence in the room had a shape, and because she was twenty-two, and because the war had been over for four years and she had not seen him for five and he was somewhere in Russia and might be dead, she had said the other thing. She had said it in the belief, which she could still remember with perfect clarity, that she was telling the truth in a way that would protect him: that he was gifted, that he was not a thug, that he was exactly the kind of priest who would be listened to, and that for that reason he should not be given young people. She had thought she was describing a danger accurately. She had thought that accuracy was a virtue.

The stenographer had typed it. The chairman had said, "Thank you, comrade."

She had walked out into the street in Lublin and bought a bun and eaten it, and it was a Thursday, and the sun was out, and she had not thought about it again for two years.
