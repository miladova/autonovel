Registrations

The inspector arrives at the hour when respectable houses are still receiving clients and houses like hers are changing the flowers. He is a man in his thirties with a clerk and a measuring rod and the careful blankness of someone who has been told, by a person who outranks him, that this inspection matters, and told, by a person who pays him, that it would be a shame if it mattered too much. Antonia receives him in the small dining room, not the great one. She has learned to size a room to a man's self-regard: too large and he must fill it, too small and he must escape it.

“You keep a lodging house,” he says, “with dining.”

“I keep a house,” she says, “where men of business may dine in private and hear music. The registrations are there.” Diomedes lays them out like a hand of cards he expects to win with: names, fees, the tax paid quarterly and stamped, the licence for the sale of wine, the lease of the premises from an owner in Ostia who has never, in eleven years, inquired what music is playing so long as the rent arrives before the Kalends.

The inspector runs a finger down the column of women's names. Each name is a legal condition before it is a person: freedwoman, freeborn, citizen, alien. Each has a fee against it. Rome taxes what it condemns with an efficiency it brings to few of its virtues, and Antonia pays the tax on the day, in full, in coin that rings. She has always paid. A woman in her position who cheats the treasury is a story; a woman who overpays it slightly, through rounding, is a footnote, and footnotes survive.

“And this one?” The finger stops. “Daphne. No family name.”

“Daphne, freedwoman of Gaius Antonius, manumitted three years ago before witnesses whose names Diomedes can supply. She sings. She also plays the lyre indifferently, which we do not register, out of kindness.”

The clerk writes. The rod measures a doorway and finds it a doorway. In the corridor, one of the kitchen boys drops a tray and the crash arrives in the small dining room as a useful reminder that this is a working house and not a proposition. The inspector, to his credit, does not flinch. He is looking, Antonia sees, for the thing he has been sent to find, and beginning to understand that if it exists it will not be written in the book she keeps for inspectors, nor in the second book either — the one Diomedes keeps for her — but in the third accounting, which is her memory, and which does not admit measuring rods.

“The premises, then,” the inspector says, in the tone of a man moving to evidence he can measure, and Antonia rises and gives him the house the way she gives a new guest the menu: in full, in order, with nothing saved for a second, more expensive telling. Diomedes walks a pace behind with the tablets. The clerk, who is younger than the rod he carries and in love, as clerks are, with the prospect of scandal in good rooms, walks a pace behind Diomedes with his eyes drinking.

They begin, at Antonia's direction, in the kitchen, which is not where an inspector of morals expects to begin, and is therefore exactly where she begins them all: landlords, tax men, a praetor's steward two years ago with a warrant he eventually used as a napkin. A kitchen argues. Hers argues in copper — pans scoured to mirrors, the new stove drawing cleanly, Ammonius enthroned among his fires with a ladle in his hand and an expression that suggests the aediles may inspect his saucepans when they have published their own. The stores are chalked and dated. The wine cellar book matches the seals. The inspector, who has inspected the kitchens of the Subura and does not speak of them the way veterans do not speak, lets his clerk write *stores in order* with a hand that has had, Antonia guesses, little practice at the phrase.

“The women eat from the same kitchen?” he asks.

“The women eat first from the same kitchen, before the guests are admitted. A house rule.” She does not offer the reason. The reason is twenty years old and lives in Baiae, in a house where the staff ate what was left and learnt their value from the leavings. “You may ask them. It is permitted to ask them anything that is written in your commission.”

He asks Philomela, because Philomela is in the corridor with a lyre and the air of a woman it would be a clerical error to overlook. Philomela is twenty-six, freeborn in Corinth, professionally a singer in the way generals are professionally soldiers: it explains her rank in any room she enters. She gives the inspector her registration particulars the way she gives a difficult interval — precisely, and with a faint suggestion that lesser instruments exist. Age. Status. The name of the agent who brought her to Rome, a man whose contract Antonia bought out in the first month and burnt in this kitchen's predecessor, a ceremony Philomela attended with a cup of the house's second-best wine, *not the best*, she still says, *she's not sentimental, our mistress; she filed it under fuel*.

“Are you content in this house?” the inspector asks. It is not in his commission. It escapes him the way questions do in clean kitchens.

“Officer,” Philomela says, “I am a singer. Contentment is for audiences.” She tunes a string; the note goes through the corridor like a wire through cheese. “I am paid, I am registered, I sing what I choose and I refuse what I don't, and when my voice goes — voices go, officer, write that down too — I have money with a Greek at the Basilica that no husband, patron or tax farmer has the address of. If that is contentment in your Latin, then I am content. In mine it is called *not being a fool*, which my mother taught me in Corinth at her usual rates.”

The clerk writes nothing. There is no column for it. Antonia watches the inspector decide — she can see him do it, the way you can see a man at her tables decide not to say the true thing over the fish — to make the rest of the inspection be about doors.

So doors it is: widths, hinges, the number of dining rooms and their disposition, the little courtyard room where linen is kept and needlewomen are given the light, the bedrooms upstairs that are, for the register, *resting rooms for artists*, and are in fact whatever paying adults have agreed between themselves behind them, under house rules the inspector has not been commissioned to imagine. He measures. The rod reports that the house is the size it is. In the great dining room the new hanging stops him — not its beauty, though Eirene, passing with flowers, notes him registering that too and tells the kitchen later — its border. A man whose trade is paper looks at two hundred small red flowers, each one stitched, each one paid for, and has for a moment the expression of a person who has wandered out of his commission into the actual economy.

“From Transtiberim,” Antonia says. “A fuller's daughter. The house commissions its cloth work across the river. Better light, better prices, better women. You may record the neighbourhood as a moral influence, if the form has room.”

“The form,” the inspector says, surprising himself into honesty, which clean kitchens will do, “has very little room, madam. It was designed by men who expected smaller facts.”

They return to the small dining room by way of the courtyard, where the damp map is drying in a bar of sun and the roses are looking, as they always look in this house, handsomely overwatered, like guests who will not be refused a third cup. Barbatus is at the street door admitting a delivery of lamp oil, checking seals with thumbs the size of small hammers. The inspector looks at him and looks away — not fear; a kind of professional reassessment, as of a wall discovered to be structural.

“Your doorkeeper is ex-arena.”

“My doorkeeper is ex-everything Rome could charge admission for. He has killed no one on these premises, which is more than the Senate can say of its foreign policy, and he breaks up fights by standing up. The house recommends the technique to the committee.”

It is more than she usually gives an official, and she gives it deliberately, because a man who has been honest about his form deserves a sentence he can carry back like a souvenir. He almost smiles — the same almost-smile the house will see again from the prefect's man in the spring, though neither of them knows it yet — and sits down to his tablets and his watered wine with the air of a soldier returning to a frontier he suspects of being quieter than Rome.

“There have been complaints,” he says at last. “Noise. Congregation. Men of senatorial rank seen leaving after the second watch.”

“Men of senatorial rank leave the Senate after dark too, in the winter. I believe the streets survive it.” She pours him watered wine herself, which is a courtesy and also a way of ending a sentence. “Record the complaint. Record also that the neighbours on either side are a fuller's workshop and a dealer in lamps, neither of whom you will find among the complainants, because I pay the fuller well and the lamp-seller's daughter was married from this house with a dowry I do not discuss. If the committee wants complainants, it will have to import them, like the marble in the emperor's forum. It can be done. Everything can be done. I merely note the cost of transport.”

He looks at her then, directly, with something that is not quite respect and not quite warning. “The proposal comes from a name that does not lose, madam.”

“All names lose,” Antonia says. “The trick is to lose at something that does not end you. Show him the kitchen, Diomedes. Let him see the new stove. Men who have seen a kitchen find it harder to describe a house as a den. A den does not usually have a pastry cook from Alexandria arguing about saffron.”

When he has gone — rod, clerk, and a report already composing itself in his head in the passive voice, that great laundry of Roman administration, in which things *are found* and *are noted* and nobody in particular has ever found or noted them — the house exhales in a way it would deny doing. Ammonius sends up, unasked, a plate of the almond cakes that are reserved for officials and emergencies. Eirene takes one and eats it standing, which in Eirene is a parade.

“Well,” she says. “We are a den with a pastry cook. It is entered in the record.”

Diomedes is already writing the visit into the second book, in the hand he keeps for facts that must survive fashion. “He asked about Daphne's manumission twice,” he says, without looking up. “Once for the form. Once —” the stylus pauses, a toll-gate “— for the person who will read the form and ask him why he asked once. Somebody gave him her name before he came, madam. Not the register. A name, carried separately, like a stone in a sling.”

Antonia is still for a moment in the small dining room, where the inspector's cup sits with a finger's width of watered wine unevaporated at the bottom. She picks it up herself; a slave appears out of the house's well-trained air and, reading her face, decides to be needed in the corridor instead. “Whose stone?”

“The aedile's office would say the committee's. The committee would say the clerk's error.” Diomedes blots a line with the edge of his thumb, an Alexandrian trick he has never explained and she has never asked him to, it being house policy to let competent men keep one mystery each. “I have noted the hour he asked, and the hour he asked again. If a name travels, madam, it leaves footprints in other people's paperwork. You taught me that. In Naples, I believe, over a landlord.”

“I teach a great deal over landlords. It is my university.” She sets the cup down in its ring — she notices herself doing it and thinks of another house, a shrine room, a wife's hands, and files the thought where she files all thoughts with that address: between quarters, unopened. “Daphne is to be told her name was carried. Not frightened. Told. In this house nobody is protected by being uninformed; I have seen the other system work and it ends in Baiae.”

Eirene, who has finished the cake, says, “She'll want to sing about it.”

“She can sing about it in Greek, for people who know.” Antonia moves to the window. The street below is doing its noon business — a water-seller, a pair of advocates sharing a joke at a client's expense, a child herding three geese with a seriousness the Senate might study — all of it entirely uninformed that it has been represented this morning, on paper, as a neighbourhood suffering from congregation and noise. “Congregation,” she says aloud. “They have inspected a dining house and discovered dining. Lentulus must be beside himself with the quality of his intelligence.”

“Careful,” Eirene says, mildly. “That's almost a compliment to his organisation.”

“It is a compliment to his father.” The street, the geese, the advocates. “The old man could settle a province with a steward and a sealed tablet. The son sends a man with a rod to find a kitchen and is surprised by soup. Families decline, Eirene. It is the one inheritance tax nobody evades.”

Diomedes closes the book. “Shall I reply to the aediles' office today?”

“You shall reply on the third day, in the first hand, thanking them for their diligence and enclosing a copy of the tax receipts they already possess. Nothing discourages an office like being thanked with its own paperwork.” She turns from the window. The house is reassembling itself around her for the evening — she can hear the day shift handing over to the night one in a murmur of keys and menus — and her face, which has worn the inspection since the third hour like a good coat, puts it down somewhere the staff will not have to look at it. “And Diomedes. The next time a name arrives here carried separately, I want the name of the carrier. Buy it the way we buy thread.”

“By the piece,” Diomedes says, “and without informing their fathers,” and goes out to spend her money in the invisible denominations.

Lentulus hears the report in a room off the Basilica where the light falls in tall, dusty slabs and every conversation sounds as if it were already being transcribed by history. The aedile's man is apologetic in the way of officials who have found nothing and must make nothing sound like diligence. Registrations in order. Tax paid. Lease valid. Women of legal age and status, each accounted for. No unregistered slaves found on the premises. The house is, on paper, a dining establishment of unusual cleanliness.

“On paper,” Lentulus repeats.

“On paper, senator, half of Rome is a temple.”

It is a good line and the man knows it and regrets it in the same breath. Lentulus lets it pass. He has the colder problem of having aimed a public instrument at a private target and struck, with great precision, her paperwork — which she will now certainly have framed by the sheer force of having survived him.

Postumus, who has attached himself to the meeting in the manner of a relative who considers proximity a form of counsel, says when they are alone, “You knew she'd be clean. She was always clean. Even when —” He stops, with the belated tact of a man who has drunk at too many tables where the past was served as a side dish.

“Even when,” Lentulus says pleasantly, “is not a period of history the Senate recognises. What do the banks say?”

Postumus brightens. Money is a country where his accent is native. “Her money is not where you'd think. Some in property through freedmen, some in loans through a Greek at the Basilica — very respectable Greek, the kind other Greeks call Roman when they want to insult him. She lends like a senator and collects like a landlord.” He hesitates. “There is a rumour she holds paper on half the young men who dine there. Debts of honour, settled into debts of record. Quietly.”

“Paper on whom, exactly?”

“Exactly is expensive. Approximately is free. Approximately: everyone you would not want named in a committee.” Postumus watches him. “Old friend. If this is policy, it is bad policy, because she is cleaner than the policy. If it is not policy, it is worse, because she will know the difference even if the Senate pretends not to.”

Outside, the Forum crowd of the previous day has thinned into litigants and money-changers and the perpetual float of men waiting to be noticed by other men. Lentulus stands a moment under the portico and does an unfamiliar thing: he considers what he is doing as someone else might witness it. A man of ancient family, a former consul, a candidate in all but announcement for the Syrian command that will precede the emperor's eastern war, devoting the first weeks of the year to the registrations of a dining house on the Caelian. There are explanations a hostile world could supply. There is the explanation he supplies himself at hours when supply is low: that the city is rotting at its pleasures, that young men of good family are mortgaging estates to freedwomen's bankers, that someone whose name still means something must be seen to mean it.

The explanations hold together like a speech. They have that in common with speeches: they work best aloud, to others.

He walks home instead of taking the chair, which his household will hear about as a philosophy. The streets between the Basilica and the Palatine are full of the dedication's second day: workmen dismantling barriers, priests' boys reliving processions with sticks, a stallholder repainting his sign to include the word *imperial* in a hand that gets less certain toward the expensive letters. A client of his father's time, now very old and carried by a grandson who is visibly charging the whole transaction to his inheritance, recognises Lentulus and raises a hand like a standard being unfurled for the last time. Lentulus stops. This is also the work. Names are exchanged; a grandson is blessed in the formula; an old man is assured, in public, at a volume that recruits witnesses, that the Cornelii remember.

It costs four minutes and the price of a small farm in the currency his wife manages. Walking on, Lentulus does the sum he has been refusing since the portico: the committee's true arithmetic. For the registry to be policy, it must cost his friends something and be seen to. For it to be revenge, it must cost one woman everything and be deniable. A measure cannot be both at the full rate; Rome, which runs two sets of books as a civilisation — Greek and Latin libraries facing each other across the emperor's courtyard, each convinced it is the original — will ask him, in the baths, in the toilets, in the terrible clarity of other men's jokes, which book he is keeping. He has been telling himself the answer in committee language for a month. The language is holding together like a speech, and a speech, as his grandfather used to say while the masks were still being made of men he had known personally, is just a wall that has decided to be admired instead of climbed.

At the house the door slave takes his cloak and the day off him in the same motion. Servilia, from the garden room, calls — without raising her voice; the house is built so that she never has to — that the Acilii have sent fish, that his son is out, that his daughter has sung the Greek one, once, to the music master only, a distinction the girl has negotiated like a harbour due. Lentulus stands a moment in his own hall being reported to like a province. Everything is in order. That is the intelligence his committee has produced, on every front, all week: order, itemised, at other people's addresses.

The embroiderer comes again on the third day, because Antonia, having seen the hanging up in the great dining room, has sent word that the border wants another row of the small red flowers whose name she does not know, and that the house will pay for the thread separately. The girl — no, Antonia corrects herself, the young woman; she dislikes in herself the diminishing word she hears all day from men — arrives with her work-box and her father's permission written in her posture, shoulders squared against a rebuke for being in such a house at all.

“You may work in the courtyard,” Antonia says. “The light is better and Barbatus will frighten away anyone who thinks a woman sewing is a woman waiting to be spoken to.”

“My father says I am not to eat here.”

“Your father is protecting your reputation from my kitchen. Very well. You will not eat. You will, however, drink, because I will not have a needlewoman faint among my roses and give the aediles a second report to write.”

A smile escapes the young woman and is retrieved, but not fast enough to be unseen. She has a narrow face, dark brows, hands that know their trade so thoroughly the needle seems to be remembering rather than deciding. Her name, entered by Diomedes in the day-book under Payments to Trades, is Vibia Prima. Nineteen. Daughter of a freedman fuller in Transtiberim and a wife who died two winters ago. Betrothed, Eirene has already discovered — Eirene can discover a betrothal through a closed door; it is her one vulgar talent — to a baker's son with a shop near the Trigarium and a mother who inspects Prima's hems as if they were character references.

Prima's day, when she has a commission on the right bank, begins before the vats are lit, in a room that smells of yesterday's work whatever she does about it. This is not a complaint. Prima's relationship to smell is professional and, in its way, loyal: fullers' earth is honest, stale urine is merely chemistry with a history, and a clean garment coming off the frames has a smell she would put, in any honest census of Rome's perfumes, above rose oil bought to impress other people's wives. She heats her father's honey drink before she washes, because his cough is worst at the day's cold hinge and because if she washes first he will get up to do it himself, out of a doctrine — learned across a marriage and now carried alone like a hod — that a man who lets a house wake around him in bed is being buried by instalments.

“You're a garrison of one,” she tells him.

“I'm a fuller,” Vibius says. “We rise so Rome can pretend it was always clean.”

He drinks. She combs her hair at the window where the light comes in off the yard — grey light, working light, nothing like the Caelian courtyard's bar of sun, and she catches herself making the comparison and is annoyed at her own treachery. The comparison is new. It has come with the commission. She has sewn for good houses before — a priest's widow on the Aventine whose hems Prima still dreams about, badly; a contractor's wife who paid in compliments and festival cakes — but the Caelian house is the first place that has ever paid her in a way that made her father's face do that thing over the soup. *Itemised.* The mistress across the river writes Prima's work down the way the guild writes a master's: by the piece, the thread separate, the sum at the bottom with a line under it. Prima has been an entry in other people's books before, usually under her father's name, as *daughter, sewing*. Under her own name she is taller. She knows this is paperwork and she knows it is not only paperwork, and she is savage with herself, combing, for being the kind of girl — woman, the mistress would say; the mistress corrects words the way Prima corrects seams, in passing, without permission — who can be bought with bookkeeping.

Her betrothed's mother has no such doubts, only different ones. On the stairs of the insula, where she stations herself on market mornings with the tactical instinct of a customs post, she receives Prima's news of a third day's work on the Caelian with a stillness you could hang cloth on.

“Three days for a border.”

“Two hundred flowers, Mother Livia. I counted them to the mistress. She pays by the piece.”

“She pays.” The older woman's eyes go, as they always go, to Prima's hands, her hem, the place where a reputation would show wear if reputations were garments — and to Mother Livia, who has raised four sons on bread and inspection, they are exactly garments. “A house like that pays everyone at the door, so the street can see. It's what happens off the street that gets into the cloth.”

“Then I shall stay on the street side of the courtyard,” Prima says, “where the light is, with the doorkeeper, who frightens the customers for a living. He is very respectable, Mother Livia. He has killed people in the arena for the emperor's grandfather and now he checks lamp oil. Rome promotes.”

It earns her, after a moment in which the customs post considers a seizure, a snort that in another woman would be a laugh. “You read too much.”

“I read Titus' accounts when his sums won't. Tell him his yeast man's bill is wrong by eleven sesterces in the yeast man's favour, which is the direction bills are usually wrong in. It's in the second column. He doesn't check second columns because the first one is his.”

This is Prima's armour and she knows it: usefulness, laid on thick as fuller's earth. A reading bride is a defect; a bride who finds eleven sesterces in a second column is a dowry with eyes. She goes down the stairs, with her work-box clutched against her ribs, rehearsing the ferry money — two coins for the boat if the bridge crowd is bad, one if she walks by the island and the water is low — and by the time she reaches the river the morning has become the kind of Roman morning that makes foreigners write letters home: light on the water, gulls from the sea quarrelling over the offal boats, the hills of the right bank standing up out of woodsmoke with their temples and tenements indistinguishable at this distance, which is the city's best angle and it knows it.

She walks. Prima always walks when the choice is hers; the ferry is full of men making room in ways that require management, and she prefers to spend management on thread. The island bridge, with its temple and its hospital slaves being aired like bedding on the parapet; the cattle market smelling of the beginning of dinner; the long climb through streets that change their prices every hundred paces. She likes the climb. She likes — she has never said this, she files it under things a fuller's daughter need not say — arriving at the Caelian house slightly out of breath, as if at the top of a sentence. Inside, the porter gives her the small room off the courtyard and a stool with its back to the traffic, *by the mistress's instruction*, and Barbatus, who frightens customers for a living, moves his own stool six feet without being asked, so that his shadow falls between her and the dining rooms. She has a fuller father's understanding of what is being laundered here. Her reputation is being washed in advance, by experts, at a cost somebody is itemising as thread.

She sets the frame. She counts, chin tucked, mouth open on the number. The red flowers have a rhythm like a work-song and she goes into it the way the men in the vats go into the treading, not thinking, in the approved manner, at all — which is when, as every needlewoman and fuller and soldier knows, the thinking arrives that has been waiting its turn. Today it is her mother's voice, two years dead and clearer than the living: *count the stairs, so the stairs don't count you.* Ninety, in Baiae. Prima has seen the sea once, from Ostia, on a guild outing that cost her father a week's coughing; Baiae lives in her head as a staircase standing in blue light, with her mother on it, young, carrying linen, counting. She does not know why the mistress of this house went quiet — *the way rich women go quiet at a price*, she told her father, and he had held his blanket like a rail and not answered — when Prima passed on the stairs saying. Perhaps the mistress has been to Baiae. Rich women go to Baiae the way other people's linen goes to the fullers: regularly, and to be improved. Perhaps the mistress once had a laundress.

The needle goes in. The needle comes out. Somewhere beyond the courtyard a singer is practising, running a phrase up a staircase of notes and down again, counting, counting, and Prima — who does not eat in this house, whose father has protected her reputation from its kitchen, who will drink water in a moment from the mistress's own hand because the mistress will not have a needlewoman faint among her roses — feels, without any language for it that her street would accept, that she is being watched the way work is watched by someone who has done it. Not the customers' watching. A tradeswoman's. It straightens her back without her permission. She sews the next flower as if it were evidence.

Antonia watches her work a moment longer than supervision requires. There is a way the girl holds her head when she is counting stitches, chin tucked, mouth slightly open on the number, that plucks at something in Antonia which she files, with the swiftness of long practice, under the general sorrow of watching the young be young in a city that charges for it. She goes back to her accounts.

She does not notice — because she is a woman who notices prices, exits and the weather in a man's voice, and no one notices everything — the small, pale mark on the inside of Prima's left wrist, shaped, if a person were fanciful, like a half-closed eye. The needle goes in. The needle comes out. The red flowers multiply along the border, each one the same, each one, if you look closely, which Prima's future mother-in-law certainly will, very slightly alive with difference.
