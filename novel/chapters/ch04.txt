What the Baths Know
The baths at the eighth hour are Rome with its clothes off and its guard, paradoxically, up. Steam, oil, the slap of feet on wet mosaic, a ball game in the exercise yard conducted with the seriousness of a border dispute. Lentulus goes twice a week and never on the days his son goes, an arrangement both of them maintain without ever having made it. A man's friendships are audited in the baths. So are his enmities; the water carries everything.

Acilius, who will be consul within the year if the emperor's favour holds, falls into step beside him in the warm room with the ease of a man who has rehearsed spontaneity. “Syria,” Acilius says, naming the whole conversation in advance so that they can both pretend to have wandered into it. “The emperor will want a governor in place before he moves east. A name that reassures the legions and does not frighten the Senate. Yours is spoken.”

“Names are spoken in baths the way steam is made. Abundantly and on purpose.”

“Yours is also spoken at dinner.” Acilius lowers himself into the water with a sigh that is half politics, the sound a man makes to suggest that what follows is reluctant. “There is one — untidiness. It is said your committee work looks personal. In a candidate for Syria, personal is a colour the emperor does not wear.”

“And who says it looks personal?”

“People who dine on the Caelian.” Acilius smiles with his eyes only, a trick of men who expect to be carved one day and are practising for the bust. “Old friend, I have eaten there. Half the men who will vote on your appointment have eaten there. The food is excellent and the discretion has been, until now, architectural. If you shake that house, things will fall out of it. Some of them will have names you need.”

The water is at Lentulus's ribs. Heat works on the old injury in his shoulder, the one from a horse in Moesia that history has since promoted into something more martial in other people's telling. He thinks of his father's voice on the subject of appetites: *A Cornelius may have them. He may not be had by them.* He thinks, unbidden and therefore with irritation, of a courtyard in a house that no longer stands, of a girl laughing with a stylus behind her ear like a flower, utterly unaware that a stylus behind the ear was, in his father's Rome, a flag planted on the wrong hill.

“I am not shaking a house,” he says. “I am proposing that houses which profit from the sons of senators be registered, taxed and inspected like any other trade that could burn a neighbourhood down. If that reads as personal, it is because Rome personalises everything except responsibility.”

“Then make it read as general,” Acilius says mildly. “Inspect ten houses. Inspect twenty. At present you have inspected one, at dawn, by committee. Rome can count, Lentulus. That is its least attractive quality.”

Marcus hears about the conversation before his father's oil is dry, because the baths have a second plumbing system, made of slaves, and his dice creditors keep him plumbed in. He hears it in the version that will do the rounds by dinner: *Lentulus warned off the Caelian by Acilius. Syria in the balance. The woman has names.* He is twenty years old and the news strikes him not as danger but as proof — proof that the house on the Caelian, where the wine is honest and a freedwoman sings like a person addressing him rather than his pedigree, is the one true place in Rome where power admits what it is.

He goes there that night because going there has acquired, in the alchemy of prohibition, the flavour of a political position. Antonia sees him come in. She sees the friend he has brought, a young Avidius with shipping money and a laugh too loud for the room he is in, and she performs, in a single glance exchanged with Diomedes, the whole arithmetic of the evening: what the boy's presence costs his father, what it earns the house, what it risks for Daphne, who is — she hears it in the first phrase — singing better than she has sung all month, which is its own kind of risk.

“You should send me away,” Marcus says to her in the interval, with the bravado of youth offering its hostess a sword to fall on. “My father would. In your place.”

“I am not in his place,” Antonia says. “That has been established by committee. Drink your wine slowly, Marcus. It is the only advice I give young men that they can follow without a tutor.”

“He wants you closed.”

“He wants to be seen wanting me closed. There is a difference, and the difference is where I live.” She is aware, even as she says it, of the Avidius boy listening with the deep attention of new money at an old lesson. She pitches the next sentence for him too. “Your father and I disagree about the governance of this city. He believes it is governed from the Senate house. I believe it is governed wherever men tell the truth after the fish course. We are both, in our way, patriots.”

Marcus laughs, delighted, disloyal, young. Across the room Daphne returns the necklace — Antonia watches the choreography, the little press of the palm, the sentence about musicians — and watches the boy receive his own lie back as a keepsake, and love the woman who returned it the way men love being forgiven beautifully: entirely, and for about a week, exactly as predicted.

Prima is there that night too, but not as guests are there. She has come after dark because the baker's mother, that inspector of hems, has kept her all day unpicking a sleeve, and the border of red flowers must be finished before the hanging is wanted for a banquet of Alexandrians. She works in the small room off the courtyard where the linen is kept, and through the half-open door she can hear the singing, and under the singing the talk, and under the talk a quality she does not have a word for, though she will learn several before the year is out: ease. The ease of people who believe the room will hold them.

Barbatus brings her water without being asked, which for Barbatus is a speech. “Your father know you're here after dark?”

“My father knows I am finishing work for a house that pays on the day.” She bites off a thread. “He knows the name of the street. He chooses the rest of his knowledge the way we all do.”

Barbatus, who killed men in the arena for eleven years and has since developed strong views about the management of doorways, considers this. “You've a look of someone,” he says.

“So have you. It's called a face.”

He laughs, a sound that turns a head or two in the courtyard, and goes back to his door. But the remark stays with him, the way a song stays, trivial and exact. He has a look-of-someone feeling about the needlewoman, and Barbatus trusts his instincts in proportion to how little language they require.

Eirene is the one who says it. Later, much later, when the guests have gone and the money has been counted and the lamps are being trimmed down to their night setting, she says it in the tone of a woman putting a cup exactly in the middle of a table. “The needlewoman. Prima. Have you looked at her, Antonia? Not at her work. At her.”

“I look at everyone. It is how this house survives.”

“You look at everyone the way a customs officer looks at cargo. Look at her the way you look at a mirror in bad light. Against your will.”

Antonia's hands, which are counting by touch — she can count coin in the dark, a skill from a life she does not narrate — do not stop. That they do not stop is information, and Eirene, who has known her since they were both somebody else's property in a house in the Subura where the stairs smelled of cabbage and ambition, receives it as such.

“She is a fuller's daughter from Transtiberim,” Antonia says. “She is betrothed to a baker. She sews a straight seam. Rome is full of girls — women — with dark brows, Eirene. It manufactures them. It is one of its industries.”

“Count her years.”

“I have counted everything in this house twice today.” The coins go on moving. “Do not make me count that.”

Eirene says nothing more. She puts the trimmed lamp down between them, where it burns with a small, upright flame, and the two women sit a while in the aftersound of the house — water somewhere, a latch, Barbatus telling the night porter a story with a long, dishonest preamble — while Antonia finishes the count and gets it wrong, and begins again, and gets it right, and writes the right number down in a hand that only shakes on the last figure, where no one who did not already know her would think to look.
