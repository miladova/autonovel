What the Baths Know

The baths at the eighth hour are Rome with its clothes off and its guard, paradoxically, up. Steam, oil, the slap of feet on wet mosaic, a ball game in the exercise yard conducted with the seriousness of a border dispute. Lentulus goes twice a week and never on the days his son goes, an arrangement both of them maintain without ever having made it. A man's friendships are audited in the baths. So are his enmities; the water carries everything.

Lentulus arrives with his own oil flask, his own strigils in a case his grandfather's campaign chest would recognise, and a slave, Onesimus, whose entire public personality is a talent for standing in the one spot in any room where a senator's conversation cannot be overheard by accident. Onesimus has served in this capacity for eleven years. He has heard, in that time, three treasons discussed as hypotheticals, one discussed as a menu, and enough provincial appointments traded over naked men exercising to staff, on paper, a second empire. He forgets professionally. It is understood in the household that when Onesimus is freed — the will is written, the sum named, Servilia has seen to it with her usual prospective kindness — he will forget his own manumission ceremony by the following morning, out of habit.

The exercise yard first: the ball game is at the stage where old friendships are being audited at speed. A former praetor with a knee like a rusty hinge is holding the centre by reputation alone, directing younger men with a general's economy: a chin here, a lifted eyebrow there, the whole art of command reduced to upholstery. Lentulus takes three rounds, no more; more would be a statement about Syria, less would be a statement about his shoulder, and the yard reads statements the way the tax office reads returns, professionally and without charity. He plays well enough to be unremarkable, which in this company is a form of excellence no one will ever carve.

In the anointing room the news is hung up with the clothes. A letter from Athens is doing the rounds in summary — Hadrian has been archon, Hadrian has been initiated, Hadrian has opinions about a temple that has waited six hundred years for a roof and will now, the summary implies, be roofed by personality alone. A young attaché recently returned to Rome is retailing it all with the exhausted air of a man whose luggage contained, besides linen, the future.

“He spoke Greek to the masons,” the attaché says. “Not through a secretary. To them.”

“Barbarous,” says the former praetor, pleasantly, towelling a knee that predates the fashion. “An emperor-in-waiting who can talk to workmen will expect answers. There'll be an end to government.”

Nobody says *emperor-in-waiting* again. The phrase does a small, satisfied circuit of the room anyway, the way the pickpocket did the Forum crowd, feeling for attention to settle in. Onesimus, at the wall, forgets it in three languages. Lentulus, submitting to oil and the scraper, files it where he files all such futures: under *weather, eastern, approaching*. Trajan is not old as emperors go and is fit as a man can be who has spent his life being carried toward wars expressly to walk through them; but the succession in Rome is a bath-house topic the way rain is a farmer's, not because it is imminent but because everything planted waits on it. A Syrian command under Trajan is a career. The same command remembered under his successor is either a recommendation or a headstone, and a man calculating the one, in warm rooms, naked as an egg, does well to calculate the other.

The scraper finds the Moesia shoulder and the old injury sends up its small, familiar dispatch: *present, not urgent, keeping the minutes*. He got it falling from a horse that had been described to him by a quartermaster as steady, a word he no longer allows in documents. History, in other people's telling, has promoted the fall into a skirmish; a nephew once asked about the skirmish at dinner and Lentulus, to Servilia's audible approval down the table, described the horse instead, at length, until the skirmish died of documentation. It remains his favourite of his own speeches. There are so few of them with a horse in that he is willing to sign.

“Your son was here at the sixth hour, dominus,” says the bath attendant, who is called Felix too — half the useful men in Rome are — and who retails the household's movements to the household as a kind of preventative medicine. “With young Avidius. They won at ball. There was a matter of a wager settled amicably, and the young master paid a debt *for* a friend which the friend had intended to settle by talking. The talk, if I may say so, was filed as insufficient.”

“Whose debt?”

“The friend's name was not in my hearing, dominus. The sum was in everyone's.”

Lentulus holds out his arm for the towel with the expression of a man receiving a committee report in a format he prefers to the Senate's. Marcus paying other men's debts: there is a future in that sentence in several denominations, and all of them will arrive at his atrium in the end, because that is what sons and aqueducts have in common in his life at forty-five — what is sent downhill comes home by the plumbing. He says, “If my son's generosity is to be a season, Felix, the house would like the weather early.”

“I shall keep the sky watched, dominus,” says Felix, and they go through into the heat in their order, senator, slave, oil, news, like a small procession at a dedication nobody has inscribed.

Acilius, who will be consul within the year if the emperor's favour holds, falls into step beside him in the warm room with the ease of a man who has rehearsed spontaneity. “Syria,” Acilius says, naming the whole conversation in advance so that they can both pretend to have wandered into it. “The emperor will want a governor in place before he moves east. A name that reassures the legions and does not frighten the Senate. Yours is spoken.”

“Names are spoken in baths the way steam is made. Abundantly and on purpose.”

“Yours is also spoken at dinner.” Acilius lowers himself into the water with a sigh that is half politics, the sound a man makes to suggest that what follows is reluctant. “There is one — untidiness. It is said your committee work looks personal. In a candidate for Syria, personal is a colour the emperor does not wear.”

“And who says it looks personal?”

“People who dine on the Caelian.” Acilius smiles with his eyes only, a trick of men who expect to be carved one day and are practising for the bust. “Old friend, I have eaten there. Half the men who will vote on your appointment have eaten there. The food is excellent and the discretion has been, until now, architectural. If you shake that house, things will fall out of it. Some of them will have names you need.”

The water is at Lentulus's ribs. Heat works on the old injury in his shoulder, the one from a horse in Moesia that history has since promoted into something more martial in other people's telling. He thinks of his father's voice on the subject of appetites: *A Cornelius may have them. He may not be had by them.* He thinks, unbidden and therefore with irritation, of a courtyard in a house that no longer stands, of a girl laughing with a stylus behind her ear like a flower, utterly unaware that a stylus behind the ear was, in his father's Rome, a flag planted on the wrong hill.

“I am not shaking a house,” he says. “I am proposing that houses which profit from the sons of senators be registered, taxed and inspected like any other trade that could burn a neighbourhood down. If that reads as personal, it is because Rome personalises everything except responsibility.”

“Then make it read as general,” Acilius says mildly. “Inspect ten houses. Inspect twenty. At present you have inspected one, at dawn, by committee. Rome can count, Lentulus. That is its least attractive quality.”

The cold room receives them like a verdict. Men go into the water with sounds they would not permit themselves in the Senate and come out of it looking, briefly, the age they were when the century was still negotiable. Acilius, who does everything in order, including spontaneity, waits until they are both in up to the waist and the attendants have withdrawn to the distance that Onesimus has arranged with a look — Onesimus and the Acilian equivalent having conducted, in five seconds of eye contact, the entire diplomatic history of their profession — before he finishes the conversation the warm room started.

“Hadrian writes that Athens is teaching him what Rome is for.”

“Athens,” Lentulus says, “has been teaching Romans what Rome is for for two hundred years. We keep enrolling. It saves thinking.”

“He also asks — through Plotina's people, so it is a question with a return address — who in Rome can draft. Not speak. Draft. A man who can put a complicated thing in a sentence that walks. Your name came up in a context I chose not to improve.”

The cold is doing its work on the shoulder: first outrage, then a deep, clean ache, then the body's reluctant agreement that it is still a going concern. Lentulus looks at the mosaic under the water, Neptune in pieces of coloured stone, his trident refracted into something pastoral. “I am flattered to be a context.”

“You are being offered a chance to be useful to a future that has not yet had the courtesy to announce itself. It is the only kind of usefulness that compounds.” Acilius ducks under (Once. Correctly. As if there were marks.), and comes up streaming. “Drop the single-house siege, Lentulus. Make it a registry, make it general, make it boring, and let Athens hear of a draftsman. Or keep the siege, and let Athens hear of a feud. Feuds are memoir. Drafts are government.”

On the far side of the plunge, a gaunt equestrian who made money in the Spanish mines is telling the water about his kidneys, in detail, with dates. Two boys — not young enough to be charming, not old enough to be listened to — are conducting a drowning contest that the attendants will stop at the precise moment it becomes the kind of death that needs paperwork. The ordinary empire, naked, wet, going about its real business of being a place. Lentulus watches it the way he watches the Forum from the portico: as a system that works best at everything except admitting what it is.

“When I was twenty-four,” he says, and stops, because the sentence has arrived without its paperwork and the cold room is not a locked one.

Acilius, who has known him since they were both young enough to be corrected by tutors, does the most senatorial thing available: he does not hear the beginning of the sentence. He hears the whole of its history, files it, and answers the part that can be answered in water. “When you were twenty-four, we all drafted badly. Some of us with juries, some with marriages.” He hauls himself out onto the edge and sits dripping, a future consul at the mercy of a towel. “I have a dinner Thursday. Plotina's freedwoman will be at it, which means the empress will know by Friday what was served, in both senses. Come. Bring Servilia. Leave the committee at home in a locked room; it's what they're for.”

“My wife,” Lentulus says, “says this family cannot feed pets.”

“Your wife,” Acilius says, “should be governing Syria. It would save everyone a great deal of ceremony, including the Syrians.” He stands; the towel arrives; the future resumes its clothes in the next room. “Thursday, Lentulus. And if your son is going to pay his friends' debts, tell him to do it before the vintage. Generosity in a young man reads as principle. After a bad harvest it reads as a system.”

He goes out with his people in their order. Lentulus stays in the water a count of fifty longer than comfort advises, because the cold is the one counsel in Rome that wants nothing from him, and because Onesimus, at the wall, has the face of a man who has forgotten three futures since the oil and will forget this one into the record too — all of it held, all of it sealed, like a tablet in a cupboard under a mask.

Marcus hears about the conversation before his father's oil is dry, because the baths have a second plumbing system, made of slaves, and his dice creditors keep him plumbed in. He hears it in the version that will do the rounds by dinner: *Lentulus warned off the Caelian by Acilius. Syria in the balance. The woman has names.* He is twenty years old and the news strikes him not as danger but as proof — proof that the house on the Caelian, where the wine is honest and a freedwoman sings like a person addressing him rather than his pedigree, is the one true place in Rome where power admits what it is.

He goes there that night because going there has acquired, in the alchemy of prohibition, the flavour of a political position. Antonia sees him come in. She sees the friend he has brought, a young Avidius with shipping money and a laugh too loud for the room he is in, and she performs, in a single glance exchanged with Diomedes, the whole arithmetic of the evening: what the boy's presence costs his father, what it earns the house, what it risks for Daphne, who is — she hears it in the first phrase — singing better than she has sung all month, which is its own kind of risk.

“You should send me away,” Marcus says to her in the interval, with the bravado of youth offering its hostess a sword to fall on. “My father would. In your place.”

“I am not in his place,” Antonia says. “That has been established by committee. Drink your wine slowly, Marcus. It is the only advice I give young men that they can follow without a tutor.”

“He wants you closed.”

“He wants to be seen wanting me closed. There is a difference, and the difference is where I live.” She is aware, even as she says it, of the Avidius boy listening with the deep attention of new money at an old lesson. She pitches the next sentence for him too. “Your father and I disagree about the governance of this city. He believes it is governed from the Senate house. I believe it is governed wherever men tell the truth after the fish course. We are both, in our way, patriots.”

Marcus laughs, delighted, disloyal, young. Across the room Daphne returns the necklace — Antonia watches the choreography, the little press of the palm, the sentence about musicians — and watches the boy receive his own lie back as a keepsake, and love the woman who returned it the way men love being forgiven beautifully: entirely, and for about a week, exactly as predicted.

Daphne sings the second set differently, and the room knows it before it knows why. The first set is always the house's: accomplished, priced, a demonstration that everyone's money is in the right building. The second set, on the nights it happens, is the singer's. Antonia, who could forbid it — she could forbid anything in these rooms; that is what ownership means when a woman has bought it in the currency she has — permits it on an economic principle she would not insult Daphne by explaining: a woman who never sings her own set will one day sing somebody else's, in somebody else's house, and take the room's heart with her as severance. Generosity, like honesty, is a long position. Eirene, who has heard this doctrine applied to rings, menus and one unforgettable negotiation with a lamp-seller over a daughter's dowry, refers to it in the kitchen as *the mistress's religion, second service*.

Tonight the second set is a song from the theatres that Daphne has slowed, the way she slows street tunes, until the comedy has drained out of it and the woman in the song — a slave girl in the original, getting the better of a master the audience has paid to see fooled — stands in the music with her trickery suddenly looking like the only arithmetic on offer. The room laughs in the wrong place, from habit, and stops. The grain men stop. The elderly advocate, who has heard this song in its fast version perhaps four hundred times since the last emperor but two, leans forward on his couch with a lawyer's outrage at new evidence.

Marcus does not laugh at all. Antonia, at the room's edge, watches a senator's son hear a comedy become a census and — count it, she tells herself, it may be the only honest minute of his education to date — watches him not reach for his cup to get out of it. Across the room young Avidius is less fortunate in his refuge: he laughs alone, a barge of a laugh, discovers the harbour empty, and converts it, with the grace his family is buying at such expense, into a cough that fools nobody except, kindly, the girl pouring his wine, who files it under *new money, improving*.

After, Daphne comes down from the music the way divers come up: in stages, with the room's applause arriving around her like light through water. Marcus is on his feet, which Romans of his order are not, for performers, in dining rooms — a small constitutional crisis conducted in footwear. He sits down again without looking at anyone, as if standing had been something done to him by the building. Antonia makes a note in the third book, the one that lives behind her ribs: *the boy has a conscience in his ears. It will be educated out of him or it will educate him; either way, in this city, somebody will charge for the tuition.*

“Again?” says the advocate, to the room, organising opinion like a man with fifty years' practice at juries. “At the fast speed, this time, so that we may all recover our characters.”

Daphne looks at Antonia. It is the house's look: *your room, your call, my voice, my terms*. Antonia nods once — fast, then home, the voice is a capital asset — and the song goes round again at its theatre pace, bright as paint, and the room, restored to itself, laughs in all the right places with an energy that has relief in it, and money. Only Prima, in the linen room beyond the half-open door, sewing by lamplight a border for a house that measures doorways for officials and drinks songs at two speeds, keeps time with the slow version. She has never heard it before. She will find, in the morning, that she has stitched seven flowers to it after it ended, like a sentence continuing itself in a hand that has just learned new spelling.

Prima is there that night too, but not as guests are there. She has come after dark because the baker's mother, that inspector of hems, has kept her all day unpicking a sleeve, and the border of red flowers must be finished before the hanging is wanted for a banquet of Alexandrians. She works in the small room off the courtyard where the linen is kept, and through the half-open door she can hear the singing, and under the singing the talk, and under the talk a quality she does not have a word for, though she will learn several before the year is out: ease. The ease of people who believe the room will hold them.

Barbatus brings her water without being asked, which for Barbatus is a speech. “Your father know you're here after dark?”

“My father knows I am finishing work for a house that pays on the day.” She bites off a thread. “He knows the name of the street. He chooses the rest of his knowledge the way we all do.”

Barbatus, who killed men in the arena for eleven years and has since developed strong views about the management of doorways, considers this. “You've a look of someone,” he says.

“So have you. It's called a face.”

He laughs, a sound that turns a head or two in the courtyard, and goes back to his door. But the remark stays with him, the way a song stays, trivial and exact. He has a look-of-someone feeling about the needlewoman, and Barbatus trusts his instincts in proportion to how little language they require.

Prima finishes the row as the music changes in the far rooms — a singer resting, instruments being argued with in low voices, the house taking the breath it takes between selves. Her eyes are dry from the lamp; her back, where the stool's edge has been conducting a four-hour negotiation, presents its invoice all at once. She rolls the work the way her mother taught her, face inward, so that whatever the world does to it in transit happens to the back of the flowers. Out of provinces of habit like this, her whole life is governed. A year ago she would not have had the thought; tonight, in a house where rules arrive with reasons attached and women correct each other's words in passing, she notices her own government and wonders — it is the second treacherous comparison of the week, and she lets this one stand, being tired — what a life would feel like in which the rules were *hers* in more denominations than cloth.

Eirene is waiting at the courtyard door with the lantern and a shawl that is not Prima's.

“It belongs to the house,” Eirene says, before the refusal can get itself itemised. “Houses own shawls the way harbours own rope. You'll bring it back with the spring work and Diomedes will enter it as thread. Everything here is entered as thread. I have been entered as thread myself, in difficult years.”

The street at night is another Rome, let out of its daytime filing. The watch goes past with its buckets and its air of a department that has seen every fire twice. A drunk is being walked home by two slaves with the tenderness of men carrying a sofa through a doorway in their employer's best room. Somewhere above the street a woman is singing a child down in a language Prima half knows from the vats — one of the river tongues, part Latin, part everywhere the river has been — and Eirene, carrying the lantern high the way she carries everything, as if it had a right to be seen with, says, “Your father will be awake.”

“He's awake when I'm out. It's his watch. He says the house makes noises a man should supervise.”

“Men say that about houses, money and daughters. It's their word for standing in a doorway.” Eirene steps around a puddle that holds the lamplight up to them like an offering. “Mine stood in doorways professionally — a porter, in the Subura, in the house where Antonia and I —” She stops. It is only the smallest check, a stitch counted twice, but Prima, whose trade is noticing the place where a pattern hesitates, notices. “— where we were girls,” Eirene finishes, at the same pace, like a woman stepping over the puddle she has just pointed out. “Count your blessings, Prima. Your doorways are honest.”

Prima nods under the borrowed shawl, not trusting the moment with a question. She has grown up in rooms where the past was present as furniture: things not sat on, a mother's saying about stairs used daily and never explained, an old woman at the vats who looks at her sometimes the way Mother Livia looks at a hem — as evidence being kept for a later inspection. She knows, the way you know weather from a roof you have lived under, that her life has a sealed room in it. She has always known. What she does not know is whether other people's lives don't, or whether, as she is beginning, tonight, walking home beside a freedwoman with a lantern from a house that files kindness as thread, to suspect: it is only the poor who are expected to live in one room and call it honesty.

At the bridge, the river is doing its night work, carrying the city's lights away in pieces too small to invoice. Tibur stone, island temple, a barge going down unlit on the current's own authority. Eirene stops at the crossing by arrangement — *to the crossing*, the mistress said, and Prima, who thought it a measure of distance, understands at the lantern's turning that it is also a measure of neighbourhood: this far a Caelian lantern may escort a fuller's daughter without becoming a story; beyond this, the street is hers again, and stories, like debts, should be contracted at home.

“Thursday,” Eirene says, “for the border's end. Bring your appetite; the mistress has lost patience with your father's foreign policy about her kitchen and is filing lunch as a materials inspection. You may tell him the house inspects *you* with soup. It has the advantage of being true in every particular the guild would check.”

Prima goes down into Transtiberim in the dark she was raised in, which receives her without commentary. Her father is awake, as advertised, supervising the house's noises from the doorway with his honey cooling beside him. He looks at the shawl. He looks at the work-box. He does not ask the questions, because keeping, in his book, includes keeping a daughter's day unopened until she chooses a knife.

“Flowers done?” he says.

“Flowers nearly. Soup Thursday,” Prima says, hanging the borrowed shawl where the damp won't find it. “Filed as an inspection. You can't refuse an inspection, Father. It's the law of houses.”

Vibius regards her with the expression she will remember, later, when she has a whole archive of his expressions and no more being added to it. “Then inspect thoroughly, girl,” he says. “And tell the house from me: we know good work across the river. We can smell it through the vats.” He coughs, and drinks, and lets her heat the honey again without either of them saying why it has gone cold.

At the door-book, later, Antonia does the night’s other accounting, the kind Diomedes enters in the first hand because it must look boring to survive: who came, who paid, who signed, who was carried out by friends conducting themselves like pall-bearers at a rehearsal. Barbatus reads the street back to her in his door-keeper’s Latin — watch passed twice, no trouble, a senator’s chair took the wrong turning at the corner and righted itself without witnesses, the rain considering a return engagement. Young Avidius left on foot with the heir of the Cornelii, which Barbatus reports as he would report weather over a harbour his family now, in effect, has cargo in. “Walking,” he adds, “at a talking pace. The young one with the laugh did most of it. The young master did the listening. I marked it as a first, mistress, in case there's a register.”

“There is always a register,” Antonia says. “That is the committee's whole theology.”

She closes the book on a night that has made the house money, cost it nothing it cannot replace, and left her with the feeling — she distrusts feelings that arrive without invoices; they tend to be other people's, delivered to her address by mistake — of a seam she cannot see being stitched somewhere offstage, with good thread, at somebody else's prices. In the corridor the lamps are going down by sections, the theatre unlit from the front. Daphne's door has its red wool on it already: a voice is sleeping; knock and be billed. Philomela is somewhere being witty to the night staff for the professional pleasure of staying in practice. And in the small dining room Eirene is doing the last flowers, which means she has something to say that requires her hands to be otherwise employed when she says it.

Eirene is the one who says it. Later, much later, when the guests have gone and the money has been counted and the lamps are being trimmed down to their night setting, she says it in the tone of a woman putting a cup exactly in the middle of a table. “The needlewoman. Prima. Have you looked at her, Antonia? Not at her work. At her.”

“I look at everyone. It is how this house survives.”

“You look at everyone the way a customs officer looks at cargo. Look at her the way you look at a mirror in bad light. Against your will.”

Antonia's hands, which are counting by touch — she can count coin in the dark, a skill from a life she does not narrate — do not stop. That they do not stop is information, and Eirene, who has known her since they were both somebody else's property in a house in the Subura where the stairs smelled of cabbage and ambition, receives it as such.

“She is a fuller's daughter from Transtiberim,” Antonia says. “She is betrothed to a baker. She sews a straight seam. Rome is full of girls — women — with dark brows, Eirene. It manufactures them. It is one of its industries.”

“Count her years.”

“I have counted everything in this house twice today.” The coins go on moving. “Do not make me count that.”

Eirene says nothing more. She puts the trimmed lamp down between them, where it burns with a small, upright flame, and the two women sit a while in the aftersound of the house — water somewhere, a latch, Barbatus telling the night porter a story with a long, dishonest preamble — while Antonia finishes the count and gets it wrong, and begins again, and gets it right, and writes the right number down in a hand that only shakes on the last figure, where no one who did not already know her would think to look.
