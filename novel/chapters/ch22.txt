Letters from Athens

The emperor will go east; that much the stone yards know, the granaries know, the letter-writers of Athens know at flattering length. Hadrian writes to Rome in the accomplished hand of a man collecting a world: architecture, cults, old friendships renewed like leases. His name goes up one side of the city's future and down the other. Syria goes — the secretaries settle it over the summer like a cloth over a table — to a safer pair of hands, a man whose private geometry contains no Caelian angles, no clauses. Lentulus receives the news from Acilius in the baths, where all great disappointments in Rome are steamed for tenderness.

The change is visible first in the volume of other people’s stationery. Couriers come off the eastern roads with dust in the seams of their cloaks. Contractors who were unbearable in January acquire, by June, the gracious fatigue of men who have already been paid to think ahead. In the stone yards the conversation shifts from what will be dedicated to what must be shipped: timber for bridging, iron, millstones, rope by the mile. The granaries are quieter about it, as granaries are, but quantities have their own eloquence if a man knows how to read a tally without making it sound like treason.

Hadrian’s letters from Athens are copied almost as soon as they are unsealed. They are not the letters of a mere traveller. They are inventories in the grammatical form of friendship: temples measured, notables sounded, grievances elegantly rephrased, offices considered from the far side of the sea as one might consider pieces left on a board in another room. Acilius, whose name appears in them with a warmth that has caused three younger men to revise their seating arrangements, gives Lentulus a summary before the baths have had it. It is an old courtesy. It is also a warning delivered at a temperature neither of them needs to name.

“There is praise,” Acilius says in the changing room, while slaves make a liturgy of towels. “Of your committee’s drafting hand. Not the clause itself. The *hand*.”

“A distinction with a view,” Lentulus says.

“Hadrian likes men who write as if words have weight. He distrusts men whose words acquire crowds.”

“I had a committee. Crowds were an administrative by-product.”

“You had a sentence, Gnaeus. It has begun to travel without your luggage.”

The appointee for Syria is not yet named in the official ink, but a safe name is being repeated with the confidence of weather already felt in the bones. He is competent. He is well connected. His domestic arrangements, so far as Rome can discover, contain no women whose filing systems might become constitutional history overnight. This last point is not made directly; directness is for engineers. It is conveyed by everybody’s sudden refreshment when his virtues are listed.

Lentulus hears the list and finds, to his own surprise, no stab of intimate outrage. There is disappointment, the old public animal lifting its head. There is also an odd sensation as of a door being shut by somebody else on a room he no longer lives in. All winter he imagined Syria as the place where the family account might be rebalanced by distance. Summer has introduced him to a more humiliating and promising theory: that distance is what has been applied to his obligations, at compound rates, for three decades.

A young secretary passes with a message for Acilius. The message is not for Lentulus, and the secretary makes the small, perfect bow men make when a document’s route is itself news. In another year Lentulus would have felt the omission as a wound dressed in protocol. Today he notices the secretary’s sandals are newly soled and that the man is left-handed. The city has become legible at floor level. It is not consolation. It may be tuition.

“It was the clause, in the end?” Acilius asks it gently; there is a consul's kindness that consists of not requiring a man to watch his own autopsy.

“It was the appearance of a man who might, in a province, write another one.” Lentulus settles into the warm water up to the ribs; the shoulder from Moesia gives its opinion in the old language. “I find I do not believe in the east anymore, Acilius. Not as a cure. My father governed provinces to keep from governing his house. The house is where the interest accrues. You may tell Athens I said something about my health.”

Acilius lets that sit in the steam before he disturbs it. He has the manners of an older Rome, in which silence between friends was not immediately subdivided into lots and sold.

“You could still make representations,” he says at last. “Not for Syria. That ship has been caulked. For the kind of work that puts a man back into the manifest in smaller print.”

“You sound as if you have been dining with shipowners.”

“I have been governing long enough to know when a metaphor has stopped being mine. The water board needs a curator with a name heavy enough to frighten contractors and a reputation light enough to be inspected. It is not the Euphrates.”

“No.”

“It does flow.”

Lentulus puts his head back against the warm stone. The shoulder is easing. In the next chamber somebody is telling a Dacian story in which the same river is crossed increasingly well by the same man. The baths are full of revisions. “When I was young, Acilius, I thought public life was a kind of architecture. Put the right name over the right arch and the centuries would keep the roof on.”

“And now?”

“Now I think it is drains. What we praise is mostly the smell not arriving.”

“You have become profound in a very unmarketable direction.”

“My wife says maintenance has no stamina for scandal.”

“Your wife should be set to draft something. With her own seal. It would clarify the century.”

There it is, light as a towel dropped between them: the old male joke made true by the year. Lentulus does not laugh at first, because the idea—Servilia’s hand on an actual public document, uncontained by dinners, weddings and the correct deployment of silence—belongs to a Rome neither of them will live to be inconvenienced by. Then he does laugh, quietly, in the water. “She already drafts the household. The public is merely where we attend the consequences aloud.”

Acilius studies him with an expression that in a less controlled man would be affection. “You are not going to make a scene, then. Athens can be spared a bruised Cornelius arriving by post.”

“I have made scenes in arrears. They were billed to other people.”

The sentence goes through the steam without applause. Acilius has known him too long to pretend it is a casual remark disguised as wit. “And the clause?” he asks. “If Hadrian’s people raise it as evidence that you are—what is the fashionable formulation?—unreliable at boundaries.”

“Then tell them boundaries are where my family has done most of its building.” Lentulus opens his eyes. The ceiling sweats in patterns no architect intended. “No. Do not tell Athens epigrams. Tell them I accept work where competence can be tested by whether people can drink.”

“That,” Acilius says, “is nearly an epigram.”

“Then let it be entered as health.”

“I shall tell Athens you are wanted on aqueducts.”

“Aqueducts,” Lentulus says, with feeling, “are the one public work a Cornelius can supervise where what flows downhill is only water.”

The aqueduct appointment comes with a staff that smells of wet lime and does not care about his grandfather except when the name must be used to unlock a store yard. This is good for everyone. On the third morning, out beyond the walls where the arcades stride over market gardens like a sentence the landscape has agreed to remember, Lentulus walks the inspection path with the Campanian engineer and a clerk whose tablets are warped by years of outdoor truth.

“Here,” the engineer says, and taps an arch with the intimacy of a physician. A fine mist of mortar descends. “The maps call this restored.”

“Was it?”

“It was paid for. In Rome the verbs are cousins, not twins.”

They find the leak not by the grand damp stain a senator could point to but by a change in the voices of women filling jars at a fountain below: pressure sighing at certain hours, disappearing when the villas above take their evening share. Lentulus listens to a woman with a child tied to her back and a jar on her hip explain exactly when the water fails. She does so without civic vocabulary and with a precision his committee papers, at their most expensive, rarely achieved.

“She should be on the board,” he says.

“She is,” says the engineer. “Unofficially. All the fountains are.”

By midsummer he has developed opinions about settling tanks. By late summer, worse still from the point of view of the baths, he can tell whether a contractor is lying about lead by watching where his eyes go when a measurement is read back. Marcus comes out once under the excuse of riding and stays three hours because nobody expects a senator’s heir to be useful and the novelty has a freedom in it. He holds a survey cord. He gets lime on a boot. He asks the engineer whether water minds the names carved on the arches.

“Water minds the gradient,” says the engineer. “Names are for the leaking to occur under.”

Marcus looks at his father in the hard roadside light. Lentulus is in a plain cloak, arguing good-humouredly about access rights with a market gardener who has planted beans in a maintenance strip and intends to defend them as a second harvest of liberty. There is no proconsular glamour to it. There is a man being improved by facts that will not march in a procession.

“You like this,” Marcus says on the ride home.

“I like arriving at things that are supposed to arrive.”

“Father, that is perilously close to a motto.”

“Then we will not carve it.”

They pass one of the repaired fountains. A boy is drinking straight from the spout with his whole face, ignoring the cup his sister offers in the name of civilisation. Lentulus watches for a moment longer than a curator’s duty requires. It comes into his head that a monument can be made of not noticing: nobody, in a year, will praise the water for being there. Anonymous success. His ancestors would have required smelling salts.

That night he writes in the plain notebook: *Work that disappears when done properly.* Underneath, after an interval in which he hears Cornelia from the Avidian house only by the intermediary of letters and Marcus’s unfinished drafts from the next room, he adds: *Not all legacies are inscriptions.*

The second sentence displeases him as sententious. He leaves it. Improvement does not have to be elegantly expressed to be entered.

He does not rage, which the household finds more alarming than rage and Servilia finds, correctly, elegiac. He takes the aqueduct work with the whole force of a disappointed competence; inside the year, two leaking arcades on the road to Praeneste will bear, in small unglamorous lettering no tourist will ever photograph with their mouth open, the name of the curator who made the water arrive where the maps had always claimed it did. Marcus, watching his father choose maintenance over monument, writes the fifth draft of his letter, in which the Greek is suddenly half the length and the sincerity twice, and sends it across the river by Barbatus — who charges, as messenger, one new-mended cloak strap, the house's real currency — with no seal and his own name, which costs him more than any seal in the collections of his ancestors.

The drafts occupy Marcus longer than most young men’s love affairs, and with a better outcome for the mail. The third he keeps. The fourth is the third rewritten after an evening at the baths where Hostilius, now paid in real money and therefore spiritually unemployed, tries to be witty about “fullers’ fortunes” and discovers that Marcus’s silence has acquired edges.

“You used to feed me,” Hostilius complains.

“I have taken up ballast.”

“It makes you very poor company for a creditor’s former art.”

“I shall enter the review.”

He goes home and cuts from the fourth draft every sentence that sounds as though it were written to be overheard by posterity. What remains has air around it. It says he knows what he knows late. It says he stood at the edge of her father’s funeral and is not asking to be forgiven for either presence or absence. It says her correction in the dining room was the first time anyone had spoken of his family as if it were a family and that he has since been trying to learn the grammar.

He writes *sister* once and nearly removes it. It stands, because removal would be another kind of theft: asking the document to pretend it does not know why it exists.

The fifth draft happens after the aqueduct ride. Marcus sees a sentence about *the house I was born to* and crosses it out so hard the stylus complains. In its place he writes *the rooms I was allowed to enter without asking*. It is not perfect. It is nearly true, which in his education has usually been the sign of a draft approaching usefulness.

Barbatus is selected as messenger not because he is discreet—Barbatus is discreet in the way mountains are quiet—but because he is already a known quantity on both banks and cannot be mistaken for an embassy. He is found at the Caelian door saddling an argument with a wine carter.

“Young master,” he says. “The street is an arrangement the whole mistress admires.”

“I am not coming in.” Marcus shows the folded letter. No seal. His name in full on the outside, a nakedness of address that makes the sight of it almost indecent. “For Prima. At her own house, if that is right. Not at the guild. Not at dinner anywhere. If she won’t take it, bring it back.”

Barbatus looks at the letter and then, more significantly, at Marcus’s hands, which are performing less than they once did. “And the fee.”

“My cloak strap is broken.”

“It is now, maybe. A strap and a delivery. Mended by the house needlewoman at trade rates. Unless the young master wants his messages travelling like his family used to: unnamed, at other people’s charges.”

Marcus hands over the cloak with the air of a man paying a small tax to become a citizen of the real. “Tell her—”

“No.” Barbatus takes the letter carefully, as if it were a sword being handed edge-first by someone who has recently learned not to wave it. “Letters do their own telling. That’s what the figures with the ink invented them for.”

At home the announcement of the Syrian appointment is received with a discipline that would have impressed a legion. Onesimus carries the official summary on a tray, as if bad news also benefits from crockery. Lentulus reads it standing, gives no speech, and asks whether the kitchen knows Marcus will be late. This is so far from the expected senatorial response that the steward reports a *quietness*, a word the household runs on for two days like an emergency ration.

Servilia waits until evening. She finds her husband not among the masks—that would be the old theatre—but in the workroom where aqueduct returns are being copied in several hands, competently, into duplicates meant to survive both fire and vanity.

“Do you want condolence in the northern manner,” she asks, “or shall I have a district flooded so that you can be needed beautifully?”

“Are those the options in our marriage now?”

“They are the ones with paperwork.”

He lays down a schedule. “I wanted it, Servilia. Let that be entered. I wanted the province, the army, the opportunity to be Gnaeus Cornelius Lentulus at a great distance from the evidence. Then the year happened at home. It is difficult to outsource a conscience once it has acquired local staff.”

She looks at the returns: channels, leaks, an arcade whose repair will cause a market gardener to correspond, a fountain pressure table annotated in Lentulus own hand. His writing has become smaller and more legible. She has never mentioned noticing.

“The appointment will give the baths three days,” she says. “Then Hadrian will praise a building and the public interest will put on sandals. What will you do on the fourth day?”

“Inspect a settling tank.”

“Then we are not ruined.”

It is so close to tenderness that both look elsewhere. Servilia picks up one of the copies and points to a figure. “This is wrong.”

“It is an estimate.”

“It is wrong in the optimistic direction, which in a husband used to be called policy.”

“Correct it.”

“I beg your pardon?”

“You have the pen in all but furniture, in this house. Correct it.”

She does. The clerk, who has gone very still, later tells Onesimus that Rome ended quietly in a workroom and nobody bothered to notify the provinces. Onesimus instructs him to forget at the professional standard. The figure remains corrected.

Acilius’s letter to Athens is a masterpiece of the second column. It reports that Gnaeus Cornelius Lentulus has accepted heavy responsibility for the city’s water; that his health, though not delicate, no longer invites provinces to compete for it; that the famous drafting hand is now employed on estimates whose consequences can be tasted. It suggests, without saying, that a man who has stopped applying for exile in the form of honour may be of more use to an emperor designing a reign than another polished governor with a clean procession of secrets.

Lentulus sees the copy because Acilius, who is old and honest in the expensive way, sends it entire. “You make me sound like a ladle,” he says.

“You should hear yourself in other people’s versions. Half of them have you building a temple to freedwomen, which even I think hasty before lunch.”

They are walking after a hearing through a porticoed street where petitioners cluster like weather at a temple. A freedwoman with a folder under her arm is arguing cheerfully with a Secretary. The word *clause* crosses the marble at a distance. Lentulus does not turn. The discipline pleases Acilius.

“Hadrian will remember you,” he says.

“That is not necessarily the mercy it advertises itself as.”

“No emperor’s memory is. But it is better than being remembered only by column height.”

In the Forum, work on the frieze is high now above readable distance. Figures climb and fall around the shaft in a sequence designed by men who assume posterity owns ladders. Lentulus and Acilius stand for a moment with other hereditary spectators, heads back, mouths not quite open because they have been trained by ancestry to congratulate the future without looking hungry.

“I used to think that was how to survive,” Lentulus says.

“What, spiral?”

“To keep telling the same story upward until nobody could ask whose hands did the carrying.”

Acilius watches a crane’s rope take the weight. Men shout in several provinces. “You are becoming a difficult companion at monuments, Gnaeus. Your conversation is all underneath things.”

“I have had a year of plumbing.”

“Write that to Athens.”

“I shall let you. My hand has caused enough provincial correspondence.”

They walk on towards a district where a fountain is failing. At the corner, a news-seller cries an inaccurate summary of appointments, embroidered with titles that may not survive the afternoon. The Syrian appointee’s name comes second in the list and attracts no theatrical attention whatsoever. It is, Lentulus notices, almost possible to hear his own prospects becoming history, and history is quieter than ambition expected.

There is one public humiliation still to metabolise, of the small daily kind by which empires teach disappointment to sit up straight. At a dedication of a repaired basin near the Praeneste road, a local magistrate begins by praising the emperor, proceeds through the Senate and arrives at the curator with a phrase clearly designed by a rival: “Gnaeus Cornelius Lentulus, whose eastern hopes have been so usefully *domesticated*—”

A silence the size of the basin follows. The water runs. It is very clear.

Lentulus rises from the magistrate’s borrowed chair and thanks the contractors by name. He thanks the women of the fountain association—there is no association, and now, because a senator has said it on a public morning, there may have to be—for keeping the queue orderly while pressure was restored. He thanks the engineer last, which is either humility or accurate billing. He does not mention Syria. The magistrate’s phrase dies in the sunlight for lack of a wound willing to carry it.

Marcus, in attendance because Servilia has decided heirs should see dignity done without cavalry, watches an old retainer of his father’s weep fractionally into a cup. “They used to cheer,” the old man says.

Marcus looks at the fountain, where children have already resumed the national sport of getting wet in defiance of garments. “They are cheering. The instrumentation has changed.”

Afterward Lentulus is shown the inscription proposed for the arcade: small, as promised, but with his full tria nomina doing their ancient semaphore. He asks for the engineer’s name to be given the same size. The clerk says this is not customary. The engineer says it is unnecessary. The market gardener whose beans survived negotiations says if names are being distributed at a useful size, the fountain women would like their neighbourhood spelled correctly, as it is wrong on two official maps.

By the time the party rides home, the inscription includes a corrected locality. It is a triumph so minor that Rome’s historians would have to break their style to record it. Lentulus finds that he can live unrecorded at this scale. The water can be heard from the road behind them, doing the oldest public work of all: arriving where people need it without asking for applause in Greek.

By the time the next letters come from the east, the theatre of expectation has changed its programme. Hadrian’s name is now pronounced less as a rumour of the future and more as a climate other men are learning to plan roofs for. Acilius sends Lentulus a paragraph, copied out whole rather than summarised: an approval, cool but exact, of curators who attend to the city’s arteries, and a sentence about laws being most Roman when they allow the state to know where its own pressure is being stolen.

“Does he mean me?” Lentulus asks.

“He means Hadrian,” Acilius writes in the margin. “Do not let it inflate the pipes.”

Lentulus reads it twice in the study, with the dented mirror on the desk and a fresh aqueduct schedule beneath it. Once, access to an emperor’s marginal mind would have been the whole object: a sign that the house still stood in the main current. Now the sentence matters to him for a different reason. Somewhere in the elegant machinery of an accomplished collector of worlds, the *pressure* metaphor has found the same broken channel that freedwomen, clerks and fountain women have been reporting at human scale.

He writes back three lines. No more. He thanks Acilius, declines to interpret imperial weather as a personal province, and reports that a leak near the Praeneste road has been found by the testimony of women who time their mornings by it. Then he takes the three lines to Servilia for inspection, an act that would once have required a civil war to extract.

She changes one adjective. “*Steady*,” she says, “not *loyal*. Athens will know the difference and charge accordingly.”

He copies the letter again in his own hand. The hand is smaller now. It no longer reaches the edge of the page as if the margin were a frontier to be personally suppressed.

Outside, the column continues upward under its scaffolding, the war becoming architecture at a steady rate. Somewhere below it, the city’s less visible works are proceeding at the speed of maintenance: a repaired pipe, a corrected figure, a letter answered in Latin. History, if it is watching at all, has stopped demanding that every meaningful event come with a statue on top.

Prima reads it at her table, twice, with the baker's bread cooling beside her in rows like the Forum's columns, and then, as advised, puts it with her tools. She writes back in Latin without a single Greek word in it, four lines: that she is well; that the shop will bake for the guild feast; that he is not to give her sister Cornelia messages he lacks the standing to deliver; and that if he wishes to be *kin* — she has the word from Daphne by now, the plumbing having delivered that too, disreputably and clean — he can begin by doing nothing where her name is spoken at his family's table, noisily, for about nineteen years. “After that,” the letter ends, “we will see what the figures say. It is apparently the family poetry.”

The letter reaches Prima in the bakery yard because Barbatus, who understands borders, stops there rather than carrying Palatine ink into the fullers’ house by any threshold that might be made to testify. He has changed out of Caelian door livery, insofar as a man of his weather can be said to wear anything but himself. He gives the folded paper to Titus first—not its contents, its existence—as one man warning another of a load coming through a narrow gate.

“From the young master,” he says. “No seal. Says if she won’t take it, it comes back to him unopened and nobody has spent her.”

Titus, who has flour to one elbow and a wedding being urban-planned by his mother, looks at the letter as at an unfamiliar tool. “Is that the custom in great houses?”

“It is this year. Customs are weakening nobly all over the city.”

Prima has come from the house with a bowl of water for the outdoor basin. She sees the name on the outside. The yard continues—ovens breathing, a cart being coaxed, Mother Livia somewhere issuing a fatwa on bench placement—but inside the circle of the three of them a smaller Rome forms: letter, messenger, betrothed, recipient.

“I’ll take it,” Prima says. She dries her hands first. Barbatus notices and files it under generalship.

Titus does not ask to read it. He does not ask later, either, which is why, after she has read it twice at her own table, Prima tells him the shape of it herself. Not every line. Enough that no third party can later rearrange him into a narrator without documents.

“He says he is learning grammar,” she says. “I am considering my fees.”

“Charge them in advance. That family has a history.”

She writes the reply on paper bought for the trousseau lists, then copies it onto better paper when she sees that her first hand has made *kin* look like a price tag. It is not a long letter. Long letters, she has decided, are what men write when they hope the recipient will do the work of summary on their behalf. Hers does its own.

Mother Livia finds her at it during the bread’s second rising. “You are writing in the careful hand,” she says. “Who is to be governed?”

“Marcus.”

“Good. Govern briefly. Men retain more.” She does not lean to read. Mother Livia’s respect takes the form of not putting her experienced shadow between a woman and fresh ink. “And after he has it?”

“Nothing is owed except the baking.”

“Mark that down as peace.”

Barbatus returns in the late afternoon for the mended cloak strap. The reply is folded, unsealed in its turn, which appears to strike him as a fine piece of return fire. “Any message to be said around it?”

“No.”

“Letters do their own telling,” he says, satisfied. “I had it from an unreliable source.”

Barbatus delivers the answer at the Palatine’s garden gate, not the atrium door, because a man can spend twenty years in service and still develop new specialisms. Marcus is there at the appointed hour in travelling clothes for a journey of forty paces back into his own house. The folded paper has no seal. It has only his name on it, written in a hand that makes no attempt to enchant him.

He does not open it on the street. He takes it to his room and reads it by the window over the garden, twice. The first time he reads for what is forbidden; the second for what is permitted, which is less and therefore more.

*Nineteen years of doing nothing, noisily.* He hears her say it in the tone of the dining room, when she was only a needlewoman who had done the unforgivable thing of being his family at him in public without papers. It is not condemnation. It is an estimate. Do the work at your own table, where no one can applaud you for it.

He goes down to dinner and behaves with such ordinary moderation that Servilia becomes alert. Lentulus, too, can see an unopened weather system at the table. Neither asks. The discipline is still new enough to be felt, like shoes before they take the shape of governing.

Afterwards Marcus knocks at his father’s study. Knock: another novelty. Lentulus looks up from a report on a fountain that has opinions.

“She wrote back,” Marcus says.

“I see.”

“Four lines. In Latin.” He smiles before he can arrest the development. “No Greek, Father. It is possible I have been overpaying for sincerity by the syllable.”

“A family weakness. We are reducing imports.”

“Am I to answer?”

Lentulus looks towards the desk where a fresh sheet had once covered a draft. “What does the letter say about the future?”

“It leaves the figures for later.”

“Then perhaps the answer is time.”

Marcus has no counter-proposal fit to be minuted. On his way out he pauses in the corridor, where the masks have had another season of being cleaned into surreal attentiveness. He says to the one whose face most resembles his own impatience: “Nothing, then. Noisily.”

Onesimus, passing below with a lamp, hears and forgets correctly. The household decides within hours that the young master has indeed become religious, though the cult appears to involve cups left unfinished for reasons no priest has yet been able to retail.

Barbatus tells it at the Caelian three days later, because doors have their own postal service and he has never seen the point in charging for air. He tells it in the courtyard while a carter is being unloaded and Eirene is close enough to audit metaphors. His version has a gate, a cup neither accepted too soon nor left meaningfully full, and Marcus saying “nothing, noisily” to a corridor.

“That last bit,” Eirene says, “you have improved.”

“A story should be paid in kind.”

Antonia hears the shape of it while going through the linen-room invoices for Tuesday. She does not ask Barbatus what the letters said. He would not know; more importantly, Prima has not told her, and Antonia is not going to install a customs office between a girl and her first family correspondence. Certain doors in this new arrangement are opened only from the side where the girl stands.

On Tuesday Prima arrives with summer estimates and a strip of braid she dislikes so intelligently that Diomedes has to sit down. Nothing in her manner advertises a letter. The work proceeds. The soup policy is observed without being named. Then, over a torn length that has belonged for years to the practice basket and will probably outlive the Republic, she says: “He wrote well enough.”

Antonia’s hands continue. This is the entire reward of practice baskets.

“Did he?”

“Less Greek than threatened. I wrote back in Latin. It seemed only fair to charge in a currency he can count.”

“And has Rome fallen?”

“The ovens are functioning.” Prima ties off the practice seam. “I told him not to tell Cornelia anything he cannot carry.”

“It is an expensive family, theirs, for messages.”

“They keep trying to send people instead.” The sentence, offered without ceremony, goes into the room like a measured length. Antonia wants to warn her, praise her, invoice the centuries. Instead she picks up the braid whose colour has just been condemned and says: “Then we shall charge by the piece, Prima, and keep the thread an honest red.”

Prima gives the laugh—wry, brief, her own—that no longer needs to be checked against anyone's attic. “You sound like my mother when she approved of something and meant to deny it later.”

“I shall take that as a reference from the senior management.”

“Take it as a warning. She counted everything twice.”

“Then we are safe at the second count.”

Marcus keeps this letter in the scroll-case under his grandfather's mask, in the sticking cupboard, where sealed tablets ripen. The household, which thinks in plumbing, decides that the young master has become religious. Servilia, who knows the cupboard's exact inventory of the century, instructs no one about it at all. The masks look down. One day, Antonia reflects when the story completes its migration to the Caelian — by way of Barbatus, who tells it as a fight he once saw in which nobody died, the rarest kind he knows — one day someone will open all the cupboards of that family at once, and the smell of old wax and newer ink will describe Rome better than the column will, whatever height it reaches.
