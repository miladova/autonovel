# Ten

## What Remains

The train to Krasnystaw left Lublin at 6.40 in the morning and took two hours and eleven minutes, and she had a compartment to herself for the first hour.

She had bought a second-class ticket and a paper and a bun, and she sat by the window with her coat on and watched the country come up out of the dark. It was flat and white and the snow lay in the furrows, and there were crows on the telegraph wires, and here and there a farm with a light in the window and smoke going up straight. In 1946 she had travelled this line four times a month in a cattle truck with a propaganda gramophone and a box of leaflets, and had been happy, and had believed that the country was being pulled out of the nineteenth century by its collar.

They came into Krasnystaw at ten to nine. The station was small and had been rebuilt, and there was a new concrete platform and a man sweeping the snow off it with a besom, and a woman selling hot tea from a samovar on a table.

She had not been in the town since 1953. It had grown. There were three new blocks on the Lublin road with balconies, and the square had been paved, and the church had a new roof of copper that had gone green, and there was a cinema called the *Iskra* where the old synagogue had been, and she stood in front of it for a moment and then went on.

The clinic was in a low building behind the hospital, and she asked at the desk for Maria Szeliga, and the woman said, "Second door, the dressing station," and she went down the corridor and stood in the doorway.

Maria Szeliga was nineteen years old and had her father's heavy jaw and her mother's hands, and she was changing a dressing on an old man's leg, and she did it competently and without tenderness, and she said, "Sit down, Panie, it will be a minute." Then she looked up.

"You are not a patient."

"No."

"Then wait in the corridor."

"I'll wait here."

Maria Szeliga finished the dressing, and helped the old man with his trouser leg, and saw him to the door, and then turned round and folded her arms.

"Well?"

"My name is Zofia Krawiec."

The name did nothing. Maria waited.

"I was born at Brzozówka. My father was Józef Krawiec, the smith."

Still nothing, and then something — a flicker, the smallest movement of the eyes, an old story heard at a table and half forgotten.

"Krawiec. The one who was taken."

"Yes."

"And your mother and the little boy were killed."

"Yes."

Maria Szeliga looked at her for a moment. "I remember my father talking about your father. They were in the peasant party together." She picked up a kidney dish and put it down. "What do you want? If it is about a pension, it is the office on the first floor and they will tell you it is not their business."

"It is not about a pension." Zofia put her handbag on the chair and stood with her hands at her sides. "On the fourteenth of June 1944 I was detained by the gendarmerie at Krasnystaw. I was seventeen. They questioned me in a room in the post on the Lublin road for five hours, and at the end of it I told them where a wounded Soviet soldier was hidden in my father's cellar. On the same day the village was burned as a reprisal. Thirty-one people were killed." She paused. "In the autumn there was a meeting in the church porch, and the village decided that your father had informed, because he had been seen talking to the gendarmes at Krasnystaw on the twelfth. He had been talking to them about a permit for the mill. I was at that meeting. I was standing at the back. I said nothing."

The room was quiet. Outside in the corridor someone dropped a metal tray and swore.

Maria Szeliga did not move for a long moment. Then she sat down on the stool by the dressing table, slowly, as if her legs had been taken away, and looked at the floor.

"You said nothing," she said.

"I said nothing."

"For five years."

"Fourteen."

"And now you have come to tell me."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Zofia had thought about this on the train for two hours and eleven minutes, and had prepared several answers, and discarded all of them, and in the end she said the true one.

"Because I am about to stop being a person who can be useful to you," she said. "On the first of December I was removed from my post. On the fourth I was excluded from the Party. In January I will be a clerk in a museum at Olsztyn with a salary of two thousand one hundred złoty and no influence over anything at all. If I had come to you in 1950 I could have had your father's name cleared in the records of the district, and I could have got you a flat and a telephone and a place for your son at the school in Lublin." She stopped. "I did not come in 1950. I came now, when I have nothing to give you, and I have come because a priest told me to and because I could not do it while I still had something to lose, and I know exactly how that sounds."

Maria Szeliga looked up.

"It sounds," she said, "like the truth."

"Yes."

"It also sounds like a coward."

"Yes," Zofia said. "It is."

Maria stood up. She was not crying. She went to the window and stood with her back to the room, and her shoulders were rigid, and she stood there for what seemed to Zofia a very long time.

"He hanged himself in the barn," she said at last, to the window. "In March 1953. I was fourteen. I found him. He had been in the barn since the morning and I came home from school and I found him, and I got the ladder and I cut him down, and he was still warm, and I sat on the floor of that barn with him for an hour before I went for anybody, because I did not want them to see him like that." She turned. "That is what you did not say at the meeting. That is what your silence was worth. Not a hangman. A ladder and a fourteen-year-old girl on the floor of a barn."

Zofia did not say anything. There was nothing to say.

"I am not going to forgive you," Maria said. "I want to be clear about it. I am not going to say that I understand, and I am not going to say that it was the war, and I am not going to say that you were seventeen, because I have heard all of those things about my father from people in this town for five years and they have never once made the barn any smaller. You will go away and you will have told me and you will feel that you have done something, and I will be here, and in a week I will still be here." She wiped her hands on her apron, once, hard. "Do you have children?"

"No."

"Then you will not know what it is to have a child find that."

"No," Zofia said. "I won't."

Maria Szeliga stood there. Then she said, in a different voice, quieter and much worse, "What was my father like? Before. In 1944. I was three."

Zofia looked at her.

"He was the deputy sołtys," she said. "He had a grey coat with the collar turned up and he was always the first man at a fire. He carried me out of the way when the village burned. He picked me up round the waist and carried me backwards and he was shouting at me that the orchard was on fire, and I did not understand him, and he carried me anyway, and then he held me against his coat while I screamed, and he did not let go, and he was the only person in Brzozówka who did anything at all that day." She stopped. "And then the village took it away from him, and I let them, and I am the only person alive who could have stopped it, and I did not."

Maria Szeliga nodded slowly. She looked at the floor.

"Say his name," she said.

"Antoni Szeliga."

"Again."

"Antoni Szeliga. Deputy sołtys of Brzozówka. He carried me out of the fire and he was innocent, and the village was wrong, and I was wrong, and I have known it every day for fourteen years."

Maria turned back to the window.

"Go away now," she said. "I have a dressing at eleven."

Zofia picked up her handbag. At the door she said, "If you ever want—"

"No," Maria said. "I will not want. That is the thing you have to understand. There is nothing you can do for me now, and that is the whole of your punishment, and I did not arrange it, and I am not sorry." She turned. "But I will tell you one thing, and then you will go. For five years I have thought that the man who did it was a German, or a Jew, or a partisan, or the government. And now I know it was a girl of seventeen in a room with a wasp. And I find that it is easier. Do you know why? Because a German is weather. A girl of seventeen is a person, and a person can be told, and now I have told you, and now it is yours and not mine." She picked up the kidney dish. "Goodbye, Pani Krawiec."

Zofia went out into the snow.

***

She walked to Brzozówka. It was nine kilometres and there was a bus at two but she did not wait for it. The road went out of the town past the sugar factory and the new housing and then between fields, and the snow had stopped and the sky had cleared and the light was the hard white light of a Polish December. She walked for two hours and met no one but a man with a horse and a cart of wood, who nodded.

***

The village had been rebuilt, and it had not.

There were houses where the houses had been, but fewer, and they were smaller, and they were brick and concrete instead of wood and thatch. The mill was gone and there was a cooperative shed in its place with a tractor standing outside it under a tarpaulin. The shop was a shop again and it was closed at midday. The school had been rebuilt in 1951 and had two classrooms and a plaque on the wall. The crossroads was paved. There was a bus shelter of grey concrete with a bench in it and a poster for a film.

And there was the church.

It was brick, and it was not beautiful, and it had been built in 1957 on the footprint of the old one with the money of the parish and a grant from the Office for Religious Affairs, and the roof was the roof she had signed for. The spire was new and did not lean. There was no bell in it. There was a place for a bell, and a beam, and no bell, and a notice board by the door with the times of Masses and a list of the dead of the parish from 1939 to 1945, thirty-nine names, and near the bottom of the second column, in letters cut small to make them fit: *KRAWIEC ANIELA · KRAWIEC PIOTR · FRYDMAN ICEK · FRYDMAN RYWKA · FRYDMAN SARA.*

She stood in front of the board for a long time.

Then she went to the place where the house had been.

It was a piece of ground between two new houses, about twenty paces by fifteen, and it had not been built on. There was a pear tree at the bottom of it, old and crooked and still standing, and there was a low wall of the old foundation coming up out of the snow, and that was all. The forge was gone entirely. There was a hollow in the ground where the cellar had been, filled with snow and the dead stalks of nettles. Somebody had put a fence around the plot, a good fence, and there was a gate, and the gate was not locked.

She went in and stood in the middle of it.

It was one o'clock. She stood there for an hour, and the cold came up through her shoes and into her legs, and she did not move. She thought about the kitchen, and where the table had been, and the stove, and the picture of the Sacred Heart, and the palm cross behind it from Palm Sunday 1935. She thought about her mother singing "Serdeczna Matko" and going off the note on the third line. She thought about Piotrek, who would have been twenty-two in 1958, and who would have been called up in 1957, and who might have been a smith, or a teacher, or a man in a cooperative with a wife and two children, and who was eight years old forever in a table in a German report.

She thought about the boy who had pulled her out of the water and shouted at her for ten minutes and then said he was sorry.

At two o'clock she turned round and went out and closed the gate behind her.

***

The parish priest was called Father Kulesza and he was fifty and had been at Brzozówka since 1949, and he was in the sacristy when she knocked, and he was expecting, that week, a great many things, but not her.

"Pani Krawiec? From the Office?"

"Not any more."

"Ah." He was a practical man with a red face. "I heard something. There is always something." He wiped his hands on a cloth. "You will stay for the Mass tomorrow? His Excellency is coming. The new bishop. He is from this parish, you know. He was born here."

"I know."

"He is coming to say his first Mass in the parish. It is a great honour for us. We have no bell, unfortunately, so we cannot ring it, but the children will sing." He looked at her. "Did you know him? You are from here."

"Yes," she said. "I knew him."

***

On the eighth of December the consecration was held at Lublin.

She had decided on the train and again in the room at the inn at Krasnystaw, lying awake with the stove ticking, that she would not go. She was not a Catholic in good standing, or a friend, or an official, or anything at all; she was a woman of thirty-one with a suitcase who had been excluded from the Party four days earlier, and there was no place for her in that cathedral that would not require an explanation. She decided this four times between Friday and Monday. Then on Monday morning she got up and put on her good suit and took the 6.10 to Lublin and stood at the back with her hat in her hands.

It was full. There were three hundred priests and nine bishops and a great many nuns and the seminarians in the front, and the whole of the nave was full of people standing, and there were people standing in the side aisles and in the porch and out on the steps in the snow, and the singing was enormous. She stood against the pillar by the door where the light came in from the west window, and she could see the high altar and the throne and the bishops in their copes.

It took three hours.

She saw the procession come in, and the bishops, and a great press of priests in black, and she looked for the Primate among the mitres and could not tell which of them he was. She saw the candidate come in last, in a white alb with his hands joined, and he looked very young and very pale and entirely composed, and he did not look at the congregation, and she found that she was glad.

She heard the Litany of the Saints, which she remembered from the Forty Hours in 1938, and the psalms, and the long Latin. She saw the laying on of hands, when the bishops came one by one and put their hands on his head in silence, and the cathedral was so quiet that she could hear a woman near her breathing. She saw the book of the Gospels held open over his head by two priests while the consecrating bishop said the prayer. She saw the anointing of his hands with chrism, and the linen cloths wound round them, and the ring put on his finger, and the mitre, and the crosier put into his hand, and she heard the acclamation when he was led to the throne and the organ went up and the whole cathedral sang.

She did not cry. She stood against the pillar with her hat in her hands and her coat over her arm, and she watched a man she had known since he was twelve be made a bishop in the cathedral of Lublin, and she thought: I have signed a great many papers, and one of them was this.

Afterwards she waited until the church was empty. The sacristan came and looked at her and she said, "A moment," and he went away and came back ten minutes later and said, "Pani, we are closing," and she said, "Yes," and went out.

***

The tenth of December was a Wednesday and cold, and the Mass at Brzozówka was at eleven.

There were four hundred people in a church built for three hundred, and the children's choir sang, and the parish had put up a platform outside for the ones who could not get in, and there was a microphone, which did not work, and Father Kulesza announced that His Excellency would preach, and His Excellency preached for nineteen minutes on the text *Nolite timere, pusillus grex*, and he preached well, in the way that a man preaches who has been doing it for five years and has something to say, and twice he stopped and looked at the church, and once he said, in the middle of a sentence about the dead of the parish, "I was baptized in the old church on this ground, and I served at that altar as a boy, and the men who built this one are in the churchyard, and some of them were not old men when they died."

Then it was over and there was a great deal of noise.

She waited outside by the wall of the churchyard where the dead were, in her grey coat, with her hands in her pockets, and the crowd came out and stood about in the snow in groups, and the children ran, and the old women crowded round the bishop and kissed his ring, and he was patient with them, and bent his head, and said something to each one.

He saw her over the heads of the crowd. She was twenty paces away, beyond the wall, and she did not move.

He finished with the old women. He spoke to Father Kulesza. Then he came through the gate in his black coat and his hat and the ring on his finger, and he stood in front of her.

"Pani Dyrektor."

"Your Excellency."

"Don't."

"Congratulations," she said. "It was a good sermon. The microphone was broken but it was a good sermon."

"Nineteen minutes. Kulesza told me twelve. I could see him at the side counting." He looked at her. "You were in Lublin. I saw you at the pillar."

"You couldn't have. I was at the back."

"The light from the west window was behind you and you were the only person in that church not wearing a hat," he said. "I have known where you were standing since ten past eleven on Sunday." He put his hands in his pockets. "Walk with me. Not through the village. Down to the water."

They went by the back lane, past the cooperative shed, along the path that went behind the orchard, and the snow was deep and unbroken and they walked in it single file for part of the way and side by side where the path widened. The river was low and dark between white banks, and the alders were there, and the fallen willow was gone, and the pool was frozen at the edges.

They stood on the bank.

"It's smaller," she said.

"Everything is smaller."

"No. It is actually smaller. They straightened the channel in 1952. There was a cooperative works project." She looked at the water. "I read the file."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. It is a fact about a river." She put her hands deeper in her pockets. "Tomasz. I have to tell you what happened, and then I have to go and get the four o'clock bus, and I would like to do it in that order."

"All right."

"I went to Maria Szeliga at Krasnystaw on Sunday. I told her. She did not forgive me. I did not expect her to and I did not ask her to. I told her what her father did on the fourteenth of June and I said his name out loud and she made me say it twice." She looked at him. "I stood in the place where the house was for an hour. It was the coldest hour of my life and it was not long enough. And on the first of December I was removed from my post, and on the fourth I was excluded from the Party, and in January I am going to Olsztyn to catalogue agricultural machinery in a museum with a leaking roof, and I have a room in the hotel there and Jerzy is staying in Warsaw because he will not leave the Commission, and that is all of it, and there is nothing else."

He was quiet for a moment.

"Nothing else?"

"Nothing else."

"The Party," he said. "You resigned before they excluded you."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because I wanted it to be a decision and not a punishment," she said. "It is the only thing I have ever done that was not a punishment."

He looked at the water. Then he said, "I have a ring on my finger that was put there by a bishop in front of three thousand people, and it means that I am the successor of the apostles, and that I am to be the shepherd of a diocese, and that I may not marry, and that I answer to God for the souls in my care." He turned his hand over. "And in my breast pocket there is a breviary, and inside the breviary there is nothing at all, because I have not yet put anything in it, and I am telling you this because I want you to give me something to put in it."

She looked at him.

"The ring," he said.

She took it out. It was on a string, and she had worn it since October 1943, and it had left a green mark on her skin for fifteen years, and she had it in her hand.

"It's not gold," she said.

"It's better than gold. Gold is soft."

She laughed, once, and it came out of her all at once, like a door banging, and then she put the ring in his hand and closed his fingers over it, and he held it for a moment, and then put it in his breast pocket.

"There," he said.

"That was not a sacrament."

"No," he said. "It was a piece of wire. But I will carry it until I die, and when I die someone will find it in my breviary and will not know what it is, and that is exactly right."

They stood there. The light was going. Up in the village a dog barked and stopped, and someone called a child in.

"Tomasz. I want to go to confession."

He looked at her.

"Now?"

"Now. Before the bus." She met his eyes. "You said not yet. On the twenty-first of November you said not yet, and not like this. I am no longer a member of the Party. I am no longer an officer of the state. I am a woman of thirty-one with a suitcase and a post at Olsztyn, and I have no power over anyone, and I cannot do anything for you or against you, and I have come to the end of what I can give up." She said it steadily. "So. Not yet has become now. Or it has not, and you will tell me, and I will get the bus."

He looked at her for a long moment.

"Come to the church," he said.

They went back up the lane in the dark with the snow squeaking under their feet, and the church was empty and cold, and Father Kulesza had banked the stove in the sacristy and gone to his dinner. There was one candle burning at the side altar and the red lamp.

He put on the stole in the sacristy and sat down in the confessional, and she knelt on the other side of the grille in the dark, and it was much colder than St Anne's had been.

"Bless me, Father, for I have sinned. It has been nineteen days since my last confession."

"Go on."

"I have already told you everything," she said. "I am not going to tell it again. I told it on the twenty-first of November and I have not done anything since except go to a woman in Krasnystaw and stand in a field. So I will say it short. I was seventeen and I was frightened and I gave a man a cellar, and thirty-one people died, and I have carried it for fourteen years and made a career out of it. I named a man in 1949 and it went into a file. I gave evidence against a man in 1951 and he was shot. I let an innocent man be destroyed in 1944 and he hanged himself in a barn in 1953 while I was in Warsaw being promoted." She paused. "And I have been proud. That is the one I have only just found. I have been proud for fourteen years, Father, of being a woman who carried a heavy thing well. I have been proud of my own guilt. It has been the last thing I owned and I have polished it."

The grille was silent for a moment.

"Anything else?"

"I let my husband love me for five years without looking at him."

"Anything else?"

"No."

"Then make your act of contrition."

She folded her hands on the shelf.

"Boże mój, żałuję za grzechy moje z całego serca, ponieważ zasłużyłem na karę od Ciebie—" She stopped. "I don't believe in God."

"I know."

"You said to say it anyway."

"Say it anyway."

She said it. All of it, in Polish, the whole formula, slowly, and it took a long time, and her voice was not steady and it did not matter, and at the end she said, "Amen."

He said the words in Latin, quietly, with his hand raised, and she heard them through the grille, and they were the oldest words she knew, older than the Party, older than the state, older than the report with the table in it.

"Ego te absolvo a peccatis tuis, in nomine Patris, et Filii, et Spiritus Sancti. Amen."

"Amen," she said.

She came out of the confessional and stood in the aisle, and he came out after her and took off the stole and folded it, and they stood in the empty church in the dark with one candle burning.

"For the penance," he said, "I want you to eat something. You are thin and you have been thin since October and I have been looking at it."

"That is not a penance, that is a prescription."

"I am a priest and I cannot help it." He put the stole in his pocket. "And I will write to you at Olsztyn. Once a month. Not about anything. About the diocese, and the roof, and the bell we do not have. You will not answer, and I will go on writing, and in ten years there will be a hundred and twenty letters, and somebody will read them after I am dead and will think that the Bishop of Lublin had a very dull friend."

"I will answer," she said.

"Then it will be two hundred and forty letters."

They went out together onto the steps. It had begun to snow again, small hard flakes out of the east, and the village was dark except for three windows and the lamp over the church door, and the bus would come at four and it was ten to.

"Zofia."

"Mm."

"I am going to tell you something and I do not want you to answer it." He stood with his hands in his pockets and the snow on his shoulders. "In 1944 I was twenty years old and I was in the forest, and a man came up from the village on the fourteenth of June and told us that Brzozówka was burning and that the Germans were in it, and my commander said we could not go, because we had eleven men and two machine guns and there was a company in the village with armoured cars, and that if we went we would be killed and the village would burn anyway. And I obeyed him. I obeyed him, and I stayed in the forest, and I sat under a tree all night and listened to it, and I have never told anyone that either." He looked at her. "So. Two people who signed. Two people who obeyed. We have been very careful with our hands for fourteen years and it has not saved one single person. I have decided that this is not a reason to stop. I have decided that it is the only reason to go on."

She looked at him.

"The bus," she said.

"I know."

"Will you bless me? Not as a priest. As a man who is standing in the snow."

He raised his hand and made the sign of the cross over her, and said nothing at all.

She walked down the path to the road with her suitcase, and the snow was coming harder, and she turned at the gate — the same gate, or a gate in the same place, at the end of the lane where the lilacs had been — and he was still standing on the steps under the lamp with his hands in his pockets.

"Tomasz."

"Yes."

"Plant lilacs," she said. "At the new presbytery. There aren't any."

"I will plant lilacs," he said.

The bus came at four o'clock and it was a new bus, with a diesel engine that smelled of the future, and there were nine people in it and a woman with a basket of eggs and a boy asleep against the window. Zofia Krawiec got on and put her suitcase on the rack and sat down at the back, and the bus turned in the yard and went out onto the Krasnystaw road, and the village went past the window, the church and the school and the cooperative shed and the plot of ground with the pear tree in it, and then the fields, and then nothing but snow and the dark and, a long way off, the lights of a train going east.

She sat with her hands in her lap. The ring was gone from her neck and there was a pale mark where it had been, and it would fade in a month, and she found that she did not want it to.

The bus went on through the dark towards Krasnystaw, and the snow came down over the fields, and somewhere behind her a man was telling his neighbour about the price of pigs, and the engine made its noise, and the road went on, and it did not care.

What remained was this: a woman on a bus; a bishop standing on the steps of a village church until the bus had gone; a ring of galvanised wire in a breviary; thirty-nine names on a board; a field where a house had been; a pear tree; and a river that had been straightened in 1952 and was still, in the bend that was left of it, deep enough to drown in.
