The Borrowed Spring

Spring arrives in Rome like a rumour that turns out to be true: almond first, then the markets changing their prices and their smells, then the great column in the Forum rising drum by drum inside its scaffolding, the war spiralling up toward its own statue at a pace even the baths' plumbing can keep up with. Prima works her trousseau at her own table, at her own prices, with a dowry now inscrutably enlarged by a Baiae bequest that the baker's mother has inspected like a hem and pronounced, in the guild parlour, with deep satisfaction, *stitched straight*. The baker’s son — Titus, broad-handed, smelling permanently of warm crust, steady at ladders and pyres — holds whatever she hands him in the way of grief or linen without taking the climb off her, and Prima, who has had a winter of finding out what people are by what they do at doors, treasures the findings in order.

The trousseau occupies the table by stages, like a small province being settled. First come the household linens, plain enough for daily use and exact enough that no one could call them poor; then the finer lengths with borders she has kept for feast days; then the two shifts her mother would have called wasteful if wasteful had ever been a word Claudia used for things intended to last. Prima does not embroider her name where any customs-minded mother-in-law will find it. She works it into a corner seam, letters so small they might be the record of a thread taking thought. What belongs to her should know itself, even if the world is not invited to inspect the evidence.

The guild women inspect everything else. They arrive singly, or in pairs, under pretexts so transparent that nobody bothers to repair them: a borrowed hoop, a question about mordant, a loaf that has taken the wrong shape and must therefore, by some domestic law, be carried across the yard. They handle the cloth with clean thumbs. They praise the evenness of the red. They ask after the bequest with their eyes and after the wedding arrangements with their mouths, and Prima gives both enquiries the same answer—dates, prices, thread—until it becomes clear that appetite will have to feed on something less nourishing than a scandal.

Mother Livia supervises from the position of a woman who has annexed the future by competence. She has firm views on linen and firmer views on men who imagine that marriage entitles them to an opinion about either. “A sheet should know its work,” she says, folding with the brisk violence of a treaty being ratified. “Same as a husband. If it is always announcing itself, it is probably too thin.”

Titus, when his work permits, stands just far enough inside the room not to become another inspector. He mends a broken stool without being asked. He carries water. He submits his own wedding tunic to Prima’s judgement and receives, in return, instructions about a neckline that suggest she has thought carefully about the precise terms on which he is to enter public life beside her. She lets out one seam, takes in another. “You grow broader every time your mother feeds you,” she says.

“That is a serious accusation against the bakery.”

“It is an observation. Stand still.”

He does, with his arms slightly raised and the expression of a man surrendering to a very local government. It is not a grand romantic tableau. No poet passing the door would reach instinctively for a tablet. But there is in it the substance poets usually have to borrow from elsewhere: one person making room for another to be altered without being diminished.

At night she goes through her mother’s leaves again. The mark beneath *Kept* no longer seems merely unfinished. It has the look, now, of a door deliberately left on the latch. Prima puts a narrow strip of new linen beside the old pages and, after some hesitation, writes the mainspring facts of the season in her own hand: the bequest, the oven share, the commissions paid by the piece, the Thursdays that became Tuesdays because the spring work altered its hours. She does not write the other ledger. Not there. She has learned from two households that truth also needs a suitable room.

The blank with the little mark stays blank. Some sums, she thinks, are not incomplete because nobody knows the answer. They are open because the answer is still being lived.

Outside, the river has lost its winter temper. Barges come down loaded with early greens and timber and gossip from the upper towns. The fullers complain that spring water has its own opinions. A new notice at the guild house advertises work for government cloth, and by noon three men have explained to Prima why she need not trouble herself with the application. By the following noon the clerk has her mark on it. The money has not made her loud. It has made refusal more expensive for other people, which in Rome is the practical form of liberty.

She goes to the Caelian on Tuesdays. Not as staff; the door-book, Diomedes has seen to it, now holds a category for *tradeswomen related by commission*, a formula of such elastic dullness that no aedile will ever measure it. They work. Sometimes they do not work and walk in the courtyard among the overwatered roses while Eirene practises, loudly, being elsewhere. Antonia tells it in pieces now, not evenings: her mother's hands in other women's hair; Baiae stairs by the counted dozen; the Naples widow whose will did the work of angels out of spite at a son-in-law; the first dining room, two tables and a lamp-seller's credit; the pastry cook's arrival, which Prima already knows by the evidence of his saffron disputes. Prima tells it back, her order: the vats, the counting, the thumbprint of the river goddess, the father who held a rail and joked. Between the two versions, on the courtyard stones, a family is being attempted the way the fullers attempt a stained garment: slowly, without rushing the soak, in water changed as often as honesty requires.

The Tuesdays acquire rules without either woman proposing them as a constitution. Prima arrives after the guild’s morning work, never with an escort beyond the crossing, always with something in her hands. Antonia is never discovered waiting in the courtyard, which would make hunger too visible; she is discovered at accounts, or issuing instructions about cushions, or standing over Ammonius while he performs a private civil war against a sauce. Then Business occurs first. Border counts, damaged corners, cloth that has taken dye unevenly and must be made presentable without lying too flagrantly.

Only when the invoice has established their public relationship do the other subjects approach, usually carrying food.

It is astonishing how much biography can be got through in the interstices of soup. Antonia speaks of Naples not as a single revelation but as weather, rent, a landlady who counted spoons and a widow who counted everybody. She explains the first loans to women: small sums, no sermons attached, interest stated plainly enough that gratitude was not required to do arithmetic in the dark. “A woman came to me for money to redeem her tools from her brother-in-law,” she says. “She paid it back in dyed wool and gossip. Both were excellent quality. Her brother-in-law believes to this day that a priest intervened.”

Prima tells her about the vat work before her hands were trusted with fine cloth: the sour air, the women singing to keep the rhythm, Claudia arriving among them with her leaves and turning other people’s disasters into columns they could stand upright in. She tells how her father once tried to teach her the names of stars from the roof and gave up in favour of teaching her which guild officials could be trusted after wine. “He said stars were more regular but less useful in a dispute.”

Sometimes there is silence, which Antonia still finds harder than narrative. Narrative can be shaped. Silence belongs to both parties equally, and therefore keeps escaping government. On the second Tuesday, Prima sits with a torn length untouched in her lap for the whole time it takes the courtyard fountain to be argued over by two slaves and a plumber. Antonia does not ask what she is thinking. It costs her approximately the price of a small villa, but she does not ask.

At last Prima says, “I keep expecting to feel that something has been given back.”

“And instead?”

“Something has been added. It is heavier. Not worse. Heavier.” She turns the cloth over, though there is nothing yet to inspect. “At home, if I said that, someone would tell me to eat.”

“In this house that is also policy. Eirene regards introspection as a symptom of an empty bowl.”

“Then I can tell you the rest after lentils.”

The rest is not dramatic. That, Antonia begins to understand, may be the form mercy takes when it is not trying to appear in a tragedy. Prima is angry on some Tuesdays, briefly and without aim, like weather caught between roofs. She is curious on others. Once she asks whether Antonia had imagined her at particular ages. Antonia, who has spent twenty years training herself not to manufacture ghosts wholesale, answers with dangerous honesty: “No. I thought if I imagined you too precisely I would begin grieving a person I had invented, and that seemed another theft. I kept the facts I had been sold and paid rent on them.”

Prima considers this with her needle suspended. “My mother did the imagining forwards. She used to plan what I would need at twenty when I was nine. Gloves. A second box. A way of saying no that wouldn’t cost a customer.”

“Did she teach you that?”

“She tried. I am still in the class.”

On the fourth Tuesday, rain shuts them into the linen room with a whole afternoon of grey light. Prima takes from her work-box the strip of new writing and, without ceremony, shows Antonia the accounts of oven and bequest and commissions. The columns are Claudia’s and not Claudia’s. The hand is firmer. At the foot of the page, under a heading of her own, she has entered: *Visits paid by the piece. Thread separate.*

Antonia looks at it for too long to pass as a customer.

“Is the wording wrong?” Prima asks.

“The wording,” Antonia says, “is alarmingly sound.”

“I thought if anyone finds it after I am dead, they should know I was not being kept in secret at luxury prices.”

“They will also know you inherited a suspicious mind.”

“I had excellent teachers on both banks.”

That day she stays until Barbatus is lighting the lamps. At the door she pauses with the roll of work against her shoulder and says, in the same tone she uses for an estimate that may change when the cloth is unrolled at home, “I am glad you told me indoors.” Then she goes out into the wet street before gratitude can become a debt either of them has to administer.

“Does Daphne know?” Prima asks, stitching. “She looks at me now like someone reading under the line.”

“Daphne suspects an entire library. She has the singer's disease: she believes every room has a song under it. I have told her that I pay you over the rate because you are the best needlewoman in Rome. It has the virtue, in this city, of being nearly true.”

“And Marcus.” It is not quite a question. The needle goes in, comes out. “Barbatus says he came to the door and stood there like weather.”

“Marcus is learning manners at the full rate. His family does nothing by instalments except debts.” Antonia watches the border grow: red flowers, spring issue. “He will write to you, eventually. It will be a terrible letter, full of Greek, and entirely sincere. Keep it with your tools. A woman should have evidence of what men cost in their own handwriting.”

Daphne’s knowledge, insofar as the house can map it, advances by the method of all gifted listeners: she notices where the music has been altered. Barbatus no longer announces Prima in the tradeswoman’s formula he uses for the chair-menders and lamp men. Eirene has begun to ask whether soup is needed *for Tuesday*, as if a day of the week had acquired a pulse. Diomedes, whose face in an emergency could be filed as blank stationery, has invented a door-book category so boring that its very dullness shines.

Daphne says nothing. This, in Daphne, is not discretion so much as rehearsal.

She begins to leave fruit where Prima will find it. Not at the table—too public, too near contract. On the windowsill of the linen room: two early plums, a small bunch of grapes out of season and therefore scandalously eloquent. Prima eats one plum and leaves the other, divided into exact halves on a clean square of cloth. Rome is full of languages spoken by women who are not supposed to have anything to say to each other except through men.

One afternoon, when rain has emptied the courtyard, Daphne finds Prima washing dye from her hands at the basin. “You have her way of listening,” Daphne says. “As if a mistake may drop something valuable.”

“Whose way?”

“Now you have her way of answering too.” Daphne examines her own nails, which have survived the winter better than her illusions. “I don’t require the score. I sing in this house; I am not its treasury. But if you ever need someone to not know something *at* Marcus, I have become very accomplished.”

Prima dries her hands carefully, finger by finger. “Did you like him?”

“I liked being looked at by a future that hadn’t yet learned its price.” The answer is too polished, and Daphne knows it; a brief, less musical honesty alters her face. “Yes. Sometimes. That is exactly why the necklace went home. If it had meant nothing, I could have kept it.”

It is the first confidence offered to Prima at the Caelian house that has not arrived via Antonia, and it changes the room’s weights. Prima does not reciprocate with the vault. She says instead, “My betrothed once brought me hot bread in the rain and tried to pretend he had been passing the street on other business. Four ovens, no other business.”

Daphne laughs, startled into her speaking voice. “Did you accept the fiction?”

“I accepted the bread. The fiction is still under review.”

After that, the fruit exchange becomes regular and unspoken, like any sound treaty. Antonia observes it and says nothing to either woman. She has paid enough for the lesson that kinship, once discovered, does not report to a single office.

Marcus remains the more theoretical relative. Prima had seen him through a doorway as a young man made of assumptions and expensive cloth. Now he occupies a category no guild register provides. She tries out the word *brother* while counting and drops three stitches. She tries *the senator’s son* and produces a border so regular it looks machine-made. She settles temporarily on *Marcus*, a name with no instructions attached.

“What if he writes badly?” she asks.

“He will.”

“What if he writes well?”

“That will be more troublesome. We shall have to believe him.”

“And if I don’t answer?”

Antonia is testing a cushion seam and does not look up too quickly. “Then you will not answer. It is a reply in every office except his family’s, which is learning.”

Prima nods, but the needle pauses. Freedom, when other people describe it on your behalf, can sound suspiciously like another arrangement. She has begun to notice how often her choices are being pre-approved by people eager to prove that they are not arranging her. “I might answer.”

“You might.”

“I might ask him a question.”

“You might ask him several. The Cornelii prefer questions in committee, but he is showing signs of independent governance.”

“What would I ask?”

This time Antonia’s restraint is more expensive. There are questions she has wanted put to that house for twenty years, elegantly sharpened questions, questions with subclauses. She leaves them all in their drawer. “That would be yours,” she says. “Not mine in your handwriting.”

Prima takes a new length of red wool and begins the next flower. “I would ask what he was told to call my father.”

Outside, Barbatus tells somebody to move a mule in a voice that suggests the mule is the reasonable party. The courtyard fills with ordinary racket. Antonia looks down at her own imperfect seam and understands, again, that entrusting the girl with the truth includes not renting her out as the courier of other people’s unfinished business.

Prima laughs, and Antonia hears — files it, with Diomedes exactitude, in the third book, the real one — the sound her own laugh made at seventeen, before it was trained, in a house on the Quirinal, over a line about a loom. It does not undo her. It has, if anything, the opposite effect; the sound arrives like a sum coming right at the second count. She gets up to be about the house's business before her face can make an entry the tax office could audit.

The spring work nevertheless has its own emergencies, indifferent to the emotional reorganisation of two families. A consignment of cloth arrives from the south with one bolt cut short and another carrying a stain so shapely it looks intentional, which is worse. The merchant’s agent maintains, in the courtyard, that both defects are features of the weaving district and possibly marks of authenticity. Diomedes receives this theory with a face wiped clean for the occasion. Eirene suggests, helpfully, that authenticity may also be authenticated by returning the money. Antonia is upstairs being informed that a newly rich customer expects his entire dining room to suggest naval victory without being so vulgar as to mention ships.

Prima has already taken the cloth to the linen-room table.

“The stain will come out if we do not frighten it,” she says. “The short bolt can make the inner curtains. Use the good lengths outside. Nobody at dinner inspects the layer nearest the wall unless the conversation is so poor that honesty would be kinder anyway.”

Diomedes looks at her with the restrained joy of a strategist discovering an ally in an unexpected province. “And the agent?”

“Pay for what arrived. Not for what he intended to send. Intentions can be stored at his warehouse free of charge.”

It is entered. The agent departs with less money and a story about the Caelian house that will become, in his own mouth, a testimonial to his influence there. Antonia returns in time to see Prima marking the temporary border in chalk, sleeves pushed up, a tradeswoman wholly in command of an emergency in somebody else’s expensive house.

“You might have called me,” Antonia says.

“I did. You were dressing a room for a naval victory with no ships.”

“It is to suggest oars.”

“Then the curtains are ahead.”

There is something in the exchange that belongs neither to the invoice nor to the confession. Antonia feels it arrive and, instead of seizing it by the shoulders to ask what it is called, takes up the scissors Prima has put down and holds them when asked. Later she will not know which of them first reached for the cloth marker, only that both hands found it at once and withdrew without apology.

Work has this mercy: it sometimes allows people to rehearse kinship as competence before they are obliged to perform it as feeling.

At the end of that Tuesday, Prima asks whether she may take one of the damaged offcuts for her own household. It is not valuable. It is hardly cloth, except in the eyes of women raised to correct the waste of other people’s worlds. Antonia says yes too quickly, then corrects herself into ordinary language. “Put it on your invoice at discard price. Diomedes faints if goods levitate.”

“What does he do if they are merely carried across a river by their purchaser?”

“He recovers at professional speed.”

Prima rolls the offcut with the spring linen. “I shall make cushion covers. Titus’s mother says cushions are how a house makes promises it may not keep. She has eleven.”

“Tell Mother Livia their seam will know its work.”

“She will inspect the saying.”

Barbatus, on door duty, watches her out with the look of a man seeing a recruit leave on leave with proper boots. In the street, Rome is all wet stone and roast chestnuts and the first swallows stitching black thread across the dusk. Prima crosses home by the island and, for no reason a ledger can recognise, stops at the middle of the bridge.

The river is doing what rivers do in family stories, which is refuse to carry symbolism at a fixed rate. Barges knock gently against piles. A boy is fishing with the confidence of ignorance. On one bank are the vats and ovens and the house where she has become herself. On the other, lanterns are being lit in streets that lead eventually to a courtyard where another becoming is being attempted, late and under business hours.

She shifts the bundle on her shoulder and continues toward home. It is, all right, heavier. It is also hers to carry.

At the Palatine house, spring is a calendar of bridal administration. The Avidian match is concluded — *allied*, the families will say until they die — and Servilia goes through the ceremonies of it as through a province she intends to leave better governed than she found it: the contract, the auspices, the staggering, priced choreography of two houses becoming one and pretending it is a festival rather than a merger. Cornelia assists with the dreamy competence of a girl who has decided, privately and against all the songs, that she will keep some part of the house inside herself unregistered. She has taken, that spring, to singing the Greek one for her music master — who has been paid better, by Avidius in advance, and no longer needs to touch anyone's wrist for any reason.

The marriage papers generate their own subsidiary marriages: between families and accountants, between ancestral pride and shipping schedules, between the bride’s musical education and a father-in-law who regards every recurring expense as a vessel requiring inspection before launch. Servilia sits at the centre of it all with three clerks, two alternative versions of the property schedule, and the calm of a general who has inherited a battlefield already mapped by men who assumed the women would walk around it.

Cornelia is required to be present for portions of the proceedings and decorative for others. She distinguishes the two with dangerous accuracy. When the question of her personal slaves arises, she interrupts a fluent exchange between stewards to state that her maid will accompany her, that the old nurse will be pensioned rather than transferred like a chair, and that nobody is to sell her music master’s replacement out of the household accounts in her name without asking her. The clerks look toward Servilia for instructions. Servilia looks at her daughter and says, “Enter it.”

It is not rebellion. Rome would have a word for rebellion and a flute-girl waiting outside court to illustrate it. It is something quieter and harder to vaccinate against: a young woman discovering that a contract, if read carefully, contains surfaces on which a hand may rest.

Avidius the younger visits under the usual diplomatic constraints. He brings, after consultation with his father and, one suspects, several maritime deities, not jewellery but a schedule of the house he is preparing: which rooms catch morning light, where a music room might be made without disturbing the stores, whether the roof can bear the modification his mother insists is traditional. He lays the plan before Cornelia as if it were cargo for inspection.

“You need not look so surprised,” he says. “I was told you ask questions.”

“By whom?”

“By a person who said it as a warning and another who said it as praise. I chose the more economical interpretation.”

Cornelia studies the plan. “This room will be dark after noon.”

“Yes.”

“Then it should not be mine.”

Avidius leans over, pencil in hand. “This one is larger, but my mother says the floor complains.”

“All floors complain. It is how they participate.” She says it deadpan, and watches him fail to decide whether she is joking. He laughs a fraction late, which is better than the prompt, well-trained laughter of young men who have been told their bride is accomplished.

Servilia observes from the third chair, her face performing genealogy. She has not procured love; even her powers have provincial boundaries. She may have procured the conditions under which respect does not have to be smuggled.

Later, alone with her mother among the dress lengths, Cornelia asks why everyone has been behaving as though the house contained an expensive silence.

“Every house does,” Servilia says. “Ours has recently improved its manners.”

“Is it about Father and the committee?”

“It is about money, family, and the difference between the two.”

“That is not an answer.”

“No. It is a direction.” Servilia takes the girl’s wrist, not to correct it, and turns the hand palm-up as she might inspect a glove for fit. There is no pale mark there, no household legend. “One day you will be told a thing which was kept from you for reasons partly good and partly ours. When it happens, remember that I said *partly*. It is a more useful inheritance than rage.”

Cornelia looks at their joined hands. “That is ominous.”

“It is Roman. We put our omens indoors and give them dinner.”

“I shall put it with the other things nobody explains until after the contract.”

“Put it at the front of that box.”

The girl goes back to the fabrics, choosing not the richest but the one with a colour that deepens in low light. Her mother watches the choice with an approval she does not cheapen by explanation. There is more of the household in the girl than gloom predicts. That is perhaps what households are for: to surprise the management without overthrowing it altogether.

On the day the contract is signed, Avidius the father lingers in Lentulus's study over Falernian that predates both their ambitions. “Your committee did you no favours in the east,” he says, harbour-fashion, stating the tide. “I lose a governor for a son-in-law's father. I gain a senator with clean hands and a new registry with his name on the clause my ships' chaplains are already quoting at the crews, may the gods restrain their enthusiasm. Overall —” the great evaluator's shrug “— I call it cargo landed. Not the manifest we filed. Landed.”

“You,” Lentulus says, “are the first man to tell me that this year in that tone.”

“I unload grain. Tides waste more fortunes than storms, senator, by arriving slightly not as promised.” Avidius sets down his cup precisely in its predecessor's ring, a dockside man made good performing, perfectly, the oldest rite of the house he is marrying into. “We need not discuss the Caelian ever again in this room, I think. My son eats there less than he did, your son stands in its doorway like a penitent, and the woman who runs it has just renewed her wine contract with my partner at a price that suggests she knew the vintage before the grapes did. Rome, eh? The accounts all settle, one way or another. The only choice we get is the currency.”

Lentulus looks at him for a long moment, this former unloader who has just quoted his own dead father's final advice back to him without knowing a letter of it, and experiences a sensation his family has historically confused with indigestion: kinship, upstream, where no committee has jurisdiction. “To landed cargo,” he says, and they drink, two old Romans of entirely different manufacture, in a study full of paper.

Before Avidius takes his leave, he asks, with the abruptness of a man who believes a hull should be knocked to hear where it rings false, whether Lentulus regrets the province.

It is not a question members of Lentulus’s order ask one another except in the forms of condolence, where the answer has been upholstered in advance. Lentulus looks toward the corridor, where the ancestors are presumably waiting for him to produce three centuries of the correct emotion. “I regret a number of arrangements,” he says. “The province has joined an old queue.”

“That is not what your friends say.”

“My friends are distinguished men. They regard ambiguity as a provincial product.”

Avidius turns his cup slowly. “At Ostia, when a ship misses its weather, there are captains who spend the whole season explaining the wind. Others unload, repair what can be repaired, and find a cargo that was never in the first manifest. Both kinds call themselves realists.”

“And which am I, in your harbour taxonomy?”

“Too early to enter it. But you have stopped hiring men to insult the wind in committee.” The older man’s eyes have a dockworker’s flat kindness: no shine provided unless earned. “Your name will travel. Names do. See it is not only travelling in other people’s mouths.”

It is superb advice and wholly unusable, which may be why Lentulus accepts it without the usual citizenship papers. “You speak,” he says, “like a man whose son learned ballast from the cradle.”

“He learned invoices. Ballast came later and cost more.”

When Avidius has gone, Lentulus remains in the study with the signed copy of the marriage contract and the little dented mirror. The mirror gives him back a face assembled from family and weather, no more exonerated than it was in January but less interested in petitioning for the status. He has begun to keep, in a private notebook too plain for a senator, a list not of debts—the Greek manages those at a level beneath poetry—but of *persons to whom precision is owed*. The wording changed weekly at first. It has settled.

Onesimus enters with the hour’s correspondence. There is a dinner invitation from a man who avoided him in February and has now remembered an ancestral intimacy. There is a request for patronage. There is a memorandum from the east, where other men’s ambitions are becoming geography. Lentulus sets the eastern paper beneath the others. “Anything from the Caelian?” he asks, and hears the impropriety too late. There is not supposed to be anything from the Caelian. Correspondence would imply a relationship the settlement carefully excludes.

“Nothing requiring the house, sir.”

The formulation is perfect, and therefore Onesimus. Lentulus nods. It occurs to him that he is being governed by a series of absences, all of them more competent than his former methods. No message where a message would make demands. No appearance at doors. Money where money can be inspected and called something dull. A son learning silence as though it were a new instrument. A daughter receiving a house plan from a young man who has discovered that pencil marks are safer than passions. A wife conducting the entire reorganisation under the heading of a wedding.

He writes in the plain notebook, under no name: *Do not make repair into another claim.*

The sentence pleases him, which makes him suspicious of it. He crosses out *repair* and writes *accuracy*. This pleases him less and is therefore allowed to remain.

From the music room comes Cornelia’s voice running up a scale, pausing, beginning again at the place where she has decided her breath should belong to her. It is spring. In the Forum the column is rising under its scaffolds, telling the official story in a continuous spiral no passer-by can read from the ground. In the study of an unsuccessful candidate for the east, more modest narratives are being revised at eye level, where they may actually be some use.

That evening the Palatine house dines privately, which in a great family still means a room containing enough ancestry to complicate digestion. The wedding contract lies safely in its case. Nobody has to be charming in the direction of a harbour. Cornelia, taking advantage of the absence of guests, eats with the direct attention of a young woman who has spent the day being a diplomatic asset and is now off duty as a continent.

“Is being allied tiring?” Marcus asks her.

“It is mostly fittings,” she says. “Documents for cloth, cloth for documents. I have been measured spiritually by three aunts.”

“Did you pass?”

“I pleaded floorboards.” It is a private joke whose provenance Marcus does not know, and Cornelia, delighted to possess information her brother cannot access, refuses to develop it. Some power at last has been distributed evenly.

Servilia asks after the music room proposed on the Avidian plan. Cornelia repeats, with improvements, the matter of the complaining floor. Her father listens as if a dispatch had arrived from a province he would rather have been sent to. “A room that complains,” he says, “lets you know where you stand.”

“Father,” Cornelia says, “that was almost a saying.”

“Withdrawn. The family has a surplus.”

Marcus drinks slowly, a habit now so entrenched that the cupbearer has ceased to hover. He watches his sister occupy her place at the table—this sister, the declared and documented one, whose impending marriage is the cover under which the household is performing several less discussable operations—and feels gratitude sharpen, dangerously, into guilt. He puts the cup down. Guilt is a form of weather, his mother would say; do not let it appoint itself captain.

“You will sing at the wedding?” he asks.

“Not on the programme. Brides are not supposed to provide the entertainment at their own alliance. It confuses the invoices.”

“But afterwards.”

“Afterwards I shall have a room with a floor that participates.” She looks at her father. “And a master who is paid properly. It is in the schedule. Mother entered it.”

Servilia says, “I enter many things. It relieves conversation.”

It is not a scene of revelation. No mask falls from the wall and confesses. But when Cornelia rises to practise, Lentulus finds himself listening not for the perfection he used to value, the polished presentation of a house achieving what it was bred for, but for the slight alterations—breath taken where she chooses, pace loosened, the second attempt at a phrase after an error. Getting something right at the second count. His family, which has historically preferred to make the first count illegal, is having to learn the music from elsewhere.
