# Pine Needles and Ether

*Świder — Warsaw, 8 August 1943*

Five kilometers past Wawer the brick tenements gave out altogether, and when Sylwia rolled the rear window of the Wanderer staff car down three inches, air rushed in smelling not of lysol, unwashed wool, or pulverized lime, but of hot dry sand, crushed wild thyme, and pitch baking in streaks down red-barked Masovian pines.

So fierce was the shock of clean resin hitting four-year-starved lungs that for ten seconds Sylwia sat dizzy against the warm grey upholstery with her eyes half-shut against the green flicker of tree-trunks rushing past at sixty kilometers an hour.

Up ahead behind the wheel, Bruno Mekelburg drove bareheaded with his field cap wedged beside the speedometer and his three-fingered left hand resting light on the ten-o'clock spoke, whistling some flat East Prussian harvest tune under engine roar. Beside Sylwia—keeping decorous six-inch military distance while passing Falenica gendarmerie posts—Ludwig wore open-necked summer field-grey cotton drill without heavy wool lining, map-board across his knees showing municipal and Wehrmacht convalescent wells marked in blue pencil between Józefów and Otwock.

"Your Stabsarzt Vesper has developed touching enthusiasm for preventive hydrology since late July," Ludwig remarked dryly without looking up from the contour sheet as another gendarmerie post waved their Wehrmacht pennant straight through without slowing. "Four countersigned sanitary transport authorizations in one fortnight, and yesterday afternoon he assured Hauptmann von Berg over iced vermouth at the Europejski that your clinical judgment is unimpeachable from Charlottenburg to Cracow. What did you catch him doing on the twenty-fourth—stealing hospital morphine, or scraping out one of his switchboard girls?"

Sylwia kept her eyes coolly on passing pine scrub. "Ilse Lindner goes back to light switchboard duty next Monday with an intact small intestine and a textbook ectopic scar. What Stabsarzt Vesper puts in his conscience between Munich and Rosenheim belongs to his confessor."

Ludwig's mouth twitched half a millimeter at the corner—needing zero further footnotes on *intact small intestine* to reconstruct a perforated twelve-week fundus inside three seconds flat. "Remind me never to let you save my career on Table One, *Kotarska*. Vesper turns pale green whenever somebody mentions Lindleya."

At half-past twelve Mekelburg swung left off the paved Otwock highway down a rutted white-sand fire-lane flanked by bilberry bushes dusty from August drought, stopping before warped wooden double gates painted peeled turquoise in Warsaw's old *Świder-style* fretwork scroll-saw ornament: **PENSJONAT LEŚNY ZACISZE**.

Before thirty-nine, prosperous Warsaw lung specialists and bank clerks rented fretwork wooden villas along the Świder river-bluffs every August so tubercular wives and rickets-pale children could breathe pine turpentine among hammocks; in forty-one the Luftwaffe had requisitioned *Zacisze* for night-fighter rest quarters, abandoned it last autumn after two pilots caught paratyphoid B from the shallow sand-point pump, and left it padlocked behind rusty wire ever since under nominal Wehrmacht *Quartieramt* key.

First came honest work—neither Sylwia nor Ludwig ever faked cover mechanics when three real glass sample-bottles took fifteen minutes to fill properly.

While Mekelburg propped the Wanderer's bonnet open under pine shade by the outer lane gate with a grease-gun and a tin of cold army meat, Sylwia took sterile ground-glass-stoppered liter bottles out of her sanitary canvas crate, flamed the cast-iron pump spout in the mossy rear yard with burning spirit-cotton, pumped forty strokes of rust-brown sand water out onto dry nettles until cold crystal groundwater ran clear, and sealed two samples labeled *Świder Q-14* for Halina's typhoid bismuth-sulfite plates tomorrow morning.

"Wait five minutes by the pump," Sylwia told Ludwig quietly, picking up a flat brown-paper parcel wrapped separately at the bottom of her crate. "Your well drains down-slope toward the river footpath—I'm checking whether septic seepage reaches the lower spring."

Ludwig glanced once at the brown-paper parcel—exact size and weight of four ten-gram sulfonamide tins, six sterile eye-pads, and folded cardboard papers—and didn't ask a single word about hydrology, turning calmly toward the villa porch steps with his brass padlock key. "Mind the dry pine needles on the bank—slippery as soap in leather soles."

Seventy yards down the sun-baked sandy bluff, where shallow amber Świder water murmured knee-deep over white sandbars behind dense alder scrub invisible from villa or road, a barefoot boy of fourteen with a fishing pole devoid of hooks sat whittling willow bark.

Sylwia paused to rinse her hands in warm river water two yards from his bare heels. "Do perch bite low in August?"

"Only below Grójec bridge when rain comes," the boy recited dutifully—Bronka's sister's youngest from Konstancin—sliding her brown-paper parcel inside his linen fish-creel in one smooth flick and handing back a folded beech-leaf holding one penciled scrap without signature: *Burn closed clean; right eye reads newsprint; carving wooden spoons under pine trees. Tell H. don't starve for lice.*

Sylwia tucked the scrap inside her shoe-lining for Halina's stove tonight and climbed back up the hot pine-needle path through shimmering resin heat toward the silent wooden villa.

* * *

Before climbing upstairs, they ate cold noon rations out on the shaded rear ground-floor veranda overhanging the pine ravine, where trellised wild hops screened the table completely from the sand lane two hundred yards west.

Out of a battered wicker game-basket Mekelburg had stowed in the boot came half a round of dark East Prussian pumpernickel, hard yellow Tilsit cheese sweating tiny oil-beads in thirty-degree heat, two green sour cucumbers pickled in dill by some garrison mess-woman, and a squat green bottle of dry Mosel wine cooled forty minutes down at the bottom of the icy iron pump-bucket. Halfway through uncorking the bottle, rustling footsteps shuffled round the lilac bushes from the caretaker's shed next door: seventy-year-old **Pan Ignacy**, deaf as an oak post since artillery service at Przemyśl in nineteen-fifteen, wearing patched linen drawers and carrying three freshly smoked silver river-bleak laid neat on a horseradish leaf.

Ignacy squinted bleary rheumy eyes first at Ludwig's unbuttoned field-grey drill collar—spitting sideways into dry nettles out of sheer seventy-year-old Masovian principle without caring who wore silver eagles—then peered closer at Sylwia's raw carbolic-scrubbed hands unscrewing glass water-sample caps and grinned three yellow teeth wide, laying the smoked river-fish in front of *her* elbow rather than the German's:

"*Woda czysta jak łza, Pani Doktor—piloci brzuchy mieli słabe od sznapsa, nie od studni!*" (*Water's clear as a tear, Doctor—those flyers had weak bellies from schnapps, not from the well!*)

"Thank your river for the fish, Grandfather," Sylwia shouted clearly into his good left ear, pressing a ten-złoty note plus two cubes of scarce ration sugar into his knotted palm before the old man shuffled contentedly back under his apple trees.

Across the unpainted pine table Ludwig watched her peel silver smoked skin deftly off tiny river-fish ribs with bare fingers, pouring four inches of cold pale-gold Mosel into two thick kitchen tumblers.

"You look ten years younger the instant brick walls drop below the horizon," Ludwig observed quietly, leaning back in his creaking wicker chair with long booted legs stretched into green hop-shadow. "Less like Poznań granite—more like how you looked walking back from Dahlem botanical gardens when chestnuts bloomed."

Sylwia paused with white fish-flake at her lip, narrowing dark eyes sharply across sunlit tumblers. "When did you ever see me walk back from Dahlem botanical gardens? Pathological anatomy met four miles north on Luisenstraße."

A faint, dry curve touched the corner of Ludwig's mouth—not victorious, almost rueful at giving away old cover after fourteen years.

"Spring twenty-nine—May, right after you came back from Poznań in black mourning bands for your mother and stopped speaking to everybody in Waldeyer's hall for six weeks," Ludwig said evenly, swirling pale wine once in thick kitchen glass. "I took the Wannsee suburban train out to Dahlem twice on Sunday afternoons to borrow Professor Warburg's respiration-manometer reprints—and saw you sitting alone on the stone bench behind the Carpathian birch-grove reading Sauerbruch's *Thoraxchirurgie* with black ribbon stitched across your grey lapel, eating one dry roll out of waxed paper so furiously you looked ready to bite the squirrels if one begged crumbs."

Sylwia stared at him across green hop-filtered light—genuinely stunned silent for three seconds because she remembered that stone bench behind Dahlem's Carpathian birches down to the birch-bark smell after Helena Kotarska's funeral in April twenty-nine!

"And you didn't come down the path to gloat or pick an argument over Warburg's numbers?" she asked slowly.

"Started down the gravel once," Ludwig admitted dryly. "Got within twenty yards, saw your chin go up three inches at two harmless theology students who merely tipped straw hats passing your bench, decided even my skull wasn't thick enough to interrupt Polish mourning with German grammar, and walked out the south gate instead." He took a slow sip of cold Mosel, green eyes turning graver over the rim. "What was your father like in Poznań before thirty-seven? Berlin's foreign-student card listed *Kazimierz Kotarski, Legationsrat a.D.*—retired diplomatic counsellor—nothing else."

Nobody inside occupied Warsaw had asked Sylwia to speak aloud of her father as a living human man since October thirty-nine; hear a dead parent's name spoken casually across sunlit pine boards with sour dill and Mosel smelling like peacetime summer, and something long-calcified inside her chest creaked painfully shifting weight.

"Quiet, ironic, impeccably shaved at six-thirty every morning even when angina kept him sitting propped in chairs all night," Sylwia said after a beat, tracing condensation wet down cold tumbler glass with one forefinger. "Served twelve years in Vienna before nineteen-eighteen under Austro-Hungarian parliamentary delegations, then eight years on the Polish-German Upper Silesian Mixed Boundary Commission settling coal-mine shafts cut in half by frontier posts—half a seam in Katowice, air-shaft in Beuthen. He insisted I study surgery in Berlin rather than Cracow or Paris."

Ludwig raised one brow slightly. "Why Berlin, after boundary commissions?"

"'Because Frenchmen think charm cures sepsis, Sylwia,'" she quoted her father's dry, precise baritone straight into pine-scented air, feeling an unexpected ghostly warmth sting behind her nose, "'whereas Prussians keep meticulous autopsy statistics even while planning to steal your horse—and if a neighbor four hundred miles wide shares five hundred miles of unfenced flat sand with your kitchen door, know how his hands work inside out before dark falls.'" She looked straight across the pine table into Ludwig's stripped, unguarded face. "He died of his third coronary in November thirty-seven—two years early enough to think he'd merely taught me vascular sutures."

"And your naval captain in Kiel?" she turned the blade cleanly back across pine boards—watching whether Ludwig shuttered up the instant his own dead entered the porch. "Frame Three on that thirty-five-millimeter strip said you handed Major von Bredow your father's nineteen-eighteen naval files."

Ludwig didn't flinch or look away into the pines; only his jaw settled quiet and clean.

"Corvette-Captain Heinrich Schoeler commanded torpedo-boat *V-69* through Jutland and Ostend without losing a boiler," Ludwig said simply. "In October nineteen-eighteen, when Kiel Admiralty staffs ordered one suicide sortie into the Thames purely so admirals could dine in honourable captivity while forty thousand twenty-year-old stokers drowned six days before an armistice already wired to Wilson, my father locked his firing-keys inside his cabin safe and steamed his flotilla back inside Wilhelmshaven mole without shooting mutinous boys or lowering his ensign. Died of Spanish influenza complication in twenty-one with court-martial papers still pending unclosed in naval archives—meaning until thirty-three my mother's widow's pension hung on Weimar ministerial grace every January." His thumb rested steady against cool green bottle glass. "Handing von Bredow my father's unpublished Wilhelmshaven log-notes gave *Abwehr*'s naval historians their neat cautionary archive file—and closed my mother's pension audit permanently before she died in thirty-eight. Cheap trade, considering."

Silence hung warm and vast across the shaded veranda—woodpeckers tapping high in red-barked trunks, river water rustling silver below the sandy bluff, neither pretending thirty-nine hadn't happened, yet sitting two yards apart across bread and smoked river-fish realizing with quiet, bone-deep shock that both of them had grown straight out of dead men who refused to drown boys for flags.

Down by the outer gate two hundred yards away, Mekelburg lay stretched contentedly on a canvas groundsheet beneath the car axle smoking pipe-tobacco at white summer clouds. Up on the wide first-floor fretwork veranda of the villa, every tall shutter stood latched against August glare save one half-open French door leading into the corner upstairs bedroom overhanging the pines.

Inside, the house smelled zero percent of war—only baked resinous pine-plank, dried lavender sachets left behind four summers ago inside empty wardrobe drawers, and hot dust sleeping in golden tiger-stripes across bare scrubbed deal floorboards where afternoon sun sliced through angled wooden shutter-slats.

Ludwig had stripped off his field-grey drill tunic, belt, holster, and boots—hanging pistol and silver shoulder-boards deliberately *outside* on a hall peg so nothing grey or metallic entered the corner room—standing barefoot in loose summer linen shirt unbuttoned to the waist beside a wide prewar maple bedstead covered only in one clean unbleached linen dust-sheet he had shaken fresh out the veranda rail.

Sylwia shut the French door softly behind her, turned the small brass thumb-latch, and stood still in the striped amber light.

For twenty-five days since the fourteenth of July—through three hurried twenty-minute Tuesday-and-Friday collisions on Królewska where clocks ticked loud and German boots passed outside—she had carried *Polizeipräsidium Berlin, 7. März 1933, Haftbefehl gestrichen lt. RWM 11:45 Uhr* locked behind her ribs without breathing one syllable aloud. Partly because until Berlin's August courier packet cleared Kroll's desk she couldn't afford shaking Ludwig's focus by half an inch; deeper and truer, because once you admitted aloud to Ludwig Schoeler that for ten years you had spat on him for saving your life with seven years of his own skin, the last wall standing between your pride and total free-fall vanished into air.

"Two hours before Vesper's driver log expects us past Wawer," Ludwig said quietly across twelve feet of warm pine floor, noticing how motionless she stood in the golden slats. "You don't have to hurry your hairpins today."

Sylwia didn't reach for her hairpins first; walking slowly across sun-warmed bare planks that creaked gentle summer resin under her steps, she unbuckled her thin leather wristwatch—the watch she checked clamping minutes and curfew bells on twenty times a day—and laid it face-down on the windowsill so neither dial nor ticking existed in the room.

Then she kicked off her dusty leather walking shoes, unrolled both dark lisle stockings down pale calves bare to warm forest air, and stood still when Ludwig stepped close in front of her to unbutton her plain grey cotton summer dress himself from throat to hem.

Nothing rushed today. Every button slipping slow through linen sounded distinct in dead pine-wood hush punctuated only by woodpeckers hammering dry bark fifty yards down the slope and wind sighing continuous sea-waves through tall crowns overhead.

When grey cotton slid off her shoulders pooling round her bare ankles on warm wood, wearing only worn white batiste slip—she owned no silk—she raised both hands to pull the steel *szpilki* pins one by one out of her heavy dark coil until thick black hair tumbled past her shoulder-blades down to her ribs, longer than anyone in Warsaw except her washbasin ever saw it.

Ludwig's fingers tangled slowly into that dark loose weight on both sides of her neck, thumbs resting light along her jawbone as he looked down at her in warm slatted light without a trace of Berlin armor left round his eyes.

"Turn round a second," he murmured.

Still bruise-sensitive? Four weeks ago on Nowy Świat that drunken *Schupo*'s rifle-butt had left green-yellow shadow across her left scapula; when she turned half-round, pulling her thin batiste slip clean over her head and tossing it onto a wicker chair so she stood completely naked in tiger-striped sunlight, warm rough fingers traced gently across the healed smooth skin of her left shoulder-blade—finding zero bruise left, only clean muscle and sharp starved ribs showing individual ivory arches whenever she drew breath—followed a heartbeat later by his warm mouth pressing slow, open kisses down each protruding thoracic vertebra from nape to small of back.

Sylwia closed her eyes with a sharp, silent shiver despite thirty-degree August warmth, leaning back half an inch against his bare linen chest until his hands slid forward round her ribs—cupping both bare breasts full in rough palms, thumbs rolling slow across tightening dark nipples while he backed her three unhurried steps onto the sun-warmed linen sheet of the wide maple bed.

He pulled his own linen shirt over his head and stripped trousers down lean sun-browned legs—white only across narrow hips and groin between July tan and that jagged white forty-one shell-scar above his right hipbone—coming down onto one elbow beside her across six whole feet of wide mattress where nobody bumped iron cot-rails or watched clocks!

For ten minutes he didn't enter her at all—simply studied her bare body stretched in golden shutter-stripes as if memorizing topography before dark fell, picking up her left hand from the sheet and turning her raw, mercury-fissured palm face-up to press his thumb gently over the four tiny white twelve-year-old stitch-scars across her thumb base.

"Still clean," Ludwig murmured almost inaudibly, bending his head to kiss the scarred base of her left thumb once—warm lips lingering over his own nineteen-thirty-one needlework—before his mouth traveled up the inner aspect of her bare forearm, across her bicep, onto her breastbone, taking her left breast deep and hot into his mouth while his hand slid slow down the flat plane of her belly into thick dark hair between her thighs.

Sylwia's breath broke audible into the high wooden rafters—no sentries outside, no walls with ears, terrifying luxury of letting sound leave her throat!—arching her spine upward off warm linen as two clever, unhurried surgeon's fingers parted slick wet folds, sliding deep inside her while the pad of his thumb found her clitoris wet and unguarded.

When his mouth left her breast to trail slow, biting heat down her ribs, across her navel, and straight down between her spread knees—pushing both her bare thighs wide across sun-striped linen with broad shoulders and settling his mouth hot and open directly against her—Sylwia's hands flew instinctively to grab his blond hair, half pulling him off in fierce, shocked reflex because nobody in ten gray years had looked at her there in broad afternoon sun, before a long, flat, devastating stroke of his tongue straight up through wet heat made her head slam back into the pillow with a choked Polish cry!

He held both her writhing hips pinned firm to the mattress with hard palms so she couldn't twist away from the unbearable precision of his mouth—tongue circling slow, relentless, sucking swollen flesh lightly between smooth lips while two fingers curved upward inside her against the exact anterior wall until every nerve from thighs to solar plexus pulled taut as violin gut about to snap. Stripped of every shred of control in broad amber light, fingers knotted white in his hair, Sylwia came apart violently under his mouth—hips bucking helplessly against his face with a long, broken gasp that echoed off pine rafters while waves of blinding heat clenched empty and aching round his fingers!

Before the last aftershock ebbed out of her thighs, Ludwig came straight up the length of her trembling body—mouth slick and salty with her own taste crushing down onto hers so she tasted herself hot on his tongue—parting her knees wide with his thighs and sliding thick, burning-hard, and unhurried all the way home inside her in one long continuous glide that joined them chest to chest, hip to hip, mouth to mouth.

No frantic bruising race today: deep, slow, rolling strokes in resin-scented quiet where sweat slicked skin gold in shutter-bars and neither looked away for thirty heartbeats at a stretch. Wrapped tight round him—legs locked high across his lean scarred flanks, raw hands sliding over smooth corded muscle across his shoulders down to the sunken purple keloid star beneath his missing collarbone where his blood hammered hot against her breast—Sylwia watched every defensive seam in Ludwig Schoeler's face come undone six inches above hers: grey-green eyes dark and unguarded down to sixteen-year-old bone, breath catching ragged in her throat whenever she clenched deliberately round him on the out-stroke, dragging it out slow and merciless until sweat dripped off his brow onto her cheek, his rhythm broke rough and deep driving hard against her cervix, and a second deeper, slower climax rolled straight up from the base of her spine—pulling him right over the cliff with her, burying his face hard against her neck with a low, torn groan as he pulsed hot and endless deep inside her.

* * *

At quarter past four shutter-stripes had shifted forty degrees eastward across the ceiling planks, turning deep honey-amber.

Outside on the upper veranda sill sat an enamel wash-basin full of small dark-purple wild wood-plums (*lubaszki*) Mekelburg had picked off overgrown orchard trees down by the lane gate at three o'clock and left silently outside the downstairs screen door before going back to sleep under a pine tree—discreet as an old sexton.

Wrapped in nothing except the single rumpled linen bedsheet pulled lazily across one hip and Ludwig's bare thigh, Sylwia sat propped against the maple headboard spitting plum stones *ping, ping* into a tin soap-dish while purple plum-juice stained her thumb and lower lip dark wine.

"Sour as vinegar," she observed, tossing him a bruised black one anyway.

Ludwig caught it left-handed—effortless overhead snag without shoulder hitch!—bit half off round the stone, winced dramatically, and swallowed it anyway. Lying flat on his back beside her hip with his right arm folded behind his sun-streaked head and left palm resting warm and idle atop Sylwia's bare knee, thirty-four vanished ten minutes at a time: flat stomach relaxed, chest scar fading paler violet in cool shade, three faint white laugh-creases at the outer corners of his eyes she hadn't seen since Wannsee before thirty-three.

"Better than Frau Geheimrat von Halden's Wannsee plum tart in thirty-one," Ludwig said dryly. "Remember when Oberarzt Weber dropped half his plate overboard trying to salute the Geheimrat from a tipping dinghy—"

"—and von Halden's fat dachshund *Waldmann* jumped straight off the dock thinking plum tart was liverwurst," Sylwia picked up before she could stop herself, "and had to be fished out of Wannsee weeds with a boat-hook by Kleist while barking like a seal!"

Ludwig's chest shook first; half a second later—catching the grotesque mental picture of fat Kleist slipping nose-first into green duckweed in white flannel trousers while holding a wet dachshund by the tail—Sylwia snorted purple plum juice onto the back of her wrist, and **both of them burst out laughing aloud at the exact same instant**.

Real laughter—loud, rusty from years of disuse, helpless belly-laughter bouncing strange and bright off dry pine walls while sunlight glinted off bare shoulders and Sylwia wiped plum juice off her chin with the sheet corner!

Three seconds it lasted. Maybe four.

Then the sound of their own two voices echoing cheerful and carefree inside four walls—twenty-five kilometers from Pawiak cells, burnt Muranów brick-pits, and nine-millimeter execution rounds in Mokotów courtyards—hit both pairs of ears simultaneously like cold iron dropped onto glass.

The laugh died cleanly off Ludwig's mouth; Sylwia's hand stopped halfway down from her chin.

Looking sideways at each other across twelve inches of sunlit sheet in the sudden ringing quiet of the pines, Sylwia felt a cold, piercing ache sharper than any scalpel cut she had ever taken—realizing for one naked, terrifying second what two ordinary human lives in peacetime Berlin or Poznań might have sounded like at thirty-three on an August Sunday afternoon if nineteen-thirty-three had never burned down the sky, and hating history so fiercely for showing her four seconds of it that her throat closed tight.

Ludwig didn't pretend he hadn't felt the exact blade twist.

His left hand tightened quietly once over her bare kneecap—not speaking a word to cheapen the silence—before swinging his legs off the maple bedstead to pick up his summer cotton shirt off the floor.

"Quarter to five," he said softly, handing her watch back off the windowsill without turning it face-up. "Mekelburg needs forty minutes before dusk checkpoints tighten at Grochów."

Twenty minutes later, hair pinned severe again, grey cotton dress buttoned neat to the collar over skin still smelling faintly of pine resin and salt, Sylwia sat three inches clear of his sleeve in the back seat as the Wanderer rolled smoothly north along the paved Wawer highway toward Warsaw's darkening smoke-haze.

Three kilometers short of Grochów toll-bar, traffic slowed to five kilometers an hour around two smoldering farm cottages beside the ditch whose thatched roofs had been torched since noon—grey greasy smoke curling low across potato rows while two dead farm-dogs lay stiff by the gatepost and four fresh pink-paper *Bekanntmachung* placards glistened wet with flour-paste on telegraph poles: *For harboring armed bandits and failing to report railway sabotage tools, the farmstead of Jan Kowalski has been burned and six male inhabitants shot under martial law, 8 August 1943.*

Inside the rolling staff car nobody turned their head away from the glass, and nobody reached across the grey wool cushion between them.

Sylwia stared straight out at the wet pink paper flashing past the window with cold black eyes while her bare left palm rested closed around Halina's beech-leaf note inside her pocket—carrying two irreconcilable worlds back inside Warsaw's gates without letting either kill the other before dark.
