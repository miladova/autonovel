# The Four-Minute Vessel

*Warsaw, 9 February 1943*

The boy on the cobbler's bench had bitten clean through his cap visor, cardboard stiffener and all, so gray pulp stuck to his lower teeth like wet bread. Whenever Sylwia drove the grooved director deeper along the wing of his ilium, his heels drummed once against the crate holding the lasts and stopped, because Marek had a knee planted across the backs of both thighs and Marek weighed seventy-eight kilos in his winter coat.

"Hold the lamp higher," Sylwia said. "Not closer. Higher. If you drop hot tallow into an open pelvis I won't bother fishing out the lead, I'll let him rot from the candle."

Waiter's hands were useless for light; engineers' were worse, because engineers corrected the angle by millimeters until they forgot they had wrists. Marek raised the carbide tin three inches. The flame hissed blue at its root and white at its tip, throwing the shadow of her Pean forceps huge across strings of dried sole-leather and three portraits of the Virgin tacked above the coal bin. All over ulica Pańska the thaw had failed at four o'clock; sleet rattled against the pavement grating overhead, and whenever a horse cart ground past toward Towarowa, grit sifted through the rotten mortar onto Sylwia's hair.

"Second fragment's turned," she said. Metal clicked against metal down in the dark red well—not bone, bone gave a wet wooden knock, unmistakable once you had scraped enough periosteum in winter—and her thumb adjusted on the ratcheted handle. "Ricochet off the coupling iron before it went through his trousers. Jacket split?"

"Copper's peeled back," Marek said, not looking down. He was watching the cellar steps through his eyelashes, counting tram bells two streets south on Aleje Jerozolimskie to mark the quarter-hours. Seven fifteen. Curfew at eight, though since the sixth of February, when the three days of German mourning for Stalingrad ended and the *szczekaczki* went back to marches and transport quotas, the Schutzpolizei patrols around Dworzec Główny started clubbing stragglers at twenty to. "Take it whole if you can. The armourer wants to see whether Bahnschutz are still shooting Belgian stock."

"Tell your armourer to come dig in a railwayman's arse himself next Tuesday."

She twisted forty degrees, felt the jagged lip of the copper jacket snag fascia, freed it with the blunt tip of her left index finger—gloveless, because latex tore on brass splinters and three pairs of boiled Continental surgical gloves remained in Pavilion III's locked zinc box for laparotomies—and drew the slug out. It dropped into the chipped enamel saucer with a flat clink. Behind it welled dark venous blood mixed with grey wool threads punched straight out of the boy's railway uniform trousers. Those threads were what killed you five days later: *Clostridium perfringens*, horse dung dried into street dust, beaten into cloth all autumn and driven three inches into torn gluteus medius where oxygen never reached.

"Irrigate," she told the cobbler's wife, who stood by the pickle barrel clutching an Esmarch jug of boiled snow-water tinted faint violet with potassium permanganate. The woman's thumb shook on the rubber clip. Fluid hissed into the cavity, steaming in the four-degree cellar air, washing clots and three more black crumbs of wool over the oilcloth into a zinc tub.

Sylwia did not stitch him. Amateurs stitched bullet tracks in cellars because closed skin looked tidy by lamp-light and made the relatives pay faster; four days later the thigh crackled under your fingers like tissue paper full of lard bubbles, smelling of sweet mouse-nest, and then you took the leg off at the hip or signed nothing at all because the patient had no *Kennkarte* you dared enter in a register. She cut four strips of boiled linen with her Mayo scissors, soaked them in yellow rivanol solution, and packed them ribbon by ribbon down to the scraped bone with dressing forceps until only an inch of wet tail protruded.

The boy spat cardboard off his tongue. His face was the greenish tallow colour of lard sold at Hala Mirowska with chalk kneaded into the bottom of the tub. "Pani doktor," he whispered. "Tomorrow night—the points at Odolany—"

"Tomorrow night you'll piss into a bottle lying on your stomach and thank Christ if your temperature stays under thirty-nine," Sylwia said, pressing a thick pad of wood-cellulose wadding—German field-gray paper cotton, stolen out of Wehrmacht freight cars at Praga—over the drain tail and strapping it down brutally with two-inch adhesive plaster that ripped the dark hair on his flank when she pulled it taut. "Don't pull the wick out when it stings. If somebody tells you to put pork fat or聖 oil on it, spit in his eye. Who turns him on Thursday?"

"My cousin," the cobbler's wife said. "She nursed at Świętego Ducha before the bombardment."

"Tell her clean boiled forceps, pull half an inch if it slides, re-pack loose if it bleeds bright, and if the skin around it goes bronze or crackles under her thumb, bring him to Lindleya gate in a vegetable cart with a typhus rag tied to the shaft so the gendarmes don't lift the blanket. Ask for Siostra Bronisława at Pavilion Three. Nobody else. Not the gate clerk, not the young doctor with the blond moustache."

She scrubbed her hands in the pickle-barrel dipper over the zinc tub—lye soap gritty with sand that stripped grease and half the epidermis together, then ninety-percent denatured spirit that bit raw purple fissures around every nailbed until her jaw locked. A surgeon's hands in the fourth winter of occupation looked like a washerwoman's scalded in beet-brine. In Berlin, twelve years ago, Geheimrat von Halden used to inspect his assistants' fingernails before eight o'clock rounds along a white towel, tapping any red cuticle with his gold pencil: *A butcher arrives raw, Fräulein Kotarska; a gentleman arrives intact.* Von Halden had three sisters in Charlottenburg and French glycerine soap in a porcelain dish.

Upstairs in the shop, behind shuttered glass still pasted with criss-crossed brown paper from September 'thirty-nine, Marek handed her into her coat. The lining under the left armpit had split again; cold iron from her instrument roll pressed through her wool dress against her ribs.

"Separate at Żelazna?" she asked.

"Walk with me as far as Koszykowa. Your night pass starts at eight; mine's good until nine on Ostbahn repair stamp." He unbuttoned his collar half an inch to scratch the red rash left by ersatz wool scarf dye. Marek had narrow gray eyes, flat cheekbones, and the dry sour smell of wet dog-hair that every winter coat in Warsaw gave off once the prewar English nap wore down to reclaimed shoddy. "You have forty minutes before Pavilion Three."

"Thirty-five. Mirecki drank half the spirit ration after noon amputations; if I'm late he'll let Jankowski touch the hernia in Six, and Jankowski sews peritoneum like mailbags."

They stepped out singly anyway, twenty paces apart until past Chmielna, out of habit deeper than courtesy. Sleet blew horizontally down the dark canyon of Sosa-blackened tenements. Streetlamps were blue slits painted down to thumbnails for blackout; only at the corner of Marszałkowska did light spill wide, dirty yellow across slush where two Feldgendarmerie troopers in crescent metal gorgets stood beside an iron brazier checking the *Kennkarte* of a man pushing a handcart of frozen rutabagas. Pasted across the round kiosk behind them, half-peeled by wet wind and over-slapped with fresh pink theater bills for the Komedia, hung Monday's black-bordered proclamation from Berlin: the Sixth Army fallen faithful to its oath at Stalingrad, venues of amusement reopened since Sunday, defeatist rumour punishable under wartime special statute. Someone had hawked phlegm squarely onto the eagle's wreath, where it had frozen into an amber bead.

At Koszykowa 33 they went up the back service stairs, four flights smelling of boiled cabbage cores, cat urine, and coal clinker used twice over. Inside Sylwia's single room—partitioned in 1941 with beaverboard out of Advocate Goldfeder's old library after the Goldfeders were marched north across the wooden footbridge over Chłodna—the green ceramic stove held barely enough banked peat ash to keep water in the enamel pitcher from skinning over with ice.

Sylwia didn't light the carbide lamp. Carbide cost forty *młynarki* a kilo on Polna and left soot inside your nostrils all morning. Enough gray glare came off the snow on the courtyard roof across the well to show the brass bedstead, the stacked *Zentralblatt für Chirurgie* bound volumes propping up its broken caster, and the tin basin.

Marek locked the door from inside, dropped his wet sheepskin gloves onto the table beside her cold tea glass, and caught her by the hip before she had the second button of her coat undone.

"Your hands are freezing," she said, though his were inside her wool waistband already, blunt-nailed and dry-chapped, shoving her skirt and grey knitted drawers down only as far as she needed to step one boot free.

"So's the stove. Turn round or lie back?"

"Stove's cold on the tiles. Bed."

Nothing about it pretended to be courtship; they had exhausted courtship in November forty-two in twenty minutes behind a blackout curtain in Żoliborz while waiting for a courier who never arrived because he was already dead on Pawiak cobblestones. Now there was the clock ticking inside her open coat pocket, the iron creak of Goldfeder's cut-down daybed, the scratch of Marek's belt buckle against her thigh before she pushed his wool trousers clear of his groin with her boot-toe. She spat into her palm because she was dry and tired and the room was six degrees above zero, reached down between them to guide him in before he bruised her dry against the pubic bone, and locked her ankles hard over his calves to take the weight off the loose caster.

He fucked with the compact, silent economy he brought to bridge demolition tables and dead-letter drops: jaw against her collarbone, breath whistling once through his crooked septum, wool coat still on so its sleet-wet lapel soaked cold through her blouse over her left breast. Halfway through, her eyes stayed open on the plaster rosette stranded half-sliced by the beaverboard wall—half a garland of stucco acanthus leaves going nowhere into raw lath. Beneath the smell of wet shoddy and coal dust sat faint carbolic and the iron stink of the railwayman's venous blood dried into the crease of her left thumb. She shifted her pelvis upward twice, hard, grinding the heel of her own hand against the bone until a sharp, mean knot broke loose low in her belly—more like sneezing out cold water than pleasure, fierce and instantly gone—and bit down on Marek's coat collar so the old widow across the beaverboard wouldn't hear through the plaster and start rattling her rosary beads against the headboard.

Marek came three thrusts later with a muffled grunt into her neck, shuddering once from shoulders to knees. For half a minute neither moved; their mingled breath hung white six inches above her chin.

Then the church clock at Zbawiciela struck the half-hour—seven flat, cracked iron notes carried downwind—and he eased back, wincing as cold air hit wet skin.

"There's lye water in the kettle behind the grate," Sylwia said, swinging her legs down to the rag rug and reaching straight for the Esmarch douche bulb and towel on the washstand without looking back. Pregnancy in February forty-three meant Vesper's replacement list or a curette by mirror-light, and she trusted no man's timing, least of all an engineer's.

Marek buttoned himself in the dark, watching the pale line of her spine where her blouse had pulled free of her corsetless waist-slip. "You're thinner than New Year's."

"Everybody's thinner than New Year's except Bronka and the Volksdeutsche on Filtrowa. My sister had real goose dripping on Sunday. Stefan brought it home from the tram sheds wrapped in *Nowy Kurier Warszawski*." She shivered as icy vinegar-and-alum water bit her insides over the slop pail, dried herself briskly with rough linen, and tugged her drawers up. "Did the boy on Pańska see who shot him?"

"Bahnschutz patrol out of Ochota sidings. Except Bahnschutz don't carry Steyr-Solothurn submachine guns with silenced barrels, and there was a black Opel Admiral parked past the water tower before the shooting started." Marek picked up his gloves, slapped dust from them against his thigh. "Something's sour down toward Grójecka tonight. Half of *Abwehrstelle* had motor-pool movement after four, and Szucha wasn't notified—our girl on the switchboard says Kroll's desk asked the Wehrmacht duty officer twice why Towarowa barrier was closed and got hung up on."

Sylwia pinned a fallen coil of dark hair back above her ear with two steel *szpilki*, pressing them flat until their points scraped her scalp. "Let them shoot each other. Saves us sulfonamide."

"Nothing saves you anything when Germans shoot Germans in our district," Marek said dryly. "They still shoot fifty of us by Friday to balance the ledger. Keep your ears open if any railway casualties come through Lindleya before morning. Don't write anything down. I'll be at the seed shop Thursday noon."

He touched two fingers briefly to the nape of her neck—his sole concession to tenderness, dry and warm as paper—checked the stairwell crack, and was gone without making the third step creak.

Sylwia drank three mouthfuls of cold boiled barley water straight from the glass, slid her clean pocket case of scalpels and arterial clamps into her coat, checked that her *Nachtausweis* with Stabsarzt Vesper's violet eagle stamp sat flat inside her grey *Kennkarte*, and went out into the sleet toward Lindleya.

* * *

By midnight the sleet had stiffened into true eastern snow, dry as ground glass, hissing horizontally between the red-brick pavilions of *Szpital Dzieciątka Jezus*.

Pavilion Three smelled on winter nights as if someone had boiled an old sheep in hospital carbolic and burnt turnip peelings under the pot. Sixty-eight Polish surgical beds occupied the upper two floors—compound fractures from unlit factory belts, frostbitten toes going black like dried plums, perforated peptic ulcers chewed into clerks' stomachs by sawdust bread and terror, three septic abortions logged tactfully as *appendicitis acuta*—while the ground-floor east wing, Rooms Ten through Sixteen behind the double swing-doors painted with a black stencil (*Reserviert — Wehrmacht-Notdienst*), stood three-quarters empty on clean bleached sheets with two radiator pipes actually hot. Stabsarzt Vesper liked four spare emergency beds ready within three minutes of Dworzec Główny in case troop-train officers caught shrapnel from partisan mines east of Mińsk Mazowiecki or burst an appendix full of French brandy at the Soldatenheim. In exchange for keeping those six rooms immaculate, Pavilion Three got two tons of Silesian coal a month and enough ether that Mirecki didn't have to operate under pure rectal chloral like poor Świętego Łazarza.

At ten minutes past one, the city electricity dropped from yellow to copper wire-glow and died altogether.

"Psiakrew," Siostra Bronisława said placidly from the sterilizer alcove, not spilling a drop of the roasted-acorn coffee she was pouring through a surgical gauze square into two porcelain urine-specimen beakers—the clean unused ones from before the war, which held heat better than tin mugs. She struck a safety match on the sole of her wooden clog and touched it to the brass *karbidówka* hanging by chains above the scrub sink. Acetylene burped once, stank of rotten garlic, and bloomed into a hard, shadowless white glare that turned Bronka's broad pockmarked cheeks the colour of turnip and glinted off her gold left eyetooth. "Elektrownia Powiśle again, or the coal froze on the grate. Drink while it's hot, pani doktor, I put half a saccharin tablet in yours."

Sylwia leaned her hip against the cold marble instrument slab, warming both raw palms round the beaker. Across the corridor in the doctors' cubby, Docent Mirecki lay asleep under his prewar camel-hair Raglan coat with his gold pince-nez dangling over his waistcoat buttons and an empty hundred-gram bottle of *Spiritus vini rectificatus* tucked guiltily behind *Chirurgia Kliniczna*. Let him sleep till four. His coronary arteries had been whistling like old kettle spouts since New Year's whenever he climbed stairs fast, and if Mirecki died, the *Amtsarzt* would put a Reichsdeutscher commissar or a Ukrainian collaborator over Pavilion Three within forty-eight hours.

"Bed Twenty-Nine's stump stopped oozing?" Sylwia asked.

"Dry as a bishop's tit since eleven," Bronka said. "I gave him twenty drops of valerian and told him it was pantopon. He went straight off snoring. Mind moves half the pain, like my father used to say when he stuck pigs on Górczewska—don't rattle the bucket till you're behind the ear." She cocked her head suddenly toward the frosted courtyard casement. "Listen."

Not an ambulance bell. Hospital horse-ambulances had brass hand-clappers, and German *Sanitätskraftwagen* used two-tone Bosch horns that echoed all the way from Chałubińskiego. This was an unmuffled four-cylinder air-cooled engine screaming in second gear up Lindleya, missing on one cylinder, chains thrashing cobblestones through packed snow—followed by a tremendous metallic bang as something hit the iron night-gate without waiting for old Walenty to pull the bolt.

Shouting broke out in the courtyard: raw German, high and cracked with panic, then two pistol shots fired straight into the snowy air to bring orderlies running.

Sylwia set the urine beaker down untasted. "Wake the Docent. Put two liters of saline on the Primus and unlock the ether tin."

Before she reached the vestibule doors they kicked inward from outside, smashing the brass latch-plate splintering out of the pine jamb. Freezing wind roared down the tiled hall in a white sheet of blown snow. Behind it came two Wehrmacht *Feldgendarmen* dragged off gate patrol, and ahead of them a bareheaded young *Gefreiter* in a torn Reversible winter parka whose left hand was wrapped in a grey woolen muffler dripping bright scarlet onto the black-and-white tiles like paint flicked from a brush. With his intact right fist and his teeth gripped into a canvas stretcher strap, the boy was hauling the front poles of a blood-blackened litter; a terrified gate porter stumbled backward with the rear handles.

"Chirurg!" the *Gefreiter* bellowed. East Prussian rural vowels, flat and broad, voice tearing raw in his throat; snow had melted and refrozen in blood down his blond eyebrows. Somebody tried to steer him toward the admissions desk ledger and the boy swung his bloody wool-wrapped left stump straight into the clerk's chest, knocking him into the coat rack, and drew a Walther P38 right-handed out of his belt. "Kein Papier! Operationssaal! Sofort—er verblutet mir unter den Händen—*Oberstleutnant*!"

"Put the pistol away before you shoot the chandelier onto your own officer," Sylwia snapped in Berlin German so flat and clipped that the two *Feldgendarmen* stiffened at the heels before realizing a woman in a patched white smock had spoken. "Table One, left through the glass doors. Lift—don't tip the head up if he's exsanguinating, tilt the feet! Bronka, four bricks under the foot-pins, Trendelenburg!"

They heaved the litter directly onto the white enameled operating table without transferring him, canvas poles and all, because the grey wool greatcoat underneath the unconscious man wasn't merely wet—it hung sagged like a hammock full of slaughterhouse offal, clotting dark red jelly sliding out through the canvas weave onto the floor drain. Across the casualty's upper left chest and shoulder socket, someone with crude but furious competence had jammed an entire compressed Wehrmacht field dressing and an officer's leather map-strap twisted with a bayonet scabbard as a makeshift Spanish windlass over the collarbone hollow, where no ordinary tourniquet could catch.

Every pulse beat—weak, racing at one-forty, fluttering like a trapped sparrow—still pushed a fresh bright-scarlet bubble of oxygenated foam and arterial blood up around the soaked wool collar. Subclavian or high axillary artery, plus a nicked apical pleura or bronchus whip-frothing it pink. Four minutes left in the vascular tree, maybe three if he had already dumped two liters onto the floorboards of a car between Ochota and here.

"Scissors," Sylwia said, grabbing the heavy curved Lister shears off Bronka's zinc tray without scrubbing—scrubbing took seven minutes, and in seven minutes this German would be cold meat on enameled iron. She poured raw ninety-percent spirit straight out of the stock jug across both ungloved hands, hissed through her teeth as the Pańska cuts blazed white-hot, and drove the blunt lower beak of the shears inside the stiffened collar of the *Feldbluse* at the throat latch.

Frozen blood, wool, silver-braided collar litzen, cotton undershirt, and linen *Soldbuch* corner parted with a thick, wet crunch. She ripped the left tunic front wide from sternum to shoulder seam, tossing the silver-piped shoulder board—braided *Oberstleutnant* cord, Heer staff crimson? no, plain white/transport dummy piping over field-gray—into the kick-bucket.

Docent Mirecki burst through the swing doors tying his apron crooked over his waistcoat, spectacles crooked, sweet cherry-spirit breath preceding him by a yard. "Meine Herren, bitte—only medical staff inside the sterile perimeter—"

"Stay at the head, panie docencie," Sylwia said in rapid Polish, tearing open the sodden undershirt and slicing the bayonet-twisted leather strap only half a turn while bracing four fingers of her left hand directly over the supraclavicular hollow to catch the geyser before it blew. "Check airway—jaw's locked—don't give ether yet, he hasn't the pressure for ether, give oxygen if the green cylinder's got twenty atmospheres—"

Blood and grey road slush caked the man's face from temple to chin where he had lain cheek-down against rubber floor-matting. Bronka dragged a wet saline sponge hard across his mouth, nose, and eyes to clear the nostrils for the nasal catheter.

The carbide lamp swung half an inch on its ceiling chain in the draft from the broken vestibule door. Hard white acetylene light dropped straight down onto the high bridge of the nose, the sharp philtrum, the flat, arrogant arch of the supraorbital bone, and—just beneath the right cheekbone, scrubbed suddenly clean of dried brown crust—a crescent-shaped white scar three centimeters long where four interrupted silk stitches had been put in slightly too tight in December nineteen-thirty, leaving tiny cross-hatched ladder marks Sylwia knew because she had watched Lotte Bergmann hold the mirror while a drunk Charité *Famulus* sewed it shut.

Above it, his eyelids fluttered half open. Not unconscious—exsanguination stupor. Beneath pale lashes crusted with salt rime, two irises the exact grey-green of winter Spree water under iron bridges stared blind straight through her chin without pupil focus.

Sylwia's heart did not skip; it dropped like an iron sash-weight into cold grease.

*Ludwig Schoeler.*

Beneath her left thumb, buried two inches deep behind the shattered middle third of his left clavicle and shredded pectoralis major, the torn mouth of his axillary artery bucked—*thup-thup-thup*—hot, slick, greased with fat globules and jagged brass splinters, squirting a thin bright jet across her white sleeve toward her throat the instant her thumb shifted half a millimeter in shock.

Nobody else in Warsaw knew that crescent scar. Mirecki saw a dying Wehrmacht *Oberstleutnant* with a smashed shoulder. Bronka saw three liters of mess on her clean tiles and a crazy Prussian corporal holding a Walther P38 against the door glass. Even the corporal staring wild-eyed over Bronka's broad back saw only two white Polish hands buried to the knuckles in his commander's chest.

*Let your thumb slip.*

The thought did not arrive in words; it arrived as an anatomical diagram from Corning's *Lehrbuch der topographischen Anatomie*, 1927 edition, page two hundred and fourteen, inked in red and blue. First part of the arteria axillaris, transitioning off the subclavian over the outer border of the first rib. Retracted proximal stump had already spasmed half-shut from cold and hypotension, which was the sole miracle keeping him breathing at one-fifteen in the morning; now that Pavilion Three's stove warmth and her own thirty-seven-degree fingers were touching the adventitia, the spasm was letting go. Lift her thumb two millimeters—keep her hand buried inside the bloody hole so the corporal saw frantic surgical heroism, call sharply for clamps, fumble half a second in dark pooled blood behind the splintered collarbone where even Geheimrat von Halden lost one in three—and in ninety seconds ventricular fibrillation would flatten those Spree-water eyes to boiled fish-glue.

Ten years since Savignyplatz. March 1933: snow mixed with black paper ash blowing down Luisenstraße, Jakob Mandelbaum's spectacles smashed on the marble stairs of the Pathology Institute, Ludwig standing inside her doorway in his new field-gray Reichswehr greatcoat with his hat in his gloved hand and that exact expressionless, intolerable stillness around his mouth while he laid a second-class sleeping-car voucher to Poznań on her trunk like money left on a whore's dresser. *Take the 23:14 from Friedrichstraße, Sylwia. Don't go back to the clinic for your notebooks.*

Now here he lay on Lindleya Street in a conquered city where children coughed up tubercle bacilli into newspaper handkerchiefs and men were hanged along railway embankments in groups of fifty, wearing silver braided boards on German wool, his hot arterial blood pumping over her ungloved weddingless left hand onto Polish floor tiles.

*Two millimeters. Count to sixty. Sign the death slip at 01:22: Exitus letalis in tabula—ruptura arteriae subclaviae sinistrae. Even Kroll couldn't hang a surgeon for failing to catch a retracted subclavian by carbide lamp.*

On the table, Ludwig's split, bloodless lips parted around a string of pink pleural froth. Not a groan—he had hated groaning even at twenty-one when an abscess in his mastoid was lanced raw—just an ugly, ragged intake through clenched molars as his right hand, cold and gray-nailed, clawed instinctively at the enameled rim of Table One and locked onto the cold steel rail so hard a fingernail split audibly.

*He won't die quiet,* some vicious, ancient nerve behind her breastbone spat back. *And you won't miss a vessel on your own table for any Schoeler alive—not if Satan himself drove the car.*

"Bone cutter—Liston forceps, large!" Sylwia barked, voice dropping half an octave into pure ice, her left thumb clamping the proximal stump flat against the first rib so mercilessly that Ludwig's whole arched torso jerked upward off the canvas despite exsanguination shock. "Bronka, stop staring at the corporal's gun and retract the pectoralis flap hard upward! Panie docencie, two hundred milliliters warm physiological saline into the right cubital vein by cut-down right now—don't hunt for a needle vein, he's collapsed flat, take the #15 scalpel and cannulate! Not too fast till I have the proximal clamp on or you'll blow my clot!"

* * *

For twenty-two minutes nothing existed in the room except the six-inch circle of glare under the carbide reflector, smelling of hot garlic gas, wet bone meal, and iron.

Sylwia resected two centimeters of splintered middle clavicle cleanly with the Liston bone shears—*crunch, crunch*, periosteum stripped back swift and ruthless with a Farabeuf raspatory—because trying to sew a retracted axillary artery blind behind jagged collarbone shards in bad light was how timid surgeons killed men politely. Once the shattered bone wedge lifted out into the kidney dish, the whole infraclavicular fossa opened beneath her eyes like a wet red anatomical plate, though no plate in Corning had two deformed steel grenade-casing fragments and a chewed lump of grey wool collar-felt driven straight across the brachial plexus cords.

"Suction's dead without current," Bronka grunted, wrist veins standing out like clothesline as she hauled the muscle hook upward with one hand and dabbed furiously with wrung-out hot saline gauze packs with the other. "Count sponges for me, pani doktor—that's seven in, seven out—"

"Bulldog clamps. Two. Wet them in sterile paraffin oil first."

Mirecki had the brass cannula tied into the right basilic vein at the elbow crease; warm saline ticked down from the glass funnel held high by the terrified gate porter while Mirecki watched Ludwig's carotid flutter under two trembling tobacco-yellow fingers. "No radial pulse at all, Sylwia. Pupils sluggish but symmetric. If you ligate the main trunk cold at this level in February, the collateral scapular circulation won't carry—the left arm goes gangrenous by Friday."

"I'm not ligating," Sylwia said between her teeth.

Ligating took ninety seconds: tie off both ends with heavy #1 silk, save the carcass, amputate the dead black arm at the shoulder joint four days later. In ninety-nine field dressing stations from Voronezh to Smolensk tonight, German surgeons were tying axillary trunks shut and throwing left arms into lime barrels by the dozen. Twelve years ago at the Charité, during the summer animal-vascular trials when she and Ludwig had spent six blistering July nights side by side in the basement dog-house sewing femoral arteries end-to-end according to Carrel's three-stay triangulation method—each counting the other's thrombi at autopsy the next morning with malicious glee—Ludwig had leaned across the slate sink smoking a cheap *Reval* and said: *Any butcher with twine can tie a pipe shut, Kotarska. Let me see your intima-to-intima everted seam hold eighty millimeters of mercury without leaking, and maybe I'll believe women have thumbs.*

"Carrel needle," she said to Bronka. "Straight fine milliner's if the curved eye's rusted—five-zero black arterial silk soaked in sterile liquid vaseline. Don't touch the thread belly with forceps."

By acetylene light that hissed and pulsed whenever snow gusted through the cracked vestibule, her raw, spirit-burned fingers stopped hurting altogether. Only the artery remained: pale yellow-pink slippery tube five millimeters across, frayed at a ragged diagonal where a twisted flake of steel had sheared two-thirds through the wall and torn the subscapular branch clean off at its root.

She trimmed three millimeters of shredded adventitia and bruised media cleanly away from both ends with iris scissors—*one cut each, never nibble*—losing precious length until mobilizing the distal trunk meant dissecting perilously close to the median and ulnar nerve cords gleaming like wet white Violin strings in the pit of the armpit. If the lateral cord took scar-strangulation later, he would wake with burning causalgia down his thumb and index finger till Judgment Day. Let him burn; she spared the nerve sheath by half a millimeter anyway, because sloppy dissection disgusted her worse than Germans did.

Three equidistant stay-sutures at a hundred and twenty degrees. Bronka held two traction threads taut—steady now as granite, the old Wola midwife sensing delicate vessel work the way a blind horse knew rotten bridge planks—converting the round lumen into a flat glistening triangle so Sylwia could never accidentally catch the back wall with her needle point. Over-and-over continuous everted stitch, less than a millimeter apart, needle sliding grease-smooth through vaselined silk.

Halfway round the posterior wall, Ludwig surfaced out of shock without warning.

Mirecki's saline was refilling his right atrium just enough to perfuse his brainstem, and there was zero anesthetic in his blood—only twenty milligrams of cold morphine someone had jabbed into his thigh out in the snow, which hadn't even absorbed out of frozen subcutaneous fat until now. His spine bowed upward; a raw, guttural sound tore up behind his teeth, and his uninjured right arm whipped out of the porter's grip toward Sylwia's wrist.

Before the porter could yell, the East Prussian *Gefreiter* dropped his Walther back into its holster, lunged across the head of the table, and pinned his commander's right shoulder and jaw down against the rubber pad with his good forearm and the side of his own stubbled cheek—careful even in violence not to jar the left side by a hair's breadth.

"Ruhig, Herr Oberstleutnant," the boy rasped straight into Ludwig's ear, blood from his own mangled left muffler dripping down onto the zinc floor pan beneath the table head. "Liegenbleiben, Herr Oberstleutnant—wir sind raus, der Koffer ist im Feuer, ich hab ihn verbrannt wie befohlen—ruhig jetzt—"

*Der Koffer ist im Feuer. I burned the briefcase as ordered.*

Sylwia did not lift her eyes from the triangle of silk by a fraction of a degree. Her needle took bite eleven, bite twelve, bite thirteen along the anterior wall, pulled snug, knotted thrice square against the third stay-thread. Only her ears registered every syllable in that East Prussian country rasp—*ambush, burned briefcase, before anybody else reached the wreck*—filing it cold behind her forehead where Marek's voice from five hours ago still sat: *Abwehrstelle had motor-pool movement after four, and Szucha wasn't notified.*

"Distal clamp off first," Sylwia murmured.

She pinched the lower bulldog clamp open. Dark retrograde blood filled the stitched segment smoothly, puffing the pale cylinder taut like a tiny white sausage skin. One pinpoint red bead sweated through between stitch four and stitch five; she pressed a warm saline patty gently over it with her ring finger without panic, waited forty seconds by her own carotid pulse, and released the upper bulldog clamp from beneath the first rib.

*Thup. Thup. Thup.*

The artery leaped alive under her bare fingertip—stronger now with four hundred milliliters of warm saline behind it, thrusting solid red hammer-strokes straight across the black silk ring down into the cold gray arm. She slid two fingers down to his left wrist, crusted with dried mud and powder-soot inside the torn cuff. Faint, thready, racing at one-twenty-eight, the radial pulse tapped back against her skin.

Mirecki let out a long wheeze through his moustache that smelled like a cherry orchard on fire. "Matko Boska. It holds. Give me five minutes of ten drops chloroform on the Schimmelbusch mask now so you can close the chest defect and skin before he wakes properly—"

"Close the apical pleura tear only," Sylwia said, picking up the heavy blunt Volkmann curette and curved tissue scissors instead of skin needles. "Three catgut mattress stitches over pectoralis minor to seal the lung dome after Bronka blows positive pressure—no, wait till he coughs on a drop of chloroform, clamp on full inspiration so we trap minimal air. The superficial wound, deltopectoral cleft, and axillary pocket stay wide open."

At the head of the table, the young *Gefreiter* stiffened suspiciously, looking at the gaping palm-sized crater in his officer's upper chest where clipped muscle bellies, resected collarbone ends, and the faint dark line of her stitched vessel pulsed behind a thin muscular curtain she was tacking loosely across it for cover. "Warum nähen Sie ihn nicht zu?" he demanded hoarsely. "Why aren't you sewing him shut?"

Sylwia looked up at him fully for the first time across the steaming tray. Boy wasn't twenty-three; turnip-blond hair plastered in sweat to his skull, pupils huge with shock from his own shattered fingers, dog-blind devotion keeping him upright on two boots full of snow-water.

"Because whoever shot your *Oberstleutnant* punched half a filthy winter overcoat, road horse-shit, and unsterile steel splinters four inches into torn pectoral muscle," Sylwia said in crisp German, cutting every trace of Slavic softness out of her consonants until she sounded like Luisenstraße Amphitheater Number Two. "Sew that shut tonight for cosmetic vanity and by Saturday gas gangrene eats across his mediastinum and kills him stinking. We heal contaminated war wounds *per secundam intentionem*—packed open from the floor up with iodoform gauze, drained, and scraped raw every forty-eight hours until clean granulation grows out from underneath. If you prefer pretty stitches and a pine box by Sunday, drive him to *Lazarett Ujazdowski* and let a Party dermatologist murder him cleanly."

Mirecki winced violently behind his pince-nez—talking about "Party dermatologists" in front of two armed *Feldgendarmen* by the vestibule door was a one-way tram ride toward Aleja Szucha—and hurried in with oiled silk Berlin deference: "Frau Doktor Kotarska trained five years under Geheimrat von Halden at the Berlin Charité, *Herr Gefreiter*. Her vascular technique just saved your commander's life and his left arm."

The boy's pale eyes flicked from Mirecki back to Sylwia's blood-caked hands, lingered half a beat on the steadiness with which she was laying yellow, pungent iodoform ribbon deep into the raw pocket around two fenestrated rubber Carrel tubes, and gave a short, jerky nod. Only then did his knees buckle two inches against the porcelain table pedestal before he caught himself on his right elbow.

"Sit down on that stool before I have two carcasses to mop around," Sylwia told him sharply. "Bronka, once Room Fourteen's bed is warmed with hot bricks, bring this idiot back here so I can trim what's left of his metacarpals before he faints onto my instruments."

Mirecki touched three cautious drops of chloroform onto the wire gauze mask just long enough to let Sylwia drive three swift, brutal catgut figure-of-eights over the deep pleural tear at peak chest expansion, sealing off the sucking whistle in the lung apex while leaving the whole overlying flesh crater unpacked and weeping freely into five layers of yellow iodoform gauze and gray cellulose wadding. No plaster spica yet—if that arterial seam blew a secondary hemorrhage at four in the morning under plaster, nobody would reach the vessel before he died. Instead she bound his left arm tight across his lower ribs in a Velpeau turn of broad calico roller bandage so he couldn't abduct the shoulder and tear six millimeters of black silk apart in delirium.

When the two orderlies slid canvas poles through to lift him toward Room Fourteen, his head lolled sideways toward Sylwia's hip.

Chloroform was light; the cold air of the corridor was already clearing it. His eyelashes—dark at the roots, bleached pale at the tips just as she remembered hating across sunlit dissecting tables when her own eyes burned from formalin—twitched once. Through a slit of clouded green iris, his gaze snagged on her throat, climbed past her chin to her mouth, and stalled on her cheekbones under the harsh garlic-white glare of the corridor lamp.

No recognition could hold at seventy-over-forty pressure with two liters of blood missing; she knew shock physiology cold. Yet his cracked right hand shifted half an inch inside the blanket fold, and one dry German syllable scraped off the back of his tongue—not *Schwester*, not *Arzt*, not *Wasser*.

"*Du,*" he breathed, barely audible under the squeak of the rubber stretcher wheels, and went slack again into gray stupor before Room Fourteen's oak door swung shut behind him.

Sylwia stood alone beside Table One while warm red water swirled sluggishly around boot-clots toward the brass floor grate. Her left thumb joint ached all the way up the radial border of her forearm from twenty-two minutes of bone-crushing compression against his first rib. Slowly she looked down at her palm. Dried brown arterial crust from Ludwig Schoeler's heart filled every life-line and knuckle crease of her skin, overlaid squarely across the pale, twelve-year-old white scar at the base of her thumb that she had taken in Berlin the last July night neither of them ever spoke about aloud.

Behind her at the zinc scrub trough, Bronka pumped freezing well-water into the basin with a screech of iron leather-washers.

"Hot bricks are under his feet, pani doktor," the matron said low in Polish, glancing once toward the vestibule where the two *Feldgendarmen* were already cranking the wall field-telephone to report an *Oberstleutnant* down to garrison headquarters. "The young corporal won't let our porter touch the bloody greatcoat—he dragged it into Room Fourteen himself and stuffed it under the iron bedstead. Who the devil did they shoot up at one in the morning in Ochota?"

Sylwia plunged both raw hands down into ice-cold sublimate of mercury until the salt-burn made gray sparks jump behind her eyelids, took up the stiff bristle brush, and began scrubbing Ludwig's blood out from under her nails.

"Bring the corporal in," she said. "And boil the small bone-nibblers twice."
