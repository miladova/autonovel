The Avidii at Dinner

To marry Cornelia well, it is first necessary to feed the people who might buy — the word the family uses is *ally with* — a large number of dinners at which nothing is proposed and everything is assessed. Lentulus dines the Avidii in the third week of the year. Servilia has chosen the menu as a general chooses ground: no dish that requires apology, no wine that suggests either desperation or the newer taste for novelty, and for the centre of the table a boar from the Cornelian woods, because a family that can serve its own boar is still, in the deepest Roman sense, a family that owns the story of its dinner.

Avidius the father is sixty, rich from grain fleets, freeborn son of a man who unloaded them, and possessed of the most dangerous quality in a negotiator: he is genuinely unembarrassed. His son, the young Avidius of the Caelian evenings, sits at the lowest couch and tries not to look at Cornelia with the expression he uses for singers. Cornelia, who has been dressed by her mother into a masterpiece of modesty with a neckline, performs the dinner the way she performs her music lessons, correctly and with an inner life entirely elsewhere, which her father, catching it, recognises as the Lentulus inheritance more truly than the name.

The seating has been worked out by Servilia on a tablet she will deny exists. A dinner of this kind is a map of a country being negotiated: who reclines where, in whose sightline, within reach of which conversation, is the constitution; the food is merely the government's daily business. Avidius the father is placed where the room's light flatters a man of sixty and where the boar, when it comes, will arrive at his left hand like an ally joining a flank. The son is placed low and opposite Cornelia, who is present because daughters being *assessed* must be visible as evidence and invisible as persons, a branch of optics Servilia has mastered on behalf of a girl she is — though the tablet does not say so — also, in her way, protecting: from this couch Cornelia can see every face at the table and no face can study hers without seeming to. It is the best seat in the room. Nobody will ever tell the guests so.

Cornelia knows. Cornelia, who has been dressed into modesty with a neckline, understands her mother's seating the way she understands her music master's metaphors: as instructions encoded for a reader. From the protected couch she conducts her own assessment, in the hours' long, glazed traffic of being looked past, and her findings, had any committee thought to subpoena them, would have shortened several negotiations. Avidius the father eats like a man who has been hungry in fact and not in rhetoric, and respects a table accordingly. The son eats like a man being watched by a father who has been hungry. Her own father eats like a man making small, exact payments on an enormous loan of attention. Marcus eats like a man refuelling an argument. And her mother eats almost nothing, beautifully, the way generals visit frontiers: to be seen to have confidence in the supply lines.

The talk does its early circuits at the safe altitude: the dedication, the column scaffolding — “A war you can climb inside,” says a cousin of the house, one of the Arpinum line decanted into Rome dinners for ballast, “it's an end to reading, I call it. My books don't have stairs.” — the price of Spanish oil, a lawsuit about a warehouse wall at Ostia, which Servilia steers off the table and into the fish course with a sentence about boundaries being the true poetry of nations. Avidius laughs like a harbour — the sound of timber and money getting along — and Lentulus, who has been waiting for the Ostia wall to come up since a morning client gave it to him like a hot coal in a satchel, disposes of it into the same laugh with a proposal about surveyors over dinner rather than advocates over documents. It is his best moment of the evening. Avidius inclines his great head. Two old Romans have just agreed, between courses, that law is what you threaten and dinner is what you do.

Young Avidius, meanwhile, is being discovered by Cornelia to have a quality the betrothal market does not list because it cannot be faked at scale: he listens with his face. When the cousin from Arpinum tells the story about the augur and the chickens — a story this family has been deploying since before Cornelia was born, with marks on it like height lines on a doorpost — the young man laughs in the new places, the ones the story actually has, rather than the traditional ones. He asks her, across the low table, in the supervised gap between a toast and a sauce, whether she sings.

“I am sung about,” Cornelia says, before she can file the sentence under *the Greek one*, “more than I sing. In this house my voice is issued. One song, at approved houses.”

“My father issues my opinions on a similar system,” he says. “One per dinner, not the nautical ones. He says a young man should be ballast.” He considers the boar being carved with the concentration of a man watching a ship unloaded by a crew he respects. “I think ballast gets a poor report in this city. Ballast is why the cargo arrives.”

It is not wit, exactly. Her brother does wit, beautifully, like a man juggling with his grandfather's glass. This is something her education has fewer words for: a young man making himself unremarkable at exactly the rate that lets a room relax around him, the way her mother's seating lets a girl see. Money from grain, her mother says, when the girl reports nothing that evening and is read anyway. A boy who can wince. There have been worse foundations.

The fish course arrives on a platter with its own escort, as fish does in this house, and with it the evening moves, as Servilia's tablet always intended, from reconnaissance to the first real exchange of fire, which in Roman dinners is called compliments. Avidius raises his cup the width of a wall at Ostia toward his host.

“Your committee does you credit,” Avidius says over the fish, because new money always compliments old money on its morals; it costs nothing and it sounds like a subscription. “The city wants order. Order is good for shipping.”

“Order is good for everything except conversation,” Lentulus says, and the table laughs on cue, and he hates the laugh a little, the purchased sound of it, though he has made the joke precisely to purchase it.

It is Marcus who breaks the evening, as water finds the crack in a jar. He has drunk exactly enough to mistake honesty for courage. “Father's order begins on the Caelian,” he says. “One house at a time. The committee has a short list.”

Silence, with cutlery. Servilia looks at her son the way an engineer looks at a bridge. Avidius the father, who built a fortune by hearing what a room costs, hears this room's price change and files it without moving his face at all. Young Avidius studies his hands. Cornelia — Lentulus sees it peripherally, the way one sees weather — stops being elsewhere. She is entirely, dangerously present.

“My son,” Lentulus says, “has the advocate's fault of stating a case before he has been briefed. The committee's list is the city's list. All unregistered establishments.”

“Of course,” Avidius says, warm, rich, unconvinced, already recalculating a dowry against a scandal surcharge in the privacy of a mind that has never once confused a dinner with a pleasure. “All Rome. Naturally.”

The guests leave in the order Servilia has arranged for their leaving, which is the order that makes it hardest to tell the evening went wrong: principals first, with embraces that have the warmth of treaties; the cousin from Arpinum next, sent off into the wet street rehearsing a version in which he said the thing about boundaries; finally the musicians, paid at the door by the steward in a transaction the household conducts, like all its transactions with art, as if money were slightly embarrassed to be seen working. In the corridor, while cloaks are being found and litters called, young Avidius finds himself beside Cornelia's music master — who has attended, unobtrusively, to tune and to be a reason the girl need not linger — and, in the manner of his family when it wishes to load cargo without a crane, engages the wrong man entirely on purpose.

“She sings the Greek songs?” he says. “The daughter.”

The master, who has survived nineteen houses by answering questions about daughters as if they were questions about weather in distant provinces, says, “The young lady's Greek is excellent.”

“Then somebody should hear it who can tell.” Avidius accepts his cloak from a slave and does the dockside thing again, the thing that has been getting him assessed kindly all evening: he thanks the slave by catching his eye, as men do who have been handed things by grandfathers who unloaded boats. “In my father's house we don't keep quality in the warehouse. It spoils the manifest.”

The music master reports this, later, in the course of being fed in the kitchen — musicians and ex-gladiators being the two professions Roman households feed after use without minuting — and Servilia hears it at third hand while her hair is being taken down, in the braid of voices through doors that constitutes her evening intelligence service. She revises the Avidii by another half degree, the way a banker revises a rate, and files the young man under a heading the family has not used since her own marriage negotiations: *possible, with supervision of a kind he may not require*.

Cornelia, in her room, with her hair also coming down, in hands not her mother's hairdresser's daughter's but a slave of her own age whose name is Tyche and who has opinions the household has not yet learnt to bill for, says, “He listens with his face.”

“That's ballast,” Tyche says, whose brother works the Ostia boats when there are boats. “My brother says the ship boys learn it or swim.”

“Marcus says I am being sold.” Cornelia says it to the mirror, which is bronze and dented and has heard the women of this house say worse to it for fifty years, usually while being got ready to be looked at. “He says it to be the only honest man in the family. It is his winter coat.”

“Are you, domina?”

“I am being *allied with*,” Cornelia says. “It is a better garment. Warmer, Marcus says, though somebody else keeps the key to the wardrobe.” She is quiet while the pins come out, each with its small sound on the tray, like a count being taken of the day's true expenditure. “If I must be ballast, Tyche, I would like to know the waters.”

Tyche, who will be freed in Servilia's will and knows it and has therefore the freedom of the almost-free, which is the most dangerous dialect in any household, says, “Ask my brother about the waters, domina. Or —” the hands do not pause, the voice does the pausing for them “— ask at the house on the Caelian. The women there know all Rome's waters. They charge by the piece, I'm told, and the thread is separate.”

Cornelia laughs — once, unissued, a sound made entirely at her own rates — and then doesn't, because the laugh has shown her something in the bronze she is not ready to have seen before her father has finished being spoken to downstairs, in a room full of cooling food and servers moving like a tide going out.

Later, when the guests have gone, Servilia does not raise her voice. She has raised it perhaps four times in nineteen years of marriage; the household still tells the stories. She says, “Your son dines at that house and then discusses yours at my table. Your daughter has just learnt that her marriage is a transaction at which her brother may heckle. And Avidius has learnt that the Cornelii can be embarrassed at their own board, which he will presently charge us for. This is your committee's work, Lentulus. I hope the Senate applauds it as warmly.”

“The boy will be spoken to.”

“The boy is twenty. Being spoken to is your family's substitute for every other form of action.” She is removing her jewellery, piece by piece, into the hands of a slave who has heard everything in this room for a decade and will die having said none of it for money, because Servilia pays for silence prospectively, in kindness, which compounds better. “End it, or win it. Do not keep it. A kept quarrel is a pet, and this family cannot feed pets.”

“Then have the pet's keeper to dinner with an explanation,” Servilia says, in the tone of a woman closing a tablet, and goes out to do the household's night round — kitchen damped, doors witnessed, the day's small treasons of waste and gossip corrected before they harden into systems. She does it on feet that make no announcement. Lentulus has sometimes thought his wife and the mistress of the Caelian are the two people in Rome who understand that authority is mostly the management of approach: no noise until she is in a room, then the room owes her an explanation. He does not say it. There are comparisons a man may think in his own house at night that would constitute, in daylight, a foreign policy.

Marcus is summoned to the study, where being spoken to has been conducted for three generations under a bust of the grandfather whose mask is in the atrium and whose opinions are therefore present twice, in case the room forgets whose walls these are. He comes in still in his dinner clothes, with the particular brightness of a young man who has been drinking just enough to have located his honesty and not yet enough to have lost the address.

“You discussed the committee at my table,” Lentulus says. “In front of the Avidii. In front of your sister.”

“I described the committee. As the baths describe it. As the aediles' own clerks describe it, Father, after the second cup, in establishments your committee is inspecting.” Marcus stands because the study only offers one chair to juniors and he is too angry, or too accurate, to take it. “If the description is a problem, the committee might try being something else.”

“The committee is a registry of—”

“One house,” Marcus says. “At dawn. With a rod.” He does a thing then that the room notices: he stops performing. The brightness goes out of him like a lamp being carried into another room, and what is left is twenty years old and tired in a way his education has no form for. “Cornelia looked at me when I said it as if I'd sold *her*, at the table, in front of the buyers. I keep thinking: that is the look this house teaches. I have been practising it in mirrors since I was twelve, I just didn't know the occasion.”

Lentulus looks at his son the way he looks at provincial reports he suspects of being true in the wrong direction. “Your sister,” he says, choosing the words as if they were good coin from a bad purse, “will marry where the family can honourably place her, with a settlement her mother will negotiate to the last clause, into a house whose money is newer than ours and whose son, by every report including his conduct at my table under provocation, is not a fool. If that is selling, then every farm my ancestors bought with a bride's dowry is a market stall, and the masks in the atrium are shop signs. We call it *alliance* because the word obliges both parties to behave as if the transaction were nobler than appetite. The obligation is real even if the word is upholstery. Most of Rome is.”

It is a good speech. It has the columns: family, honour, the etymology of a euphemism audited like a harbour due. Marcus listens to it the way a man listens to weather in a country he means to emigrate from.

“And the house on the Caelian?” he says. “What word obliges us there? Because I dine there, Father — you know I dine there; Onesimus knows by professional forgetting, every bath attendant in Rome knows by retail — and the woman who runs it sends back my messengers with lessons in drinking slowly, and her singer returned a necklace this week because the stones were—” He catches himself, too late, as young men do; the sentence completes itself in his father's face.

“Glass,” Lentulus says. “You gave a woman glass and called them emeralds, and she returned them with a compliment, and now you are hers for a month and truthful for a week.” Marcus stares. Lentulus, who does not know he is quoting the mistress of the house word for word — who cannot know, unless the plumbing now runs in both directions and uphill, which in this city it generally does — finds he has produced the analysis out of whatever men of his experience produce such things: the archive behind the ribs. “It was done to your grandfather by a singer from Alexandria in the consulship of — it doesn't matter. The technique is older than the aqueducts. What matters, Marcus, is whether you learned the price of the stones or the price of the lie. A Cornelius may pay either. He may not remain ignorant of the exchange rate.”

“I learned she wouldn't be bought at my rate,” Marcus says, quietly. “Which in this family makes her either a bad investment or—”

“Or kin to nobody we can afford.” It comes out before Lentulus can file it. The room with the duplicated grandfather receives the sentence in the order of the evening, not the order of the questions, and Marcus — who is twenty, and who will remember this winter in a scroll-case for years — looks at his father with the first intimation that the locked rooms of this house are not a style of carpentry.

“Go to bed,” Lentulus says, dismantling the moment with a father's authority, the cheapest tool in the house and the only one that always fits. “Not out. Not at dawn, not by the garden gate. Tomorrow you will call on the Avidii with a note in your mother's Latin apologising for your advocate's manner at dinner. You will not mention committees, emeralds, or honesty. Honesty, Marcus, is a long position. Take it from a house that has been short of it before.”

Marcus goes to bed in the sense the house records: his room is occupied, his sandals are by the door, a slave has seen the lamp go down. Rome's great families run on such records; whole treasons have foundered on the sandals. He lies in the dark doing the arithmetic his tutors never set: the cost of a glass necklace, professionally returned; the cost of a sister's face at a dinner table; the exchange rate, currently quoted in his father's study, between a lie and its price. Somewhere below, the house does its night water sounds, the garden fountain conversations it has with itself when the clients have gone. He has grown up over this plumbing the way Prima grew up over the vats. Neither of them, tonight, can hear the similarity. Similarity is for people with a fourth set of books.

He thinks about Daphne's room at the house — not as men of his age are supposed to think about singers' rooms, which is the part he would least survive having minuted, but as a place with accounts in it. She showed him once, between songs, not to impress him but because he had been holding forth about his family's papers like a man showing a wound, the little tablet she keeps: earnings, the Greek's receipts, a note of what her former owner's contract had cost to buy out, *paid*, in a hand that got steadier as the sum got older. “My first copy,” she said. “I cried over a balance sheet.” He had laughed, from the great height of twenty, at the available targets. Tonight the memory stands in his dark room like a person who has been shown in without being announced.

By the time the watch changes he has done what his family does with unwelcome arithmetic for three hundred years: he has decided to take it to a committee of one, at another address, where the lamps are better and the minutes are kept, as he has started to say at dinners, for the pleasure of the heckle, by women who count. The garden gate is not locked against the heir of the house; it is merely witnessed when he uses it, which is the Cornelian system in its entirety. The door slave, who is paid by Servilia prospectively and in kindness, says, “Young master,” in a tone that goes into no book, and Marcus, going out into the rain with his father's last sentence still spending itself in him — *honesty is a long position; take it from a house that has been short of it before* — thinks: I will just be rained on, at her door, in the back street, until somebody instructs me in manners. It is not a plan. At twenty, in this family, it is as close to a conscience as makes no difference.

A dinner at which two houses are considering becoming a tide requires more staff than a small war and produces better intelligence. In the kitchen, quantities are being treated as diplomacy: too much luxury and the Avidii will suspect the Cornelii of covering debt with saffron; too little and the Cornelii will have filed themselves under decline. Servilia descends personally to inspect the fish because a woman who has run a household for decades learns that some metaphors must be gutted before guests arrive.

“Which course is the shipping interest meant to admire?” the cook asks.

“The one that arrives warm. Let admiration follow competence for a change.”

Marcus is intercepted on his way past the pantry by Onesimus, who removes from his hand a cup he has already filled with the optimism of a young man expecting to be bored. “Young master,” Onesimus says, “dinner is a succession of small doors. Enter later ones sober enough to know which house you are in.”

“I am always in my father’s house.”

“That,” Onesimus says, “is the ambiguity under management.”

Cornelia, in the music room, is trying out the Greek song at a pace her master describes as “maritime,” meaning apparently that it no longer hugs the coast of his instruction. He has been paid a fee Servilia described in the accounts as *continuing*, a word that has soothed his tendency to correct from the wrist. When he ventures that a bride should not sound “so knowing”, Cornelia asks whether knowing can be detected from the dining couches or only by professional appointment.

Her mother, entering at exactly the moment an answer might become useful to someone other than Servilia, says, “A bride should sound paid for. Sing the Roman one first.”

“And the Greek?”

“If the harbour asks.”

“It will ask in figures.”

“Then consider the figures foreigners.”

Downstairs Avidius the elder is being shown the dining room by Lentulus and appears to be pricing the past by the square foot. He has the expression of a man trying to work out whether three centuries are load-bearing. “A fine room,” he says.

“It has held worse decisions.”

“Most of mine took place near salt water. The room helped dissolve the evidence.”

It is not yet alliance. It is the sound of two kinds of Roman testing whether the floor complains under both.

Marcus, spoken to, goes straight to the Caelian, because at twenty the shortest distance between a rebuke and its repetition is a straight line. The house is quieter tonight: rain, a thin Roman rain that makes the streets shine and keeps the merely curious at home. Daphne is not singing. She is sitting in the small dining room mending a torn strap on her costume — they all mend, Antonia insists on it, a house policy older than Daphne's freedom: no woman here is only ornamental, it rots the soul and the budget — and Prima is beside her, detained by the rain, showing her how to set a stitch so the tear will not remember itself.

Marcus, wet-shouldered, eloquent with grievance, tells the room about the dinner. He tells it well. He has his father's gift for a sentence and none of his father's instinct for an audience. Prima, listening, hears a senator's son describe his sister's marriage as a sale and his father's morals as a siege, and she thinks — with the practical clarity of a girl whose own betrothal contract was read aloud to her once, quickly, by a father who stumbled on the clause about her dowry of tools — that the rich are simply poor people whose transactions have better Latin.

“You shouldn't talk about your sister like that,” she says.

The room looks at her. Daphne's needle stops. Marcus, who is accustomed to being the most interesting thing in any room under senatorial rank, experiences the novel sensation of being corrected by the furniture, and — to his credit, or to Daphne's influence, or to the rain — he does not reach for rank.

“Why not? It's true.”

“Because she isn't here to tell it wrong herself.” Prima ties off the stitch. “My mother used to say a hem tells you how a garment was treated when no one was looking. So does a story.”

“My mother,” Marcus says, “says nothing at all, in several languages.” But he says it without the laugh he arrived with. He looks at Prima then, properly, the way the baths slaves say men look when a lane opens unexpectedly not before them but beside them. “Have I seen you? At the house of — no. At the fuller's. You're the fuller's girl who reads.”

“I am the fuller's daughter who sews,” Prima says. “Reading is what I do instead of sleeping. Like your drinking.”

“Reading,” Marcus says. “Instead of sleeping.” He has found the one dry cushion in the room by instinct and taken it by right of pedigree, and now he addresses Prima across Daphne's mending with the air of a man discovering a province where his coinage is not yet legal tender. “What does a fuller's daughter read? This is not baths talk. I'm asking.”

“Accounts,” Prima says, “that other people do wrong in the second column. Letters for the women in our street whose men are at Ostia with the fleets — reading them, writing back; that's most of reading, in my street: other people's news, held steady.” The phrase arrives in her mouth with a familiarity she can't invoice; painters of words would call it inheritance. “And a book my mother kept. Not a book. Leaves. Recipes. Counts. Names of women in Baiae with the stairs they worked and what the houses owed them when they died. She called it her *ledger*, though there was no money in it that ever got paid.”

Daphne's needle stops. Daphne, who can hear a false note through a closed door and a true story through Vestals, says, “Your mother kept a ledger of the unpaid.”

“She kept the stairs counted. So they wouldn't count us.” Prima bites a thread. “When she died the guild women wanted to burn it with her — *her things*, they said. My father pottered about with the honey and said no, very quiet, the way he says things that are already in the accounts. It's in my work-box now, under the needles. It weighs nothing. That's how you can tell it's ours.”

Marcus is looking at the work-box with an expression his tutors were paid to correct and didn't, the exact face Diomedes will see in the spring on a banker's clerk being shown a fourth set of books. “My family keeps the opposite kind,” he says. “Everything paid, entered twice, three hundred years of it, and nobody can say where the money *is*. My sister is the money now. We're trading her for a harbour.” He says it to the work-box, having learned in the last four minutes not to say it to the room. “I shouldn't talk about her like that. You told me. I'm — practising on the furniture.”

“Practise on your father,” Prima says, and then, because she is nineteen and has been correcting a senator's heir for an evening in a house her father has forbidden her to eat in, she hears her own audacity arrive like a dropped tray and has to decide, fast, whether to pick it up.

It is Daphne who picks it up, with a singer's timing, threading a needle by a light she seems to be personally on terms with. “Fathers,” she says, “are the advanced classes. Furniture first. Then brothers. There will be examinations.” She holds the strap up: the tear is a rumour now, a place where light changes its mind. “My father sold my contract to a touring company when I was fourteen, against a debt with the word *honour* in it. The first thing Antonia did when this house bought me out was give me my own copy of the accounts, every quarter, to keep. I cried over a balance sheet. Singers are not supposed to. It was that or the fish course, she said; the house has a policy about doing both at once.”

“Everybody in this house has a policy,” Marcus says. “It's like the Senate, with food.”

“It's like the Senate,” Daphne says, “with the women counting.” It gets the room's laugh — Prima's too, unwilling, unfiled — and into it Barbatus puts his head round the door with a dry cloak over his arm and the expression of a man appointed by the weather.

“Young master. Rain's easing. Street's deciding.” He looks at the sewing, the heir, the general constitutional position. “Mistress is doing the late round. She does it on feet like a tax audit. No noise until she's in a room, then the room owes her an explanation.”

“I was just—” Marcus begins, and gets up, and discovers, standing, that he has been in the one dry cushion and Prima's stool has been taking a drip from somewhere in the old ceiling with the patience of a province. He looks at the damp flower-dark stain being made, stitch by stitch, irrelevant by her shoulder. “Your seat is wet. All this time. You might have said.”

“I might have been offered,” Prima says, to her needle, at her usual rates, “the dry one.”

It is that sentence — its cadence, filed flat as a coin on a counter — that Antonia, doing her late round on feet like a tax audit, hears from the corridor with the towels for a different room entirely over one arm.

Antonia, coming in at that moment with dry towels for a guest who is not expected to be in her house at all during a committee investigation, hears her own cadence come out of a stranger's mouth — the dry, level understatement she has spent twenty years believing she invented out of necessity in Baiae, on stairs, under water jars. She stands in the doorway with the towels on her arm and the whole house, the rain, the lamps, performing their little conspiracy of ordinariness around her, and she thinks: *No. Not yet. I have a house to lose first. One catastrophe in its proper order.*

“Marcus Lentulus,” she says, “your cloak is dripping on a mosaic older than your family's debts. Barbatus will lend you a dry one and the use of the back street, and if you love your sister as much as you love talking about her, you will be home before your absence becomes a second course. Prima, the rain is easing. Eirene will walk you to the crossing with a lantern. And Daphne —” she looks at the mended strap, the stitch that will not remember itself “— good. That is how all repairs should look. As if nothing were ever torn. We are none of us required to believe it.”
