# Eight

## The Confession

He came to Bagatela on the twentieth at eleven, in the rain, with his hat dripping, and Hanna took his coat and hung it by the stove, and he thanked her by name, which surprised her, and she went out pink.

"Sit down, Father."

"I'll stand. I've been sitting in a train since six." He stood in the middle of the office with his hands behind his back, and looked at the portrait of Gomułka, and at the map with the pins, and at the brass ashtray, and then at her. "You've moved the desk."

"The light."

"It was better before." He turned to the window. "You can see the Palace from here. Do you look at it?"

"Every day."

"It's a terrible building."

"Yes."

"I like it," he said.

She laughed, once, before she could stop herself, and then put her hand over her mouth, and he smiled at her with his whole face, and for a moment the office was a room in a village in 1940 and there was a book behind the stove.

Then he said, "The twentieth. You asked for the twentieth. I have come for the answer to the question you did not ask me on the seventh."

She opened the drawer and took out the paper. It was one page, typed, with a space at the bottom for a signature and a date and a line for the witness.

"That's it?" he said. "That's the famous paper?"

"That is it."

He took it and read it standing, in the light from the window, and it took him less than a minute, and when he had finished he put it down on the desk very carefully, as though it were something that might break.

"It's shorter than I expected."

"It always is."

"It says that I undertake to furnish information to the organs of the Security Service concerning persons and matters coming to my knowledge in the exercise of my office, and that I will maintain the confidentiality of this cooperation." He looked up. "It does not say the word priest anywhere. I notice that. It is written for a factory foreman."

"Yes."

"And if I sign it, I am a bishop in December."

"If you sign it, the Office's opinion will be favourable and the nomination will go forward. I will not pretend that the rest is certain, but that is what my opinion is worth."

"And what is my opinion worth?" He picked it up again. "They will have this in a file in a room, and in ten years there will be a man at that desk—" he nodded at her chair — "who is not you, and he will open the file and he will find this paper with my signature, and he will know what I am. And then he will telephone me about something, and I will do what he asks, because he will not have to threaten me. He will only have to mention the paper." He put it down. "That is how it works, isn't it. It is not the first signature that costs. It is the second, which is cheaper."

"Yes," she said. "That is how it works."

"You are very honest with me."

"I have decided to be. It is a recent decision and I am not sure it will last."

He walked to the window and stood with his back to her, looking out at the yard and the wall and the plane tree, which was bare now.

"Zofia. I want to ask you something, and I want you to answer as the official, not as yourself."

"Ask."

"If I refuse — if I go back to Lublin and I am not made a bishop, and Pacewicz is made a bishop instead, and he is a decent dull man who will do nothing for twenty years and will sign whatever is put in front of him — what happens to you?"

She was silent.

"Answer the question."

"I will be removed from this office," she said, "within a month. There will be a commission. The commission will read a German deposition and a note I wrote in 1949 and a hearing at which I gave evidence against a man who has just been rehabilitated. I will not be expelled from the Party, because expelling me would require an explanation, and explanations are expensive. I will be transferred to something small — a museum, perhaps, or a publishing house, or the cultural section of a voivodeship council in a town where there is nothing. My husband will keep his post, because his post has nothing to do with me. In three years no one will remember my name."

He turned round.

"And you knew that when you refused Wrona's note."

"Yes."

"Then why did you refuse it?"

"Because it was a lie," she said, "and because I am tired of writing sentences that are not true, and because —" She stopped.

"Because?"

"Because you said in a cell in 1951 that you would like there to have been one piece of paper in the world with the truth on it," she said, "and I have been thinking about that for eight days, and I find that I would like the same thing, and I have left it very late."

He came back to the desk. He stood there looking at the paper, and then he picked up the pen from the tray, and unscrewed the cap, and she felt her heart turn over.

"No," he said. "I'm sorry. I am not going to sign it in front of you as a way of being dramatic. I am not going to sign it at all." He put the pen down. "But I want you to know that I stood here for a moment and considered it, and the reason I considered it was not the bishopric. It was you. It was the thought that if I signed it, you would keep your office, and you have built your whole life on that office, and I know what it costs a person to lose the thing they have built." He looked at her. "That is the only reason I have. I want it on the record, since we are being honest."

"Thank you."

"It isn't a kindness. It's a confession." He put on his hat. "I will be at St Anne's until six. If the Office requires anything further, it can write to the Curia, and the Curia will lose the letter, and in the spring I will be a curate in a parish in the country and this will all be a story that someone tells at a table." He went to the door, and then turned. "Zosia. Come to the church. Not to the Office. To the church."

"Why?"

"Because you have something to say and you have been carrying it for fourteen years, and you are not going to say it in an office with a portrait of Gomułka on the wall."

She sat for a long time after he had gone. Then she rang for Hanna and told her to cancel the afternoon, and went home, and slept for two hours in the middle of the day, which she had not done since 1952.

***

She went at half past five.

The queue for confessions at St Anne's was eight people long and mostly women, and it moved slowly, and she stood at the end of it in her grey coat with her hands folded, and the church was cold and smelled of wet stone and candle wax, and the candles on the stand by the pillar burned with small steady flames that leaned when the door opened.

The confessional was a wooden box on the north side with a curtain and a grille and a little shelf for a hand. She had not been in one since she was seventeen. She knelt down in the dark on the other side of the grille and put her hands on the shelf, and she could hear him breathing, and the rustle of the stole, and beyond the curtain the low murmur of the church.

"Bless me, Father, for I have sinned."

Her voice came out wrong. He was silent for a moment.

"Zofia."

"Yes."

"Say the rest."

"It has been fourteen years since my last confession." She heard herself say it, and it was such an ordinary sentence, and it was the most extraordinary thing she had said in her adult life. "I do not remember the formula. I have not been to communion since 1944."

"Then we will do it badly together, and it will still count." He moved behind the grille. "How long has it been since you were last at Mass? Not confession. Mass."

"June 1944. Except that I sat at the back of a church three weeks ago at six in the morning and did not go up."

"Fourteen years." A pause. "That is a long time to be hungry and not know it."

"I'm not hungry."

"You are in a church at half past five in the evening in November with your hands on a shelf."

She looked at her hands. They were shaking. She put them in her lap.

"I don't know where to start."

"Start with the worst thing," he said. "It is usually at the top, and people spend a great deal of time at the bottom."

She sat in the dark for a moment. Then she began.

"I was taken on the fourteenth of June 1944 in the meadow east of Brzozówka. There was a patrol, three men, and they found a message in my basket, and they took me to the post at Krasnystaw and put me in a room at the back with a table and two chairs and a wasp on the window. The gendarme was called Kliemann. He was fat and pleasant and he had a bottle of beer and he did not offer me any. He asked me where the partisans were and I said nothing. He hit me twice, or the Ukrainian did, and then he told me that at six o'clock he was going to send a company to the village and burn it, and that if I told him where the wounded Russian was, he would send four men instead and it would be a police matter and the village would be spared." She stopped. "And I told him. I told him the cellar under the forge. I gave him my father's cellar and the man in it, and I signed a paper, and it said that there were Jews in the house as well."

"Did you say that?"

"I don't know." She said it flatly, looking at the grille. "That is the truth, Father, and it is the worst thing I have. I have gone over it for fourteen years and I cannot hear my own voice. I remember the wasp. I remember the table. I remember him writing, and the pen going slowly, and I remember thinking that if I said the right thing he would stop writing. I do not remember saying the word *Jews.* It is in the paper. I signed the paper. That is all I know."

The church was quiet. Somewhere a door opened and closed.

"They were in the orchard by then," she said. "My father had moved them the day before, because of the Russian. They were in the hole under the woodpile, and they had been there two days, and Sara had come out at night and looked at the stars and said *oh.* And they found them anyway, because the paper said so, or because the gendarmes searched the orchard anyway, because they searched everything. It does not matter which. They were shot at the edge of the birch wood with the Russian, five of them, and my mother and my brother were in the house, and my father was taken to Lublin, and the village burned. And I have never said this to a living person. Not to my husband. Not to anyone. I let a man be blamed for it — Antoni Szeliga, the deputy sołtys, who was innocent, and who hanged himself in 1953, and whose daughter still lives at Krasnystaw and does not know my name."

"Go on."

"I went to work for the Party in 1945. I told myself it was for the school and the land reform. It was for the school and the land reform, partly. It was also because I could not bear to be a person who had done what I had done and had nothing to show for it, and because a man told me that guilt was a bourgeois luxury and I believed him because I wanted to." She looked at the grille. "His name was Wiktor Sroka. He was a good man in the way that a hammer is a good thing, and in 1951 I gave evidence at his hearing, and my evidence was correct and it was not generous, and in 1953 they shot him, and in April of this year they quashed the sentence. And there is a document with my name on it in a file, and the file will be read, and I have known that for seven years and I have never once written to his wife."

"Anything else?"

"In 1949 a commission asked me about my wartime contacts and I gave them a sentence about a man I loved. It is in his file. It went with him to Mokotów. I wrote that he was gifted and should not be permitted to teach youth."

"Why?"

"Because I was twenty-two and frightened and because it was true," she said, "and because the truth, said in a certain room, in a certain voice, is the most dangerous thing in the world, and I have spent nine years learning how to say true things in that voice. I have signed circulars that put questions to parish priests about their politics. I have written opinions on men's lives. I have sat in this city and been promoted twice." She stopped. "And on the fourteenth of June 1944 I said a sentence in a room to save my own life, and thirty-one people died, and the sentence I said in 1949 was the same sentence. It was the same sentence, Father. It was the same sentence with better grammar."

Neither of them spoke for a while.

"Zofia," he said at last, "I am going to do something now that will seem to you like a betrayal, and I want you to listen to the whole of it before you say anything."

"All right."

"I cannot absolve you."

She sat very still.

"It is not because of the size of what you have confessed. If it were only the size I would absolve you and give you a penance and send you out, and you would go home and be a person who had been to confession, and it would do you no harm and some good. But there is a decree, from 1949, of the Holy Office, which our bishops have accepted in this country, and it says that a Catholic who knowingly and freely joins the Communist Party, or who defends or spreads its doctrine, may not be admitted to the sacraments unless he renounces it. You are not merely a member. You are an officer of the state whose function is to administer the Church. I cannot give you absolution while you are that, and if I did, I would be giving you a piece of paper that means nothing, and you and I both know exactly what a signature that means nothing costs, because we have both signed one."

She looked at the grille. Her hands were cold.

"So the answer is no."

"The answer is: not yet, and not like this." His voice was low and steady. "I am not refusing you. I am telling you the truth in a box in the dark, which is the only place where it is any use. If I told you that you could have both — the office and the absolution, the Party card and the Host — I would be a liar in a stole, and there is nothing lower than that." A pause. "And I will tell you something else, which is not doctrine. I forgave you in 1951, in a cell, before I had finished reading the German paper. I have forgiven you every morning since. It does not stay done — that is the thing nobody tells you about forgiveness, it is not an event, it is a labour — and I have done it in the dark in a room in Lublin for seven years, and I have never once been able to tell you, and I have wanted to, and now I have."

She put her hand over her mouth.

"That is the man's forgiveness," he said. "It is worth what it is worth. The other thing — the thing in the sacrament — is not mine to give on my own terms, and I will not pretend it is, because the pretending is the whole disease of this country." He shifted. "Do you understand what I am saying to you?"

"I understand," she said, "that I have to choose."

"You have always had to choose. You have been choosing since 1944. What is new is that someone has finally told you the price." He was quiet for a moment. "Would you like to make an act of contrition? You may. It does not require me."

"I don't remember it."

"Say the first line and stop."

She sat in the dark. She could hear the candles guttering beyond the curtain, and someone's shoes on the stone, and her own breathing.

"Boże mój," she said, "żałuję za grzechy moje—"

Her voice broke on the last word. She stopped, and sat there, and did not go on, and after a while she said, in a perfectly ordinary voice, "That is all I have."

"That is enough for tonight."

She got up. Her knees hurt. She pushed the curtain aside and stood in the aisle, and the church was darker than it had been, and the candles had burned down, and there were three people left, all of them old, and they were all praying.

He came out of the confessional a moment later and stood beside her, and he had taken off the stole and folded it over his arm, and he was a thin man in a black cassock with a bad shave, and he looked tired and about ten years older than he had at eleven that morning.

"Walk with me to the sacristy," he said. "I have to lock up."

***

They went down the side aisle together. The sacristy was a small room with a cupboard and a sink and a table with a clean cloth, and a smell of starch and old incense, and a window with a bar.

He put the stole in the cupboard and turned.

"Ask me the question," he said.

"What question?"

"The one you came to ask. You did not come here for the sacrament. You could have gone to any priest in Warsaw for that and you would have got a kinder answer and a shorter one." He folded his arms. "Ask me."

She stood in the middle of the sacristy with her gloves in her hands.

"If I asked you to sign," she said, "would you?"

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he said, "Yes."

She closed her eyes.

"I would sign it," he said, "and I would do it badly, and I would hate myself in the morning, and I would do it, and I would be a bishop in December, and every time I stood up in that cathedral I would know that I was there because a woman asked me at six o'clock in a sacristy on a Tuesday in November." He picked up his hat from the table. "That is what you have, Zofia. That is the whole of my offering. It is not much and it is not clean and it is yours."

She opened her eyes.

"Then I will never ask," she said.

"Zofia—"

"Never. Not tonight and not in ten days and not in ten years." She put on her gloves, one and then the other, and she was surprised to find that her hands were steady. "You said I had to choose. This is the choice. I am not going to buy a bishop, and I am not going to buy an absolution, and I am not going to spend the rest of my life being a woman who did a thing for a man because he loved her." She looked at him. "I have done that once. It cost thirty-one people. I am not doing it again."

He stood there with his hat in his hands.

"You will lose your office."

"Yes."

"You will lose more than that. They will not leave you alone. Wrona will not leave you alone."

"I know."

"And you will have nothing to show for it. Not even me." His voice was not quite steady. "I will be a curate in a parish in the country, and you will be in a room somewhere being asked questions, and there will be nothing between us but a letter that was never sent."

"There will be a piece of paper in the world with the truth on it," she said. "You said you wanted one. I am going to write one."

She went to the door of the sacristy, and then stopped, and turned round.

"Tomasz. The penance."

"I didn't give you one."

"Give me one."

He thought for a moment.

"Three Hail Marys," he said, "and go to Brzozówka, and stand in the place where the house was, and stay there for an hour. That is not a penance, it is a prescription, and I am giving it to you because I am a priest and I cannot help it." He put on his hat. "And find Antoni Szeliga's daughter and tell her. That is the penance. The rest is advice."

She went out through the church, past the last three old women, and down the steps into Krakowskie Przedmieście, where it had begun to snow, the first snow of the year, small hard flakes coming sideways off the river.
