# Forty Point Four

*Warsaw, 27–28 February 1943*

Rigors in a six-foot man did not look like shivering; they looked like mild strychnine poisoning. Every skeletal muscle from trapezius to calf fired at twelve cycles a second to drive core heat upward, setting the brass caster-cups under Bed Fourteen chattering against the floorboards like loose teeth while Ludwig's jaw locked hard enough to crack enamel.

Sylwia shook the mercury column down twice before trusting the glass, because hospital thermometers bought through Kraków medical depots since forty-two sometimes separated an air bubble in the capillary stem. Forty degrees point two. Pulse one hundred and thirty-four, bounding and water-hammer hot beneath her fingertips before collapsing between beats.

When she pressed two bare fingers gently above the resected lateral stump of his clavicle—outside the open granulating crater, high beneath the anterior border of the deltoid where skin had looked flat and quiet yesterday afternoon—deep fluctuance yielded softly beneath brawny, angry plum-red edema, and even through forty-degree toxic stupor Ludwig's breath tore ragged through his nose.

A blind subdeltoid pus pocket had walled itself off behind the coracoid process, out of reach of her Carrel tubes.

"Keep him flat and don't pile coats on him when he shakes," Sylwia snapped at Mekelburg, who had flung two Wehrmacht greatcoats over the blanket in peasant terror at seeing his officer's teeth chatter blue. "Pile wool on forty degrees two and you cook his brain to boiled egg-white by midnight. Strip one blanket off, wring linen cloths out in snow-water at the tap, lay them across his forehead and groin creases. Bronka—sterile platinum loop and two clean glass slides!"

Three minutes later she took the cellar stairs two at a time with the flamed glass slides tucked warm inside a tin Petri box against her ribs.

Beneath Pavilion Three ran low-vaulted Prussian-brick service tunnels laid in nineteen hundred and one, sweating saltpeter crystals and smelling perpetually of wet coke, guinea-pig urine, and steamed agar made out of boiled horse-hoof broth. Behind a padlocked iron-sheathed door labeled in peeling black German and Polish paint *Fleckfieber-Labor — Eintritt strengstens verboten!* (*Typhus Laboratory — Entry Strictly Forbidden!*), nobody from the *Ortskommandantur* ever poked a booted toe. Germans feared Polish body-lice worse than partisan grenades; paint a red skull and *Fleckfieber* on a basement door and SS inspectors read the clipboard from six yards up the stairs through scented handkerchiefs.

Sylwia rapped three quick, one slow with her signet knuckle. Bolts slid back; Dr. Halina Różańska opened four inches of crack, saw Sylwia's white smock, and pulled her inside.

"Shut it quick, my stove pipe's drawing backward in the thaw wind and half my cages go sluggish under twenty degrees," Halina said, already turning back toward her Zeiss binocular microscope under a low tin reflector.

Halina wore a man's moth-eaten grey cardigan buttoned over her lab coat and thick brown woollen stockings rolled roughly below her knees because forty minutes ago she had finished the afternoon feeding: strapped around her bare pale upper right thigh, visible for two seconds as she hiked her skirt hem to scratch a livid red wheal with carbolic ointment, sat the grid of four hundred tiny purple pinpricks left by Rudolph Weigl's wooden louse-boxes. Ten tiny mesh-screened matchboxes containing eight hundred healthy, uninfected *Pediculus humanus* larvae drank forty minutes of Halina's blood every afternoon so the municipal sanitary institute kept breeding stock for vaccine gut-harvest—and so "Panna Irena Kwiatkowska, Catholic pharmacist's daughter from Łowicz, baptised St. Stanislaus Parish 1916," stayed entirely indispensable on German municipal typhus-control payrolls even when the hydrogen peroxide bottle ran low and a quarter-inch of stubborn, blue-black Różańska hair showed dark along the center parting of her brassy yellow dye job.

"Room Fourteen?" Halina asked without sentimentality, taking Sylwia's two pus-smeared slides with forceps and passing their backs thrice through the blue tongue of her spirit Bunsen burner to heat-fix. "Tell me your Prussian bastard has gas gangrene and save my evening."

"Smell's sour-purulent, not mouse-sweet, and zero crepitus under my thumb," Sylwia said, leaning both palms hard on the scarred slate bench while Halina flooded Slide One with crystal violet, counted sixty seconds under her breath, rinsed into a pickle jar, and dropped Lugol's iodine over the purple film. "Spiked from thirty-seven eight at noon to forty point two inside four hours with violent rigors. Walled-off subdeltoid pocket behind the coracoid—three centimeters above my arterial suture. If it's *perfringens* or *oedematiens* eating downward behind pectoralis minor, my silk blows before morning."

Halina decolorized swiftly with acetone-alcohol, counterstained forty seconds in carbol-fuchsin, blotted dry between filter paper sheets, dropped cedarwood oil onto the glass, and racked the hundred-power oil-immersion lens down until brass nearly kissed smear.

For thirty seconds neither woman breathed; only the coal gas whispered blue in the burner tube and something small rustled dryly inside the warm wooden incubator cabinet where eight thousand lice digested Jewish blood under Polish stamps.

"Relax your sphincter, Kotarska," Halina muttered, squinting through the right ocular while rolling the fine micrometer screw half a hair with her thumb. "Not a single Gram-positive boxcar bacillus on five fields. No spores, no shredded ghost-leukocytes. What you've got is chains—long wicked violet pearl-necklaces everywhere, forty cocci to a chain—plus fat grape-clusters inside polymorphs. Beta-hemolytic *Streptococcus* and *Staphylococcus aureus* having a wedding feast in dead fat."

Air went out of Sylwia's lungs so fast her ribs hurt. Streptococcal phlegmon and staphylococcal pus could still kill a debilitated man by septicemia or erode a vessel wall if left dammed under tension—but streptococci bowed their necks to wide surgical incision and sulfathiazole, whereas gas gangrene inside the shoulder girdle meant disarticulating the entire forequarter through the scapula or watching a man swell black and burst awake.

"Thank God," Sylwia muttered before catching herself under Halina's sidelong dark stare.

Halina raised her head slowly from the brass eyepieces. One plucked eyebrow—still dark despite peroxide—arched sharp above cheeks hollowed thin by eighteen months underground since climbing out over the Krochmalna court roof in forty-one. "Thank *whom*, Sylwia? Three weeks ago you wouldn't waste two grams of Cibazol on a Volksdeutscher stationmaster with pneumonia. Now an *Oberstleutnant* shivers upstairs and you sprint down my stairs three steps at a jump."

"If that *Oberstleutnant* dies of septic blowout under my chart while Aleja Szucha and Saski Square are fighting over who shot him," Sylwia said coldly, meeting Halina's stare straight on without blinking, "Kroll's grey boys clear Pavilion Three down to the cellar bricks looking for negligent sabotage—including why 'Irena Kwiatkowska' hides eight ampoules of insulin behind her agar tins and hasn't been to confession since Palm Sunday."

Halina's mouth twisted wryly—not cowed for an instant, because nothing since forty-one cowed Halina except silence from north of Świętojerska. "Fair arithmetic. You want Cibazol or Eleudron?"

"Intravenous Eleudron sodium—twenty-percent ampoules if Bronka didn't trade the last box to Praga. He's vomiting barley water through locked teeth; tablets won't stay down."

Halina unlocked the false bottom behind the horse-serum rack and laid four five-milliliter glass ampoules of German *Eleudron*—sulfathiazole, stamped *Wehrmachtseigentum, I.G. Farbenindustrie Bayer*—into Sylwia's palm. As their fingers brushed, Sylwia noticed a tiny tremor in Halina's right ring finger that hadn't been there Thursday.

"Did the municipal telephone plumber get through to Bonifraterska today?" Sylwia asked quietly.

Halina closed the wooden panel hard enough to rattle empty Petri dishes. Since the January roundups inside the remnant Ghetto—four brief fierce days when pistol shots had cracked back at SS clearing squads from Zamenhofa rooftops for the first time instead of passive marching—only two illicit line-taps through the sanitary sewer exchange still carried thirty-second voice scraps out of the brushmakers' shop where Halina's twenty-four-year-old brother Dawid sorted horsehair bristles twelve hours a day.

"Ninety seconds at noon," Halina said flatly, wiping cedar oil off her slide with xylol rag. "Tobbens and Schultz are trying to move the brush-shop machines to Lublin camps—Poniatowa, Trawniki—and nobody in Dawid's courtyard is packing suitcases. Boys are buying vitriol and six-round Belgian pistols for five thousand złoty apiece through the wall." Her dark eyes lifted, dry as hot cinders. "Dawid asked if I could send twenty packets of sterile gauze and picric acid burn-jelly in with Tuesday's municipal lime cart. Not bread. Burn-jelly."

Burn-jelly meant petrol bottles brewed in tenement cellars against tanks and flamethrowers. Both of them knew what happened to twenty-four-year-old boys throwing home-made benzine bottles against German armor behind brick walls.

Sylwia didn't insult her with church comfort. "Tell Bronka to pack forty tins of picric ointment and six rolls of oiled silk under the chloride-of-lime sacks Monday night," she said simply. "Take them out of Vesper's German reserve cupboard—I'll log two fictional scald cases on Ward A to cover the stock sheet."

Halina nodded once, chin sharp, and turned back to her burner before anything human could ruin her voice. "Go stick your Prussian before he boils over. Drain that subdeltoid pocket wide—streptococci hate air almost as much as Germans hate drafts."

*Berlin, December 1930*

Snow mixed with soot off the Wannsee suburban locomotives fell thick down Potsdamer Straße the night Ludwig Schoeler stole Sylwia's venous-valve numbers and beat her by one ballot for the Rudolf Virchow Undergraduate Medal.

Not stole on paper—had he lifted a table from her typescript she could have laid both notebooks before Geheimrat von Halden and broken his spine across the faculty table. Worse: three weeks earlier, over wet slate sinks in the *Pathologisches Institut* while both of them were injecting red cinnabar gelatin into fifty cadaveric internal mammary and intercostal veins past midnight, she had argued fiercely for twenty minutes that collateral thoracic venous return reversed across valve-less paravertebral plexuses whenever superior vena cava pressure exceeded eighteen centimeters of water. Ludwig had leaned against the radiator smoking a crumpled *Reval*, calling her manometer tubing leaky Polish plumbing and laughing at her numbers. Then tonight, standing straight and insolent in a borrowed dark suit under the gilt portraits of Helmholtz and Du Bois-Reymond in the great hall, when old Professor Kraus tried tripping Ludwig's arterial-collateral thesis on venous back-pressure congestion, Ludwig had calmly walked to the blackboard, chalked *eighteen centimeters water threshold via Batson-type paravertebral valve-less shunts* without a single note in his hand, added *"as recent manometric observations in our own institute suggest,"* and inclined his fair head half an inch toward the third bench where Sylwia sat frozen in her refurbished black velveteen collar!

Four professors applauded his magnanimity toward a junior female colleague's "preliminary bench work"; five voted him the hundred-mark gold-washed medal and thirty-two hundred Reichsmarks' research stipend; Sylwia got honorable mention on parchment and zero marks toward January coal.

By eleven o'clock she had walked five kilometers south across gleaming wet tram-tracks without feeling her toes inside thin leather boots, carrying the rolled parchment certificate like an iron pipe in her fist, looking to break his teeth with it if she had to knock on every door in Charlottenburg.

He never went home to Mommsenstraße after faculty nights. She knew that from Lotte Bergmann—twenty-five, crop-haired, tubercular-bright, who drew wicked charcoal caricatures of Reichstag deputies for *Ulk* by day, sniffed cocaine off nail-files in Nollendorfplatz ladies' rooms by night, and slept occasionally with half the clinical assistants at the Charité except Ludwig, whom Lotte called *der Eisschrank mit Zähnen*, the icebox with teeth, and followed around out of sheer baffled malice. *Go look behind the horse-butcher's yard on Bülowstraße,* Lotte had laughed at nine-thirty outside the cloakroom, pulling her faux-astrakhan collar high. *Below the elevated U-Bahn girders—old brewery cellar where communist meat-cutters from Central Cattle-Yard fight three-minute bare-knuckle or four-ounce-glove rounds for ten marks a purse. Whenever Margarethe Schoeler sends her bastard a cheque signed with her maiden hyphen, he goes down to Schöneberg to let dockers punch his ribs until he feels poor again.*

Under the roaring iron viaduct of Bülowstraße, where overhead trains showered blue electric sparks down into black slush every four minutes, the cellar reeked of sour *Schultheiss* beer, wet sawdust, sweat, and blood spat into tin buckets.

Sylwia pushed down the slick wooden steps inside her wet black coat, collar turned up, cloche pulled low over her eyebrows. Eighty men in cloth caps, leather aprons, and damp wool jackets jammed four deep around a sixteen-foot rope square slung between cast-iron cellar pillars—no Corps ribbons here, no Heidelberg *Mensur* goggles or padded silk neck-wraps where rich boys stood flat-footed trading decorative cheek-nicks for society brides. Inside the ropes a squat Moabit coal-heaver with shoulders like an ox-yoke and cauliflower ears had a taller, leaner twenty-one-year-old boy pinned against the corner post, driving short brutal hooks—*whack, whack*—into bare white ribs while blood ran bright from the tall boy's split nostril down across a grinning, terrifyingly alive mouth.

Ludwig wore faded grey athletic flannel trousers tied at the waist with surgical cotton tape and nothing above his belt; sweat glistened over flat muscle, every rib showing clean when he twisted inside the coal-heaver's clinch, pivoted off the wet pillar with vicious anatomical precision, and drove a straight right cross not at the heavy jaw—skulls broke bare knuckles—square into the solar plexus notch below the xiphoid cartilage where the phrenic plexus sat unprotected.

The coal-heaver folded forward as if poleaxed, wheezing sour beer onto the sawdust; three men in *Rotfront* badges booed furiously; Lotte Bergmann, perched atop two stacked beer crates by the stove in green silk stockings with a sketchbook across her knees, whistled piercing shrill between two fingers.

Ten minutes later, out in the pitch-black service passage behind the horse-butcher's ashbins where snow drifted down between brick fire-walls, Sylwia stepped out of the doorway shadow directly into Ludwig's path as he came up buttoning his collarless white shirt beneath his threadbare naval pea-coat, wet blond hair steaming in the freezing draft.

She threw the rolled parchment honorable-mention tube straight at his face.

He caught it one-handed six inches from his nose—reflexes still razor-lit from three rounds—glanced down at the Friedrich-Wilhelms wax seal in snow-glare from the streetlamp out on Bülowstraße, and looked up at her standing six feet away with snow melting in her dark lashes.

"Eighteen centimeters of water," Sylwia said, voice shaking with pure white-hot murder. "Three weeks you called my manometer leaky Polish tin while you memorized every column out of my bench book backward—and then tossed me half a bow from the podium like table-scraps to a terrier so Kraus would call you chivalrous!"

Ludwig tucked her parchment cylinder neatly under his arm rather than dropping it into the slush, dabbing knuckles split purple across the third joint against his split lip and inspecting the dark red smear. Even winded, bruised red along his left ribs under his half-buttoned shirt, he looked more awake in a filthy Schöneberg alley than he ever looked on Mommsenstraße carpets.

"Had Kraus caught me stammering on paravertebral back-flow," Ludwig said coolly, breath smoking white between them, "he'd have handed all thirty-two hundred marks to Kleist's lymphatic-dye drivel, and next spring Kleist would have locked both of us out of the dog-house. You didn't publish your numbers before November fifteenth because you wasted ten days re-calibrating zero-drift like a nun counting beads. Next time print first or shut your notebook when I walk past."

"Thief!" She lunged forward without thinking, not slapping like a girl in a parlor—driving the heel of her gloved palm hard into his bruised left ribs exactly where the coal-heaver's hook had landed twice.

Air exploded out of his lungs in a sharp curse; his right hand shot out, grabbed both her coat lapels in one iron fist, and slammed her back flat against the wet brick fire-wall before she could kick his shin.

Before either of them drew another breath, heavy hobnailed boots splashed round the ashbins from the street mouth—four young men in brown storm-trooper shirts under short winter windbreakers, caps pulled low, hunting stragglers out of the red brewery cellar with lengths of rubber hose and unbuckled heavy brass belt-straps wrapped round their fists.

"Sieh mal einer an," the biggest one snarled, beer-drunk and itching after losing street ground off Nollendorfplatz earlier. "Zwei feine Herrschaften aus dem roten Keller—oder boxt der Herr Student für Moskau?"

"Run back down the cellar steps, Sylwia," Ludwig said very low without looking round, shoving her left behind his own hip and pulling her rolled parchment tube clear out of his armpit only to drop it safe onto an ashbin lid.

Sylwia didn't run half an inch; she reached straight into her coat pocket and closed her fist round the folded Collin dissecting scalpel she carried home every Friday to hone.

She never got the blade open. The first brownshirt swung two feet of heavy leather belt weighted with its rectangular brass buckle whistling through snow-dark aimed at Ludwig's temple; Ludwig ducked inside the arc—almost fast enough, two inches short—taking the sharp corner of the brass buckle edge-on across his right malar bone just beneath the eye while driving his left boot-heel crunching straight through the man's kneecap and slamming a second brawler skull-first into the iron ashbin rim.

Sylwia's heavy umbrella ferrule caught the third brownshirt square in the throat cartilage as Lotte Bergmann burst out the cellar door yelling *"Schupo! Polizei an der Ecke!"* at the top of her cabaret lungs and flinging two empty *Schultheiss* quart bottles smashing in green glass showers across the cobbles. Whistle blasts echoed down from the U-Bahn stairs; within fifteen seconds the four brownshirts dragged their howling knee-smashed comrade backward into the blowing snow and vanished toward Potsdamer Straße.

In the sudden quiet under the viaduct girders, blood poured bright black-red in the streetlamp glare down Ludwig's right cheek, dripping fast off his jaw-point onto snow. Buckles tore on bone like blunt chisels: three centimeters curved clean down to periosteum of the zygomatic arch. Let a casualty station intern stitch facial skin with thick casualty gut and it puckered his lower eyelid down in cicatricial ectropion for life.

"Sit on the crate," Sylwia snapped, fury abruptly swallowed whole by cold surgical reflex. "Lotte—have you got matches or a pocket torch?"

"Better—gin and a powder compact mirror!" Lotte crouched delightedly beside the upturned beer crate, holding a tiny silver-backed cabaret mirror angled to catch yellow electric light from the open cellar transom while overhead an U-Bahn train thundered past shaking soot onto their shoulders.

Out of Sylwia's pawnshop pocket roll came one fine curved Hagedorn skin needle threaded already from afternoon rabbit-ear work with thin black braided silk, soaked thirty seconds in the mouth of Lotte's flat bottle of forty-five-percent juniper schnapps before Sylwia splashed the rest of the schnapps straight across Ludwig's split cheekbone.

He hissed sharply between his teeth, sitting bare-headed on the wet crate with his shirt still half-open to the December freeze, knees spread wide so Sylwia had to stand wedged squarely between his thighs to steady his chin with her left palm while driving the unanesthetized needle point through cold, tough facial dermis six inches from his open eyes.

*One. Two. Three. Four.*

Every time steel pierced skin raw, his hands—resting loose on his own thighs two inches from the damp wool flanks of her coat—clenched once into white-knuckled stones and opened again without touching her. Up that close, wedged between his knees in the dark while gin-vapor, fresh hot blood, snow, and warm male skin steamed up into her face, Ludwig didn't look at Lotte's mirror; he stared straight up at Sylwia's mouth pinched grim in concentration, her dark lashes dusted white with melting snow, a smudge of his own cheekbone blood smeared across her chin where her wrist had brushed it.

"Pull stitch three half a millimeter tighter," Lotte offered helpfully, lighting a cigarette. "Give him something to remember Schöneberg by—he was hideously symmetrical anyway. Going to hold the spool for me while I sketch you two, or do I freeze out here alone?"

"Go inside and get warm, Lotte," Ludwig said without moving his eyes half a millimeter off Sylwia's face.

Lotte looked once from Ludwig's locked jaw to Sylwia's ungloved hands trembling faintly between his knees—not from cold—gave a low, wicked whistle through cigarette smoke, and banged the cellar door shut behind her, leaving them in dead blue snow-shadow behind the ashbins.

Sylwia bit the black silk thread off half an inch from the fourth knot with her own front teeth because her scissors were still buried inside her bag. Copper-salt blood and raw juniper gin coated her tongue.

Before she could step backward out from between his thighs, Ludwig's bruised right hand came up and caught the back of her coat at the small of her spine, pulling her four inches closer until her shins hit the crate edge and her knees wedged hard against his ribs.

"You bit dirty street silk with your teeth, *Fräulein Doktor*," he murmured, voice rough and low in his chest, breath hot against her cold mouth. Blood still oozed in one thin dark bead between stitch two and stitch three where she had pulled tightly enough to leave faint ladder-marks forever. "Bad asepsis."

"I hope it suppurates," Sylwia whispered, heart hammering so violently against her breastbone she felt half-sick with hatred and twenty-year-old hunger indistinguishable from wanting to kill him. Her cold, blood-sticky left fingers were still tangled in the damp collarless neck of his shirt over his collarbone; instead of letting go she twisted the linen tight against his throat. "Give me half the lab bench grant on Monday or I'll tell Kraus whose manometer tubes broke eighteen centimeters."

"Half the bench hours and zero pfennigs on your name," he breathed back, tilting his head up until his cold nose grazed hers and the unsplit corner of his mouth brushed—not kissed, scraped, salt-wet and furious—against her lower lip so she tasted his blood twice over. "You come work nights at my sink without whining to von Halden, *Polack*, or go starve in Posen."

For three seconds her mouth parted hot against his in the freezing dark—teeth clicking once against teeth, her thumb digging hard into the hollow above his sternum while his fingers bruised her waist through wet wool, both of them shaking on the edge of dragging each other down onto beer crates and dirty snow—until footsteps crunched loud at the street mouth and Sylwia shoved back out of his grip so savagely her elbow hit the brick wall, wiping the back of her hand hard across her mouth and snatching her parchment cylinder off the ashbin without looking back down Bülowstraße until the snow swallowed him.

*Warsaw, 28 February 1943*

At two-fifteen on Sunday morning the thermometer shook down from forty degrees point four.

Outside Room Fourteen the night corridor was dead black save for one blue blackout nightlight burning behind wire mesh; inside, Sylwia had turned off the overhead bulb and set a single shaded candle behind the porcelain washbasin so Stabsarzt Vesper's night patrol, if anyone drove past Lindleya gate, wouldn't see bright windows blazing in the quarantined wing.

At midnight she had incised the walled-off subdeltoid pocket herself right in the bed rather than risking an operating-room carry—one swift, deep five-centimeter stroke with a pointed #11 Bistoury along the anterior deltoid border while Mekelburg pinned his half-conscious commander's right shoulder down. Sixty milliliters of thick creamy yellow streptococcal pus under fierce tension had gushed straight into the kidney basin before she slid a fenestrated rubber tube high behind the coracoid process, flushed until clear saline ran back out the lower axillary cleft, and injected two full grams of Halina's stolen *Eleudron* sodium slowly into his right cubital vein over ten minutes.

For two hours afterward the bacterial toxin dump from opening the abscess sent him higher instead of lower: forty point four, skin dry and burning like an iron stove-lid under her bare palms, breathing shallow at thirty-six a minute, muttering broken half-syllables in North German childhood dialect about salt water and a boat hook at Kiel before lapsing into terrifying rigid silences where Sylwia sat perched on the edge of the mattress itself with two fingers fixed permanently over his carotid artery.

Around two o'clock Bruno Mekelburg slid sideways off his wooden chair in the corner, worn clean out after nineteen days sleeping upright with a healing three-fingered stump, snoring mouth-open with his blond head resting against the cold radiator pipe.

Sylwia wrung out another coarse linen towel in the enamel basin of melted courtyard snow cut with hospital vinegar, folded back the damp sheet to Ludwig's waist, and wiped down his burning throat, right chest, ribs, and flat stomach in long steady strokes to pull heat off the great skin vessels by evaporation.

Under faint amber candle-shadow his torso gleamed wet; above the white gauze mountain strapping his left shoulder, the skin over his right clavicle and neck radiated dry furnace heat against her knuckles. Across his right lower ribs, just above that older jagged shell-scar from forty-one, gooseflesh prickled sharply beneath the cold vinegar cloth even while blood boiled underneath.

Without warning, his right hand whipped up out of stupor and locked round her wet left wrist.

Not sluggish invalid strength—pure brain-stem delirium force, fingers closing like five iron shackles directly over her radial styloid until bones ground together and she bit her lip hard not to cry out and wake Mekelburg six feet away.

His eyes were wide open in the dim candle-glow, dark pupils blown enormous across winter-green irises, staring straight up into her face bent twelve inches above his chest—seeing her and not seeing Pavilion Three at all.

"Geh nicht zurück in die Klinik," he rasped in a hoarse, cracked whisper that raised every fine hair along the back of Sylwia's neck. *Don't go back to the clinic.* "Sie stehen schon unten am Tor in der Luisenstraße—lass die Hefte liegen, Sylwia, nimm den Zug—"

Sylwia stayed stone-still on the mattress edge, cold vinegar dripping off the forgotten linen cloth between her fingers onto his bare ribs while his burning thumb crushed her pulse point. March nineteen-thirty-three. Ten years buried under rubble, and inside forty-point-four septic fire he was standing in her Savignyplatz room again.

"Ludwig," she breathed almost inaudibly, trying with her free right hand to pry his iron thumb loose before he bruised her wrist purple enough for Bronka to see at morning rounds. "Let go. You're in Warsaw."

His brow furrowed fiercely, eyes flickering sideways toward the dark ceiling corner as if hunting shadows behind her shoulder, then snapping back to her face with sudden, chilling half-lucidity mixed into fever.

"Jakob wollte nicht einsteigen," he whispered raw through cracked lips, chest heaving short against the white Velpeau turns. *Jakob wouldn't get on.* "Ich stand vierzig Minuten am Anhalter Bahnhof mit den Prager Papieren—er ist zurückgelaufen wegen seiner Präparate, der alte Narr, wegen zwanzig Gläsern in Formalin—"

Sylwia's breath stopped dead inside her throat.

*I stood forty minutes at Anhalter Bahnhof with the Prague papers—he ran back for his specimens, the old fool, for twenty jars in formalin.*

Anhalter Bahnhof handled southbound expresses toward Dresden and Prague. On the eighth of March nineteen-thirty-three, when two SA trucks and criminal-police detectives had hauled Professor Jakob Mandelbaum off the steps of the Neuroanatomy Annex, every student left whispering on Philippstraße had sworn—Lotte Bergmann herself had sworn to Sylwia through tears at Friedrichstraße station!—that *Ludwig Schoeler* had unlocked Mandelbaum's private cabinet key for the Party commissar at noon in exchange for Mandelbaum's first-assistant stipend.

Why would a man burning alive at forty degrees point four with hemolytic streptococci in his blood invent standing forty minutes at Anhalter Bahnhof holding forged Prague papers for a dead Jew ten years ago?

Before her stunned brain could move a muscle, his grip shifted convulsively on her wrist, pulling her three inches lower as present-day terror crashed across the Berlin ghost behind his glassy eyes.

"*Chłodna zweiundzwanzig,*" he breathed straight against her cheek, switching without seam from nineteen-thirty-three into the rough, guttural Warsaw-German whisper of *Abwehrstelle* nights. "*Nicht vor dem Zehnten zum Optiker—wenn Kroll den Uhrzeiger hat, brennt das ganze Netz bis zur Spiska...*"

*Chłodna twenty-two. Not to the optician before the tenth—if Kroll has the clock-hand, the whole net burns as far as Spiska.*

Sylwia slapped her free right palm lightly—not gently, crisp and cold with snow-water—flat across his dry lips before a single louder consonant could reach Bruno Mekelburg's sleep-heavy ears by the radiator.

Beneath her wet palm his mouth burned like hot brick; she felt the faint, rough scar-ridge on his lower lip from biting Mekelburg's belt eleven days ago, and above her fingers his dilated eyes blinked once, twice, swimming violently upward out of delirium as cold snow-water hit his skin.

For five endless heartbeats she stayed pinned just like that: sitting sideways on his narrow iron bed in candle-darkness, her left wrist crushed inside his hot right fist against his bare wet sternum, her right palm pressed firm across his mouth, staring straight down from eight inches into grey-green eyes slowly, horribly clearing enough to realize somebody had been listening while his skull leaked.

 Sweat broke suddenly—not cold shock sweat like Tuesday week, true defervescent crisis sweat as two grams of intravenous sulfathiazole and wide pus drainage finally cracked the hypothalamic thermostat—pouring out of his hairline, neck, and chest all at once in a hot salt flood underneath her wrist.

Slowly, finger by stiff finger, Ludwig loosened his grip around her bruised left radius.

Sylwia lifted her hand off his mouth. Neither of them wiped the wet away. His breathing slowed from thirty-six to twenty-four, ragged and exhausted; when he tried to speak, barely a thread of sound came out—dry, wary, searching her stony dark face in the amber gloom to read how much had spilled out of his mouth while the room spun.

"How high?" he whispered.

"Forty point four," Sylwia said, voice steady as cold slate though inside her skull two dynamite caps—*Anhalter Bahnhof* and *Chłodna twenty-two, der Uhrzeiger, Spiska*—were ringing until her ears hummed. Gently, with clinical detachment more brutal than any slap, she pulled his soaked blanket back up to his chin so his sudden sweat wouldn't chill into pneumonia, lifted her tin basin of pinkish-vinegar snow water off the night-table, and stood up. "Thirty-eight nine now by skin feel. Your pocket's draining clean and Halina's sulfathiazole caught the cocci before they reached my silk. Go to sleep, Schoeler."

His eyes tracked her shadow against the whitewashed wall all the way to the basin tap—narrowed, sleepless again despite exhaustion, haunted by the certainty that he had dropped a loaded pistol somewhere in the dark room and couldn't see whose pocket held it.

Out in the empty sluice corridor at four in the morning, with nobody awake in Pavilion Three except dying men upstairs and eight thousand lice in the cellar, Sylwia leaned her forehead for ten seconds flat against cold sweating tiles above the slop sink, looking down at four livid violet finger-marks already blooming purple around her left wrist where Ludwig had held on.

She did not write *Chłodna 22* down anywhere—Marek had taught her better than paper—and she did not tell herself what she was going to tell Marek at Bagno on Tuesday noon.
