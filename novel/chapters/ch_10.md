# Anastomosis

*Warsaw, 17 April 1943*

At twenty minutes to twelve on Saturday morning, Bronka's cousin Pelagia set a chipped saucer of pickled mushrooms down on the sewing-machine table without speaking and went back into her kitchen so she wouldn't know whom the hospital lady was watching through the darned Nottingham lace curtain.

Two floors below, ulica Elektoralna glared white and gritty in sudden warm April sun. Horse carts loaded with spring lime creaked toward Hala Mirowska; directly opposite Pelagia's window yawned the bombed archway of Number Fourteen, its courtyard right-hand glazier's shed roofless since September thirty-nine, nothing inside its blackened brick walls except dead nettles, broken bottle glass, and a stray ginger cat licking its paw on an upturned wheelbarrow.

Nobody on earth had sent a postcard to a leather-goods mender at *Elektoralna fourteen, courtyard right*—because Sylwia had invented the leather-goods mender, the kitchen boy, and the courtyard out of whole air forty-six hours ago beside the stone sundial in Saski Garden while looking straight into Marek Wolski's grey eyes.

At eleven-forty-two, a dark-grey four-door Adler Trumpf sedan turned unhurriedly off Orla, slowed without braking squeal thirty yards east of Number Fourteen's arch, and let a thin man in a greenish loden coat and soft felt hat step out onto the kerb to buy a newspaper he didn't read while leaning his shoulder comfortably against the tobacconist's shutter opposite the gate.

Sylwia did not touch the lace curtain. She stood two feet back inside Pelagia's dim room, counting the licence digits painted black on white above the Adler's rear bumper as it glided away toward Bankowy Square: *Ost-44189*. Security Police pool plate.

Down in her stomach nothing felt hot or theatrical; everything went twenty degrees below zero, clean and surgical as liquid ethyl chloride sprayed onto skin before lancing a boil.

Forty-six hours. Thursday two p.m. into Marek Wolski's ear; Saturday eleven-forty-two a.m. out Reinhart Kroll's boot-leather.

Did that prove Marek himself took Swiss francs or Danzig transfers? Not necessarily—Marek reported verbally and on cipher slips every Thursday night to Section II's Warsaw District desk officer, whom Sylwia had never met and knew only as *"Inżynier Stach."* Either Marek was *"Zegar"*, or *"Stach"* was *"Zegar"*, or the girl who keyed Section II's Thursday slips into carbon flimsies slept with an SD clerk. For practical survival inside occupied Warsaw, the distinction mattered no more than which tooth on a circular saw took your fingers off: anything spoken to Marek Wolski walked up Aleja Szucha inside two nights.

And yesterday morning at eight-thirty, Stabsarzt Vesper had marched into Pavilion Three puffing cognac and lavender water to announce that *Oberstleutnant* Schoeler's Friday discharge was postponed seventy-two hours until Monday the nineteenth at nine a.m., because following the "regrettable hemorrhage disturbance of twenty-six March," *Sanitätsabteilung Warschau* required a formal orthopedic range-of-motion clearance signed before returning a senior staff officer to duty!

Meaning whoever waited with Sten guns at Koszykowa and Chałubińskiego yesterday morning had waited in empty wind—and by Monday morning Ludwig would leave by Nowogrodzka while Marek never received another true syllable out of Sylwia's mouth as long as she breathed.

When she walked back into Pavilion Three at half-past two, she found Ludwig waiting not in Room Fourteen, in the narrow tiled plaster-and-splint room off Theater One—door shut, Mekelburg posted outside in the corridor—sitting shirtless on the high leather-padded orthopedic table with his left arm dangling free.

"Who unlocked the splint room for you?" Sylwia asked, hanging her outdoor coat on the peg behind the door and rolling her black wool dress sleeves past her elbows before tying on a clean white apron.

"Docent Mirecki, before he retired upstairs with his mysterious tachycardia," Ludwig said dryly, watching her scrub at the corner porcelain basin. Mirecki's atropine-and-amyl-nitrite "coronary crisis" on Wednesday had spared him Vesper's Katyń press delegation splendidly, though Ludwig's left eyebrow told her inside half a glance exactly how much faith he placed in Mirecki's electrocardiogram. "Stabsarzt Vesper writes *partial ankylosis left shoulder girdle, unfit for field service* on Monday's sheet unless abduction clears a hundred and fifty degrees. Write *unfit for field service* on an *Abwehr* personnel card in April forty-three, and Berlin transfers me within ten days to a desk in Dresden sorting postal censorship while Hauptmann von Berg hands Saski Square's Warsaw files over to ‘liaison coordination’ with Aleja Szucha."

Sylwia turned off the brass tap and shook water off her fingers. "You hit a hundred and ten degrees Wednesday night. Push past a hundred and ten before week nine and you tear dense subdeltoid and coracoacromial adhesions raw."

"Precisely why orthopedic surgeons perform *brisement forcé* before fibrous scar calcifies," Ludwig said calmly.

She walked across the tiles until she stood right beside his left knee. Even sitting down on the high padded table, his eyes were level with hers; pale April light slanting through the high frosted transom caught the sunken crimson-purple scar trough beneath his resected collarbone gap and the hard, lean curve of pectoralis and deltoid knotted tight around it.

"Under deep ether or intravenous Evipan," Sylwia said flatly, "so pectoral muscle spasm doesn't snap the surgical neck of the humerus in half like dry pine when the lever goes up. We have zero Evipan left until May, and fifty grams of ether puts you vomiting on a stretcher till dusk."

"You broke frozen cavalry shoulders with von Halden in the outpatient clinic using ten milliliters of intra-bursal procaine and two steady hands," Ludwig said. "I didn't ask for Evipan, and I don't need ether."

"You aren't twenty-two years old showing off for nurses, Ludwig, and your middle clavicle is missing two centimeters of strut!"

"Which means you stabilize the scapula manually against the ribs with your left palm so leverage falls purely across the subdeltoid bursa instead of the vascular bridge," he answered without raising his voice a decibel, unbuckling his leather officer's trouser belt right-handed, folding four inches double—the exact grim routine from sixteen February, except today there were no nurses, no orderlies, and zero septic haze—and holding the folded hide in his palm. "Ten minutes, *Kotarska*. Break the adhesions clean now behind closed doors, and Monday morning I lift my left arm over my head in front of Vesper's spectacles and stay in Warsaw where Kroll can't eat your hospital alive."

*And where Elektoralna fourteen proved ten times over what Kroll already owns,* clicked silent behind Sylwia's forehead.

She didn't mention Elektoralna—telling him even half a Polish counter-intelligence failure felt like stripping naked in winter wind—simply turned to the locked glass drug cupboard, drew ten milliliters of one-percent procaine into a long spinal needle, swabbed his lateral deltoid skin cold with spirit, and slid the steel three centimeters straight beneath the acromion into the obliterated subacromial space.

Procaine numbed synovial lining; against thick finger-wide bands of tough six-week collagen gluing torn muscle bellies to periosteum, ten milliliters of one-percent was spit on hot iron.

"Lie flat?" she asked five minutes later, testing skin numbness over the deltoid tuberosity.

"Supine lets the shoulder blade slide across leather," Ludwig said, bracing his bare soles against the iron foot-stirrup bar along the table side instead. "Do it seated—brace your hip inside my left knee so the trunk can't twist away when the band gives."

Pure textbook Lexer mechanics; any orthopedic manual printed in Munich or Vienna showed the identical plate in black-and-white halftone, two sexless mannequins in loincloths demonstrating scapular fixation. Neither Munich nor Vienna printed what happened when the surgeon stepping inside a six-foot man's spread knees to wedge her hip hard against his inner thigh and pelvis was five feet six in thin wool, smelled of rain and carbolic soap, and hadn't stood this close to his bare skin without gauze between them since thunderstorm lightning lit zinc sinks on Luisenstraße twelve summers ago.

Sylwia stepped squarely between his knees.

Rough grey Wehrmacht breeches scraped both outer sides of her thighs through her black dress and white apron; to lock his left scapula immobile against chest-wall rotation while her right hand took the humerus lever at the elbow, she had to plant her bare left palm flat and heavy across the hot, scarred hollow behind his left armpit and brace her own breastbone four inches from his bare chest.

Ludwig set the folded leather belt between his back molars. Up this close his grey-green eyes didn't close or look at the ceiling; they stayed wide open, locked straight onto hers from six inches away while heat radiated off his skin like sun off dark slate.

"Exhale on three," Sylwia said quietly. "Don't fight my right hand with your latissimus or you fracture bone. One. Two. Three."

On *three* he blew breath out; before pectoral reflex could clamp down, Sylwia sank her body weight hard against his pelvis to pin him upright, locked his shoulder blade down with her left heel-of-hand, and drove his flexed left elbow smoothly, relentlessly *upward* past a hundred and ten degrees—one-twenty, one-thirty, one-forty—straight into the wall of solid fibrous scar.

Beneath her left palm, deep inside living muscle three inches from her ear, tough collagen bands tore apart with three wet, sickeningly audible snaps—*pop, crk, pop*—like ripping heavy wet canvas seam by seam!

A muffled animal sound tore behind the leather belt in Ludwig's teeth; every muscle in his neck, abdomen, and thighs turned instantly to white-hot iron round her hips. Before he could help himself, his uninjured right hand—searching blindly for counter-purchase rather than grabbing her operational arm—clamped hard onto the curve of Sylwia's left hipbone and waist, fingers digging straight through thin apron linen and wool into flesh while his damp forehead dropped forward three inches until it rested rock-hard against the bare side of her neck and collarbone!

"One band left—posterior capsule—don't lock!" Sylwia gasped against his sweat-wet temple hair, her own heart slamming wild against her ribs as she rotated the elevated humerus thirty degrees externally—*rip!*—feeling the final dense cord part clean beneath her palm as his left elbow cleared full one hundred and seventy degrees vertical beside his ear!

She eased the arm down slowly—never drop a manipulated joint fast—cradling his trembling, sweat-slick left forearm against her own breast with both hands while holding him upright between his knees until the grey vagal wave of pain-shock passed without dropping him off the table edge.

Ten seconds passed. Twenty.

Neither of them moved an inch.

Ludwig had spat the leather belt onto the padded table beside his thigh; his forehead still rested heavy and burning hot in the hollow between Sylwia's neck and right shoulder, his breath rasping ragged and wet directly across her bare collar-skin down inside the open V of her dress collar while his right hand stayed locked hard over her hipbone—thumb pressing slow, involuntary white-knuckled force into the soft hollow above her iliac crest. Beneath her hands holding his left forearm against her ribs, she felt both his racing pulse and the violent, unmistakable kick of her own heartbeat hammering straight back through thin cotton into his wrist.

Smell of hot salt sweat, crushed hair, clean lye soap, and blood-iron filled her throat until her knees went treacherous inside his braced thighs.

One turn of her head—two centimeters left—put her mouth directly against the damp corner of his jaw below his ear. And from the sudden dead-still tension across his back and the slight shift of his gripping fingers upward along her ribs—not pushing her away, *pulling* half an inch closer before freezing rigid—she knew in every nerve of her body that if she turned those two centimeters inside this locked white room, neither war nor uniform nor ten years of corpses would stop what happened across this leather table in the next sixty seconds.

*Berlin, July 1931*

At midnight during the great July heatwave, when asphalt melted sticky along Unter den Linden and thunderheads stacked purple-black over the Spree without dropping rain, the experimental surgery cellar beneath Geheimrat von Halden's clinic smelled of dog hair, ozone, and hot sublimate.

Sylwia hadn't eaten since noon on Wannsee, where von Halden's annual summer clinic picnic had trapped twelve sweating junior staff and student assistants aboard two rented yachts tacking sluggishly past Pfaueninsel while Frau Geheimrat served lukewarm potato salad and Ludwig Schoeler—brown-armed in rolled white tennis sleeves, hair bleached two shades lighter by sun, steering Boat Two with infuriating north-coast competence—had spent four hours deliberately luffing across Sylwia's bow wind every time she took the tiller from clumsy Oberarzt Weber.

At eleven p.m., back in Berlin with lightning flickering silent sheet-white behind the Pathology chimneys, Sylwia had slipped down into the animal quarters to check Dog Seven—her tawny mongrel bitch whose right femoral artery she had resected and bridged forty-eight hours earlier with a preserved jugular-vein segment for her doctoral trial—and found the dog's right hind paw ice-cold, cyanotic, and pulseless.

Not surgical error along her suture line: sitting beside the cage water-pan lay half a chunk of fatty pork rind laced white with barium meal powder somebody from Kleist's morning stomach-X-ray bench had tossed carelessly (or *not* carelessly!) into a vascular-fasting cage, sending the dehydrated animal retching violently for three hours until hypotension clotted the fresh venous graft solid!

Let Dog Seven's leg go necrotic before seven a.m. rounds, and von Halden struck Sylwia's entire six-month venous-graft series out of the autumn *Archiv für klinische Chirurgie* volume in favor of Ludwig's collateral-ligation paper.

By eleven-forty she had the twenty-kilo bitch strapped supine under light open-drop ether on Zinc Table Two, hair shaved clean, skin painted brown with iodine, femoral incision re-opened under one buzzing green-shaded bulb—working furiously *alone* without an assistant to hold stay-sutures or drip warm Ringer's solution while sweat dripped off her chin onto her gauze mask and a fresh mural thrombus six millimeters long bulged dark blue inside the delicate vein graft!

Try milking a clot out of a four-millimeter dog vein single-handed while holding two bulldog clamps and keeping ether dripping up by the muzzle—impossible with two hands; inside ninety seconds the thin jugular wall tore if nobody held the third stay-thread!

Behind her the heavy iron fire-door from the pathology tunnel clicked open and shut.

Sylwia froze with iris scissors half-open over the exposed femoral sheath, heart leaping into her throat—night watchman meant a ten-mark fine and expulsion from animal rooms; Oberarzt Weber meant disgrace.

Ludwig walked into the cone of green light without a white coat.

He wore dark evening trousers and the damp white linen shirt from Wannsee, collar open two buttons at his sunburned throat, jacket slung over one shoulder—coming back late from some Charlottenburg supper by shortcut through the clinic yard to fetch books before the storm broke. Five paces from Table Two he stopped dead, taking in at one sweep the dripping ether cone, the cyanotic paw, the half-eaten barium pork rind lying on Sylwia's stool as evidence, and the bulging blue thrombus between two bulldog clamps while Sylwia stood trapped with only two hands.

Walk out that door—he didn't even have to report her, merely walk upstairs to bed!—and by nine o'clock tomorrow morning the autumn *Archiv* slot belonged exclusively to Ludwig Schoeler uncontested.

"Your ether cone's drying out—she'll kick inside forty seconds," Ludwig said irritably, dropping his jacket onto a dry radiator, rolling both shirt-sleeves above his elbows in three jerks, and plunging his bare hands straight into the sublimate basin without asking whether she wanted him within a mile of her dog.

"Get away from my table," Sylwia hissed through her wet gauze mask, pride blazing hotter than ninety-degree July air. "I don't need charity from Boat Two!"

"Call it curiosity whether your jugular patchwork holds eighty millimeters when somebody isn't feeding your dogs barium grease," Ludwig snapped back, shaking sublimate off his wet fingers, dripping ten fresh drops of ether onto the wire muzzle cone so the bitch settled deep into surgical plane, and stepping squarely opposite her across the two-foot-wide zinc trough to take two fine Carrel stay-threads gently in his steady fingers. "Open the anterior suture line three stitches only—don't take the whole graft down or you'll narrow the lumen re-sewing. Flush proximal clamp half a second to blow the tail clot out before you close."

For twenty-eight blistering minutes while thunder finally cracked straight overhead—shaking dust off basement steam-pipes and making the single bulb blink yellow—they worked four hands inside three square inches of living flesh like two halves of one brain that hated its other hemisphere. Neither called an instrument twice; before Sylwia's scissors finished snipping three silk loops, Ludwig's paraffin-wetted forceps had lifted the intimal lip without bruising endothelium; when she cracked the proximal clamp half a turn and dark worm-clot shot cleanly out into gauze followed by bright scarlet arterial spurting, his traction threads pulled the triangle taut before she drew breath, letting her lay four microscopic five-zero stitches home in ninety seconds flat.

Clamps off. Smooth crimson flow raced down the pale blue vein bridge; inside sixty seconds the grey-cold hind paw pads flushed warm shell-pink, digital pulse tapping lively against Ludwig's fingertip!

Only afterward—at twelve-forty a.m., when Dog Seven lay sleeping warmly in clean straw under a blanket and Sylwia stood at the slate sink scrubbing dried blood off a thin-walled liter Erlenmeyer flask of carbolic rinse—did exhausted fingers slip in soapy lather just as a tremendous bolt of lightning hit the Charité lightning-rod outside and plunged the basement lights black for two seconds.

Glass exploded against slate edge directly inside her left grip.

When the bulb flickered back on yellow, a curved jagged shard of thick flask-neck stood driven deep across the thenar eminence at the base of Sylwia's left thumb, pumping dark red venous and palmar-arch branch blood down her wrist into the soapy grey sink water!

"Verdammt noch mal, Kotarska—let go the glass!" Ludwig cursed, shoving her hip sideways away from the broken shards, grabbing a clean rubber Esmarch tourniquet band off the shelf to twist tight above her wrist in two turns so the bleeding dropped instantly to slow ooze, and pulling her palm straight under the cold boiled-water tap.

No fracture, flexor pollicis longus tendon gleaming white and intact at the bottom of a clean four-centimeter gash—miss that tendon by two millimeters and her surgical life ended at twenty-one!—just skin and superficial muscle fascia sliced wide open, stinging fire from soapy five-percent carbolic.

"Hold your own wrist steady on the slate," Ludwig ordered without asking permission, snatching up her leftover boiled Hagedorn skin needle and black silk off Table Two's sterile corner—zero procaine drawn because drawing procaine meant unwrapping a fresh syringe while tourniquet ischemia burned her forearm. "Four stitches. Revenge for Schöneberg—let's see if you sit quieter than I did."

He didn't sit her on a crate; he backed her hips straight against the cool front lip of the slate sink, pinning her leaning slightly backward while standing six inches in front of her in sweat-damp shirt-sleeves that clung transparent across his chest and shoulders in the ninety-degree cellar steam, cradling the back of her cut left hand gentle as porcelain inside his broad left palm while his right drove four swift, razor-clean interrupted silk bites through her raw thumb base.

*One. Two. Three. Four.*

It burned like hot wire, and Sylwia didn't make a sound—staring fixed at the tiny crescent ladder-scar *she* had put beneath his right cheekbone seven months earlier, shining pale against his sun-browned skin six inches from her eyes while outside torrential July rain hammered down onto the courtyard skylights like gravel flung by shovels.

Ludwig snipped the fourth silk tail with scissors, untwisted the rubber tourniquet band off her forearm, and didn't drop her hand.

Capillary flush rushed hot back into her fingers; wrapped in sterile gauze, her left palm lay trapped flat against the damp linen over his ribs where his heart was knocking fast and heavy—hundred and ten, hundred and twenty—nothing to do with sewing four stitches.

"Kleist threw that pork rind in," Ludwig said quietly, voice rough in the thunder-hush, looking straight down into her dark, dilated eyes. "I saw grease on his white cuffs when he came up the dog stairs at six. Want me to break his collarbone tomorrow, or ruin his stomach X-rays yourself?"

"Ruin them myself," Sylwia whispered—or started to whisper, because before the last consonant cleared her teeth her eyes dropped involuntarily to his mouth, remembering juniper schnapps and copper blood in Schöneberg snow, and his right hand came up cold-wet from the tap to catch the back of her neck beneath her pinned dark hair, tilting her chin up hard as his mouth came down on hers.

Nothing cautious or polite lived inside ten yards of either of them.

Her mouth opened furious under his—salt sweat, soap, sharp male breath—biting his lower lip hard enough to make him groan deep in his throat as he lifted her bodily right up by the thighs onto the cold slate rim of the scrubbing bench so her wet shoes swung clear of the floor and her knees parted instinctively round his lean waist! Bandaged left palm tangled in his soaked hair, intact right hand ripping two horn buttons clean off his damp shirt front to get bare palms onto burning skin over ribs she had watched under boxing-cellar ropes, while his rough surgeon's hands slid hot under her thin summer cotton skirt up bare thighs above her stocking tops—fingers digging bruising-hard into bare hip flesh, pulling her groin flush and grinding hard against the rigid ridge straining inside his dark wool trousers until Sylwia's head fell back against the cool tiled wall with a ragged, shameless gasp that echoed loud across the dog-pens!

His mouth dragged scorching down her throat to the hollow of her collarbone, right hand already fumbling between their crushed bodies at her cotton knickers elastic while she hooked both heels frantic behind his thighs to pull him straight inside her on cold wet slate right beside bloody sink-water—

—when three sharp iron raps rattled the outer tunnel padlock thirty feet away, followed by old night-watchman Krause's lantern beam sweeping yellow through the upper wired transom glass: *"Ist da noch Licht im Hundestall? Herr Oberarzt?"*

Both of them froze stone-dead in the shadow behind the sink pillar—panting without sound into each other's skin, her skirt bunched round her waist, his forehead pressed slick against her bare breastbone above her slipped camisole—until Krause muttered about defective switches, clicked the corridor master-breaker outside off, and clumped away down the wet stone passage.

In pitch blackness smelling of rain-ozone, ether, and raw sex stopped two seconds short of the cliff, humiliation and terror at what she had just let Ludwig Schoeler feel her begging for hit twenty-one-year-old Sylwia like ice-water thrown across hot glass.

She shoved both palms hard against his bare chest, sliding down off the slate lip onto unsteady legs and yanking her rumpled cotton skirt down in the dark.

"Touch me again when lights are on, *Schoeler*," she breathed shaking through clenched teeth, "and I'll put a #10 blade between your ribs."

Out of the blackness six inches away came his ragged laugh—ugly, breathless, just as shaken and twice as proud to bleed before showing it.

"Buy thicker buttons for your blouse before autumn, *Kotarska*," he whispered back in the dark, "or lock your cages earlier."

Neither of them ever mentioned that Tuesday night aloud through twenty more months in Berlin—fighting ten times more viciously across every ward round, lecture hall, and laboratory table until the Reichstag burned—and every single morning when Sylwia scrubbed her hands she saw four tiny black-silk dots healed white across the base of her left thumb.

*Warsaw, 17 April 1943*

Sylwia wrenched backward out from between Ludwig's knees in the tiled splint room as if the leather table had caught fire.

Her apron front was damp where his forehead sweat had pressed against her collar; her left hipbone throbbed dull and hot under wool where five fingers had dug straight to bone during the capsule tear. Across the room by the porcelain basin she grabbed a folded roller bandage without looking round until her breathing flattened out by sheer murderous will.

Behind her on the high leather table, Ludwig didn't jump down or make a single clever Berlin remark. Slowly he sat upright, wiping sweat off his jaw and throat with the clean linen shirt beside him, then raised his bare left arm—stiffly, grimacing once at raw bruised muscle, yet smoothly, without mechanical block—straight up past horizontal, past one-forty, all the way vertical beside his left ear until fingers brushed the frosted light chain overhead!

"Hundred and seventy degrees," he said, voice still low and rough-scraped down in his chest as he lowered the arm slowly and slid into his grey wool shirt. "Vesper can't write *unfit* on Monday now if he brings three micrometers."

"Ice pack twenty minutes tonight, active wall-climbing exercises thrice daily starting tomorrow," Sylwia recited toward the glass instrument cabinet, counting shiny curved scissors she didn't need to count while curling her scarred left thumb unconsciously inside her fist. "Granulation crust separates inside ten days; apply plain sterile boric ointment if dry. After Monday nine a.m. your surgical chart closes here."

Silence stretched three beats behind her back while horn buttons slipped through buttonholes one-handed.

Then paper rustled faintly, and heavy leather boots crossed three paces of white tile to lay a stiff, pale-green folded card stamped with the dark blue eagle-and-swastika of *Wehrmachtkommandantur Warschau* squarely onto the marble shelf beside her elbow.

Sylwia looked down without touching it.

*SONDERAUSWEIS — Ärztlicher Außendienst.* Valid throughout the Warsaw municipal and German residential districts (*einschließlich Sperrbezirk Palais Brühl / Królewska*), authorizing **Dr. med. Sylwia Kotarska** (*Szpital Dzieciątka Jezus*) unhindered passage with surgical instrument bag Tuesdays and Fridays between 16:00 and 21:00 hours for mandatory specialist vascular-and-orthopedic follow-up attendance upon **Oberstleutnant Dr. L. Schoeler**, *Królewska 16, Wohnung 4*. Counter-signed *Dr. K. Vesper, Stabsarzt* and *i.A. von Berg, Hptm.*

She turned round slowly, fury rising hot enough to burn away the dizzying phantom heat still clinging to her skin.

"Your wound needs zero twice-weekly specialist house calls once epithelium closes," she said coldly. "Have an orderly at Saski Square dab zinc paste on it, or walk into *Lazarett Ujazdowski*."

Ludwig stood eighteen inches away buttoning the grey gabardine collar of his fresh *Feldbluse*—looking abruptly, chillingly no longer like her half-naked surgical quarry on Bed Fourteen, looking every inch a senior Wehrmacht staff officer with silver cord gleaming on grey wool shoulders and cold river-green eyes cutting down into hers.

"Walk into Ujazdowski twice a week," Ludwig said quietly, "and Vesper sees how little dressing I need while you lose the single stamped pass in Warsaw that lets a Polish surgeon cross German police cordons past curfew with an unsearched leather bag—and lets us talk twice a week where neither Docent Mirecki's thin walls nor whoever leaked Chłodna twenty-two out of your underground mailbox can press an ear to the plaster."

Sylwia's breath caught silent behind her ribs. *He already assumed she had tested her Polish line—and saw from her eyes right now what she had found on Elektoralna!*

"Królewska sixteen is inside the German residential block," she said tightly. "Walk me through a *Nur für Deutsche* front hall in Polish shoes twice a week and every janitor on the block reports a Polish whore visiting an officer."

"Service stair from the garden passage comes straight up into the pantry—and half the German medical officers on Królewska bring Polish masseuses and dentists in by stamped *Außendienst* card without raising an eyebrow," Ludwig said without softening a fraction. His gaze dropped for half a heartbeat to the pulse beating fast in the bare hollow of her throat—two inches from where his sweating forehead had rested five minutes ago—and lifted straight back to her eyes. "Tuesdays and Fridays at five, starting the twenty-seventh of April, *Frau Doktor*. Miss an appointment without sending word, and I assume either Reinhart Kroll or your own leak on Chłodna picked you up—and I act accordingly."

Before she could throw the green card back at his silver buttons, he picked up his peaked cap off the stool, inclined his head two millimeters with immaculate, infuriating Prussian correctness, unlocked the splint-room door, and walked out to Mekelburg waiting in the corridor.

Alone on the white tiles, Sylwia stared down at *Królewska 16, Wohnung 4* stamped in violet Reich ink beside her own name—then picked up the green card with two fingers and slid it deep inside her pocket beside the bruised bone of her hip.
