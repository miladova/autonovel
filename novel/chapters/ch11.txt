The Sealed Tablet
He opens it on a night of rain because the rain keeps the clients away and leaves the atrium to the masks, and because a man does everything eventually for a reason he would strike from the record. He tells the household he is reviewing the estate papers. He is, in the largest sense his family permits the word: the estate is the past; the papers are its rent book.

The cupboard under his grandfather's mask sticks, as it always sticks in winter. The tablet is where he left it nine years ago, in the scroll-case whose leather has gone the colour of everything in this house that is not wax or wool. His father's seal: the cornel-wood sprig, the old joke of the Cornelii, a family that turned a tree into three centuries of magistracies. He breaks it with his thumb the way you break the skin on cooling milk, hating the delicacy, wanting the violence minuted somewhere.

His father's hand had been a public instrument even in private: large, exact, unhurried by death, which was already in the room when he wrote this — Lentulus can see it at the ends of lines, where the letters gather themselves like men deciding not to run.

*Gnaeus to Gnaeus. You will open this late. We are a family that opens things late and calls it dignity. Attend.*

*The girl from Baiae did not die there. The child — a daughter — lived. I paid the midwife to say otherwise and the steward to place her where the Cornelii would never have to spell her name. A freed couple across the river, fullers, decent people in the way that matters, which is to say they were paid and then, to their credit, continued after the paying stopped being their reason. I had the payments put through Philodorus so that you could truthfully say you knew nothing. You asked me once if there was a child. I said there was nothing living with a claim on this house. It was the truest sentence available that preserved the house, and I offer you now the observation, too late to be useful to me, that a lifetime of such sentences will preserve a house the way brine preserves meat.*

The rain. The compluvium dripping into its basin in the old rhythm, the one that measured his childhood lessons. He reads on, because stopping has never yet been a policy of his family's that survived contact with a document.

*I do not write this to be forgiven; your forgiveness would be worth what mine was, a generation too late and addressed to the wrong person. The person wronged is not you. The girl was sold on your muster morning with her increase, as the contract said. I wrote those words too. If you have lived as I surmise you have lived — quietly with it, like a stone in the shoe of a marching man — then you may draw what comfort there is in knowing the girl Antonia lives and prospers in Rome under a name she credits to herself, which is more than I can say for the name I left you. The daughter, if she lives, is called by whatever name decency gave her. I never asked it. There are questions a man of my sort does not ask in case the answer turns out to have a face.*

*Do nothing dramatic. Drama is a tax the rich impose on the poor to pay for their remorse. If the matter ever stands where this family can settle it at a price that is not the girl's life a second time, settle it. A Cornelius pays his debts. We merely prefer, historically, to choose the currency. Choose better.*

Lentulus sits with the tablet open until the lamp needs trimming and no slave comes, because Servilia has instructed the household, with the tactical sense that has kept this marriage upright for nineteen years, that the master reviewing estate papers is not to be improved upon by attendance.

A daughter. Called by whatever name decency gave her. He does the arithmetic that Antonia, across the city, in a house with damp in one wall, has done in exactly the same hour of the same winter: nineteen. A person of nineteen, across the river, in a fuller's household, walking about Rome in a body half-made of the Quirinal and half of a mirror-girl's, unknown to herself as either, while her father's committee inspects her mother's drains and her half-brother drinks Falernian downstairs from the accounts room where her existence has just been itemised as thread.

He has, until tonight, been able to tell himself that his quarrel with Antonia Cassia is about Rome. It is a story with columns: public morals, young men's debts, Syria. The tablet does not contradict the columns. It simply shows him the ground they stand on, and the ground is a muster morning and a contract phrase and a question asked once by a young man who accepted a statesman's sentence as an answer because the alternative would have cost him his inheritance of himself.

He closes the tablet. He does not reseal it; a Cornelius does not pretend with wax. He puts it back in the scroll-case, under the mask, in the sticking cupboard, and stands a while in the company of the ancestors, who look down with their smoke-dark patience, every one of them a man who chose his currency.

“Choose better,” he says aloud, to no one, and the atrium, being Roman, keeps the remark in the family.

Servilia is awake when he comes to bed. She lies in the posture of sleep that she maintains, like the household order, for the benefit of anyone conducting inspections, and says, without opening her eyes, “The estate papers.”

“Yes.”

“Olives?”

“Not olives.” It is the most intimate sentence they have exchanged in some years. The rain goes on sending its small messengers down through the compluvium of the old house into the basin that has received them for three centuries, and somewhere below, a slave who has not been told to stay away from the whole house, only from one room of it, is damping the kitchen fire for the night, banking it — keeping it, the way the poor keep everything: alive under ash, against the morning, at a price that appears in no one's ledger because it has always been paid.

Servilia says, “Then we will need to be more careful than your father was.”

He looks at her. She has still not opened her eyes. “How long have you—”

“Since the oysters,” she says. “Nineteen years. Sleep, Lentulus. Tomorrow you have a committee. Committees are how this family thinks. God knows it isn't how it acts.”
