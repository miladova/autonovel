# The Louse Feeders

*Warsaw, 8 March 1943*

March arrived smelling of wet dog, rotten cabbages thawed out of courtyard snowdrifts, and dead mortar blowing off the rubble of the Small Ghetto whenever wind came round the corner of Żelazna.

In the ironmonger's cart-passage off Krochmalna, two apprentices in burlap aprons were hammering rust scale off salvaged boiler flues with ball-peen hammers—*clang, clang, clang*—loud enough to drown five conspirators shouting into a rain barrel. Sylwia stood behind a stack of cast-iron stove grates watching muddy thaw-water run black between the cobbles while Marek shook rainwater off his cap visor.

"Your Prussian isn't dying very fast," Marek said without preamble. "Nineteen days since the ninth of February. Yesterday afternoon Hauptmann von Berg from Saski Square spent forty minutes inside Room Fourteen with a locked dispatch pouch while two military policemen cleared your corridor."

"Wounds packed open across a two-centimeter clavicular gap take four weeks to granulate over a naked artery," Sylwia said, keeping her gloved hands buried deep inside her coat pockets where the faint yellow fade of Ludwig's four finger-bruises still ringed her left wrist bone. "You told me on Bagno to keep him under Lindleya's roof and listen."

"And what have you heard besides military policemen striking matches?"

Sylwia watched the smaller apprentice spit brown tobacco juice onto a rusty elbow-pipe. Eight days she had carried the fever-fragments around behind her teeth like bits of broken amalgam—turning them over during appendectomies, weighing them on tram platforms, testing where one cut led if you pulled the string. *Chłodna zweiundzwanzig—nicht vor dem Zehnten zum Optiker—wenn Kroll den Uhrzeiger hat, brennt das ganze Netz bis zur Spiska.* Today was the eighth of March. Two days before the tenth.

Give Marek *everything*—optician, tenth of March, *der Uhrzeiger* ("the clock-hand," unmistakably *Zegar* if translated back into Polish!), and *Spiska*—and if Marek's own *Kontrwywiad* chain was clean, they caught a German drop red-handed on Wednesday. If, on the other hand, someone high enough up Warsaw District's ladder to have met an *Abwehr* car in Ochota with twelve hundred Swiss francs sat anywhere above Marek's reports, handing over the whole sentence signed Sylwia Kotarska's own death warrant because nobody on earth except the night surgeon in Room Fourteen had been inside four feet of Ludwig Schoeler's mouth when he hit forty point four.

Half a loaf tested the oven without burning the baker.

"Six nights ago he spiked past forty with a subdeltoid abscess," Sylwia said evenly. "In rigor delirium before the pus drained, he muttered one street number twice in German: *Chłodna twenty-two*. Something about an optician, not before the tenth of the month."

Marek's narrow grey eyes sharpened instantly—not surprise, recognition clicking onto an existing map grid behind his forehead. *Chłodna 22* stood halfway between Żelazna and Waliców, right along the narrowed Aryan carriageway corridor where trams clanged between the old northern and southern halves of the Ghetto wall, every ground-floor shopfront watched day and night by Blue Police posts and German *Zollgrenzschutz* customs guards.

"Optician," Marek repeated softly under the *clang-clang* of the boiler hammers. "Kaminski's spectacle-grinding shop was at twenty-two before forty-one; some Reich-German trustee from Łódź took over the lease making magnifying lenses for railway signalmen. Did he say a name? A cover-word?"

"Just boat hooks in Kiel and some dead professor from Berlin," Sylwia lied cleanly, looking straight at the wet collar button of Marek's coat without blinking. "Fever hash. Forty degrees four boils syntax before you get whole sentences."

Marek nodded once, calculating dates rapidly. "Tenth is Wednesday. Nobody from our shop goes near Chłodna twenty-two inside forty-eight hours—we'll put two girls with shopping baskets across the carriageway in the bakery queue from seven a.m. Wednesday and photograph every bastard who walks under the spectacle sign." His mood lightened visibly with concrete quarry underfoot; stepping half a pace closer into the dark angle behind the stacked cast-iron grates, he slid one ungloved hand inside the open front of Sylwia's coat, cold fingers finding her waist above her skirt band and pressing her hip back toward the dry brick wall. "Come up to Koszykowa at seven tonight? My pass runs till nine on Tuesday shifts now."

Sylwia's spine went stiff before her brain gave permission.

Not revulsion—Marek smelled no different today than on the ninth of February, the same wet shoddy wool, iron rust, and dry male skin—simply a sudden, blank wall of physical refusal so absolute that if he had pushed his hand two inches higher under her blouse she would have struck his wrist off like a drunk orderly's.

"I'm on Praga supply run till curfew and taking Mirecki's night call afterward," she said, stepping sideways out of the angle and buttoning her coat over his withdrawing hand as briskly as shutting a ledger. "His angina flared twice on the stairs yesterday; he took nitroglycerin behind the X-ray screen and thinks nobody saw."

Marek paused with his hand half-raised between them, looking closely at her gaunt, closed face in the grey passage light. Engineers noticed tolerances shifting by half a millimeter even when they misread the load.

"That German keeping you up nights?" he asked dryly.

"Seventy surgical beds and three grams of stolen sulfonamide keep me up nights," Sylwia said, already turning toward the street arch where cold drizzle blew across Krochmalna mud. "Tell your girls in the bakery queue on Wednesday to watch for grey cotton gloves if Aleja Szucha knows the address already. Kroll hasn't stopped sniffing round Lindleya's gate-book."

* * *

At five-fifteen a red municipal Number Seven tram ground across Poniatowski Viaduct above a Vistula split into dirty grey ice-floes grinding northward toward Modlin like broken graveyard slabs.

Sylwia stood wedged on the rear Polish platform between a peasant woman reeking of wet calfskin who carried two live pigeons buttoned silently inside her bosom and a one-legged railway pensioner leaning on an ash-wood crutch. Up ahead in the front half of the car behind the wooden chain—*Nur für Deutsche*—three Wehrmacht pay-clerks rode sitting down with six empty benches around their polished boots, reading the *Warschauer Zeitung*. Every time the tramwheels screamed round the curve descending into Praga, icy river wind whipped sleet under the canvas platform apron across Sylwia's ankles.

Inside her shabby black leather Gladstone bag—heavy enough to pull her right shoulder socket half an inch low—rode two sealed half-kilo tins of pure *Merck* anesthetic ether skimmed ten milliliters at a time off Vesper's German theater allotment over five weeks by Bronka, plus twelve hundred of Stefan's crisp *Volksliste* złoty folded inside a hollowed bar of grey clay ration soap.

Praga on the east bank always felt ten miles closer to Russia and fifty years rougher than left-bank Warsaw: low wooden fences rotting black beside brick railway tenements, horse-manure steaming outside vodka monopolies on Targowa, narrow-gauge sidings clanking behind tarred palings around **Dworzec Wschodni** where white-painted *Lazarettzüge*—hospital trains thirty cars long hauling frostbitten, shrapnel-chewed Wehrmacht remnants back from Kursk and Orel—stopped three hours to coal, water, and dump dead men before rolling west toward Breslau.

Hospital trains carried heaven in locked zinc cupboards: Bayer *Eleudron*, *Prontosil*, *Evipan* sodium, Dutch and Danish forty-unit insulin kept for diabetic colonels, genuine catgut sealed in glass tubes that didn't snap like rotten cotton inside a belly.

Behind the ruined spirit-rectification warehouse off Kijowska, thirty yards from the outer coaling fence where locomotive steam hung low and yellow-grey with sulfur in the March dusk, Pan Zygmunt was waiting inside a bombed coal-weigher's brick booth with his brass Ostbahn lantern turned face-to-the-wall.

Zygmunt had driven sleeping cars on the Warsaw–Vienna line before thirty-nine and still waxed the points of his grey moustache with axle-tallow. Without wasting civilities he lifted the canvas flap of Sylwia's bag, weighed both *Merck* ether tins on his palm—railwaymen and Wehrmacht dental orderlies paid gold for real Merck ether, either to trade to underground field stations east of Siedlce or to brew *hoffmannstropfen* spirit punch that knocked soldiers blind drunk on ten drops—and counted her twelve hundred złoty under his thumbnail.

"Two boxes intravenous Eleudron, five ampoules each, unbroken Bayer seal," Zygmunt muttered, pulling flat cardboard cartons out from inside his quilted railwayman's vest where body heat kept glass from cracking. "And two ten-milliliter vials Danish Leo forty-unit, good till December forty-three—came off a Stabsarzt's refrigerator car out of Königsberg at noon."

"You promised three vials Tuesday on the telephone," Sylwia said sharply, checking the rubber diaphragm caps under the tiny crack of his hooded lantern to make sure no clever orderly had drawn half the insulin out with a fine needle and topped the volume up with tap water.

"Three when Berlin trains weren't getting blown off the rails outside Minsk twice a week, pani doktor. Take two or leave them—a pharmacist from Grochów offered eighteen hundred apiece an hour ago."

She pocketed both insulin vials deep inside her wool dress lining against her own ribs and slid the two Bayer *Eleudron* cartons into her bag on top of a clean layer of pickled-beet rags Bronka packed for cover. Fifty paces back along the broken cinder-path beside Kijowska's dead brick wall led out to the lighted tram stop at Targowa, twenty-five minutes before eight o'clock curfew.

She had covered thirty of those fifty paces through drifting yellow coal-steam when a man in a belted leatherette trenchcoat and checked cloth cap stepped out from behind a rusted switch-box directly across the cinder path, whistling *Rozszumiały się wierzby płaczące* cheerfully off-key between crooked teeth.

Sylwia didn't break stride, shifting her Gladstone handle to her left hand so her right stayed free inside her pocket around her heavy brass flat-key—and knew instantly from the practiced half-circle shuffle of his galoshes that he wasn't a drunk after purses.

*Szmalcownik.* Or railway-siding Gestapo tout—same vermin in Praga, thirty-year-old Warsaw gutter-rats who hung round Dworzec Wschodni arches smelling black-market butter, escaped Jews with sewn-in gold coins, or Wehrmacht medical contraband at forty yards like ferrets smelling rabbit holes.

"Evening, pani doktor," the tout said amiably in Warsaw street-corner Polish, blocking the two-yard gap between brick wall and barbed-wire railway ditch so close she smelled pickled herring and bad dentist's clove oil off his breath. Small wet pig-eyes flicked straight down to the rectangular bulge inside her worn Gladstone bag. "Careful on those cinders in the dark—drop glass ampoules stamped *Wehrmachtseigentum* out here by the Bahnschutz fence and those Ukrainian guards shoot before they count boxes. Let's have a little look inside the bag, sweetheart, or we stroll sixty yards down to the guard-hut together and split the reward legal."

Rage—cold, murderous, crystal-clear—dropped Sylwia's heartbeat instead of speeding it. Hand over the bag and Staś ran dry of insulin by April third while Pavilion Three lost ten ampoules of sulfathiazole; scream on Kijowska sidings and two Bahnschutz riflemen came running inside forty seconds to find *Wehrmachtseigentum* Bayer cartons in a Polish surgeon's bag—straight to Pawiak women's ward "Serbia" by ten p.m.

"Twelve hundred's all the cash on me," Sylwia said flatly, pretending terrified compliance as she dipped her right hand slowly into her coat pocket—not for notes, hooking her middle finger squarely through the heavy brass bow of Koszykowa's four-inch corridor key with its jagged ward-bit protruding forward between her knuckles like a chisel. "Let me take the insulin out—it's for a child—"

"Bag and coat-pocket both, lady, and pull that little gold watch off your blouse pin while you're—"

He reached his left hand out to grab her collar.

Before Sylwia's brass key cleared wool, something grey-white and silent materialized out of the locomotive steam four paces behind the tout's right shoulder.

Zero shout, zero warning movie-hero bark—just two fast muffled crunches in wet coal cinders and a twenty-inch length of iron lorry tire-lever wrapped twice in burlap sacking whipped horizontally out of the fog into the side of the tout's right clavicle with a sodden *whock* like splitting wet marrow-bone with an axe-back.

Collarbone snapped audibly on impact. Before the tout could get half a scream past herring-stinking teeth, a booted knee drove up into his groin, folding him nose-down onto his face, and the same right fist—swinging alone because the attacker's left forearm stayed tucked rigid inside a grey reversible Wehrmacht parka front—brought the burlap-wrapped iron heel down once, short and economical, across the bridge of the man's nose and upper incisors against the frozen cinders.

Blood snorted black into coal mud; the tout twitched twice at the heels and went slack, snoring wetly through crushed nasal cartilage without waking a single Bahnschutz dog sixty yards down the fence.

Sylwia stood motionless against the damp brick wall with the brass key clenched between her knuckles, staring down at **Gefreiter Bruno Mekelburg**.

He wore no helmet and no silver belt-buckle shine—parka turned grey-side-out, field cap stuffed inside his pocket, breath steaming short through parted country-boy lips as he knelt one-kneed beside the unconscious tout, flipped the leatherette coat lapels open one-handed to verify no Gestapo metal warrant-disc hung inside on a chain—nothing, only a greasy blue *Kennkarte*, three pawn tickets, and a flick-knife—and wiped two drops of blood off the burlap-wrapped tire-iron onto the tout's checked cap before sliding the iron cleanly down inside his boot-top.

"Walk to the tram stop, *Frau Doktor*," Mekelburg whispered in flat East Prussian German, rising without looking down again at the groaning heap in the cinders. "Number Seven pulls round the loop in two minutes. Don't run—running makes the bridge sentry look twice."

Sylwia did not move a muscle for two seconds, eyes blazing straight into his turnip-pale face in the sulfur mist. "You followed me across the river from Lindleya."

"Talk on the bridge, not beside sixty railway guards," Mekelburg said grimly, tilting his chin toward Targowa's dim blue streetlamp. "Move your feet, *bitte*, or I carried this iron across Warsaw for nothing."

Common sense beat fury by half a length. She walked—brisk, steady, chin high, Gladstone bag knocking lightly against her calf—out onto Targowa pavement just as the red Number Seven clanged round the terminal loop. Mekelburg swung aboard the *front* German platform twenty feet ahead of her, flashing his Königsberg signals *Soldbuch* to the Polish conductor without once glancing rearward toward the wooden dividing chain where Sylwia stood gripping a cold brass upright behind two washerwomen's laundry baskets.

Halfway back across Poniatowski Viaduct, when wind roared loud off the ice-choked Vistula below and the front German compartment happened to empty out at Solec ramp, Mekelburg stepped back against the dividing chain to light a crumpled *Juno* cigarette one-handed—striking the match deftly with thumbnail against box wedged beneath his splinted left armpit—back half-turned to Sylwia two feet behind him so anyone watching saw only a sullen Wehrmacht corporal blocking Polish wind with his shoulders.

"He sent you after me," Sylwia said low in German straight at the shaved nape of his neck above the grey parka collar. "From a hospital bed with an open chest."

"Der *Oberstleutnant* didn't send me anywhere," Mekelburg muttered around the unlit cigarette without turning his head, eyes fixed forward through sleet-streaked tram glass. "He slept from three till four after Hauptmann von Berg left. I saw your old fat nurse packing two ether tins out of the German cupboard into beet rags before five, and I know what railwaymen trade at Dworzec Wschodni because I traded two cans of pork fat there myself last Tuesday for clean socks."

Sylwia's grip tightened on her bag handle. "And what business is it of yours what my matron packs in beet rags?"

"None—so long as half of what came out of that railwayman's vest goes into Bed Fourteen's veins when his fever climbs again instead of all going upstairs to Polish carpenters," Mekelburg said with blunt, unembarrassed peasant cunning that nearly took her breath away. Gently under his parka front he shifted his three-fingered left hand in its wooden splint. "Besides—catch you with Bayer army boxes at Kijowska fence, and tomorrow morning that white-gloved SS bastard from Aleja Szucha locks Pavilion Three up tight, throws my *Oberstleutnant* into an SS ambulance before his artery's tough, and puts me in a penal battalion clearing mines with a stick at Rzhev. Breaking one Warsaw rat's nose saved everybody trouble."

Wind howled across the viaduct ironwork; below them black water chewed white foam along stone piers blown up in thirty-nine and patched with raw pine timbers.

"I bought two boxes of Eleudron," Sylwia said coldly at the back of his big red ear. "Ten ampoules. Five stay locked in Room Fourteen's washstand for your commander if streptococci flare again; five go upstairs where I please. Breathe one word to Vesper or your *Oberstleutnant* about Kijowska—or trail my coat again—and next time I change your finger dressings I'll leave just enough silk knot deep in the third web-space to keep your palm suppurating till Christmas."

Mekelburg's narrow shoulders actually gave a single silent heave that might, in East Prussia, have passed for laughter. "Agreed, *Frau Doktor*. Though if you want forty-unit Danish insulin cheaper than railway thieves sell it, tell me next Monday before you cross the river—our *Sanitätspark* clerk on Królewska owes me forty Reichsmarks from skat, and he steals cleaner than train guards."

Before Sylwia could frame an answer sharp enough to flay him, the tram ground screeching into Aleje Jerozolimskie at Marszałkowska corner and Mekelburg dropped cleanly off the front step into blowing sleet, vanishing down Brackia three minutes ahead of her so Lindleya's gatehouse would never log surgeon and corporal walking through the arch inside the same quarter-hour.

* * *

At ten minutes to ten Room Fourteen was quiet except for rain ticking against blackout tin and the slow dry turn of thin Bible-paper pages.

Ludwig sat propped upright on two bolsters with a faded grey linen Leipzig pocket edition of Thucydides—*Der Peloponnesische Krieg*, Greek facing German—resting open against his bandaged left forearm like a music stand while his uninjured right hand pared tiny shavings off a rock-hard wedge of smoked sheep's cheese Mekelburg had conjured from God knew which peasant market, laying each transparent curl onto dry ration crispbread with surgical neatness.

Twenty-seven days since Table One. Eight days since forty point four. Without toxic fever puffing his face, he looked leaner than ever—collarbone hollow on the intact right side deep enough to hold walnut shells, dark blond hair grown long enough over his collar to curl slightly damp from evening washing—yet the grey putty death-cast had vanished completely. Healthy colour sat faint beneath wind-roughened skin; when Sylwia unlocked the washstand cupboard with her iron ward-key and laid one unbroken five-ampoule carton of Bayer *Eleudron* silently on the zinc shelf beside his irrigation bottles, his winter-green eyes lifted over the top of Thucydides without missing a stroke of his pocket-knife blade.

"Generous of the municipal pharmacy," Ludwig remarked mildly.

"Your corporal snores louder than he walks, *Herr Oberstleutnant*," Sylwia said without turning round from prepping the evening Dakin's syringe. "Tell him next time he takes evening strolls across the river in reversible parkas to scrape yellow Kijowska coal-ash off his boot welts before standing at attention on my white floor."

Behind her in the corner chair, Bruno Mekelburg went brick-red clear up into his shaved blond bristles, staring fixedly at the ceiling moulding as if reciting artillery tables.

On the bed, Ludwig slowly closed his folding pocket-knife with a soft *snick* and looked first at Mekelburg's muddy boot-heels, then back at the hem of Sylwia's wool skirt peeping two inches below her white ward smock—where three identical dried grey-yellow spatters of Praga locomotive cinder-slush marked the black cloth.

Clearly Mekelburg *had* kept his word and hadn't breathed a syllable since returning at eight; watching Ludwig put the entire Praga equation together in three silent seconds—stolen ether out, Bayer sulfathiazole and black-market contraband back, a broken-knuckled corporal playing uninvited bodyguard—was like watching steel tumblers click inside a Chubb safe.

"Bruno," Ludwig said pleasantly, "go ask Siostra Bronisława whether the kitchen stove still has hot water for shaving tomorrow morning. Take ten minutes."

Mekelburg escaped into the hall as if shot from a mortar.

Sylwia unpinned the outer dressing pad without waiting for commentary. "Lean forward two inches so the towel catches the flush."

He leaned forward off the bolsters—effortless abdominal core strength now compared to two weeks ago, no tremor at all—letting her peel away the yellow iodoform strips. Underneath, the wound looked magnificent in the ugly, uncompromising way only *per secundam* surgery ever looked magnificent: zero grey slough, zero pus, the subdeltoid counter-incision already flat and dry, and across the entire palm-sized deltopectoral floor a carpet of firm, bright beefy-red granulation buds bleeding tiny pinpricks where gauze lifted off, already contracting inward three millimeters round the edges to bury her deep muscle bridge over the axillary artery forever under tough fibrous scar.

"Ten more days and skin grafts wouldn't even save time," Ludwig observed, craning his chin down sideways to inspect his own raw chest wall with clinical detachment while Dakin's solution hissed faintly down the shallowing trough. "Clean granulation. My compliments, *Frau Doktor*. Even von Halden's septic ward rarely looked cleaner by week four."

"Save your compliments for Stabsarzt Vesper's discharge ledger," Sylwia said briskly, packing half the previous depth of gauze into the shrinking cavity. "Inside twelve days that scar shelf is tough enough to bounce from here to Königsberg in an ammunition lorry without tearing a stitch, and Pavilion Three gets its six beds—and its quiet corridors—back."

He didn't lean back onto the bolsters when she finished the first calico turn; he stayed forward four inches, bare right shoulder and warm throat level with her chin while she passed the linen roll behind his spine, gray-green eyes resting steady and unhurried on her profile from ten inches away.

"You really want your corridor quiet right now, Sylwia?" he asked softly.

Her hands didn't pause on the bandage roll, pulling the cross-turn snug across his sternum. "My corridor was quiet before nine February."

"Your corridor was lucky before nine February," he corrected her quietly. "Right now two military policemen with *Abwehrstelle* warrant brassards sit outside your east doors night and day, which means neither Schutzpolizei street patrols nor Reinhart Kroll's junior clerks from Aleja Szucha can walk unannounced into Pavilion Three without signing a Wehrmacht counter-intelligence logbook twenty paces from your stairs. Take my sentries away next week—after Kroll has already smelled something clever round your gatehouse clock—and how long before white cotton gloves come back alone at three in the morning to count every patient and laboratory girl under your floorboards?"

Sylwia's fingers stilled for half a second on the split calico knot above his right clavicle.

He didn't know Halina's real name—couldn't possibly know about Dawid or Weigl lice on Jewish thighs unless walls had X-ray eyes—yet a professional counter-espionage hunter lying awake twenty-seven nights in a Polish municipal hospital heard clogs on cellar stairs at midnight, smelled extra broth pots carried past locked doors, measured the exact nervous sweat on Docent Mirecki's forehead whenever German boots echoed in the hall, and knew an underground burrow by the draft under the wainscoting!

"Are you threatening my hospital, *Schoeler*, or charging rent?" she whispered, bitter and low above his bare collarbone.

"Neither," Ludwig said, eyes dark slate-green at close range, not a trace of mockery in them now. "Just reminding a clever surgeon not to pull a tamponade plug out before she knows what bleeds behind it. And don't curse Mekelburg for walking behind you at Kijowska. Half the touts working Dworzec Wschodni coal fences report twice a week to Referat IV N on Szucha for vodka money. Had you put your brass house-key into that pig's eye tonight and left him alive enough to describe a tall dark woman doctor with Berlin German, Kroll would have lined up every female physician in Warsaw by noon tomorrow."

Blood stung hot behind Sylwia's ears—furious at being seen right down to the brass key inside her right pocket! "Tell him to stay inside this room where he belongs."

"Tell an East Prussian farm boy twice not to do something after you saved three of his fingers and you might as well talk Greek to a mule." Ludwig shifted back onto the bolsters as she clipped the knot tails short, reaching his right hand underneath the corner of his grey Wehrmacht blanket where his leather map-wallet sat wedged against the iron frame. Without ceremony he tossed three flat square silver-foil packets onto the white sheet beside her instrument tray: *Dextropur — Reiner Traubenzucker — Luftwaffen-Notration*. Pure crystalline medicinal glucose blocks issued to bomber crews against high-altitude exhaustion. "Whoever you're buying black-market forty-unit insulin for on railway ramps—when ration turnips miscalculate starch against forty-unit depot peaks at three in the morning and there's no sugar lump in the flat, half a square under the tongue beats boiling potato peelings."

Sylwia stared down at the three silver-foil squares glinting on the coarse hospital sheet.

Last month, during the January freeze when Polish sugar coupons went unhonored for three weeks, Wanda had woken screaming at four a.m. to find nine-year-old Staś drenched in icy sweat and twitching in hypoglycemic convulsions at twenty-eight milligrams blood glucose, forcing Sylwia to sprint eight blocks through curfew snow with twenty milliliters of hospital fifty-percent intravenous dextrose to pull him out of coma before brain damage set in.

She looked up from the silver foil straight into Ludwig's eyes.

Giving them back out of patriotic theater meant gambling a nine-year-old boy's cortex for her own pride; saying *thank you* to an *Oberstleutnant* of the occupying Wehrmacht choked in her throat like ground glass.

 Ludwig didn't wait for her to say a word—already picking up his tattered Leipzig Thucydides one-handed and propping it back open against his bandaged sling as if the foil squares had fallen out of the ceiling plaster by themselves.

"Good night, *Kotarska*," he said to the Greek page without looking up. "Tell your Docent his cherry alcohol smells all the way down the radiator pipe when he hides in the dispensary."

Sylwia swept the three silver *Dextropur* packets silently into her smock pocket alongside her scissors, picked up the soiled kidney basin, blew out the extra washstand candle, and walked out into the corridor with her jaw locked so hard her back teeth ached.
