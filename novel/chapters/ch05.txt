A Short History of Stairs
She does not tell it. That is the first thing to understand about Antonia and the past: it is not a story she withholds, the way other women withhold a lover's name for the pleasure of the withholding. It is more like a room in a house she owns, whose door she keeps locked not from shame but from housekeeping. What would be the use of opening it daily? The furniture does not change. And yet a house is shaped by its locked rooms. the people outside them learn to walk certain ways.

There was a house, though. Let the record — which is only her, at night, settling accounts the tax office will never audit — admit that much. A house on the Quirinal, old, cold in winter through some principle of its architecture, where her mother, freed too late to enjoy it and dead too soon to describe it, had been a hairdresser; where Antonia, born while her mother was still enslaved and therefore born, as the law so gracefully puts it, into her owner's property, grew up as an accessory to other women's mornings: holding the mirror, fetching the pins, learning, with the speed of a child who understands that knowledge is the one ration that cannot be short-weighted, to read from the letters left open on dressing tables.

The son of the house was seven years older and home between tutors. This is the locked room's main piece of furniture and she has stopped dusting it: Gnaeus at twenty-four, between a military tribunate and a marriage being negotiated like a border, discovering in his father's house a slave girl of seventeen who could read his Greek over his shoulder and correct, once — once was enough; the room still contains the sound of his laugh — his scansion of a line about a woman at a loom, waiting, which the poet calls faithful and the girl, before she could stop herself, called *unpaid*.

What happened after is not romance, whatever the poets who now eat at her tables would make of it for a fee. It was what happens in houses where one person's *no* is a legal nullity and the other person's *yes* is the weather. And it was — this is the part she has never found a filing place for, not in twenty years of excellent systems — also tenderness. He taught her letters properly, then accounts. He talked, in the manner of young men discovering principles, of manumission as if it were a gift he had personally invented. She believed the teaching and discounted the talking, which is to say she was the only adult in the room, and she was seventeen, and pregnant by the spring.

His father also kept accounts. The old Cornelius — another Gnaeus, they are all Gnaeus, it saves carving — settled the matter the way his class settled rain through a roof: by paying someone else to stand under it. The marriage to Servilia was advanced by a year. Antonia was sold, on a morning when Gnaeus was at a muster on the Campus, to a dealer with a household to staff in Baiae; sold, specifically, *with her increase*, as the contract's Latin puts it, a phrase she read upside down on the dealer's tablet while standing very still with her hands folded, already practising, though she did not know it, for a life in which stillness would be her most expensive garment.

What she remembers of Baiae is stairs. Houses for pleasure are built down hillsides and serviced up them. She remembers the weight of water, the weight in her body changing its address month by month, a midwife with hennaed nails, and a morning — the locked room's floor is worn here, this is where her memory paces — when the child had come in the night and gone by the morning, *not strong*, said the midwife, *a girl, not strong*, and there was money in the midwife's sleeve that had not been there the day before, and Antonia, feverish, emptied, seventeen going on a hundred, did the arithmetic she had been taught by the son of the house and arrived, correctly, at the only conclusion that let her stand up and climb stairs again within the fortnight: that her daughter was dead, because the alternative arithmetic — sold on, given away, exposed on a hillside where the pious left unwanted arithmetic for the gods — did not permit standing up at all.

She stood up. That is the whole of the heroic part, and it took years. A steward in Baiae liked her head for figures more than her face, a rare ordering she blessed nightly; a widow in Naples freed her in a will, whether from affection or to spite a son-in-law Antonia never asked; freedom turned out to be a staircase too, only now she could charge for the climbing. Loans at interest to women the banks would not see. A share in a laundry. A dining room in Rome, then two, then the house on the Caelian with the damp in the courtyard wall and the pastry cook arguing about saffron. Antonia Cassia is a name she assembled the way she assembled everything else: good materials, no one's permission.

She does not tell it. Eirene knows the shape of it, the way a person who shares a house knows where the locked room is without ever being shown. And if a needlewoman from Transtiberim holds her head, when counting, exactly the way — no. The count of coin is finished. The count of years she will do when she must, and she has decided, nightly for some days now, that she must not yet.

Lentulus does not tell it either. His locked room is better built; architects cost money, and the Cornelii have always spent on structure. In his version, which he has not rehearsed aloud in twenty years and therefore believes to be unrehearsed, he came home from the muster to a father's study redolent of wax and finality and was told that the girl had been sold south, comfortably, *the matter concluded*, and that his marriage would be celebrated in the autumn, and that a man's youth is a province he governs badly and then, if he is fortunate in his family, is recalled from before the audit.

He asked one question. This is the worn floor in his room. He asked whether there had been a child. His father, who had governed provinces and understood the load-bearing capacity of a pause, said: “There is nothing living with a claim on this house.”

Lentulus was twenty-four. He took the sentence as an answer because he needed it to be one, and he has spent twenty years paying interest on that act of interpretation — in the small hours, in the Senate when a debate touches the exposure of infants or the rights of freedwomen and he finds his own voice arriving before his judgment; in the particular ferocity, which his friends call principle and his wife calls by no name at all, with which he pursues the regulation of houses where other men's sons go to make Gnaeus's arithmetic and call it love.

He married Servilia in the autumn. She knew. He understood that she knew within the first month, from a sentence about Baiae dropped into a conversation about oysters with the precision of a stone into a well, to hear the depth. They have never spoken of it again. It is one of the load-bearing silences of the marriage, along with the Campanian debts and the fact — observed by both, acknowledged by neither — that Servilia would have made the better senator.

His father died consul and honoured. The will, when it was read among the masks, contained the usual bequests and one unusual one: a sealed tablet for Gnaeus's eyes, which Lentulus, being his father's son in the matter of locked rooms, has kept sealed for nine years in the cupboard under his grandfather's mask. He tells himself it concerns the estates. He does not open it for the same reason Antonia does not count Prima's years: some arithmetic, once performed, must be acted on, and he has Syria in prospect, and a daughter to marry, and a committee proceeding against a house on the Caelian whose mistress taught him, though she would laugh to hear it, everything he knows about the difference between what a thing is called and what it is.

You tell yourself the tablet is about olive groves. You have always been good at telling yourself things in the tone of a committee, as if a minuted untruth were sturdier than a spoken one. The masks look down. Wax, smoke, paint. Every one of them a man who locked a room and called the house honest.
