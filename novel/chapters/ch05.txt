A Short History of Stairs

She does not tell it. That is the first thing to understand about Antonia and the past: it is not a story she withholds, the way other women withhold a lover's name for the pleasure of the withholding. It is more like a room in a house she owns, whose door she keeps locked not from shame but from housekeeping. What would be the use of opening it daily? The furniture does not change. And yet a house is shaped by its locked rooms. the people outside them learn to walk certain ways.

There was a house, though. Let the record — which is only her, at night, settling accounts the tax office will never audit — admit that much. A house on the Quirinal, old, cold in winter through some principle of its architecture, where her mother, freed too late to enjoy it and dead too soon to describe it, had been a hairdresser; where Antonia, born while her mother was still enslaved and therefore born, as the law so gracefully puts it, into her owner's property, grew up as an accessory to other women's mornings: holding the mirror, fetching the pins, learning, with the speed of a child who understands that knowledge is the one ration that cannot be short-weighted, to read from the letters left open on dressing tables.

The son of the house was seven years older and home between tutors. This is the locked room's main piece of furniture and she has stopped dusting it: Gnaeus at twenty-four, between a military tribunate and a marriage being negotiated like a border, discovering in his father's house a slave girl of seventeen who could read his Greek over his shoulder and correct, once — once was enough; the room still contains the sound of his laugh — his scansion of a line about a woman at a loom, waiting, which the poet calls faithful and the girl, before she could stop herself, called *unpaid*.

The Quirinal house ran, as such houses do, on a staff of small, complete governments. Philodorus the steward governed paper. A freedwoman called Lalage governed the women's side with a voice she could lay across a courtyard like a plank. Antonia's mother governed hair, which sounds like the least of the provinces and was, in a house receiving bridal embassies, something close to foreign affairs: a Cornelian daughter-in-law was being negotiated that year the way provinces are, in stages, with scouts, and every scout came through the dressing rooms first, where the women of the visiting houses sat under Antonia's mother's hands and talked, as women under hairdressers' hands have talked since hair, about the things their men believed were being settled elsewhere.

Antonia held the mirror for these sessions from the age of nine. A mirror held steady is an education you cannot confiscate. She learnt the Servilii's finances before she learnt the word for them, from a visiting aunt whose hair was being dressed for a dinner at which money was not to be mentioned. She learnt that the young Gnaeus was *difficult about the match*, this from Lalage retailing and her mother pricing, in the shorthand of women who run the rooms where matches are actually audited. She learnt the phrase *a settlement*, with its whole future curled inside it like a hair on a heated iron. And she learnt to read — this is the inheritance no will itemised — because letters left open on dressing tables are a library for a girl whose job is stillness, and because her mother, who could not read four words in a row, understood with the whole of her thwarted intelligence that letters were the one luggage a slave carries that no steward can search.

“Sound it,” her mother would say, at night, in the little room behind the women's corridor where the combs slept in oil, pointing at a laundry list Antonia had rescued from a basket. “Not loud. The house doesn't need to hear its linen counted twice.”

So her literacy arrived the way everything in her life would: quietly, out of other people's leavings, at the full rate of attention. Greek came later and worse — from a tutor's slave who copied texts in the library and traded declensions for kitchen privileges, in the freemasonry of the unfree who are building weapons out of the household surplus. By fifteen she could follow a letter; by sixteen, an account; by seventeen, she knew which of the two a man was lying in, a distinction that has since capitalized a house on the Caelian.

It was the accounts, officially, that brought her to the young master's rooms. Gnaeus, home between tutors and tribunate, was supposed to be learning the estates; the estates, presented by Philodorus in rolls that smelled of oak galls and other people's weather, bored him in the particular way of clever young men who believe boredom is a form of judgment. He set the girl who brought his mother's messages to copying a rental roll because her hand was quicker than his secretary's and because — the locked room admits this much furniture — he liked the way she mocked nothing while seeing everything, a discipline no one had taught him and everyone in his family performed as sneering, which is different.

“You read over my shoulder,” he said once, not looking up from the Greek.

“The master reads aloud,” Antonia said. “The room is entitled.”

“The room is entitled to silence.”

“Then the room is owed arrears, dominus.” She said it to the rental roll. She was seventeen years old and her whole life was a staircase people carried things down; she did not know yet that a sentence like that, in a house like this, to a son like this, is a door being built by its own noise.

He laughed. It was the laugh, still. He began, after that, to leave texts where the room was entitled: a page of history open at a siege, a speech against a governor his father admired, the poem about the woman at the loom. He taught her the letters properly because teaching her the letters improperly had already happened, in the way his class does everything — by accident, then by system, then by claiming the system. He talked about the law’s categories the way young men talk about constellations: as if naming them were governing them. *Manumission* entered the conversations, as money enters a bridal negotiation, warmly, vaguely, and without a date. She banked the teaching and discounted the talking, which is to say she was the only adult in the room, and the room, in the end, sent the bill for both of their educations to the usual address.

Her mother knew. Lalage knew. Philodorus, who governed paper, began to keep a kind of paper that is only kept in stewards' heads, in columns. There was a morning in the women's corridor when her mother, arranging another woman's hair for a betrothal visit, said — to the hair, to the air, to the government of stillness in which her daughter stood holding a mirror — “A house will forgive a son anything except being *seen* to need forgiveness. Remember which of the two of you a house can sell to restore its view.”

Antonia remembered. She remembered it on stairs in Baiae with her whole body doing the work of memory. It is one of the sentences — there are perhaps six — that she has carried unspent through every currency Rome has offered her since, and it is the reason that, in her own house, twenty years later, no woman is ever asked to be invisible in the service of anybody's view. The rooms can be discreet. The women are registered. There is a difference, and the difference, as she once told a tax official who had not asked, is where she lives.

What happened after is not romance, whatever the poets who now eat at her tables would make of it for a fee. It was what happens in houses where one person's *no* is a legal nullity and the other person's *yes* is the weather. And it was — this is the part she has never found a filing place for, not in twenty years of excellent systems — also tenderness. He taught her letters properly, then accounts. He talked, in the manner of young men discovering principles, of manumission as if it were a gift he had personally invented. She believed the teaching and discounted the talking, which is to say she was the only adult in the room, and she was seventeen, and pregnant by the spring.

His father also kept accounts. The old Cornelius — another Gnaeus, they are all Gnaeus, it saves carving — settled the matter the way his class settled rain through a roof: by paying someone else to stand under it. The marriage to Servilia was advanced by a year. Antonia was sold, on a morning when Gnaeus was at a muster on the Campus, to a dealer with a household to staff in Baiae; sold, specifically, *with her increase*, as the contract's Latin puts it, a phrase she read upside down on the dealer's tablet while standing very still with her hands folded, already practising, though she did not know it, for a life in which stillness would be her most expensive garment.

What she remembers of Baiae is stairs. Houses for pleasure are built down hillsides and serviced up them. She remembers the weight of water, the weight in her body changing its address month by month, a midwife with hennaed nails, and a morning — the locked room's floor is worn here, this is where her memory paces — when the child had come in the night and gone by the morning, *not strong*, said the midwife, *a girl, not strong*, and there was money in the midwife's sleeve that had not been there the day before, and Antonia, feverish, emptied, seventeen going on a hundred, did the arithmetic she had been taught by the son of the house and arrived, correctly, at the only conclusion that let her stand up and climb stairs again within the fortnight: that her daughter was dead, because the alternative arithmetic — sold on, given away, exposed on a hillside where the pious left unwanted arithmetic for the gods — did not permit standing up at all.

Baiae in the season is Rome with its guard down and its prices up: great houses terraced into the bay like an audience settling for a long performance, oyster beds in the shallows making their slow, litigious fortunes, and everywhere the water doing the thing water does to Romans who can afford it, which is persuade them they are simpler people than their tailors know. The house Antonia was sold to belonged to a dealer who supplied staff the way other men supply lamps: by the gross, with replacements. She was entered in his books as *girl, literate, with increase* — he kept books; she read herself in them one morning while delivering a tally to his office, upside down, the position in which Rome has allowed her to read most of the important sentences of her life — and she was put, for her literacy, not to the hardest carrying but to the count of it, which in a pleasure house means counting other people's carrying and carrying enough yourself to keep the count honest.

She kept the laundry tally for ninety stairs. She kept the wine tally against a steward who watered by doctrine and confessed by arithmetic, his figures having a tell, like a gloss in a text: they improved toward festivals. She kept — this is the locked room's apprenticeship, the one her Caelian house would one day be built out of — a private tally besides, on a potsherd, in a hand she let nobody see. Days. Weeks by her body's calendar. The fee a midwife charged women who could pay not to be recorded, overheard in a kitchen and stored. The price, quoted by a dealer at dinner, of a healthy child sold young into a good house — *a good house*, the phrase sat at the dealer's table like a guest who has not been introduced to the dishes. She did sums on the sherd that no one had set her. All of them came out the same way: whatever happened in the winter, it would happen to a person with no column of her own, and the only column she would ever get was the one she taught herself to keep standing in.

The midwife's nails were hennaed. That is filed. What is also filed, and has been walked over in the locked room until the floor shines, is a conversation on the stairs two nights before, between the midwife and a man whose Latin had a Quirinal polish on it that Baiae money does not buy. Antonia heard half of it from a landing with a basket — half is the servant's portion everywhere; it is why servants' histories have the soundness of bridges and the completeness of ruins. The man's half contained the phrase *no record in this house*. The midwife's contained a sum, and then a sum improved, the way sums at dealers' tables improve toward festivals. Antonia was seventeen and vast as a harbour with the child and giddy on the stairs with the weight of the two of them, and she understood — the understanding arrived the way it would arrive all her life, first in the hands, carrying on carrying, while the mind took the stairs behind her — that the arrangements being made around her body were not medical.

She thought of saying it. To whom: that was the whole of the rest of the conversation, conducted with herself at the tally desk that night, in columns. To the dealer, who had bought her *with increase* and would sell the increase as a separate line, being a man who kept books? To the steward, Philodorus' southern colleague in the government of paper? To the young man on the Quirinal, whose laugh was by then a room she could no longer afford to stand in, who was being married that autumn to a settlement with good hair? A letter — she could write letters; letters were the luggage — a letter would be read by everyone whose hands it passed through, which is to say it would arrive as evidence in an inquiry she would not survive attending. She turned the potsherd over and wrote nothing. It is the only unwritten letter in the archive she keeps between her ribs, and its text, all these years, is still just the address.

What she did instead — the record should be allowed this, since Rome has so little paperwork on the virtues of seventeen-year-old slaves in winter — was work. She worked the stairs until the week her body informed the house otherwise, and she arranged, with a laundress from the town who had a sister across the river in Rome and a face like reliable weather, a thing that had no column: that if anything survived the winter with her name on it in any sense the gods inspect, the sister across the river would hear of it. It was not a plan. Plans require standing. It was a thread put through a needle in the dark, by a girl who had learned counting so the stairs would not count her, and who believed — believes; the tense is a locked room too — that a thread is what you hold while the rest of the house decides whether you were ever in it.

She stood up. That is the whole of the heroic part, and it took years. A steward in Baiae liked her head for figures more than her face, a rare ordering she blessed nightly; a widow in Naples freed her in a will, whether from affection or to spite a son-in-law Antonia never asked; freedom turned out to be a staircase too, only now she could charge for the climbing. Loans at interest to women the banks would not see. A share in a laundry. A dining room in Rome, then two, then the house on the Caelian with the damp in the courtyard wall and the pastry cook arguing about saffron. Antonia Cassia is a name she assembled the way she assembled everything else: good materials, no one's permission.

She does not tell it. Eirene knows the shape of it, the way a person who shares a house knows where the locked room is without ever being shown. And if a needlewoman from Transtiberim holds her head, when counting, exactly the way — no. The count of coin is finished. The count of years she will do when she must, and she has decided, nightly for some days now, that she must not yet.

The Naples widow — let the record, which is hers at night and Rome's by daylight, give the woman her charges in full — was called Octavia Rufa, she was sixty, and she freed Antonia in her will for the stated reason, read out by a notary in a room full of relatives behaving like weather over someone else's harvest, that *this woman kept my books and my temper for four years, the second being the harder account*. The son-in-law challenged it. The son-in-law is one of the people the Caelian house now overcharges on principle whenever his agents book a room, a tax he has never identified across fifteen years of dinners. Antonia attended the hearing in a borrowed veil and watched a praetor's clerk read her freedom aloud in the tone men use for the minutes of other people's earthquakes. Then she walked out into Naples owning: one set of good clothes, one potsherd she no longer needed and could not throw away, a sum the will called a legacy and the son-in-law called an insult, and the knowledge — it sat on her like a cloak cut for somebody taller — that every paper in the world now had to go through her own hands before it could happen to her.

Freedom, she discovered that winter, is a staircase with a toll at every landing. A freedwoman of no family may keep books for widows, lend small sums, stand surety for women the banks will not see, and be cheated by exactly the same men on exactly the same terms as before, except that now the law will call her a party. It is not nothing, being a party. It is not what the young man's talk in the library promised, either, back when manumission was a gift he had personally invented. She kept the widow's successor's books for a year, at a salary she negotiated standing up — the first thing in her life she had ever negotiated; the sensation, she told Eirene later, in another city, in another life, was like being allowed to be *late* — and she lent the legacy out in pieces so small a Greek banker laughed at the ledger: to a laundress for a mangle, to a midwife (unhennaed, competent, terrifying) for a room with a door, to a freedman baker whose ovens a landlord was converting into leverage. Every loan was written. Every repayment was witnessed. She charged interest she could defend to the women's faces and collected it with a memory they came, in time, to treat as a neighbourhood utility: *ask Antonia, she'll know what your husband signed*. It is a short walk, in Rome's sister cities, from being the woman who knows what the paper says to being the woman the paper is brought to before it is signed. Short, and uphill, and lit by other people's lamps.

Eirene arrived in the third year, out of the Subura, out of a house whose owner's death had distributed its staff like an estate — because they *were* the estate, in the older papers — with a freedom bought by a subscription among the laundry women and a scar of temper where her trust used to be itemised. They recognised each other in a Naples street the way people from sunken houses do: not with embraces, at first, but with information, traded at full value across a bakers' stall — who was dead, who was sold north, whose mother, whose stairs. The embrace came later, in lodgings, with the same economy. Eirene looked at Antonia's books, at the little loans marching like a border of red flowers, each the same, each slightly alive with difference, and said, “You're building a fuller’s shop for money. Takes it in dirty, gives it back clean, charges by the piece.”

“I'm building the thing that would have caught us,” Antonia said.

“Then it wants a bigger door,” Eirene said, “and a kitchen. In Rome. Nobody was ever caught in Naples except by the view.”

So: Rome, two tables, a lamp-seller's credit, a dining room over a fuller's that smelled, on wet days, of home. The rest the city knows in its baths version — *a freedwoman with a Greek banker and a pastry cook, who collects senators' secrets like other women collect jet* — and the house version, which is Diomedes' three books and the truth filed between the quarters, is nobody's but hers. She has been asked, by men who think a life like hers is a menu they have paid to have explained, how she *rose*. She considers the word the way she considered a carter's amphora in a January yard. She did not rise. Rising is for bread and empires. She climbed, professionally, a staircase Rome built to be carried up by other people, counting as she went, and at some landing the toll-keepers began, one by one, to be her tenants. That is the whole miracle. It took twenty years. It is still, on the nights when the accounts are done and the house is loud with other people's appetites, the only story in the world she is certain she can tell in the order of the evening and not the order of the questions — because the questions, all of them, arrive from Baiae, and Baiae is a room she owns, whose door she keeps locked not from shame but from housekeeping.

Lentulus does not tell it either. His locked room is better built; architects cost money, and the Cornelii have always spent on structure. In his version, which he has not rehearsed aloud in twenty years and therefore believes to be unrehearsed, he came home from the muster to a father's study redolent of wax and finality and was told that the girl had been sold south, comfortably, *the matter concluded*, and that his marriage would be celebrated in the autumn, and that a man's youth is a province he governs badly and then, if he is fortunate in his family, is recalled from before the audit.

He asked one question. This is the worn floor in his room. He asked whether there had been a child. His father, who had governed provinces and understood the load-bearing capacity of a pause, said: “There is nothing living with a claim on this house.”

Lentulus was twenty-four. He took the sentence as an answer because he needed it to be one, and he has spent twenty years paying interest on that act of interpretation — in the small hours, in the Senate when a debate touches the exposure of infants or the rights of freedwomen and he finds his own voice arriving before his judgment; in the particular ferocity, which his friends call principle and his wife calls by no name at all, with which he pursues the regulation of houses where other men's sons go to make Gnaeus's arithmetic and call it love.

He married Servilia in the autumn. She knew. He understood that she knew within the first month, from a sentence about Baiae dropped into a conversation about oysters with the precision of a stone into a well, to hear the depth. They have never spoken of it again. It is one of the load-bearing silences of the marriage, along with the Campanian debts and the fact — observed by both, acknowledged by neither — that Servilia would have made the better senator.

The marriage, as marriages of settlement go, was competently unhappy only in its first season and workable thereafter, which among their kind is the material other classes call a success story. Servilia brought land in two provinces, a mind that ran the Palatine house the way Antonia's runs the Caelian — by sections, lit from the back — and the knowledge she dropped into the conversation about oysters at exactly the depth a well requires to be sounded. Lentulus brought the name, the masks, the Moesia shoulder, and a locked room whose existence he announced, as men do, by walking around it for nineteen years. They became, in time, the thing neither of their families had itemised in the contracts: allies. Alliance is not the word the poets dine out on. It outlasts most of the words they do.

There were children in the order the contracts hoped for, which Rome calls Fortune and households call management. Marcus first, loud as a gate. A daughter between who lived nine days and is spoken of in this family, if at all, in the grammar people use for weather in previous consulships — Lentulus, holding the nine-day girl on the last morning with a competence that astonished the women, whose professional opinion of senators' hands was formed elsewhere, understood for the first time the true exchange rate of the sentence his father had sold him at twenty-four: *nothing living with a claim on this house*. Nothing living was doing heavy work in that sentence, like a slave carrying water in a pleasure house. He paid the midwife — a Roman one, unhennaed, expensive — three times her fee and told the steward to record it as household linen. Servilia, coming off the childbed into a house performing a grief it was not certain it had budgeted for, looked at the accounts a month later and said nothing about the linen. It is one of perhaps six sentences of hers he has carried unspent. It had no words in it. That was the denomination.

Cornelia came three years later and stayed, with the air of a person who has reviewed the house and decided to improve it from inside, at the pace of music. By then Lentulus was on the ladder his name had built: quaestor's accounts audited like sieges, a praetorship, the consulship (suffect, the kind history pronounces with a footnote, which his father received with a toast to *the family's momentum*, as if they were a stone going downhill in good order). He governed nothing larger than his opinions in those years and governed them in public with distinction. The locked room furnished his speeches the way cellars furnish dinners: nothing was served from it directly; everything tasted of storage. When the Senate debated the exposure of infants on the hillsides — a practice every man in the chamber deplored in the manner of men deploring rain from inside good roofs — Lentulus heard his own voice arrive before his judgment, hot as a boy's, and watched the benches register *a Cornelius with a conscience*, a phrase that would follow him to the committee, to the baths, to a Greek banker's back room. When the freedwomen's tax was last revised he spoke for putting the collectors under the aediles instead of farming them out to private appetites, and carried it, and came home and drank one cup more than his rule and stood among the masks telling himself — the house has the record of it in the wear on one particular floor tile — that a man may settle a debt in general legislation if the currency is chosen well enough. His father would have agreed. That was the part that kept him standing there.

He saw Antonia twice in those years, before the Caelian house made seeing her a municipal question. Once in Naples, on business of an estate inspection: a woman at a banker's table in a widow's office, no longer carrying anything, signing with the hand he had — the locked room again; the laugh; the stylus — taught its letters, now filing a widow's loans with a competence that made the banker sit like a client. He was conducted past her room on other business. Neither of them was in a position to make it an event, and the city, which appreciates economy, recorded none: a senator looking at paperwork, a freedwoman being paperwork, a corridor functioning. The second time was Rome, the Subura, years later, rain: a dining room's doorway, lamplight, a woman sending a carter back into the street with an amphora in each hand and a sentence that had the whole street laughing with her and at nobody. He sat in his chair unrecognised behind wet leather and did not get out. He has since decided — committees are how his family thinks — that there was no decision to make, both times. A man's life arrives in the order of the evening, not the order of the questions. He did not, until the tablet, permit himself to itemise whose evening it had been.

His father died consul and honoured. The will, when it was read among the masks, contained the usual bequests and one unusual one: a sealed tablet for Gnaeus's eyes, which Lentulus, being his father's son in the matter of locked rooms, has kept sealed for nine years in the cupboard under his grandfather's mask. He tells himself it concerns the estates. He does not open it for the same reason Antonia does not count Prima's years: some arithmetic, once performed, must be acted on, and he has Syria in prospect, and a daughter to marry, and a committee proceeding against a house on the Caelian whose mistress taught him, though she would laugh to hear it, everything he knows about the difference between what a thing is called and what it is.

You tell yourself the tablet is about olive groves. You have always been good at telling yourself things in the tone of a committee, as if a minuted untruth were sturdier than a spoken one. The masks look down. Wax, smoke, paint. Every one of them a man who locked a room and called the house honest.
