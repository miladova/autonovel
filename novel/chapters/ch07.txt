Paper

Diomedes keeps three sets of books in three different hands, none of them his own. It is not dishonesty; it is calligraphy in the service of survival. There is the book for the tax office, upright and dull. There is the book for the house, in which the pastry cook's saffron and the enlargement of the kitchen stove stand in their true relations. And there is the book — tablets, actually, wax renewed each quarter so that some kinds of history melt as a matter of policy — in which Antonia's money goes out into Rome in the evening clothes of other men's names and comes home in the morning with interest.

The audit happens on the grey mornings, before the house has put its face on. Diomedes comes with the tablets under one arm and a stylus behind his ear — the stylus only; he has never in eleven years been known to put anything else there, having observed, early in their acquaintance, what Antonia does with remarks about it — and he reads the quarter back to her the way a priest reads entrails: without inflection, so that the future cannot be accused of being his idea.

The tax book first, because Rome comes first even in her kitchen. Registrations renewed. The aediles' compliment on her paperwork entered, deadpan, in the margin where Diomedes keeps remarks the tax office is welcome to read and unlikely to survive understanding. Wine licence. Lamp oil, a continuing scandal. The inspector's visit has left, as such visits do, a small fiscal bruise: a fee for re-inspection, a fee for the certificate attesting that the fee for re-inspection was paid, and a third sum, unlabelled in the upright hand, which Antonia eyes and Diomedes pretends is a smudge.

“The smudge is new,” she says.

“The smudge has a cousin in the aediles' office. It keeps the doorway to a width the committee can live with.”

She lets it stand. A house like hers pays for its drains twice: once to the men who dig them and once to the men who certify, in washed Latin, that the digging was decent. She has paid both since the two-table years and has the receipts in three hands. It is the undistinguished, quarterly heroism of her life, and nobody will ever carve it spiralling anywhere.

The house book next, and with it the empire in its true borders. Ammonius' saffron, defended like a frontier. The kitchen stove, enlarged in the autumn, now paying for itself in the rapid alkalis of banquet cookery — Diomedes has a column for this; Diomedes has a column for everything except, famously, the things he writes nothing down about. Daphne's contract at the new rate, Philomela's strongbox with the Greek, a payment for a midwife in the Subura entered as *linen, extra*, a payment to a lamp-seller's daughter entered as *oil, festival*, the whole female economy of her protection racket against Rome going past in code, at a walking pace, while the pastry cook argues with somebody's nephew in the yard about the density of almonds.

“You are aware,” Diomedes says, “that *linen, extra* now exceeds the house's actual linen.”

“I am aware that if the tax office ever learns to read women, Diomedes, we shall all be registered as linen. Proceed.”

Then the third book, which is not a book. The wax comes out of its cedar box with a smell of forests and old evenings. Here her money walks about the city in other men's names: a share of a grain barge, prudently sunk on paper while it floats in fact; a loan to a silversmith secured on plate the silversmith's wife believes is in Ostia; a mortgage on a dyer's vats in the name of a freedman who owes Antonia his freedom's paperwork and repays it in invisibility. The sums are good. The sums, this quarter, are better than good, the committee's cold wind having driven half the younger senatorial order indoors to borrow discreetly against expectations. War, she has observed before, is not the only thing that is good for business. Shame is excellent for it, provided the paperwork is sympathetic.

“The Greek sends a compliment,” Diomedes says, in the voice he keeps for weather in other countries. “He says madam's portfolio is the only one in his office that improves when Rome behaves badly. He says, if I may render the Greek, that you collect on the city's *conscience*.”

“The Greek flatters his depositors in their own languages. It is why his office is so quiet.” But she is listening. The Greek at the Basilica — Eutyches, which nobody calls him; Rome calls its bankers by their function the way it calls its emperors by their building programmes — has handled her serious money for eight years. He took it at first through a freedman's name, sniffed it like a coin, and within a year was suggesting, in the silences between items, that madam might prefer to hold certain paper in a form even her own bookkeeper should have to work for. It was Diomedes who worked for it, for three evenings, across the quarter's wax, following a Campanian estate's paper as it changed hands the way weather changes armies: from a dead man's executors to a syndicate, from the syndicate's patience to the Greek's strongbox, and from the strongbox, in slices, out again into the evening clothes of investors who must not be seen holding a senator's vintage.

One of the slices is hers. It became hers in the ordinary way — the Greek offered, Diomedes priced, the wax recorded — and it sat in the third set of books for a full quarter as money without a face, earning like furniture. Its identifying marks, estate agent's name, olive country, the shape of the surety, were visible to anyone who read the line whole. That it took Diomedes three evenings to read the line whole is, Antonia understands as he lays the tablet down, the real item in this morning's audit. He did not miss it. He followed it, the way he follows everything with her name hidden in it, to the bottom of the page, and then he decided, on his own authority, in the freemasonry of long service, to confirm it twice before he brought it up the stairs to a woman dressing her hair.

“You might,” Antonia says at last, “have brought it to me in the first quarter.”

“I might have brought madam a rumour in the first quarter. In the second I bring her a fact with the Greek's hand on it and the agent's seal under it.” He turns the tablet so that she can see, not the sum — she never looks at sums first; Diomedes taught her, or she taught Diomedes, it is no longer minuted which — but the names. “Money without a face I manage, madam. This has arrived at a face. It is the rule madam taught me.”

He looks at her hands, as clerks do, to see if she is ready to hold a stylus or only to listen. Her hands are in her hair. The damp on the courtyard wall has dried since January into the ghost of its Spanish map. Outside, the house is negotiating with a carter, and somewhere across the river a girl is counting stitches with her mouth open, neither of which facts is in any of the three books, in any of the three hands.

“You hold paper,” Diomedes says, on a grey morning when the damp has come through the courtyard wall in the shape, Eirene says, of Spain, “on the agent of an estate in Campania. It came to us through the Greek at the Basilica. It is good paper. Land, olives, a family's good name standing behind it like a guarantor at a wedding.”

Antonia is dressing her hair, which she still does herself on principle, another relic: a hairdresser's daughter does not sit idle in that particular chair for any woman living. “Whose good name?”

Diomedes, who can usually deliver any sentence as if it were a weather report, looks at the tablet as if it might revise itself. “The Cornelii Lentuli.”

The pins go in. One, two. Her hands do not ask her permission. “How much of it?”

“Enough that if we called it in the autumn, the olives would be ours by spring. We will not call it. The Greek advises holding. The family will find money — a marriage, an appointment. Families like that always find money eventually; it is lying around in their past like furniture.”

“And if the family does not find money? If the appointment — let us say a province, let us say the east — goes to another name because this name has become, through its own committee work, untidy?”

“Then the paper becomes land. Or it becomes an embarrassment we can sell to someone who collects embarrassments.” Diomedes closes the tablet. “I am telling you because you taught me to tell you everything with a face on it. Money without a face I manage. This has a face.”

Antonia looks at herself in the bronze mirror: a woman of thirty-eight with her hair going up correctly and a creditor's knowledge arriving, as the best weapons do, by post, unrequested, already paid for. For twenty years she has imagined — on stairs, in bad winters, in the years when the dining rooms were two tables and a debt to a lamp-seller — the instrument of revenge as something she would have to forge: a letter, a witness, a scene in a basilica with her voice carrying like a trumpet. Rome, being Rome, has delivered it instead as an investment. Lentulus is, through agents and Greeks and the sweet anonymity of paper, in debt to Antonia Cassia. His olives ripen toward her autumn. His son drinks in her house. His committee inspects her drains. The city has tied them together with its favourite knot, the one that looks like business and tightens like fate.

Eirene hears about it by noon, which is Diomedes' way of telling no one. She comes into the small dining room while Antonia is tasting a sauce that Ammonius has rebuilt for the second time that week out of spite and genius, and stands with a bowl of olives she has entirely forgotten to put down.

“His olives,” Eirene says. “In the ground. Ripening toward your autumn. Antonia. If a slave in the Subura house had prophesied that when we were seventeen, you'd have had her whipped for poetry.”

“I was nobody in the Subura house. I held mirrors. Poetry was above stairs.” Antonia sends the sauce back with a message consisting of one word, which the kitchen boy delivers as he delivers all such messages: at a run, for the sport of the return journey. “And they are not his olives. They are his grandfather's olives, technically. The Cornelii have always been technicians. It is why their debts are so well drafted.”

Eirene sets the bowl down at last and eats an olive out of it, house privilege, thirty years in the accruing. “What will you do?”

It is the question. It has been the question since the mirror, going round under the morning's work like a song under a hallway: her singer's phrase, Daphne's disease, the tune under every room today. What she will do with it. The menu of doings is long and Diomedes has, tactfully, already priced it in the margins of his own mind. She could call the paper in the autumn, before the vintage, in a message routed through the Greek so deniable that Lentulus would learn of his creditor the way Rome learns of everything: in the baths, unattributed, at the exact moment it becomes unusable. She could sell the paper on, to a collector of embarrassments — there are two at the Basilica, Diomedes says, and a third in the Senate who collects nothing else and calls it public life. She could let it be known, in the invisible denominations, that the committee's chairman is mortgaged to the woman his committee inspected at dawn, and then watch Syria subside, in Acilius' phrase, like steam.

Or she could hold it. Holding is also a doing. Holding is the doing that women in her family have always done with the valuable and the unbearable alike: mirrors steady, ledgers closed, children — no. The thought is put down where the first catastrophe lives, uncounted. Hold the paper; take the interest quarterly; let his olives ripen toward her autumn and his marriage settlement ripen toward his; say nothing to the Greek, who will know anyway in his Greek's way, the way bankers know weather; and keep, in the box between the quarters, beside an unsigned note that taught her a phrase, the knowledge that once — for one quarter of one year, from a grey morning when the damp came through her wall in the shape of Spain — the arithmetic of twenty-one years balanced itself on paper, without her having to lift anything heavier than a stylus.

“I have imagined it wrong for twenty years,” Antonia says, and is surprised to say it aloud, in a dining room, over olives. Eirene has that effect: thirty years of locked rooms in common will make a corridor of any woman. “I imagined a scene. A basilica, a letter. My voice carrying.” She looks at her hands, a hairdresser's daughter's hands, at thirty-eight still doing everything before she has decided. “I never imagined it arriving as *yield*.”

“Rome,” Eirene says, “is bringing everything to you as business these days. It is the house style. You invented it, love. In Baiae.” She says the place-name the way they both do: like a word in another language that they are, for professional reasons, fluent in and will not translate in front of the flowers. “If you call it in, you will get olives and a story. If you hold it, you will get interest and a longer story. I note, as your junior partner in catastrophe, that this house has always collected the longer story at the shorter rates.”

“The story has a girl in it across the river who counts,” Antonia says, so quietly that the room has to decide whether it heard. “Stories with girls in, Eirene, are not to be spent at the Greek's table. That is the whole of my policy. It is the whole of my religion, second service.”

Eirene does not refill the silence. She has kept Antonia's books in ways Diomedes never sees — the fourth set, unwritten, in the freemasonry of two women who were once entered, in a dead man's property, in the same column. After a moment she picks up the olives, remembers they are for a table, and goes to set them where guests will presently eat them without the slightest idea that they are dipping into a secured loan. It pleases her. Eirene's pleasures at forty are principally structural.

What Antonia does not say, then or later, to Eirene or to the mirror or to the third book's melting wax, is this: that holding the paper is the first revenge she has ever been offered that does not require anybody across a river to pay for it. The basilica scene, in the form she imagined it at seventeen on the stairs, had a cost column too, and the cost was never going to be billed to a Cornelius. Rome bills its scenes to women; it is a tax she has paid quarterly and knows by heart. Paper bills nobody. Paper just sits in a Greek's strongbox earning like furniture, while a man in the Senate discovers — at his own pace, in his own committees, with his wife scheduling the discovery between the oysters and the aqueducts — the exact rate of interest on a sentence accepted at twenty-four.

“Hold it,” she says to Diomedes, when he comes back for the tablets, in the level voice of the first book, upright and dull. And then, because the room is empty except for a bowl of olives with one missing and a clerk who has brought her a fact with a face on it across three evenings, she adds the rider that belongs in no book in any hand: “And if the olives do come to us in the autumn, Diomedes — I will not have them pressed in this house. Sell the oil forward. A woman's kitchen should not have to render her own metaphors.”

Diomedes bows over a joke that is not one and goes to write *hold* in a hand the Greek will read as investment advice.

“Hold it,” she says. “Say nothing to the Greek. And Diomedes—”

“Madam.”

“Find out what a fuller in Transtiberim named Vibius paid for his freedom, and when, and whether his household, nineteen or twenty years ago, received — a consideration. From Baiae. Discreetly is not the word. Find out as if you were buying thread.”

Diomedes is still for exactly one breath. He has kept her books for eleven years. He knows which questions have faces on them. “Thread,” he says, and writes nothing down, which in his system is the most serious form of record.

In the Basilica Julia, where the money-changers have set up their tables in the same relation to the law courts that remoras bear to sharks, Lentulus instructs his own agent in the music of delay. The banker — the same Greek, though Lentulus does not know the Greek's other client, because the Greek's whole fortune rests on no client knowing any other — is to be assured of the Syrian appointment as if it were a fact slightly too delicate for daylight. The Avidian negotiation is to be mentioned as all but concluded. A payment signifying intention will be made at the vintage. The agent, who has carried this family's intentions downhill for a decade, bows under the weight of them and goes to perform confidence in Greek.

The agent is called Soter. He is fifty, Greek in the way that has stopped being a nationality and become a profession, and he has carried the Cornelii's Campanian business since the old Gnaeus was alive to seal tablets and settle families. In the Basilica's crowd he does a thing that Lentulus, watching him arrive, has always found faintly shameful and wholly impressive: he becomes, without changing his clothes or his accent, exactly as Roman as the room requires and no more. To the money-changers he is a colleague. To the advocates wrangling in the aisles he is a client. To the Greek behind the cedar door he will shortly be, there is no other phrase for it, a remora's remora, feeding off a shark that feeds off the law.

“You are thin,” Lentulus says, because greeting a man of Soter's years with his health is the down payment on asking him to lie well.

“It is the season, dominus. Everyone's paper is thin before the vintage.” Soter looks over the hall with the eyes of a man reading weather off other men's shoulders. Two rows of tables away a young equestrian is weeping, quietly and with great self-control, into a contract; a money-changer is patting his hand with an intimacy that suggests the weeping, too, has been costed. “The Greek asks whether the payment at the vintage will *signify*.”

“The word I used?”

“The words we use travel, dominus. They are the only luggage that is never searched.” Soter says it without knowing he is quoting, at one remove and in a minor key, the doctrine of a house on the Caelian; the city is a single plumbing system, and ideas, like debts, arrive at addresses their authors never wrote. “He would prefer a sum. He has a Greek's superstition that sums signify intentions better than the word *intention*.”

Lentulus instructs him — the music of delay, orchestrated now at speaking length. Syria to be mentioned as a climate, not a claim. The Avidian match as a harbour in sight, with the pilot boat out. A payment at the vintage of a size Soter is to imply the Greek has already agreed, since nothing recruits a banker's agreement like the news that he has given it. Soter listens with his whole posture, a man loading cargo he expects to redistribute at sea.

“There is a question the Greek did not ask,” he says at length, “which in his office is the expensive kind.”

“Ask it for him. He charges me enough for the mail.”

“Whether dominus's committee will be *noisy*.” Soter lets the word stand in the Basilica's din like a cup set down carefully. “Paper dislikes noise, dominus. It is not a moral position. My cousin keeps the books for two of the syndicate that hold —” he corrects himself with the smoothness of a man stepping over a puddle he himself pointed out “— that hold an interest in the neighbourhood of dominus's estates. They read the notices of inspections the way farmers read clouds. A senator making weather over registered houses is, pardon the Greek, a change in the climate of his own security.”

It lands, as things in the Basilica do, without ever being formally filed. Lentulus looks at his agent of ten years and sees, for a clear instant, the whole architecture from underneath: himself on the Senate benches drafting morality in period sentences, while below, in the money-changers' shade, his family's olives support a structure of paper held by men who price his speeches the way captains price a sky. He cannot tell Soter that the committee is personal. He cannot, either, any longer, quite tell himself it is policy. Both sentences are available, in the washed Latin his father issued him. Neither fits through the cedar door behind which the Greek is presently being Eutyches to nobody.

“Tell the Greek,” he says at last, “that the committee will report generally. Twenty houses. A registry. It will be as interesting as a tax return, and for the same reason: it will be about everyone, in figures, with the names filed where figures keep them.”

Soter bows. “Figures,” he says, “the Greek will accept. They are his poetry, dominus.” He does not know — the plumbing again, going uphill both ways across the city — that he has just returned, to the man it was invented about, a sentence minted at a grain merchant's dinner table and already spent by a woman over rebuilt fish sauce. He goes through the cedar door to perform confidence in Greek, and Lentulus is left in the Basilica's huge, echoing shade with the sensation he has had since the baths: of being read, accurately and in advance, by people whose books he is not permitted to see.

He does not go home by the short way. Senators of his order are supposed to be carried over their thoughts, in litters, like despatches; but it is a bright morning going grey at the edges, the kind that makes Rome look temporarily like a place where the past has been done and can now be walked through like a building. He walks. Clients find him even so — a former officer with a pension file, a freedwoman with a petition folded to the size of embarrassment who has clearly been told that this is the senator with the freedwoman's conscience, the secretaries' joke, working its way down to street level like a small coin through a market. He takes the petition himself, to Onesimus' visible filing of the irregularity. The woman's name is on it in a scribe's hand; under it, in pencil the Romans do not have and therefore in charcoal, which they do, she has made her mark: a cross, carefully, like a stitch counted twice.

“What does it ask?” he says, though he can read, because the forms require that the powerful be told things aloud by the powerless until both parties believe in the acoustics.

“That my late master’s son return my savings, dominus. He says they were the house's. The manumission says the savings were mine. Both papers are in Greek, dominus. I am in Latin.”

She says it without tears, with the humour of the street filing itself exactly. Lentulus stands in the Forum of Caesar with a freedwoman's charcoal cross under his thumb and the emperor's stone drums groaning toward their spiral behind her, and finds he has run out of committee language in the open air. “The Basilica will hear it,” he says. “I will see that it is heard by men who read Greek *and* Latin. It is the least the building can do. It has libraries in both, facing each other, like married people.”

She bows as if a sum has been named, which in the currency that matters for the walk home, it has. Onesimus takes the petition and forgets it professionally into the family's real book, where a clerk at a side table writes *who came, what they asked, what it will cost when it comes back* — and everything comes back. Lentulus watches the column stone inch past on its rollers and thinks, with the part of him the Basilica has just read from underneath, that a man's name is spiral-cut exactly as deep as the debts he acknowledges, and no deeper, whatever the drums cost.

Walking home through the Forum of Caesar, Lentulus is overtaken by the emperor's works traffic: stone for the great column, blocks groaning on rollers, each drum already carved inside with its stair. A foreman tells the senator, unasked — the city tells senators things unasked; it is a tax on the broad stripe — that when it rises a man will be able to climb inside the war and come out on top of it. Lentulus stands a moment watching other men's history inch past on rollers. He thinks of his own name, spiral-cut somewhere between offices and olives, readable only from a distance no living Cornelius will ever stand at.

At home, Servilia has a letter from her brother about Cornelia and a face like a prepared speech. “The Avidii have named a figure.”

“And?”

“It is a figure that pays the Greek and re-ropes the Palatine roof and allows your son to be twenty for two more years without consequences. In exchange they ask that the wedding be before the vintage, and that you speak for the father's petition about the harbour dues, and — this is the interesting clause, Lentulus, attend to it — that this family not be *in the mouths of the city* between now and the ceremony. Avidius used those words. He has bought his way out of the docks. He will not buy his way into a farce.”

“So my committee must conclude.”

“Your committee must conclude, your son must be invisible, and you must be a wall. Not a window.” She hands him her brother's letter like a verdict she has already appealed in private. “I do not say take them. I say this is the price of refusing them, itemised. You may now perform the Cornelian tradition of refusing at length and in excellent Latin, and in the autumn the Greek will perform the Greek tradition of owning our olives.”

He reads the letter. The figures are what figures are: an argument that has stopped pretending to be anything else. Somewhere in the house Cornelia is at her lesson; he can hear the lyre being corrected, note by note, by a man forbidden to touch her wrist. The sound comes through the walls the way light comes through shutters, in stripes. He thinks of the sealed tablet under his grandfather's mask, and of the possibility — he has never permitted it a whole thought; it arrives in stripes too — that what his father sealed was not about land at all, and that a man can inherit a debt that does not appear in any Greek's ledger because it is denominated in a currency bankers fear to touch.

Servilia does not wait for his answer to begin hers. This is a marriage's constitutional arrangement, arrived at over nineteen years and never minuted: he composes positions; she composes the correspondence by which positions discover they were decisions. She sits at the little table where the household accounts are reconciled — a table with more real power over Campanian olives than any bench in the Senate — and drafts to her brother in a hand that makes even refusals look like bookkeeping.

“Read it to me,” Lentulus says, from the window, where the light is failing in stripes.

“'*We are sensible of the honour. The figure is a serious one, as a figure should be between houses who intend to be one. Gnaeus will wish to give his answer in person, as the connexion deserves; the vintage is a husbandman's deadline, not a father's, but we see no cause why goodwill should wait on grapes.*'” She lays the stylus down in exact alignment with the tablet's edge, an act of government over a province still obeying her. “It agrees to everything except agreeing. Avidius will read it as a yes with manners. My brother will read it as a family keeping its price off the record. You may read it as a wall.”

“And Cornelia reads it as?”

“Cornelia does not read her own file, Lentulus. She sings in it.”

From beyond the garden wall, as if the house were cueing her, the lyre begins again: the lesson's second hour, the master's metaphors arriving fainter with the light. The figure corrects itself, begins again — the second way. She always takes the second way now. Servilia listens with her head a fraction turned, the way she listened, at the dedication, to an aunt itemising a marriage, and says, in the neutral voice she uses for provincial appointments and family earthquakes, “She asked me yesterday whether the Avidii keep musicians.”

Lentulus looks at her. “What did you tell her?”

“That ship-owners keep whatever a wife insists is ballast.” Servilia's face does its thorough nothing. “She said, 'Then I shall insist loudly.' Your daughter, husband, is composing her instructions to be disobeyed in. One does what one can with the material. You come of a family that chose its currencies for three hundred years. Perhaps she will be the generation that changes the money.”

It is as close to tenderness as this room permits before dinner. Outside, the household is assembling the evening in its order: clients' leavings cleared, the day-book shut with its futures inside, Marcus's absence reported in the formula — *the young master is at the baths*, a sentence which in this house now functions the way *a payment signifying intention* functions at the Basilica, as a climate rather than a fact. Servilia has already instructed the steward that if the young master is at the baths after dark for the third evening this week, the baths are to be sent, with the family's compliments, an invoice for his board. The steward, who is paid prospectively and in kindness, received this wit in complete silence and has docketed it, somewhere in the family's real epic, under *the mistress knows*.

“She knows about the Caelian evenings as she knows about the harbour dues,” Lentulus says, to the failing light, because there are sentences a man can only say to a window in his own house. “In outline. With the costings.”

“Cornelia,” Servilia says, “knows what every girl in a great house knows: the acoustics. We raise them inside the walls where men compose their intentions aloud, and then we are surprised at the quality of their information.” She sands the letter — an old-fashioned touch; Servilia distrusts modern blotting the way her husband distrusts the word *steady* for horses — and holds it a moment before sealing, weighing, as she weighs everything, the difference between what a thing is called and what it is. “Seal it tonight and you are a father accepting a figure. Seal it after the committee and you are a senator accepting a family. The Avidii will pay the same either way. That is the interesting clause, Lentulus. It is always the interesting clause: the sum is fixed; only the wording is still ours to spend.”

The lyre stops. A door in the garden side of the house performs its small announcement. Cornelia crosses the far end of the room's sightline with her music held like something rescued, not looking at her parents in business hours, per the fortifications — and her father, with his agent's warnings, his Greek's unasked questions and his olives ripening toward somebody's autumn all standing in him like clients in an atrium, does the most un-Cornelian thing available to him at the end of such a day: he leaves the letter unsealed on his wife's table, between the quarters of the household's accounts, and goes to stand for a moment where the music was.

“Tell your brother,” he says, “that we are honoured, and that I will give Avidius my answer after the committee reports.”

“When does the committee report?”

“The committee,” Lentulus says, with the exhausted accuracy of a man reading his own inscription, “reports when I tell it to. That has become, I gather, the problem.”
