The Clause
The registry passes into the city's habits the way weather does: complained about, evaded at the margins, priced into everything by the second quarter. Antonia files her house — the upright hand, the fees on the day, the women’s legal conditions entered like a roll of honour, which in the book's intention they are — and discovers, as she has discovered every regulation aimed at her survival since Baiae, that compliance, pursued to the point of theatre, is the best camouflage a working house can wear. The aediles’ inspector returns in the spring quarter, by appointment now, and is given the pastry cook's almond cakes as a matter of administrative routine. He measures no doorway. He asks after the new stove. Rome has decided that the house on the Caelian is an institution, and Rome protects its institutions with the same energy it denies its people: totally, and without apology.

What Rome has not entirely decided is what to do with the clause. *No house to profit by a person whose refusal has no legal existence.* It was drafted, if the city only knew it, at dawn by a barber's client in a sentence — the single fully honest sentence, Antonia privately believes, of Lentulus's public career — and it passes through the courts that spring and summer like a coin through a market: cited by an advocate here, *distinguished* (that advocate’s word for buried standing up) by a praetor there, ending up, as such coin does, in the purse of a freedwoman of the Subura who uses it, in September, to walk out of a lodging house near the Carinae on its proprietor, a man who had held her manumission money — *against breakages*, as his paperwork put it — for six years. She walks out with her papers, her savings and a copy of the clause made by a scribe who charges her half, the revolution coming as it does in Rome not with trumpets but with discounts.

Daphne hears the story at the baths and tells it in the small dining room with a singer's instinct for the final note. “She walked past him, they say, holding the paper like a torch.”

“Paper burns,” Antonia says. “That is why we also keep the Greek.” — meaning the banker, meaning the copies, meaning Diomedes three hands, meaning everything she has learned about survival being a filing system with a soul. She looks at Daphne, who is twenty-two and free three years and holding, these days, real stones of modest size purchased with her own fees and kept, by house advice, in the Greek's strongbox against a bad winter. “Your contract here comes up at the new year.”

“I know the date. Singers count bars.”

“Renew it at the new rate, with the clause cited in your own hand at the foot.” Antonia pushes across a tablet on which Diomedes — who has aged, this year, a full decade in the eyes, as people do who keep other people's centuries in thread drawers — has written the terms in the hand reserved for things that must look boring to survive. “Every woman in this house. One by one. If Rome is going to register us, it will register *that* in this house first.”

Daphne reads. The room is quiet. The house beyond performs its evening overture: water, linen, the pastry cook whose saffron has this week been vindicated by two separate Alexandrians. “He wrote this,” Daphne says finally. “The senator. In the committee that was supposed to close us.”

“He wrote a sentence. What it closes and opens is our business.” Antonia stands; the evening requires her in three rooms at once and she will accomplish it, as ever, by being fully in one at a time and letting the house's memory do the rest. “Sing the Greek tonight, Daphne. For people who know. I am told there are more of them every quarter.”

In the Forum the column has cleared the rooftops. Tourists from the provinces stand in its scaffolding's shadow with their heads back and their mouths open, counting, exactly as Prima counts stitches. Lentulus passes it on his way from a committee — a real committee now, on aqueducts, which flows through his remaining public life at a respectable rate — and a foreman he half-knows tells him the inside stair is continuous to the top now: a man can climb inside the war, when it is dedicated, and come out above it.

He walks home the long way, past the Greek's office, where he does not call; past the Basilica, where his agent is performing confidence in two languages; up the Palatine in the cooling light, a senator of ancient lineage and diminished prospects whose name the baths now pronounce in the past tense of Syria and the present tense of one clause that law students have begun, this term, to be set to argue about. Acilius has written that Hadrian, from Athens, inquired of the clause's draftsman — *a Cornelius with a freedwoman's conscience*, the secretaries are saying, which is the kind of joke that gets a man remembered in the wrong way by the right people, or the other way, in reigns to come.

At home there is a letter half-written on his desk in Marcus's hand — Greek, terrible, sincere; his son leaves drafts like flags — addressed across the river. Lentulus does not read it. He puts a fresh sheet over it, weighs it with the dented mirror, and goes to stand a moment among the masks in the last of the light.

*Choose better,* his father wrote, dying, in the hand that gathered itself at the ends of lines.

“I chose worse for thirty years at the full rate,” Lentulus tells the wax, conversationally, as a man settles with his creditors in private. “The interest is the estate now. I am merely keeping it lit.” The masks say nothing. They have been saying it, eloquently, for three hundred years.
