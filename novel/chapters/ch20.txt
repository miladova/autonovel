The Clause

The registry passes into the city's habits the way weather does: complained about, evaded at the margins, priced into everything by the second quarter. Antonia files her house — the upright hand, the fees on the day, the women’s legal conditions entered like a roll of honour, which in the book's intention they are — and discovers, as she has discovered every regulation aimed at her survival since Baiae, that compliance, pursued to the point of theatre, is the best camouflage a working house can wear. The aediles’ inspector returns in the spring quarter, by appointment now, and is given the pastry cook's almond cakes as a matter of administrative routine. He measures no doorway. He asks after the new stove. Rome has decided that the house on the Caelian is an institution, and Rome protects its institutions with the same energy it denies its people: totally, and without apology.

The filing itself takes a whole morning and two excursions of Diomedes into official weather. The registry office has acquired what new Roman institutions acquire before wisdom: a queue, a smell of damp cedar, and three clerks whose understandings of the same form differ sufficiently to constitute a mystery religion. Diomedes arrives with duplicate rolls, triplicate patience and a household schedule so neatly ordered that the chief clerk looks faintly disappointed, as though propriety had deprived him of sport.

He is a freedman himself, this clerk, with ink permanently in the second knuckle of his right hand. He reads the women’s conditions not as a prurient but as a man testing a bridge before traffic. Names, status, fees retained, rights of departure, savings held where, by whom, under what seal. At one point he taps the clause at the foot of Antonia’s house rules. “You cite it before the courts have settled its width.”

“Madam likes a door measured before someone else brings a ram,” Diomedes says.

“It may be distinguished into a thread.”

“Threads are also entered separately.”

The clerk looks up. For an instant the office contains neither queue nor cedar nor the great republic of euphemism, only two men who have made lives out of other people’s documents. He stamps the roll. “Then let the record show a thread where it can be found.”

At the Caelian, compliance is staged with the luxury of a house that intends to make inspection boring. The strongbox schedule is not shown—some rooms, as the Greek has taught everyone, must remain smaller truths—but copies exist in hands that can appear at an office before rumour has finished putting on its sandals. Barbatus is instructed which visitors constitute patrons for the doorway measurements and which are merely weather in boots. The women are briefed by Eirene in the courtyard, with bread, on what an inspector may ask and what he may be told to find in the roll.

“Are we to say we are happy?” asks one of the newer girls, who is not a girl in any but the city’s lazy vocabulary and has a singing voice with an adult’s judgement already installed.

“You are to say what is in your contract,” Eirene replies. “Happiness is not a taxable condition. If he wants a poem, he can pay Daphne.”

The inspector arrives in spring by appointment, a development which strikes him, visibly, as a decline in the romance of office. He is younger than in winter, or daylight is less forgiving to the fiction of experience. He checks the tax receipts. He looks at the new stove with the reverence of a man whose own kitchen remains a scene of marital negotiations. He asks whether the price of almonds is really what his wife claims and receives a briefing from Ammonius so complete that forms are signed as an act of escape.

In passing the small dining room, he hears rehearsal: Daphne, one phrase, over and over, each repetition less decorated than the last. He pauses. “Greek?”

“Music,” Diomedes says.

The state, having been given cakes, accurate figures and a door held by a former gladiator who could fold its representative into a petition, departs reassured. Rome is good at this form of transcendence. If a thing is entered in enough books, it ceases to be scandal and becomes urban furniture.

Antonia watches him go from the upper landing. She does not exult. Exultation is for people who believe offices stay bought. She has only made the house legible on terms she helped choose. Legibility is not safety. It is a better kind of exposure.

What Rome has not entirely decided is what to do with the clause. *No house to profit by a person whose refusal has no legal existence.* It was drafted, if the city only knew it, at dawn by a barber's client in a sentence — the single fully honest sentence, Antonia privately believes, of Lentulus's public career — and it passes through the courts that spring and summer like a coin through a market: cited by an advocate here, *distinguished* (that advocate’s word for buried standing up) by a praetor there, ending up, as such coin does, in the purse of a freedwoman of the Subura who uses it, in September, to walk out of a lodging house near the Carinae on its proprietor, a man who had held her manumission money — *against breakages*, as his paperwork put it — for six years. She walks out with her papers, her savings and a copy of the clause made by a scribe who charges her half, the revolution coming as it does in Rome not with trumpets but with discounts.

The first advocate to make serious money out of the clause does so by losing with it beautifully. His client is not romantic material for law students: a middle-aged bath attendant who claims that his former owner has kept back the savings accumulated toward his freedom after freedom itself had been granted in a will. The praetor distinguishes, narrows and, in the great legal style, applauds the principle while declining to let it interfere with lunch. Still, the phrase enters circulation. Men begin to quote it in porticoes with the inaccurate confidence of people fresh from hearing a song once.

A copyist near the Basilica starts selling a neat extract: the clause, three lines of commentary and a warning that commentary is not counsel. The warning costs extra. Students buy it. A freedwoman who sells ribbons buys it and has it read to her by her brother. A lodging-house keeper buys it to discover whether it can be made to mean the opposite and comes away disappointed in both law and punctuation.

The woman near the Carinae is called Saufeia—no, *Saufia* in her own pronunciation, which the documents repeatedly overrule. She has worked six years in rooms let by the man who once bought her freedom as an investment and then developed a proprietary interest in defining what freedom might reasonably require. Her savings remain in his chest *against breakages*. There have been no breakages. There have been deductions for towels, for lamp oil, for a cracked basin owned jointly by fate and the house. What begins as a dispute over money becomes, when she tries to take other work, a dispute over whether her refusal to remain has any civic substance beyond a noise a creditor can choose to hear.

She goes first to a patron’s clerk, who advises patience as men advise weather to a roof. Then to a scribe, who copies the clause at half price because his sister once worked in a house where accounts were kept *for* women in the way traps are dug *for* deer. Then to a young advocate with more appetite than clients. He reads the clause aloud, slowly, in front of the lodging-house door, with two witnesses he has borrowed from a bread queue.

The proprietor says, “She owes.”

“Itemise,” says the advocate, who has discovered the revolutionary power of asking men to put their habits into columns.

There is an hour of it, at street level, without swords. A towel that was already torn. Oil consumed in the course of business. A basin whose crack predates the reign. Neighbours gather with the moral seriousness Romans bring to any entertainment not requiring a ticket. The chest is opened because not opening it has become more expensive in reputation. Saufia’s papers come out. Her savings come out, diminished but no longer metaphysical.

She does not process in triumph. She ties her bundle twice because her hands are shaking and asks the bread-queue witnesses whether one of them will walk with her as far as her cousin’s street. Only afterward, in baths and bakeries and the coppery afterlife of a story improving by handling, does the paper become a torch. The reality is less flammable and more useful: a woman crosses a threshold carrying her own documents, and a man behind her discovers that the word *breakages* has lost its magic because other people have read it in daylight.

By September, three other cases have cited the clause, two successfully and one into a dignified provincial burial. The law has not remade Rome. It has put a handle on one side of a door. In a city built by men who specialise in walls, handles are not a small technology.

Daphne hears the story at the baths and tells it in the small dining room with a singer's instinct for the final note. “She walked past him, they say, holding the paper like a torch.”

“Paper burns,” Antonia says. “That is why we also keep the Greek.” — meaning the banker, meaning the copies, meaning Diomedes three hands, meaning everything she has learned about survival being a filing system with a soul. She looks at Daphne, who is twenty-two and free three years and holding, these days, real stones of modest size purchased with her own fees and kept, by house advice, in the Greek's strongbox against a bad winter. “Your contract here comes up at the new year.”

“I know the date. Singers count bars.”

“Renew it at the new rate, with the clause cited in your own hand at the foot.” Antonia pushes across a tablet on which Diomedes — who has aged, this year, a full decade in the eyes, as people do who keep other people's centuries in thread drawers — has written the terms in the hand reserved for things that must look boring to survive. “Every woman in this house. One by one. If Rome is going to register us, it will register *that* in this house first.”

Daphne reads. The room is quiet. The house beyond performs its evening overture: water, linen, the pastry cook whose saffron has this week been vindicated by two separate Alexandrians. “He wrote this,” Daphne says finally. “The senator. In the committee that was supposed to close us.”

“He wrote a sentence. What it closes and opens is our business.” Antonia stands; the evening requires her in three rooms at once and she will accomplish it, as ever, by being fully in one at a time and letting the house's memory do the rest. “Sing the Greek tonight, Daphne. For people who know. I am told there are more of them every quarter.”

The renewals happen over six weeks, because nothing intended to be real at the Caelian house is ever done in a single evening for effect. Diomedes prepares each tablet separately. Eirene sits in, not as chaperone—the word would make her spit into an expensive rug—but as a witness whose memory has jurisdiction in places courts do not reach. The Greek, or one of his terrifyingly polite subordinates, attends when savings are discussed, bringing with him the odour of cedar and the implication that numbers can be made to stand in a room without apologising.

Some women sign at once. Some take the tablet away overnight and return with questions in hands that have clearly consulted sisters, lovers, bathhouse philosophers or the small hours. One asks whether the right of departure includes departure in anger. Antonia says it includes departure in weather, provided the fees for work already promised are settled as the contract states. “I am not purchasing your gratitude,” she says. “I have enough furniture.”

Another, a dancer with an actor’s superstition about written promises, wants the clause read by someone not paid by the house. Diomedes makes no face. He sends for the registry clerk on his free afternoon and pays him an interpretation fee which is entered, with scrupulous deadpan, under Educational. The clerk reads. He explains the difference between a legal phrase and a key: one can be carried without fitting every lock. The dancer listens with her chin in her hand.

“And if the lock wins?” she asks.

“Then the fact that you have a copy proves somebody thought you might have the right to try the door,” says the clerk. “It is not nothing. In my office, nothing has a whole archive.”

She signs.

There is also the question of jewels. Antonia has never allowed the house to hold gifts as informal hostages, but other customs die more slowly than rules. Necklaces are produced from drawers, from the Greek’s office, from secret linings and one memorable pot of face powder. Each is entered as the property of its owner, with any stones valued and storage chosen. The exercise produces the atmosphere of an audit at a temple and two confessions about men whose names improve by being omitted.

“Mine came from a Syrian who believed garnets prevented seasickness,” says one woman. “I have never been on a ship.”

“Prevention,” Eirene says, “proven.”

Daphne is present for more of these sessions than her own contract requires. At first Antonia thinks it is curiosity. Then she sees the singer explaining, in simpler language, the difference between money held *for* a woman and money held *from* her. She has the born advocate’s talent for giving a hard sentence a human pulse. She no longer performs every feeling as if an audience were paying by the shiver; some expressions she now says in her speaking voice.

Near the end of the six weeks, the youngest of the musicians—twenty, ambitious, and not yet resigned to the fact that talent ages more slowly than skin—asks Antonia directly: “Did you wish someone had done this for you?”

The room becomes very interested in its lamps.

Antonia might make a house policy of the moment. She might make a tragedy. Instead she puts her hand flat on the table, a gesture whose meaning has changed over the year from testing for damp to keeping things from being swept away. “I wished for a copy of anything I had supposedly agreed to,” she says. “That was the first form of it. Grandeur came later and cost more.”

The musician signs her renewal and then, shyly, initials an additional line acknowledging receipt of her own savings statement. It is the least poetic act in the house that month, and Eirene, who has buried three musical careers and assisted at the birth of several bank accounts, regards it as one might a civic monument.

Later Antonia finds the Educational fee in Diomedes ledgers and approves it. “We are becoming an institution.”

“With curriculum,” Diomedes says. “Rome will have us on a plinth by winter if the accounts maintain their morals.”

“Let it begin at floor level. Plinths are hard to sweep.”

Saufia comes to the Caelian once, in October, not for sanctuary and not for work. She comes because a bath attendant who knows a pastry cook who knows Eirene has told her there is a house where a woman can have a document read without a man first explaining why it says less than she thinks.

She arrives at the trades entrance with her papers tied in blue thread and her manner prepared for an argument. Barbatus, who has a sixth sense for persons expecting to be charged for air, takes her first to the courtyard and then, on Eirene’s instruction, to soup. The papers come out only after the bowl is empty.

Diomedes reads by daylight, aloud, at reading speed rather than official speed. He does not improve the sums. Saufia points with one finger whenever a word appears to be doing more work than its size justifies. “There,” she says, at *patronal*. “He used it as if it were a lock and a roof and an opinion.”

“It means the office of a former owner toward a freed person,” Diomedes says.

“I know what it means. I want to know when it stops meaning he keeps the key.”

It is an excellent question, and the linen room receives it as one receives an expert in any trade. Antonia, passing with a list for glassware, stops in the doorway but does not enter. This is not her interview. Office by office, the house is learning to keep its benefactions from becoming a new venue for dependence.

Daphne waits until the reading is over and then says, from across the room where she is mending the strap of her own sandal with unnecessary artistry, “Did you really hold the paper like a torch?”

Saufia looks down at the roll. “I held it so the ink wouldn’t spoil. It had rained.”

“The baths have upgraded you.”

“Tell them not to. Torches go out.”

It is the best critique the clause receives in Rome that quarter.

She leaves with a fair copy, a narrower but more realistic understanding of her former owner’s claims, and no money from Antonia. She has not asked for money. Eirene has put bread in her bundle, because human government must be allowed its loopholes. At the door she turns back—not to Antonia, who is in another room, but to the house at large. “You register all this?” she asks Diomedes.

“Those parts the city can be trusted to pronounce.”

“Then register that I came in a tradeswoman and left the same. No miracle. A plural noun at my own table, if I can keep it.”

Diomedes writes nothing, which in his language is an honour. “The door-book has categories under review.”

After she has gone, Daphne remains standing too long with the sandal strap in her hand. “She isn’t even one of ours.”

“Exactly,” Eirene says. “Pass the soup pot. Principles that only open inward are cupboards.”

Prima hears the story in its upgraded version at the fullers’ guild, where Saufia has become by Friday a woman carrying fire through a locked gate with soldiers at the margins. By the following Tuesday, on the Caelian, Daphne gives her the bathhouse edition with orchestration. Prima listens while mending the hem of a spring hanging that has met a chair leg and lost.

“Did she really hold it like a torch?” she asks.

“No,” Daphne says, with the melancholy of an artist corrected by reality. “It had rained. She held it so the ink wouldn’t spoil. It may be the most serious sentence said in Rome this year. If I sing it that way, nobody will pay.”

“Try a lower line.”

“Oh, the needlewoman has opinions on tessitura.”

“I have opinions on people telling a woman’s departure as if she needed to be on fire to be believed.”

The needle goes in and comes out. Antonia, who is examining cushion braid with more attention than braid can reasonably reward, says nothing. She is still learning when maternal pride—if that is the legal description of the upheaval—can be allowed into a room without changing the price of the work.

Prima has brought her own copy of the clause, purchased from the island-bridge scribe before the price rose. It lives now in her work-box under the needles, not with her mother’s leaves. A difference in filing that Antonia notices and does not annotate aloud.

“Will it apply to guilds?” Prima asks.

“Begin by assuming it applies wherever a man says it doesn’t,” Eirene says from the door, where she has arrived with water and an instinct for questions that can use a coarse first draft. “Then find your Greek.”

“I have a clerk.”

“Then you are civilised. Water?”

Later, when the room suggests refreshment at several speeds, Prima shows Antonia the copy—not for approval, but to ask where the difficult phrase sits if a woman’s refusal is not of work itself but of being held answerable for other people’s tools, debts, breakages. Diomedes, summoned by the ancient authority of a document unrolling in his vicinity, explains what can be explained without turning the explanation into a moat.

“Law is a border,” he says. “It tells where the design repeats and where somebody has put one flower wrong on purpose. Within it, the cloth still has to be kept.”

Prima looks at her border. “Then I want copies for the women who sign the government cloth. At my expense, half rate if the scribe remembers me.”

“Half rate if you remind him of his sister.”

“I do not know his sister.”

“Neither does he, half the year. Rome is run on contextual memory.”

Antonia hears in the joke the sound of machinery changing hands. The clause has travelled from a senator’s shaken conscience to a copyist’s extract to a fullers’ work-box, acquiring in transit the only authority that lasts in the city: use by women who can count.

On her way home Prima stops, not at the bridge this time, but at the scribe’s stall. The price has indeed risen. She buys three copies, negotiates the fourth as thread taken in part exchange, and has them folded small enough to survive other women’s aprons. A revolution may arrive by discount. Distribution is organisation, and she has been raised by people who understood both.

In the Forum the column has cleared the rooftops. Tourists from the provinces stand in its scaffolding's shadow with their heads back and their mouths open, counting, exactly as Prima counts stitches. Lentulus passes it on his way from a committee — a real committee now, on aqueducts, which flows through his remaining public life at a respectable rate — and a foreman he half-knows tells him the inside stair is continuous to the top now: a man can climb inside the war, when it is dedicated, and come out above it.

He walks home the long way, past the Greek's office, where he does not call; past the Basilica, where his agent is performing confidence in two languages; up the Palatine in the cooling light, a senator of ancient lineage and diminished prospects whose name the baths now pronounce in the past tense of Syria and the present tense of one clause that law students have begun, this term, to be set to argue about. Acilius has written that Hadrian, from Athens, inquired of the clause's draftsman — *a Cornelius with a freedwoman's conscience*, the secretaries are saying, which is the kind of joke that gets a man remembered in the wrong way by the right people, or the other way, in reigns to come.

The aqueducts committee suits him more than he first understands. Water, unlike ambition, announces its failures promptly. A channel silts; a pressure calculation is wrong; a district complains in language no rhetorician's Secretary can entirely beautify. Lentulus finds himself in the company of engineers, maintenance contractors and one unglamorous genius from Campania who speaks of gradients as moral philosophy. It is the first committee of his life in which the minutes frequently describe things that will happen.

“You have a talent for flow,” the Campanian tells him, without any observable irony. “Everybody important wants a fountain. Somebody has to ask where it comes from and what stops on the way.”

Lentulus hears the Caelian registrar in the words and keeps his face in the respectable columns. “I am lately interested in what does not reach its destination.”

“Leakage,” the engineer says, delighted. “The empire is mostly that.”

His old set has divided on him, as sets do. Some have filed the Syrian disappointment under weather. Some have discovered with hindsight that they always doubted him. A few, unexpectedly, have begun to invite him for precisely the qualities that were supposed to have disqualified: he can sit through a drainage estimate; he does not perform youthful hunger at the table; he knows what a clause costs when quoted by people who were not at the dinner where it was drafted.

Acilius, whose correspondence arrives as if from a better century, has taken to sending him extracts of other men’s talk. Hadrian in Athens, asking after the draftsman. A jurist in Baiae describing the clause as *rough carpentry, but a door*. An anonymous wit—there is a thriving population—saying that the Cornelii have at last contributed plumbing to both public morality and the water supply.

Lentulus writes back less often than courtesy recommends. What would he say? That the sentence was written by a man still believing a committee could launder history? That its authority was improved by a freedwoman in the Subura and two witnesses borrowed from a bread queue? He composes, in his head, several versions of the truth, each insufficiently senatorial.

His agent at the Basilica has no such inhibitions. “They are calling you the conscience of the drainage interest,” he says, nursing a contract that has gone through three languages and now means something in none. “I could sell that in a mild winter.”

“Kindly do not.”

“Humility as product has a short season, senator.”

“Then let it perish unsold.”

The agent looks at him as at cargo abandoned on a dock. “It is a name,” he says. “Most men in this building are paying interest on theirs.”

Lentulus thinks of the olive transaction, which has proceeded exactly as the Greek said it would: modestly, with papers. The debt is respectable, the obligation minuted, the temptation to disguise it as patronage resisted mostly by the fact that the Greek would have corrected his metaphors. He is learning the dignity of small transactions from people his family formerly employed to disappear them.

At a family dinner that week, an elderly cousin says, “At least Syria would have had weather,” and the table waits for the wound to declare itself. Lentulus watches Marcus put down his cup before it can become an answer. Servilia says, “Syria has weather whether Cornelius attends it or not. It is one of the province’s efficiencies,” and the evening is saved by administration.

Afterward Lentulus tells her, in the corridor between generations of wax, “You make even disappointment sound governed.”

“It is governed. I do it.”

“And the clause?”

“Also governed,” she says, “though not by you. Do not look so tragic. Things improve when your family’s authorship is diluted. Rivers, marriages, law.”

He has no answer in the first hand. In the second, after thirty years, he is beginning to think that dilution may be a form of survival his ancestors neglected because they preferred to remain undrinkable.

At home there is a letter half-written on his desk in Marcus's hand — Greek, terrible, sincere; his son leaves drafts like flags — addressed across the river. Lentulus does not read it. He puts a fresh sheet over it, weighs it with the dented mirror, and goes to stand a moment among the masks in the last of the light.

The draft has been there for two days because Marcus, still apprentice to the new domestic order, has not yet decided whether unfinished honesty should be hidden or merely not exhibited at dinner. He left it under the pretext of asking for a stylus. Lentulus recognised the manoeuvre from his own youth: a sensitive document abandoned in a parent’s landscape in the hope that weather will take responsibility for reading it.

He does not. It is the most difficult act of authorship occurring in the study that week.

Instead he calls Onesimus and asks for a fresh sheet, ostensibly for committee business. When Marcus appears at the door after dinner—too promptly, as if summoned by paper—Lentulus is writing a note about maintenance access to a channel outside the walls. Both men contemplate this respectable labour.

“Your Greek improves,” Lentulus says.

“You have not read it.”

“No.”

“Then how—” Marcus stops, and with real discomfort identifies the trap his own question has set. “Mother said you would not.”

“Your mother knows my current policies.”

“She writes most of them.”

“She improves their expression.”

Marcus sits, uninvited and already normal. His father keeps the fresh sheet in place with two fingers, not yet using the mirror as a weight; that small theatre belongs to later, when he is alone with all the unlit centuries.

“I keep saying *sister* too soon,” Marcus says. “Then I take it out. Then the letter sounds as if I am writing to a guild.”

“Perhaps you are writing to a person.”

“Father, I am attempting profundity. You might open a door.”

“I am avoiding one.” Lentulus puts the stylus down before it can be conscripted. “If I compose the sentence, Prima will receive a document from this house. She already has enough of those by inheritance.”

“She has none.”

“She has the consequences of several.”

The word *Prima* on his tongue is still a managed impropriety. He says it because the new discipline requires precision even where precision has no public use.

Marcus looks at the covered draft. “What did you want to say, when you were twenty-four?”

The study receives the question as it receives all dangerous furniture: by appearing unchanged. Lentulus has had the entire winter to formulate a reply suitable for the record. None of the versions have survived Antonia’s exit lines, his son’s third drafts or his own notebook. “I wanted the consequences to be administered elsewhere and the feelings to be admired at home. In that order.”

Marcus winces. “That is very plain.”

“It has been trying to enter the family language for some time.”

“And now?”

“Now I endeavour to keep my hands where I can see them.” He holds them, absurdly, on either side of the aqueduct note. It makes Marcus laugh once, not heralded, and the study—scrolls, mirror, corridor—allows a moment in which father and son are not competing to be the ruin of a narrative.

“She may never answer,” Marcus says.

“That possibility is not a defect in the letter.”

“Mother says a refusal is not an interruption. It is part of the sentence.”

“Your mother has been reading the committee.”

“No, Father. The committee has been catching up with her.”

He takes the draft away with him, folded once, not smoothed. Lentulus leaves the fresh sheet where it was and, after a moment, sets the dented mirror on it. The gesture is now less about keeping a document covered than about marking an empty place correctly.

Servilia finds him still in the atrium after the lamps have begun their small domestic campaign against the dark. She is in the indoors dress she wears when she is no longer distributing herself among guests, and she carries, incongruously for a woman who has made an art form of not being interrupted, a cup in each hand.

“Onesimus says you are consulting the management,” she says. “I have brought the committee wine.”

“The management is unanimous.”

“It frequently is, after death. It is their least burdensome qualification.” She gives him a cup. Together they look at the masks, which daylight has lately made cleaner and lamplight makes less forgiving. “Your grandfather looks as though the aqueducts have disappointed him.”

“My grandfather believed water reached the house by status.”

“Most men do, until a pipe is specified.”

For a while neither speaks. The house goes on around them: Cornelia’s music stopped in favour of sleep, Marcus somewhere abusing Greek in a private and improving manner, Onesimus converting evening into the shape it will need tomorrow. It is an ordinary peace, and Lentulus is sufficiently altered to notice that ordinary peace is not a substance automatically renewed by ancestry.

“Hadrian asked after me,” he says.

“So Acilius thinks we should be told.”

“It may mean nothing.”

“It may mean a provincial joke with good postal service.” She drinks. “Do you want it to mean more?”

The honest answer is in three columns and only one belongs in the atrium. “I want the work not to have been reduced to a scandal with one quotable sentence.”

“Then do work,” Servilia says. “Scandals dislike maintenance schedules. They lack the stamina.”

“And if that is all?”

She turns then, not towards the masks but toward him, which in their marriage is still less frequent than negotiations through architecture. “Gnaeus. A man may be remembered for a sentence he was forced by dawn, debt and a woman’s filing system to make true. It is not the worst thing written about this family on a wall.”

The use of his first name is so rare in her mouth that for a moment it has no address. Lentulus looks into his cup. “I am not forgiven.”

“No.”

“I do not expect it.”

“Then stop listening for it like weather.” She says it without cruelty. That may be what reaches him. “Accuracy does not applaud.”

He almost smiles. “I wrote that in a notebook. Or something unnecessarily close.”

“Marriage is plagiarism under management.”

They finish the wine in front of three centuries, who have heard worse settlements.

*Choose better,* his father wrote, dying, in the hand that gathered itself at the ends of lines.

“I chose worse for thirty years at the full rate,” Lentulus tells the wax, conversationally, as a man settles with his creditors in private. “The interest is the estate now. I am merely keeping it lit.” The masks say nothing. They have been saying it, eloquently, for three hundred years.
