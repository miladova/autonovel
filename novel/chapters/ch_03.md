# Debridement

*Warsaw, 16 February 1943*

The seed merchant's shop on ulica Bagno smelled of dried marjoram, mouse droppings, and dead brick dust blown across from the emptied streets of the Small Ghetto fifty yards west, where every window frame behind the bricked-up boundary wall had been pried out for firewood before Christmas so the tenements stared blind down onto Plac Grzybowski like skulls stacked in an ossuary.

Sylwia stood before the counter weighing small brown paper twists of winter radish and fodder-beet seed in her gloved palm as if calculating balcony pots for spring. Behind the counter old PanCzarnecki coughed wetly into a blue workman's handkerchief and counted zinc ten-grosz pieces into a cigar box so the brass bell above the door wouldn't catch them talking if a Blue Policeman ducked in out of the wind for warm stove air.

In the back storeroom behind stacked burlap sacks of vetch, Marek had spread three damp Leica contact prints no larger than postage stamps on an upturned herring tub.

"Look at the collar tab on the dead driver they fished out of the ditch at Szczęśliwice," Marek said, tilting his pocket magnifying lens over the middle square.

"Mekelburg isn't dead," Sylwia said, bending over the lens without touching the wet gelatin border. "I took two fingers off his left hand Wednesday morning. He eats three bowls of barley groats a day sitting on a stool behind Room Fourteen's door."

"Not Schoeler's driver. The *other* car's driver." Marek tapped the blurred grey rectangle with a matchstick. Snow banked along a railway drainage culvert; half a burned Steyr staff car canted on three wheels; beside the running board lay a man in civilian sheepskin coat with his throat torn open by pistol fire at contact range and snow drifted into both eye sockets. "Found at five-thirty Tuesday morning by municipal track-walkers before the SD arrived from Aleja Szucha and cordoned half Ochota. One of our boys on the track gang pulled the left boot off before the gendarmes chased them back. Inside the felt insole were twelve hundred dollars in Swiss twenty-franc gold notes? No—paper Zürcher banknotes, serials non-consecutive, and the charred corner of a temporary *Durchlassschein* stamped *Abt. III F — Ast. Warschau*."

Sylwia straightened slowly inside her thin wool coat. *Abteilung III F.* Even someone who loathed military acronyms knew what *Drei-Eff* meant in German intelligence: counter-espionage against enemy underground networks and foreign wireless rings. Not troop logistics, not bridge security, not Wehrmacht medical liaison.

"Ludwig Schoeler took a *summa cum laude* doctorate on collateral arterial pathways of the mediastinum in nineteen-thirty-two," she said, hearing how flat her own voice sounded between the burlap sacks. "Geheimrat von Halden groomed him five years to inherit a university surgical clinic before forty."

"Well, he isn't cutting dogs now," Marek said crisply, sliding the three tiny contact prints into a hollow brass curtain-rod section and screwing the end cap tight. "Since forty-one he's been Canaris's troubleshooter between Königsberg, Minsk, and Saski Square—rank *Oberstleutnant*, carried nominally on an East Prussian signals replacement roll so his name doesn't cross regular garrison rosters. Our girls at the central post telegraph office pulled three coded Berlin wires addressed to his desk since Wednesday noon. One from Tirpitzufer itself."

Tirpitzufer in Berlin—down by the Landwehrkanal, ten minutes' walk south of the Tiergarten trees where she used to cut across snow in worn-out galoshes with physiology books burning her ribs. Everything in this cursed war turned out to sit four blocks from some street corner where you had once been twenty and hungry and stupid enough to think hatred was a private hobby.

"Then give me two milliliters of strophanthin-K undiluted," Sylwia said calmly, "or four cubic centimeters of air into his Carrel venous line when Mekelburg steps into the corridor latrine at four in the morning. Ventricular standstill in ninety seconds. Vesper signs post-traumatic pulmonary embolism before breakfast and goes back to his cognac."

Marek looked up from screwing the brass tube shut. In the grey shaft of light leaking through the frosted transom from Bagno, his cheekbones looked planed out of dry ash-wood; a shaving cut on his chin had scabbed black from cold.

"No," he said.

She stared at him. "You just told me he runs *Abteilung III F* counter-espionage against Warsaw nets."

"Which is why *Góra* wants him breathing inside your pavilion for another two weeks," Marek said, using the flat organizational word—*Upstairs*, High Command—that always shut doors in faces without leaving anybody present to argue with. "Think, Sylwia. Two cars met behind the Ochota coal-water tower at twenty minutes to one on Tuesday morning without notifying Aleja Szucha. Somebody opened up from the embankment above *both* vehicles with silenced submachine guns. Whoever was riding in that second car either died in the snow, got dragged into the black Opel Admiral our switchman saw before the shooting, or walked back into Warsaw carrying an Abwehr safe-conduct and twelve hundred Swiss francs' worth of Polish blood-money."

Cold crept up from the packed clay floor through Sylwia's thin leather soles. "You think someone from our own District staff was in that second car."

"I don't think anything; engineers measure loads," Marek said dryly. "If Szucha's SD ambushed an Abwehr meet on their own turf without telling Saski Square, then either Schoeler was turning one of our people, or one of our people was selling somebody else to Schoeler behind Kroll's back, and Kroll shot the table over to grab the ledger. Either way, if Schoeler dies tonight on Lindleya, Tirpitzufer sends a stranger we can't reach behind six walls at the Brühl Hotel, and whatever name burned in that briefcase stays ash."

He stepped closer between the burlap vetch sacks, resting one gloved hand briefly against her waist—possessive only in the functional way a carpenter tests whether a beam has shifted under snow load.

"You saved him Tuesday night," Marek said. "Good. Now use it. You're the only Pole in Warsaw allowed inside Room Fourteen with the door shut, and you speak their Berlin gutter-German better than half the Volksdeutsche on Marszałkowska. Keep his wound open long enough that Vesper daren't move him to Ujazdowskie. Listen to every fever-word, every visitor's boots, every name he asks his corporal to burn or telephone. Did you know him in Berlin?"

The question dropped without emphasis—too casual by half. Marek never wasted breath on decorative curiosity. Somebody in *Kontrwywiad*'s biographical card-index had checked the dates on Sylwia's *Charité* diploma against Ludwig Schoeler's university matriculation year between Wednesday and Tuesday noon.

Lie to Marek now on a verifiable university calendar and by Friday she would have a nineteen-year-old boy in borrowed boots shadowing her down Koszykowa to see whom she met at tram stops.

"Everyone at the Charité between twenty-eight and thirty-three knew Schoeler," Sylwia said without blinking, keeping her chin level and her voice soaked in ten-year-old acid so authentic no listener alive could mistake it for fondness. "He was von Halden's prize Prussian bastard and we despised each other's guts across every exam board from anatomy to state finals. When the brownshirts cleared Luisenstraße in March thirty-three, he walked into a Reichswehr commission and I took the night train to Poznań."

Marek studied her dark eyes for three full seconds. Whatever harsh, ugly constriction sat around her mouth clearly satisfied him; nobody faked that particular reflex around a rival's name.

"Does he know who you are?"

"He recognized me Thursday night. And if he intended telling Vesper or the SD anything inconvenient about student politics from nineteen-thirty-two, I'd be on Szucha already instead of buying radish seed." She pulled back half an inch out of Marek's gloved hand, buttoning her top coat button against her throat. "Don't ask me to bat eyelashes at him, Marek. Try playing salon spy with Ludwig Schoeler and he'll smell it before you've crossed the doorsill."

"Don't bat anything," Marek said, almost smiling—a thin, joyless crease. "Hate him all you please. Men starting back from two liters of lost blood talk faster to someone they want to beat in an argument than they ever do to a pretty nurse. Just keep him under Lindleya's roof until we find out whose Swiss francs were in that boot."

* * *

At three o'clock that afternoon, Bronka laid out the boiled debridement tray in Pavilion Three's sterilizer room while water hissed white steam under the lid: two pairs of curved Mayo dissecting scissors honed that morning by old Walenty on the oilstone, toothed Kocher forceps, long anatomical thumb-forceps, a sharp Volkmann spoon, three kidney basins, iodoform ribbon, and one solitary brown five-milliliter ampoule of two-percent procaine.

Sylwia picked up the solitary ampoule between thumb and forefinger, holding it against the grey courtyard window. "Where's the rest of the box?"

"Herr Stabsarzt Vesper signed out eleven ampoules this morning for 'officers' dental blocks' at the Soldatenheim," Bronka said grimly, slamming the brass sterilizer lid down with her hip. Everyone in Pavilion Three knew Vesper traded hospital procaine to private dentists on Hoża for French cognac and real coffee beans. "Left us two ampoules for the whole week—one for the carpenter upstairs when we take his crushed toes off tomorrow, one for Room Fourteen. Unless you give the German a drop of ether on the mask—"

"Ether makes him vomit for four hours afterward, and one violent retching spasm with his clavicle resected tears my axillary suture line straight out of the first rib," Sylwia said, setting the single five-milliliter ampoule back onto the sterile towel with a dry clink. Five milliliters infiltrated skin margins if you diluted it half-and-half with physiological saline; deep down inside a palm-wide pectoral crater where bruised muscle bellies had turned grey-purple over seven days and had to be trimmed back raw to living, bleeding red fiber before anaerobic rot took root, five milliliters was pissing on a coal fire.

Inside Room Fourteen, someone had propped two hard horsehair bolsters behind Ludwig's good right shoulder so he sat tilted at forty-five degrees against the white iron headboard. Daylight was cruel to him in a way Thursday night's green-shaded sixty-watt bulb had not been: five days of resorption fever, meager hospital broth, and refusal of opiates had pared every gram of spare flesh off his jaw and temples, leaving skin the colour of wet putty stretched taut across high, unforgiving bone. Mekelburg had shaved him that morning—better than the butcher job on his chest, leaving his throat and chin smooth around the pale crescent scar beneath his right cheekbone—and a fresh grey wool Wehrmacht blanket borrowed from his own field roll lay folded squarely across his waist instead of hospital cotton.

On his knees rested a yellow German message pad and a fountain pen held awkwardly in his right hand, writing left-to-right while pinning the paper corner down with his heavy silver pocket-watch so the pad wouldn't slide. The instant the door latch clicked, he flipped the top sheet face-down under the blotter without hurry.

"Lock the hall door while we're open, *Gefreiter*," Sylwia told Mekelburg as Bronka wheeled the enameled dressing cart up to the left rail. "I don't want corridor draft blowing ward dust into an open thorax, and if your military police poke their noses in halfway through they'll faint on my clean floor."

Mekelburg shot the brass bolt obediently and took up his post at the foot of the bed, wooden-splinted left hand tucked inside his tunic front like Napoleon's, watching Bronka lay waterproof rubber sheeting beneath his commander's left flank.

"Herr Stabsarzt Vesper honoured me for twenty minutes before noon," Ludwig remarked conversationally, eyes tracking Sylwia as she rinsed her raw hands in warm sublimate basin at the washstand. Without uniform collar tabs, bare from waist up except for the yellowed calico Velpeau bandage binding his left arm across his ribs, the sheer bony breadth of his shoulders made the narrow Polish municipal mattress look built for schoolboys. "Smelled like a Schwabing hairdresser's shop after closing time. Informed me at considerable length how fortunate I was that his Polish staff acted under his strict vascular protocols."

"Had you rather be at *Lazarett Ujazdowski* listening to gramophone records of Zara Leander while a dermatologist lets your axillary artery slough?" Sylwia dried her hands on a sterile linen towel, lifted the heavy bandage shears, and slit the outer Velpeau turns cleanly down the right ribs.

"God forbid. At Ujazdowski I'd be dead of boredom by Thursday, and half the SS liaison clerks from Aleja Szucha would be bringing me sour grapes to see whether my tongue works." He didn't wince until Bronka eased the stiffened inner gauze pads free from the dried serum crust around the skin edges; even then, only a tiny muscle below his left ear jumped once as dark stale blood and sweet-rotten iodoform stink lifted into the cool room.

Sylwia bent low over the crater—six inches from his bare sweat-damp throat—and inspected every millimeter in silence.

Deep at the floor of the cleft, beneath the thin viable bridge of pectoralis minor she had tacked across the neurovascular bundle on Tuesday night, rhythmic arterial pulsation beat steady and clean. Good. No expanding pulsatile hematoma, no ominous dark thrill of mycotic blowout. Above and laterally, however, where shredded pectoralis major fibers and crushed subcutaneous fat had taken the full muzzle blast of shattered glass, steel splinters, and dirty overcoat cloth, seven days of compromised capillary blood supply had done their ugly winter work: two finger-thick shelves of muscle belly had turned dull greyish-plum, slimy with yellowish fibrinous slough and wholly avascular. Leave dead meat sitting inside an open wound past Day Seven and by Day Nine proteolytic bacteria liquefied it straight down through your protective muscle bridge onto the five-zero silk.

"What does it look like?" Ludwig asked directly above her temple. Because his neck was stiff from muscle splinting, his breath—dry, faint salt and black ersatz coffee, zero sweet acetone of starvation yet—brushed the dark hair pinned above her right ear every time he spoke.

"Like six-day horsemeat left behind a radiator," Sylwia answered briskly, nodding to Bronka for the Record syringe. "Deep bridge over your vessel held and is starting to granulate pink. Lateral pectoral shelf and superior clavicular pocket are necrotic slough two centimeters deep. They come out today down to bleeding muscle."

Ludwig's eyes flicked sideways onto the sterile towel—taking in at a single practiced surgeon's glance the Mayo scissors, the toothed forceps, the Volkmann curette, and the solitary five-milliliter brown ampoule of procaine diluted to ten in the glass cup.

Silence stretched two seconds. He did the milligram arithmetic faster than Mirecki could have done it on paper.

"Vesper drank the rest of the Novocain?" he asked dryly.

"Sold it, probably," Sylwia said, refusing him lies she wouldn't have insulted a third-year student with. "I have ten milliliters of one-percent for the skin rim where cutaneous nerves are dense, or Bronka can put fifty grams of ether onto the wire mask and knock you under for twelve minutes."

"No ether," Ludwig said immediately. Not machismo—cold practical refusal, identical to his corporal's on Wednesday morning, except twice as alert: a man who knew twenty minutes of post-ether delirium loosened tongues in rooms with thin doors. "Save your ten milliliters for some chimney-sweep's toes upstairs. Cut fast and don't chatter like von Halden over fascia."

Sylwia paused with the syringe needle poised an inch above his bruised skin margin. Slowly she raised her eyes four inches into his.

Up this close she could see tiny flecks of slate-rust inside the grey-green ring of his irises, and three fine silver threads—grey hair at thirty-four, buried in the dark blond at his left temple—that hadn't existed in nineteen-thirty-three.

"Skeletal muscle bellies still carry deep nociceptive C-fibers along every arteriole, *Herr Doktor*," she said low in German, voice flat as scalpel steel. "Twenty minutes of sharp debridement raw isn't pulling a tooth."

"Then don't take twenty minutes," Ludwig said, jaw setting hard while a mocking, unbearable spark glinted deep in the winter-water green. "Unless Warsaw dulled your wrists, *Frau Doktor*. Give me the strap, Bruno."

Mekelburg didn't even blink; clearly this wasn't the first field table he had stood beside with his officer since nineteen-thirty-nine. The boy unbuckled his own heavy brown leather Wehrmacht equipment belt one-handed, folded four inches of thick saddle-hide double, wiped it once on his clean tunic sleeve, and laid it between Ludwig's back molars.

Sylwia set the unused procaine syringe down untouched beside the kidney dish. *Good,* she thought savagely—five milliliters saved for the seventeen-year-old carpenter upstairs whose gangrenous frostbitten toes didn't come with German pensions—and infiltrated nothing at all, picking up toothed Kocher forceps in her left hand and curved Mayo scissors in her right.

"Bronka, steady the right shoulder so he doesn't jerk left onto the scissor points," Sylwia said in Polish. "Warm Dakin's flush ready as soon as the dead shelf comes off."

She took hold of the plum-grey necrotic wedge of pectoralis major with the toothed jaws, lifted firmly until the demarcation line between dead grey pulp and living crimson muscle showed taut as string, and cut.

Dead flesh itself had no nerve endings; the first two snips gave only a wet, gristly *crunch* like slicing boiled gizzard. The third snip crossed half a millimeter into healthy, hyperemic viable muscle where inflammatory capillaries bled bright scarlet—and where raw, unanesthetized nerve twigs fired straight up the cervical cord like white-hot piano wire.

Ludwig's entire torso locked rigid as seasoned oak beneath Bronka's heavy red forearms. Leather creaked aloud—an ugly, wet saddle-groan—between his back teeth; instantly sweat broke not in drops but in a slick grey sheet down his temples, throat hollow, and flat sternum, smelling sharp and acrid under the hospital carbolic. His right hand gripped the vertical iron spindle of the headboard above his crown so violently that white paint flaked off onto his knuckles and the whole iron bedstead vibrated faintly against Sylwia's hip.

Sylwia did not slow down, and she did not flinch—hesitating halfway through living debridement only doubled the cuts and tripled the shock. Blade tip open, slide under dead grey fascia, *snip*; rotate wrist thirty degrees away from the pulsating pectoral bridge below so one involuntary spasm couldn't drive steel through his repaired artery, *snip*; sponge dark oozing blood clear with hot saline gauze; scrape dirty periosteal tags and black powder-grit clean off the sawn lateral end of the clavicle with three ruthless strokes of the sharp Volkmann spoon—*scritch, scritch, scritch*—bone grating right up through her own fingertips into her teeth.

Halfway through clearing the deep axillary pocket, her left hand had to go three inches inside the hot, wet cavity of his chest wall to shield the median nerve cord with two bare fingers while her right scissor-tips excised a grey sloughing pocket where wool threads had festered.

Inside there he was thirty-nine degrees warm—slick, pulsing, shockingly alive around her knuckles, every frantic hammer-stroke of his heart slamming through thin intercostal muscle directly against the back of her fingers while his ribs heaved short and shallow against the leather strap in his mouth. For three dizzying seconds the sheer carnality of surgery—having her bare hand buried inside the living chest of the boy who had stood across Slate Table Nineteen in clean starched cuffs, feeling his hot blood slick her palm while his breath hissed ragged six inches from her mouth—hit her stomach with a jolt so physical and perverse it felt like vertigo on high scaffolding.

She excised the last necrotic tag cleanly, dropped three grey-purple lumps of dead muscle into the enamel kick-basin with a wet slap, packed two steaming hot saline sponges tight into the bleeding red cavity to tamponade capillary ooze for sixty seconds, and kept steady pressure down with the heel of her hand.

Ludwig spat the bitten leather belt onto his chest.

His chest heaved; dark blond hair clung dripping to his white forehead; pupils dilated black nearly to the outer rims of his irises from catecholamine surge, giving him the feral, dangerous stare of a wolf caught in steel jaws and chewing at the chain. On his lower lip a thin bright bead of blood welled where his upper canine had slipped off the wet leather.

"Eight minutes," he rasped, voice torn down to gravel, swallowing spit mixed with salt blood and staring straight up into her face six inches away while her hand stayed pressed firm over the hot sponges inside his shoulder. "Slowing down in your thirties, *Kotarska*. Von Halden's dog-house record was six."

"Dogs don't carry dirty Prussian wool four inches under their collarbones," Sylwia said evenly, though her own pulse was thudding hard inside her throat and her palm burned from the heat of his chest through wet gauze. "And dogs bite quieter."

Across the foot of the bed, Bronka blinked once behind Sylwia's shoulder—catching not the German words, since Bronka knew only fifty words of market German for lard, coal, and *weg*, but the shocking temperature drop when two people who ought to be surgeon and conqueror spoke to each other like knives scraping in one drawer.

Sylwia pulled the tamponade sponges out—clean, brisk capillary ooze now from healthy brick-red granulation buds across the entire wound floor, zero grey rot left, zero foul gas bubbles—irrigated generously with warm Dakin's solution while Ludwig locked his jaw bare without the belt and took the chlorine fire staring unblinking into her eyes, and laid fresh yellow iodoform gauze strips loose into every recess.

"Bronka, take the soiled tray out to boil and bring fresh adhesive strapping from the cupboard," Sylwia said in Polish. "Mekelburg—help her carry the zinc pail down the hall to the sluice sink so the sentry unlocks once instead of twice."

Mekelburg hesitated half a beat, looking to the pillow. Ludwig gave one micro-nod of his damp chin. When the oak door clicked shut behind corporal and nurse—leaving one armed MP visible only as a blurred grey silhouette beyond frosted glass—the quiet inside Room Fourteen tightened instantly until the drip of melted frost between the double windowpanes sounded like small lead shot hitting tin.

Sylwia stood winding a fresh three-inch calico roller bandage across his bare ribs and right shoulder to anchor the dressing without adhesive tape tearing sweat-raw skin. Every pass round his back required him to lean forward four inches off the bolsters—unsupported, breathless, bare skin hot and damp with salt three millimeters from her white smock breast—while she reached both arms around his torso to pass the linen roll from right hand to left.

On the third pass, as she brought the roll forward beneath his good right armpit, he spoke six inches from her ear—not in German.

"*Dobra robota, pani doktor,*" Ludwig murmured in rough, guttural, unmistakably grammatical Polish—the hard *r* slightly over-rolled, the *ł* in his throat stiff with effort, yet every vowel placed where Warsaw streets placed it rather than phrasebooks.

Sylwia's fingers stopped dead on the calico roll against his ribs.

In Berlin between nineteen-twenty-eight and thirty-three Ludwig Schoeler had not known five words of Polish except *pszek* and *cholera*, and had mocked Slavic consonant clusters in Charlottenburg wine-cellars whenever he wanted to make her throw bread rolls at his head.

She did not pull back—pulling back ceded ground—keeping her eyes level with the wet hollow above his intact right collarbone before raising them slowly to his face. "Where did they teach *Abwehr* officers to speak Polish like cattle-dealers from Mława? Over Pawiak tables?"

His mouth curved faintly around the dried blood-fleck on his lower lip, catching the immediate give-away—no mere hospital doctor knew by Tuesday week that a signals-depot *Oberstleutnant* wasn't from signals unless somebody had looked under his rock—though he didn't pounce on the slip; he merely filed it behind those pale winter-water eyes with lethal amusement.

"Grodno and Białystok in forty-one, Lemberg in forty-two, here since November," he said, sliding back into quiet Berlin German as she pulled the fourth bandage turn deliberately tight enough across his lower ribs to make his breath catch sharp. "One learns the local vernacular when one gets tired of interpreters lying through their teeth for ten Reichsmarks a page. Though I confess your Poznań consonants used to ring in my ears whenever some schoolmaster in Wilno corrected my genitives."

"Keep my consonants out of your mouth," Sylwia said coldly, splitting the end of the calico roller up the center with her scissors and knotting the tails hard over his right shoulder seam. "How did an arterial surgeon from Luisenstraße end up riding round Ochota railway ditches at one in the morning burning leather briefcases?"

There. Dropped flat on the iron sheet between them without coyness—exactly as Marek would have hated and exactly the only way anyone ever rattled Ludwig Schoeler: straight into the solar plexus while his ribs still burned raw from her scissors.

Nothing in his face twitched, yet the pulse beating visibly in his carotid triangle slowed rather than raced—that cold reptile stillness she remembered from oral state exams whenever an examiner tried trapping him on rare cerebral aneurysms.

"Mekelburg talks when his hand hurts," Ludwig observed mildly.

"Mekelburg talked to keep you still while I had five-zero silk through your subclavian transition and zero anesthetic in your veins," Sylwia said, collecting her bloodstained Kocher forceps and Mayo scissors tip-down into a clean towel with small, metallic clicks. "Don't dock the boy's rations; without his belt on your neck Tuesday night you'd be under frozen lime at Powązki Military Cemetery right now, and Stabsarzt Vesper would be stealing your boots."

Ludwig leaned back slowly onto the horsehair bolsters, letting his head rest against the iron bars while grey exhaustion pooled dark beneath his lower lashes. Yet his eyes followed her raw hands wrapping steel in linen—lingering on the pale twelve-year-old scar at the base of her left thumb before lifting straight to her face.

"Why did you really leave Luisenstraße without taking your state surgical certificate from the dean's clerk on the tenth of March, Sylwia?" he asked very softly. "Your certificate sat stamped in Fräulein Weber's pigeonhole until June. All you had to do was send the Polish Legation messenger down Unter den Linden for twenty minutes."

Her knuckles turned white through the linen instrument roll. Out in the corridor two heavy jackboots clumped back toward the frosted glass panel—Bronka and Mekelburg returning with the boiled pail.

"Ask Jakob Mandelbaum's widow why nobody went back down Luisenstraße that week," Sylwia whispered, voice shaking once—just once, furious with herself for even that hairline crack—with ten-year-old poison. "If you still remember which camp ditch they threw her spectacles into after you moved into his corner room."

Something violent and dark crossed Ludwig's chalk-white face—so fast, raw, and utterly unlike guilty evasion that for half a heartbeat she didn't recognize the expression at all before his teeth snapped shut and the door bolt rattled back from outside.

When Bronka pushed the door open carrying fresh white adhesive rolls, Sylwia was already standing three paces cleanly away by the porcelain washstand, writing *16.II.43 — Debridement necrotic pectoral fascia; arterial pulse radialis sin. strong, 84/min; wound remains packed open; unfit for transport minimum 12 days* across the yellow Wehrmacht chart in firm black ink.

She did not look back at the bed on her way out, though all the way up the stone stairs to the Polish wards on the second floor—where the carpenter with black frostbitten toes lay waiting for his saved five milliliters of procaine—her left hand inside her smock pocket still felt thirty-nine degrees hot, as if she had plunged it to the wrist into scalding tallow and couldn't wash the grease off.

* * *

Upstairs in Male Surgical B, forty-two iron cots stood jammed so close together that when Bed Twelve rolled onto his side to spit into his tin cup, his elbow bumped Bed Thirteen's traction pulley and set six pounds of sash-iron swinging. No German steam radiator had been bled properly up here since December; frost furred the lower putty of the tall casements in grey fern-fronds, and every breath hung visible above gray horse-blankets darned with butcher's string.

Antek, the seventeen-year-old joiner's apprentice hauled in three nights ago after hiding eleven hours inside an unheated timber wagon at Praga to dodge an *Arbeitsamt* labor sweep, lay at the far end behind a three-panel canvas screen whose oilcloth had cracked yellow like old parchment. His right first, second, and third toes had passed dry demarcation two days ago—shriveled hard and matte black as smoked sprats, tapping against forceps with a dry horn click—except along the cleft of the first metatarsal web, where damp foul pus had begun creeping pink up the dorsum of the foot since morning. Wait till Friday for dry auto-amputation and streptococci would race up the lymphatics to his groin before Lent.

"Look what fell out of heaven," Sylwia said in Polish, holding the intact brown five-milliliter ampoule of *Polfa* procaine up to the gray window light before scoring its neck with a steel file and snapping the glass top off cleanly inside a gauze square. "Real Novocain, Antek. None of Bronka's valerian water today."

The boy's sunken brown eyes widened above two weeks of wispy blond chin-fluff. Hunger made seventeen look twelve round the collarbones and sixty round the mouth. "Will it take the whole foot, pani doktor?"

"Three toes and half the big-toe knuckle head if the bone bleeds red underneath," Sylwia said, drawing every clear drop up into her boiled syringe and diluting it to twelve milliliters with warm sterile saline so she could ring-block all three digital bases plus the dorsalis pedis twig above the ankle. "You won't dance mazurkas on Easter Sunday, and you'll limp when rain comes off the Vistula, but you'll stand at a joiner's lathe without a crutch. Look at the ceiling moulding and count the plaster roses aloud for me while the needle goes in."

He got to fourteen before his voice cracked on the deep plantar injection—plantar skin hurt worse than chest muscle because nature packed weight-bearing leather solid with Pacinian corpuscles—then blew out a shaky laugh five minutes later when Sylwia pinched the black big toe hard with toothed Kocher claws and he felt nothing at all except distant tugging up his shin.

Docent Mirecki came shuffling behind the canvas screen halfway through the bone-nibbling, tying a clean gauze mask beneath his pince-nez with fingers stained faint violet from gentian ink. Downstairs in his office someone had been telephoning; she could always tell when Germans had telephoned Mirecki, because a livid pink patch the size of a five-złoty coin stood out high on his left malar bone while the rest of his face went dough-pale.

"Clean marrow?" Mirecki murmured automatically in Latinized ward shorthand so the boy wouldn't panic at Polish butcher-words, peering over Sylwia's shoulder as she bevelled the first metatarsal shaft oblique with the rongeur.

"Punctate red bleeding two centimeters proximal to the joint line," Sylwia replied, snipping blackened extensor tendons high so they retracted cleanly out of the septic field and leaving the plantar flap loose over two tiny rubber glove-strip drains instead of tight sutures. "Lymphangitis streak stops below the malleolus. Give him four grams of Bronka's stolen Eleudron crushed in barley water over forty-eight hours and we save the tarsus." Only when young Antek had been wheeled back to his cot—staring down incredulously at a short white club of bandage where five black horn-claws had stunk all week, whispering clumsy thanks over Sylwia's red-cracked wrist—did she follow Mirecki into the narrow linen closet between Wards B and C and shut the pine door.

Stacks of grey boiled sheets smelled faintly of scorching iron and lye. Mirecki took off his pince-nez, polished both lenses furiously on the tail of his threadbare tweed waistcoat, and did not put them back on. Without spectacles his eyes looked naked, watery blue, and twenty years older.

"Aleja Szucha telephoned *Stabsarzt* Vesper's clerk ten minutes ago," Mirecki said quietly.

Sylwia's towel stopped moving between her wet palms. "Vesper isn't at Szucha. Vesper belongs to the Wehrmacht *Ortskommandantur*."

"Which is precisely why Vesper just rang me screaming until spit hit his mouthpiece," Mirecki said, leaning his narrow shoulders back against a shelf of unbleached pillowslips and closing his eyes briefly as a dull retrosternal ache tightened his jaw—not full angina, just the small cold iron fist he carried behind his fourth rib whenever Pawiak or Szucha entered a sentence. "An *SS-Hauptsturmführer* Kroll from *Abteilung Vier* inquired very politely why a Wehrmacht officer wounded inside the Warsaw municipal perimeter during an unnotified firearm incident on the ninth of February has not yet filed a signed deposition with Security Police ballistics."

*Kroll.* The exact name Marek had spoken two hours ago behind the vetch sacks on Bagno: *Kroll's desk asked the Wehrmacht duty officer twice why Towarowa barrier was closed and got hung up on.*

"And what did our courageous Munich gynecologist reply?" Sylwia asked.

"That *Oberstleutnant* Schoeler remains under toxic post-hemorrhagic wound fever on Lindleya and is strictly barred from non-medical questioning by joint order of *Sanitätsabteilung* and *Abwehrstelle Warschau* until Sunday," Mirecki said with a ghostly, bitter twitch of his moustache. "Whereupon Kroll thanked him most courteously and announced he will pay a brief pastoral visit to Pavilion Three on Sunday afternoon at four o'clock—merely to inspect municipal hospital admission ledgers from the night of the ninth and exchange three minutes' civility at the bedside."

Silence hummed inside the dark linen closet. Outside on Ward B somebody dropped an enamel bedpan with a ringing clang, followed by Bronka's carrying baritone cursing clumsy thumbs back to their mothers' bellies.

Sylwia calculated rapidly down the columns of the night register from February ninth. Between midnight and six a.m. only three admissions stood inked in old Walenty's copperplate hand: a sixty-year-old tramway widow with strangulated femoral hernia (dead Thursday morning, buried Saturday), *Oberstleutnant* Schoeler at 01:16, and *Gefreiter* Mekelburg at 01:55. The nineteen-year-old railwayman from Pańska hadn't come near Lindleya; his copper-jacketed Belgian slug sat right now inside Marek's pocket. On paper Pavilion Three was clean as snow.

Except Kroll wasn't coming to read old Walenty's handwriting. Kroll was coming because two German services had shot at each other in the dark behind an Ochota water-tower over a burned briefcase and twelve hundred Swiss francs, and caught squarely between their teeth was one Polish surgical pavilion full of counterfeit X-rays, hidden boys from Radom, and a Jewish bacteriologist downstairs feeding typhus lice on her own thighs under a dead Catholic schoolteacher's *Kennkarte*.

"Did you tell Schoeler?" Sylwia asked.

"How could I tell him? You had his chest open to the ribs ten minutes ago, and those two gorillas outside his door check my pockets for fountain pens!" Mirecki hooked his pince-nez back onto his nose with a tiny snap, looking straight at her through thick convex glass that magnified his pupils huge and frightened. "Sylwia—listen to me as an old man who knew your father before he went to Berlin. Whatever happened or did not happen when you were students on Luisenstraße—do not get clever between two German millstones. Patch his collarbone, sign Vesper's discharge slip the first morning that silk artery holds eighty paces, and get him out of my hospital before Kroll smells methylated spirit on our fingers."

"Ten days minimum before that vessel takes car jolts without blowing," Sylwia repeated evenly, hanging her damp hand-towel squarely on its brass peg. "Even for the Gestapo, collagen fibroblasts don't granulate faster than forty-eight microns a day, panie docencie."

Yet when she went down the back stone staircase at dusk toward the basement pathology Passage to sign off Antek's pus swab with Halina, passing twenty feet behind the locked east-wing swing doors, she heard through two oak panels the steady, dry scratch of a German sentry striking a match—and behind that second door, silent and sleepless on two horsehair bolsters with raw unanesthetized muscle burning beneath three turns of Polish calico, she pictured Ludwig Schoeler turning his yellow message pad face-up again on his knees, calculating Sunday at four o'clock four moves ahead of every living soul in the building.
