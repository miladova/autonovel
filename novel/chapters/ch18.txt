Marcus Learns a Word

Marcus discovers he has a sister the way he discovers most structural facts about his family: late, loudly, and in the wrong room. The room is the baths. The instrument is a dice creditor with a cousin at the fullers' guild, where a dowry for a fuller’s daughter has just been registered in circumstances the guild's clerk described, in drink, as *Baiae money, old money, Quirinal-handled* — the three adjectives travelling, as information does, faster disreputably than clean. It reaches Marcus between the warm room and the cold plunge, unattributed, deniable, and with Prima's trade attached like a label. He stands in the plunge with the water shocking up his body and the arithmetic shocking up his mind — faces at a doorway, a correction in a dining room about a sister — *his* sister, as it transpires, twice over — and does the first adult thing of his life: he does not say it aloud in the baths.

The creditor's name, for the record his father will never be offered in the study, is Lucius Hostilius, he is twenty-three, and he owes Marcus money at dice and pays in information because it is cheaper and, in the baths' economy, less taxable. He has the information today in the showy condition of a man who has carried it across three rooms without spilling the best part: still foaming. He delivers it at the exact moment the ball game breaks up, when young men are towelling themselves into temporary philosophers and a story can be tested against a whole audience of wet elbows.

“You'll like this,” Hostilius says, meaning the opposite, as men do when a story has a door in it other people may presently be invited to walk through. “The fullers' guild registered a dowry last week that their clerk — cousin of mine, a throat like a wine shop — says smells of the Quirinal through cedar. Baiae bequest, the papers say. Dead lady of property, remembering a laundress. Money for tools and a share in a bakery oven.” He could stop there. The baths, which have produced three false deaths in a fortnight and buried them at no expense, lean in as one man for the part they have really booked. “Only the bequest's papers were drafted in the Greek's outer office at the Basilica. The one whose small room —” He leaves the small room in the air between the warm room and the cold plunge like a song at the wrong speed, and young Avidius, who has been oiling himself with the industry of a man ballasting for weather, says without looking up, “Lucius. Plunge. Now. It's a rule with information.”

It is the first true act of friendship Marcus has been offered in a public place, and it arrives in the form of a shipping heir ordering a third party into cold water.

In the plunge Hostilius, penitent and blueing, gives him the rest of it the way men give evidence when the room has shrunk to a temperature: a needlewoman's name, a trade, a phrase in drink — *old money, Quirinal-handled* — a girl across the river who has been noticed, this winter, correcting senators' sons in dining rooms and being paid at a great house by the piece with the thread itemised separately. Avidius, up to his waist in the same water, helps the catching of it not at all, which is his own contribution: he becomes, for four minutes, furniture with a pulse. He does not say *I was in that dining room*. He does not say *my father's figures are about to be weather in your house*. He says, when Hostilius has been in long enough for the truths to cool to their right temperature: “You owe him the dice still. This is not a currency either of us banks.”

Marcus, whose education this winter has included, term by unpaid term, a returned necklace, a corrected sentence, a funeral at an edge, and the entire sound the Senate makes not answering, hears the arithmetic go up his mind the way it must once have gone up someone else's in a house with damp in one wall, counting with her mouth open on the number. *Nineteen.* A face at a doorway, with music. Dark brows. A girl who said *she isn't here to tell it wrong herself* about his sister — *his sister*, in every construction his tutors ever parsed, arriving now in duplicate like a verb with two endings and a will of its own.

The cold does the rest of his thinking for him, the way it did once for Acilius' counsel in another plunge on another floor of the family's plumbing. It clarifies. It makes the body's opinion of shouting unanimous. He climbs out, accepts the towel a slave hands him — Onesimus is not here today; this is the son's establishment, manned by a Felix of the rising generation who forgets at slightly cheaper rates — and says, to the room at large, in a voice he has never before used in public for the reason that until today he had nothing to spend it on: “A bequest from Baiae. Half the laundresses in Rome had mistresses there. The stairs alone would keep a guild of widows in property.” The laugh goes up where he has put it — at the stairs, never mind whose, at Baiae in the abstract, at the idea of laundry bequests as a class — and the story, deprived at its source of the only reaction that could have made it a fortune, subsides back into the baths' general weather: one more rumour in the steam, unminuted, walking home underneath its author's ribs at a pace very close to a religious procession already, except that no god Marcus has been brought up with uses doors in Transtiberim.

He goes home. He finds his father alone, which the household arranges for him with a speed that tells its own story; Servilia, it develops, has been scheduling this conversation since the oysters, and intends it to happen in a room with no knives in it. The study qualifies. There are only scrolls, and the dented mirror, and three centuries looking on from the corridor.

“Is it true?” Marcus says. He has rehearsed seventeen openings between the baths and the Palatine, each more forensic than the last. This is the one that survives the doorway.

Lentulus, who has had weeks, has rehearsed one, and it deserts him even faster. He says, “Yes.”

The boy sits down without being offered the chair, which in this house is an earthquake in a minor key. “The needlewoman. Daphne's — the woman at the house. Prima.”

“Vibia Prima, of Transtiberim. Betrothed to a baker. Nineteen.” Lentulus hears himself delivering a client's particulars and makes his voice change gear, at cost. “Her father, who raised her, is a month buried. Her mother, who raised her, is two years dead. They were decent people in the way that matters.” He is quoting a dead man's tablet. Quoting is what is left when the family runs out of original sentences.

“And the other mother,” Marcus says. It is to his credit — his father notes it with a grief that has pride in it like a stone in a shoe — that he does not make it an accusation. He makes it a request for the record.

“Antonia Cassia was a slave in my father's house. She was seventeen. I was twenty-four, which my family called a mitigating factor and history should call the whole charge sheet. When she was pregnant she was sold south on a morning I was at a muster, by a father settling a marriage — your mother's and mine, Marcus; your existence is minuted in this too, which is not your fault and is now, unavoidably, your information. The child was reported dead. The report was purchased. I asked once whether there was a child, and accepted an answer in statesman's Latin because the Latin was cheaper than the truth. She lived. She sews. She corrected your manners in her mother's dining room before either of you knew why it fitted.”

Marcus is looking at his hands. They are his mother's height and his father's appetite and for once entirely still. “All winter,” he says, “you've — we've — the committee. Her *house*. You inspected her house at dawn for a province. While my — while she was sewing the border in the next room.”

“Yes.”

“Did you know then?”

“I knew nothing then that I had not taken nineteen years to avoid knowing, which is a worse sentence than the one you asked for, so I will give you the plain one: no. I opened my father's tablet in February. Your mother has known since our first month, and married the settlement anyway, and if you intend to distribute blame in this house you will find the queue forms behind your grandfather and extends through me. It does not reach her.”

His mother comes in without knocking because in this house Servilia *is* the knock: rooms arrange themselves at her approach the way clients arrange their faces, and by the time she is inside the door, time has been made for her. She looks at her husband; at her son, in the junior chair for once, earned by earthquake; and at the scroll-case Lentulus has taken, somewhere in his son's geometry of standing up and sitting down, from the cupboard under his grandfather's cleaned face and set, unopened for the second time in a Lentulus's life, on the side table.

“He has it from the baths,” Lentulus says. “In the plumbing's version. Baiae money.”

“Then the baths are ahead of my schedule by a day,” Servilia says, “and behind my information by nineteen years. Marcus: your face is performing. Either arrange it or spend it. This is a difficult room to be young in; I apologise for the furniture.” She does not sit. She has never needed the room's permission for altitude. “Let us be clear in the first hand, since the second is where this family keeps its truths and the third melts quarterly. I knew your father had not come to our marriage as a blank tablet. I did not know a child lived across the river because your grandfather — whose wax is grinning in the corridor as if it had been cleaned for a festival — paid for my ignorance as he paid for almost everything: at a distance, through stewards, in a currency of other people's decades. Do you follow me? I am not the injured discovery in this study, Marcus. I am its management committee.”

Marcus looks at her with a young man's first, unscheduled respect, the kind no tutor can issue. “It doesn't reach you,” he quotes, because quoting is what is left when a family runs out of original sentences, and because his father said it nine minutes ago and Marcus is trying it for size in the room where it belongs. “The queue for blame.”

“It reaches me everywhere,” Servilia says calmly, correcting the sentence as a general corrects a map. “Blame is not the department. The department is consequences. Your sister, Marcus.” She says the word the way it is currently spent in this house, without additions, as though the singular were still legal tender; the room, taking it, knows exactly how much plural has just been managed. “Cornelia is to sign papers. Among the papers there will be settlements pointing in every decent direction: money to this house, a name to theirs, a roof re-roped, a father standing like a man who has given a daughter and not sold a storm. The Avidii have already surcharged this family for embarrassability — I believe the boy's father used the phrase *in figures*, which are his poetry — and I will not present them, on the doorstep of the registry office, with a stepsister in a fuller's guild for their cousins to rhyme with.”

“Then Cornelia is to be — not told,” Marcus says. He hears, even as he produces it, the family sentence: a young woman not told, by men who love her in the second column and file her in the first. “Father said: when the telling is for her benefit.”

“Your father,” Servilia says, “is learning a new ledger at an encouraging speed. Cornelia will be told by me, when she has a house of her own with a music room, a husband who pays her master properly, and a copy of every paper with her name in it in a box she can reach. That is not concealment, Marcus. That is timing purchased on behalf of a third party's safety, which is a thing this family is historically illiterate in and is, this season, taking tuition.” She looks at the scroll-case with the expression of a woman pricing furniture at an estate sale. “Will you show him the document?”

Lentulus opens it — not to read, because the tablet has already had that of him in a rainy atrium with nobody improving upon it — and holds it once in the light so that his son can see a dead man's large exact hand and the sentence at the end, which Marcus reads upside down, standing very still, like somebody else's ancestor in a dealer's office. *Choose better.*

“He had a style at the end,” Marcus says, finding, to his own horror, a laugh that is not a laugh at the end of it. “In a crisis, the Cornelii produce *style*.”

“Hold your temper with his ghost for the porticoes,” his mother says. “Here he is only the late management. We have the running of the place.”

“I wasn't in a queue,” Marcus says. “I was at the baths.” He stands up, sits down, a young man doing geometry with furniture. “Does Cornelia know?”

“No. Your sister is to be married in the summer into a family that has already surcharged us for embarrassability. She will be told —” Lentulus consults, visibly, the new ledger, the one kept between quarters “— when the telling is for her benefit and not our relief. That distinction is apparently the whole of morality. It has taken this family three hundred years to itemise it, and we owe the wording to a woman on the Caelian.”

Marcus laughs once, a sound with no herald before it. “She gives good advice, that woman. *Drink your wine slowly, Marcus.* I thought she was being a hostess. She was being —” He stops at the word. They both look at it, where it stands in the room like an uninvited client at the morning hour: not *mother* (not his), not *ruin* (not anymore), not *the woman*, that bath-house filing. Something for which his education, expensive and classical, has handed him only the Greek. “She was being kin.”

“Kin,” Lentulus says. “Yes. It is the word your family has been avoiding at the full rate of eloquence. Learn it cheaper than I did, Marcus. That is the whole of the inheritance I can improve on your grandfather: the wording.”

Dinner that night is an exercise in a new household discipline: the saying of everything except one thing, correctly, at length, and with music. Cornelia sings — one song, the Roman, at her father's request and, it is observable to anyone who knows the family's plumbing, at his *need*, which in this house is a denomination rarely allowed into the dining room undressed. She sings it beautifully. She has begun, since the Acilii, to take the kept-house song at a slower pace, the way a singer on the Caelian slows street tunes until they sound like truth at a funeral; and in the middle verse, where the woman in the poem *maintains* the household through the war, Cornelia puts in the unlicensed turn her master has given up correcting and Servilia has given up minuting, and Marcus, on his dining couch with a cup he is drinking slowly at a rate that has begun to attract the steward's attention, hears his sister the first sing himself a letter he is currently not writing.

He has written three since the study. They are in his desk under a commentary on sieges, in the place where his younger self kept racing programmes and IOUs.

The first began *Vibia Prima* and then, appalled by its own courage, spent four lines apologising for knowing the name before announcing, in Greek cadences, that he too was a victim of the Cornelii. He burned it in the brazier Servilia maintains against the household's feelings about cold, watching it go with the knowledge that self-pity, written down, looks remarkably like the family handwriting.

The second began with money. It offered money for everything: for the pyre, for the oven, for the unnameable decades, in sums his father's Greek would have had to launder through two bankers and a small war. He showed it to nobody, because even at twenty, with his education arriving to him nightly like despatches from a province he didn't know he governed, he heard his sister across the river, *in his ear's exact memory of a mending room*: “I might have been offered the dry one.” A sister is not a catastrophe to be purchased out of. The letter joined the first. The smoke went up the atrium in a manner Onesimus noted and forgot at the highest professional standard.

The third is not finished. It says *Prima* — not the guild name, not the baths name, the name decreed by a laundress in a ledger he has never seen, whose existence in the world, flat and unprefixed, is the bravest piece of paperwork executed in his family for three centuries. It says he stood at the procession's edge and will not describe it, because grief is not an audience and he is learning, late and at the full rate, which rooms his presence improves. It says he corrected nobody about his sister. It does not yet say which one. The sentence keeps opening inside the letter like a room in a banker's office: smaller truths first, a window in the expensive one. He writes *kin* in the draft and stares at it in Latin letters, too naked, then in Greek ones, too clothed, then puts the tablet away because dinner and the kept-house song and his father's face at the distribution of the courses are all performing a difficult, joint manoeuvre: a family covering a silence with everything it owns, including, at the last and for the first time on Marcus's watch, an attempt to deserve the silence it is keeping.

“You're not eating,” Cornelia says across the courses, in her new voice for saying things in doorways at full volume.

“I'm ballast,” Marcus says, and gets the reference past the room's inspectors by the skin of its sailing metaphors. “Ballast doesn't eat. It arrives.”

It gets her laugh — once, unissued, the sound she makes now in rooms she does not yet own but has begun to take the dimensions of — and his father, hearing both his documents laugh in two registers at one table, makes a decision in a room with knives in it, where it will not have to be scheduled by anyone: that when the time comes for letters, in this family, to be carried by the right messengers at the right rates, his son's third draft will be a better piece of seamanship than anything in the cupboard, undented mirrors included.

His father finds him the next morning in the garden, in the cold hour when the fish pond is still taking its instructions from the sky and Acilius, lately, has been doing his thinking on tiled floors in other houses.

“I have had the baths quieted,” Lentulus says, with no preamble — it is the new third-column manner, and sounds in his mouth like a difficult language improving. “Not by threat. By settling a dice account of yours before its owner could take it to market as conversation. It appears you then settled the same account in full, in coin, in a garden room.” He looks at the fish making their slow committee under the water's ceiling. “The household reports a surplus of honour. Your mother's office is disoriented. It has had to create a heading.”

“I wanted it to stop being a messenger,” Marcus says. “Money.”

“Money rarely accepts demotion so young.” Lentulus sits down on the bench his father used to fall asleep on between secrets, at an angle that makes room, and Marcus — sitting, uninvited, as if the study's earthquake had permanently changed the house's geology — looks across the pond at the wall his family has been being a name against for three centuries and says the thing that has been queuing in him since the plunge:

“I'm not going to her house. I thought you should have it in the first hand. I have drafts—” He shapes the plural like a man owning a cellar. “I have a third one that doesn't disgrace anybody in it. I'll finish it when it's for her benefit. I think I can now tell the difference between that and my relief, most days, with Onesimus checking my workings from two paces behind.”

His father looks at him then the way a man looks at a column in a courtyard he has lived beside for twenty years on the morning the scaffolding comes down: as though up to now he has been seeing the preparation and mistaking it for the architecture.

“When you do,” Lentulus says, “your mother's Greek will carry it. It has become the household's better hand. Choose better, Marcus.” The words arrive flat, unquoted, at the new rates — not a dead man's style any more, but house money, in circulation. Marcus files them under kin and goes in to breakfast, where Cornelia is prescribing the singer's breakfast to a kitchen that has survived four revolutions of regime already this winter and is prepared, at a word, to survive the Avidii.

The city, for its part, spends the following week trying to invoice him for the information. Hostilius tries again at the races, on a bench where the wind takes men's papers and gives them to gods with a taste for other people's losses; he has refined the story in the interval the way baths water refines a knife. Now it has a young laundress in it who is actually a vestal's niece, and a chair-committee, and an emperor laughing under a marble key.

“You see how it improves with handling,” Hostilius says, delighted. “Like your wine. Four hands and it's a classic.”

“It improves away from the truth,” Marcus says. The horses are running. Down on the sand the colours are doing what fortunes do in every generation: answering to their names in public while being owned elsewhere. “In my father's house we say: the second hand is where the giving happens. Yours is mostly taking.”

Hostilius, who is not a fool — foolishness is an expensive hobby and his is dice — looks at the heir of the Cornelii sideways over the rim of an afternoon and does the arithmetic of careers: a young man who has stopped retailing his household at other people's prices is either ruined or has become, in the third column, somebody. “You sound like a caulker,” he says, and young Avidius, on Marcus's other side, occupied with being ballast in a new installation, remarks to the weather: “It's a rule with menaces. They should arrive at your own prices or be filed as weather.”

Marcus pays Hostilius the dice debt that evening in full, in a garden room, with Onesimus in attendance and a receipt in silver-instead — the first time in their acquaintance that a payment between them has not been partly denominated in secrets. Hostilius counts it with the air of a banker invited, embarrassingly, to a wedding. “To what do I owe the full settlement?”

“To a family system,” Marcus says. “It has spread across the river. Money stops being a messenger and starts being money. Don't let it disorient the benches.”

Onesimus forgets the exchange at the full professional standard the instant it becomes necessary, and on the walk home — the slave two paces behind, occupying the exact back-stage geography he has occupied since Marcus's grandfather was the future — says, out of the plumbing of a house that is re-plumbing itself nightly: “In the old master's time, master Marcus, information in this household was freighted like grain. A lot of it spoiled in transit and nobody dared open the sacks. The young mistress” — he means Cornelia; the corridors have not yet been re-indexed, and Marcus feels the miss of the word in his sternum like a stair trodden wrong — “the young mistress is to have papers in a box. The master has a Greek doing sums at the street door. And now the heir pays a creditor in *coin*. One hardly knows the address.”

“I am learning a word, Onesimus. It has overheads.”

“Most of the useful ones do.” The old man falls back into his wake with his face doing, at veteran rates, very little indeed. “At Baiae the master once threw a sandal at a steward. I mention it because it cost him the set. He paid, in the end, for a new pair for the household, in a town where they could not be linked to the incident. That was the old management's idea of privacy, sir. New footwear, far away, unwitnessed.”

There is no information in it and Marcus knows, with the part of him Onesimus has been quietly educating from two paces behind since he was twelve, that it is a dowry of a kind: an old servant handing the third generation a story with no knives in it, chosen out of a cellar of sharper vintages. He takes it home and puts it, as one does with the household's assays, in the drawer over the unfinished third letter, where it sits for a week of dinners being ballast, of baths being managed, of a committee dissolving in the background of Rome like a cake in rain, until — on an evening when the house is between musical instalments and the spring has finally taken the city the way dye takes linen — he finds himself counting his way after dinner toward a street of olives and sofas, where a doorkeeper's shadow makes a small, governed area in the lamps in which a young man may stand choosing not to come in, at his own rate, with the thread entered separately.

He goes to the Caelian that night — the household will report this; households report everything; that is what the plumbing is for — but he does not go in. Barbatus is at the door in his weather of a body, and Marcus, who has swept past Barbatus forty evenings through sheer pedigree, stops on the threshold as at a temple he has not been purified for.

“Is she — Prima. Is she here, working?”

“Young master,” Barbatus says, in the tone he keeps for men deciding, “the mistress's needlewoman keeps guild hours now. Mourning. And the spring work's done at her own table, at her own prices. It's an arrangement the whole street admires.”

“She's to be married.”

“To the baker. Wedding's after the mourning, guild permitting. Good family. Mother like a customs office, which in a baker means the bread's honest weight.”

Marcus stands in the doorway of his father's committee's most famous casualty, a senator's heir with a sister sewing trousseau across the river and a word newly in his pocket with Greek roots and Roman consequences. From inside comes Daphne's voice, doing, for people who know, the Greek song. “Give the house my —” he says. He cannot find a noun small enough to be true. “Tell the mistress a client of the house is learning manners. She'll know the rate.”

“Young master has a face doing the geometry,” Barbatus says, when Marcus has been in the doorway long enough for the street to have filed him as part of the house's evening architecture. “I've seen men work out whether to come into an arena with less of it.”

“I'm not coming in. That is—” Marcus corrects himself, in a doorway, in front of a former gladiator, like a man saying a word in a language he intends to be examined in later. “I'm choosing not to come in. It isn't the same thing and I am collecting the difference, this season, at my own prices.”

“Thread separate,” Barbatus says equably. “Mistress's whole position.” He moves, by the six inches his shadow specialises in, so that a party of eastern merchants can be admitted round Marcus without either the merchants or Marcus having to become, for the door-book, an incident. The house receives them with its full, registered warmth. Inside, cutlery and a navigational conversation argue in another room like a marriage that is going to land its cargo. “Mistress will be told a young master was attending to his manners in the street like a client at a closed office. There's worse in the register. I've entered most of it, in my time. Some of it with swords.”

Marcus nearly laughs, and then Daphne's voice comes up through the house's back rooms in the way that trained voices do when the building is the instrument — first the rooms, then the courtyard, then the door, Greek, lower, travelled, for people who'd know — and the laugh dies in him respectably, because a song is arriving that he may no longer use as an entrance. It is a new deprivation for a senator's heir, being auditioned by his own restraint at a doorway he has swept through for a year by sheer pedigree. He finds, standing there in spring dark with a word in his pocket that has Greek roots and Roman consequences, that it is not deprivation. It is ballast. He has been collecting the metaphor ironically all winter; it comes home tonight as cargo, and arrives.

“Tell Daphne —” he says, and hears the whole house correct him in four generations of Eirenes and stewards and women who file kindness as thread. “No. Don't enter Daphne. The mistress only. A client of the house is learning its manners. She'll have the rate for that.”

“By the piece,” Barbatus agrees. “Master Marcus.” The name, in that doorway, delivered without the *young* in front of it like a small weapon laid down between honourable men, does more for the heir of the Cornelii than any tutorial invoice his family has ever paid. It has been a season of names being called at him correctly at last: by a singer who returned his glass, by a needlewoman who declined his cushion, by a mother who briefed him as an asset, by a doorkeeper with a past that Rome once charged admission for and who now decides, nightly, what constitutes an entrance.

“I'll tell her,” Barbatus says, “the vowel sounds and all.” And watches him go down the Caelian in the spring dark at a walking pace, which for Marcus, Barbatus reflects, is as close as the young ones get to a religious procession.

He walks home the long way, through streets where the winter is considering retirement and other people's daughters are being counted home by women with lamps. At his own door the slave on duty — not Onesimus, a junior officer of the same diplomatic corps — receives him with the house's current formula, lately amended in committee by his mother, and says only, “Master Marcus is returned,” to nobody in particular, in the tone for provincial appointments. It is entered nowhere. The atrium is quiet; the masks have the house to themselves and the house, for once, to his son. He stands among them in the dark longer than a thanksgiving requires and says, at the volume of a person opening a shutter, the word by itself, to see how it behaves in the room where his family keeps its faces.

“*Kin*.”

The atrium, being Roman, keeps the remark in the family. It has, after three hundred years, heard worse Latin.
