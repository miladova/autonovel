# Sublimate

*Warsaw, 1–6 July 1943*

Dawn after an air raid smelled of wet lime-plaster shaken loose by flak concussions, crushed linden blossoms, and ozone washed down into gutters.

Sylwia woke at twenty minutes to five without opening her eyes first, knowing where she was solely by the scratch of coarse grey Wehrmacht wool beneath her bare hip, the dull ache between her thighs where ten weeks of near-celibacy had collided twice with zero restraint against lean pelvic bone, and the steady, deep six-second rise and fall of ribs pressed warm along her spine.

Ludwig's left arm—scarred shoulder and all—lay heavy across her bare waist, fingers resting loose and possessive over the flat hollow of her stomach.

Carefully, millimeter by millimeter so iron cot springs didn't betray her, Sylwia lifted his wrist by two fingers, slid out onto cool oak floorboards, and carried her crumpled clothes barefoot into the adjoining white-tiled bathroom before looking into glass.

Above the chipped porcelain washbasin hung a beveled Venetian mirror left behind by the Silbersteins.

In pale four-forty-five pearl light, the woman staring back did not look thirty-three or surgical or composed: dark hair tangled wild down past bare collarbones with three steel *szpilki* pins still dangling crooked behind one ear; lips swollen dark plum-red and bruised along the lower vermilion border where stubble had rasped skin raw; two livid violet thumb-prints blooming low over her right iliac crest; and right in the hollow above her left clavicle—half an inch too high for an ordinary tailored blouse collar to hide unless pinned high with a brooch—a sharp crimson tooth-mark where she had arched backward at three in the morning while his mouth bit down to keep them both quiet under the open air-shaft.

Sylwia turned both cold brass taps full on, gasped silently as icy city-main water splashed over soapsuds, and washed fiercely from throat to knees over the porcelain bidet with a hard cake of grey Wehrmacht pumice-soap until skin burned bright pink and every trace of foreign salt vanished down Judge Silberstein's lead drainpipe.

When she buttoned her smoke-faint linen blouse all the way up to the top throat hook—hiding the collarbone bruise cleanly—and pulled the bathroom door open, Ludwig stood leaning shirtless against the dressing-passage doorframe in unbuttoned grey uniform breeches, barefoot on parquet, holding a plain white enamel mug steaming with clear boiled tap water.

Not Lisbon coffee. He remembered everything.

Across his own bare right trapezius and ribcage ran four angry red parallel nail-tracks five inches long where her ungloved mercury-rough nails had dug hard during the flak barrage, and above his missing collarbone segment the purple keloid star looked flushed deep wine-dark from four hours of reckless blood pressure.

Neither said *good morning*, and neither insulted the room with nervous apologies.

Sylwia took the hot enamel mug by its rim without brushing his fingers and drank three scalding swallows straight down to clear dry soot out of her throat.

"All-clear sounded at three-forty," Ludwig said quietly, watching her pin her dark hair back into its severe surgical twist above her nape with fast, ruthless stabs of steel wire. "Mekelburg drives back from Modlin around ten. Where are you sleeping tonight if Koszykowa's burned?"

"Third-floor night-interns' dormer cubby above Pavilion Three," Sylwia said, setting the empty mug on the hall chest and pulling on her charcoal jacket. "Two iron cots under the eaves nobody uses except during winter blizzards, behind thirty typhus-suspect beds and Bronka's padlock. If Marek's friends want to climb three flights of *Dzieciątka Jezus* after dark, they'll have to step over sixty coughing men first."

Ludwig studied her closed, pale face in grey morning light—measuring the exact thickness of clinical frost she was packing back into place around last night before stepping onto Warsaw cobbles, and refusing either to mock the frost or help her pretend nothing had broken open.

"Don't walk alone between pavilions after dusk," he said evenly, walking behind her down the silent hall to unbolt the iron security bar across Service Stair B's pantry door. "If *Inżynier Stach* hears from your engineer before noon today that you cracked his Dachau nephew open, Stach has forty-eight hours to either silence both of you or run across to Kroll for extraction."

Sylwia paused on the threshold with her Gladstone bag in her left hand. Six inches left of her shoulder sat little Róża Silberstein's five pencil height-marks notched into pine; ten paces behind Ludwig's bare scarred chest sat the rumpled grey army blanket still warm from both their skins.

"If Kroll pulls Stach inside Aleja Szucha alive," Sylwia said coldly, "Stach brings three years of Warsaw medical rosters in his head—including who treated railwaymen on Pańska."

"Which is why Stach won't reach Aleja Szucha alive if my afternoon note reaches Chłodna's church kneeler by noon," Ludwig answered with terrifying, matter-of-fact calm—meaning one three-line carbon slip from his typewriter into London's independent audit drop today put Polish counter-intelligence executioners onto *Inżynier Stach*'s doorstep before Stach finished packing a toothbrush! His winter-green eyes dropped once to her top blouse button hooked high at her throat, lingered half a beat on her bruised lower lip, and came back to hers. "Tuesday at five, *Kotarska*."

"If Vesper doesn't send me to Wola first—typhus broke out in the tram sheds yesterday," she said shortly, and went fast down the back stone steps into bright five-o'clock July sun without looking back.

Half an hour later, down in Pavilion Three's empty ground-floor sluice room before day nurses came on duty, Sylwia rolled both sleeves past her elbows, plunged bare hands and forearms into fresh one-in-a-thousand sublimate of mercury solution, and scrubbed with the stiffest hog-bristle bone-brush on the rack—one minute, three minutes, five minutes—until three winter fissures across her knuckles split wide open dripping bright pink threads into milky mercuric water.

Behind her wooden clogs clacked once and stopped. Siostra Bronisława's massive red hand reached over Sylwia's shoulder, plucked the hog-bristle brush cleanly out of her bleeding fingers without a word, tossed it clattering into the zinc basin, and wrapped a dry boiled linen towel tight round both raw hands.

Sylwia stood staring down at red spots soaking through white linen without lifting her chin.

Bronka looked sideways at the top throat-button fastened tight despite twenty-four-degree morning air, glanced once at the swollen plum mark on Sylwia's lower lip that no dental spoon had made, and let out a single low grunt through her gold eyetooth—containing zero convent sermonizing and forty-six years of Wola butcher-shop realism about what men and women did when death sat on every roof.

"Interns' cubby upstairs has clean ticking laid out already, pani doktor," Bronka muttered gruffly in Polish, pumping hot water into an enamel kettle for acorn coffee. "Old Walenty carried your spare coat and books over from Koszykowa in a laundry sack at six yesterday evening after your landlady said some fellow in grey smoked up your stove and left the door-latch loose. Drink something hot and sleep two hours before Mirecki comes in—you look like three days drowned."

* * *

By Sunday afternoon the fourth of July, Warsaw's heat had climbed to thirty-four degrees in the sun, turning the sprawling corrugated-iron roofs of the **Municipal Tramway Repair Workshops** out on ulica Młynarska in Wola into five-hundred-foot baking tins where air shimmered visibly above oily steel pits.

Normally municipal epidemic calls went to junior sanitary inspectors; when six skilled armature-winders and two lathe-fitters collapsed inside forty-eight hours at the main motor-rewinding bay—threatening Monday morning's tram schedule carrying forty thousand armaments workers across Warsaw—Stabsarzt Vesper panicked lest General Stahel blame *Sanitätsabteilung*, commandeered Docent Mirecki's fastest diagnostician plus "Panna Kwiatkowska" with her portable slide-box, and stayed safely outside in his open-topped staff car fifty yards upwind holding a lavender-scented handkerchief over his fat nose!

Inside Shed B, heat hit Sylwia's face like opening an autoclave door too early: forty degrees under rust-eaten iron skylights, reeking of hot transformer varnish, rancid whale-oil cutting fluid, sour unwashed shirts, and burnt ozone.

Along the rear brick wall on greasy tarpaulins lay four men in grey canvas overalls shivering violently under coats despite forty-degree furnace air, faces dusky mahogany-flushed, conjunctivae bloodshot red like rabbit eyes, dry brown sordes crusting cracked tongues, and across their sweaty flanks when Sylwia unbuttoned stiff grease-caked shirts—unmistakable dull rose-pink maculopapular spots fading momentarily on glass-slide pressure where older petechial hemorrhages had already turned dark purple.

Classic day-six louse-borne *Typhus exanthematicus*.

Standing six paces upwind beside two nervous German works guards stood *Herr Betriebsleiter* Direktor Krehl from Essen—sixty, iron-grey crewcut, gold Party badge pinned neat onto white linen dust-coat—flanked half a step rearward by **Inżynier Stefan Bielski**, sweating streams down plump grey cheeks as he held workshop attendance clipboards like shields in front of his stomach.

"Malingering hangover grippe half the time with these Wola fellows after payday, Frau Doktor," Direktor Krehl barked across the oily pit, refusing to come closer than twenty feet to the tarpaulins. "Whoever can't stand at a winding bench goes straight into the Skaryszewska sanitation transport lorry waiting outside the gate so healthy crews keep forty cars rolling by six a.m.!"

Sylwia didn't answer Krehl yet. Kneeling beside the youngest patient—a boy barely sixteen with shaved apprentice skull, burning past forty degrees and picking deliriously at invisible threads in the air (*carphologia*, classic typhus brain congestion) while two live grey body-lice crawled right out of his warm collar seam onto Sylwia's white sleeve cuff—she plucked both lice deftly with fine forceps into Halina's alcohol vial, checked the boy's spleen two finger-breadths palpable below left ribs, and stood up slowly.

Forty feet down the silent aisle of idle lathes stood sixty gaunt Polish fitters and blacksmiths watching in dead silence—caps in calloused hands, eyes sunken black in grease-smudged faces—waiting to see whether a Polish woman doctor speaking Berlin German signed four delirious comrades onto a Skaryszewska lorry (where typhus suspects got shot in gravel pits behind Lublin inside forty-eight hours rather than treated) while Stefan Bielski held the clipboard!

And at the front of that silent workman's rank, leaning one scarred knuckle on a turret-lathe tailstock while staring not at Krehl—staring cold, unblinking executioner's lead straight through the back of **Stefan Bielski's** damp white dust-coat—stood two lean forty-year-old Wola toolmakers Sylwia recognized instantly by posture alone as shop-floor *Armia Krajowa* industrial-sabotage cell elders.

Sylwia walked deliberately past Stefan—ignoring his pleading, terrified whisper (*"Sylwia, be reasonable, Krehl reports straight to Governor Fischer's transport office"*)—and stopped four paces from Direktor Krehl, holding up the clear glass alcohol vial where two fat grey *Pediculus humanus* lice floated legs-up at eye level!

Krehl took two hasty steps backward so fast his heel nearly went down into the grease inspection pit.

"Confirmed clinical *Fleckfieber* in second week of bacteremic rickettsial dissemination, *Herr Direktor*," Sylwia announced in ringing German clear enough for Vesper's clerk by the door to hear every syllable. "Load these four men into an open lorry through Warsaw streets or dump louse-infested overalls into Skaryszewska transit barracks, and inside twelve days three hundred Reich railway guards and every German passenger riding red front-compartments on Tram Lines One, Nine, and Seventeen comes down with forty-degree spotted typhus—with fifty percent mortality over age fifty!"

At *fifty percent mortality over age fifty*, sixty-year-old Krehl turned the exact grey-green of sour curds.

"Under Generalgouvernement Epidemic Decree Paragraph Seven," Sylwia pressed home mercilessly, pulling Vesper's pre-stamped municipal quarantine pad out of her pocket, "these four patients transfer immediately by closed municipal typhus-hearse to Infectious Ward Six at St. Stanislaus Hospital—*not* Skaryszewska—while Shed B closes forty-eight hours for mandatory hydrocyanic or sulfur delousing of every locker, overall, and tram cushion! And if I learn—as two men on that floor just whispered—that workshop clerks here have been docking bread-and-fat ration cards from feverish workers on day one so men with thirty-nine-degree typhus ride crowded trams to work three days shedding lice rather than starve their children, Stabsarzt Vesper's Monday epidemic report names *Wola Workshop Administration* personally to the Wehrmacht Garrison Surgeon!"

Out in the yard twenty minutes later, while municipal infectious-ward orderlies lifted all four typhus workers gently onto clean stretchers bound for Polish hospital beds at St. Stanislaus rather than SS gravel pits, Stefan Bielski caught Sylwia's elbow behind the brick transformer hut out of Krehl's sight, face mottled purple-white with panic and fury.

"Are you trying to put me against a wall, Sylwia?!" Stefan hissed, dabbing gray sweat off his moustache with a shaking silk handkerchief. "Krehl just told me if Shed B loses forty-eight tram-hours this week, Berlin audits *my* absentee sick-lists back to April! It wasn't my rule docking three-day bread cards—Ostbahn labor office ordered strict anti-absenteeism deductions in May, I merely counter-signed routine payroll sheets!"

Sylwia looked coldly down at his soft pink fingers gripping her white sleeve until he dropped his hand.

"Look behind you through that doorway at the third turret lathe on the left, Stefan," she said very quietly in Polish. "See those two toolmakers in blue caps watching your back right now? Four workers from your April sick-sheets never came home from Skaryszewska—two died at Majdanek in June. Keep counter-signing Krehl's absentee lists to save your green *Kennkarte* butter ration, and German auditors will be the very least of Wanda's widow troubles before autumn."

She climbed back into Halina's municipal sanitary van without waiting for his answer, leaving Stefan standing alone beside humming sixty-thousand-volt transformers in blinding July dust.

* * *

At five minutes to five on Tuesday the sixth of July—six days since the air-raid storm—Sylwia climbed Service Stair B at Królewska Sixteen with dry palms, clean unwrinkled grey linen, hair pinned steel-tight, and every defensive wall inside her skull mortared six bricks thick.

On the walk across Marszałkowska she had sworn three separate iron rules to herself:
1. Twenty minutes strict clinical check, sitting upright in wooden chairs.
2. Zero step past the study threshold toward that back dressing-room passage.
3. Treat Wednesday night exactly for what wars made it: two trapped animals colliding under flak guns when lightning knocked out lights—finished at dawn Thursday without romantic drivel or bourgeois encore.

Bruno Mekelburg opened the pantry door wearing his usual clean braces and calm country face—zero sly grin, zero awkwardness, handing her a clean glass of cold well-water from an earthen pitcher because Warsaw sat at thirty-two degrees today and walking from Lindleya left salt damp along her temples.

"He's in the study, *Frau Doktor*," Mekelburg said, picking up his market string-bag from the table. "I'm going down to Hale Mirowskie for sour cherries before they close—back at quarter to six."

The pantry door clicked shut behind Mekelburg's boots, leaving forty-five empty minutes echoing loud down sixty feet of sunlit oak parquet.

*Damn the boy to East Prussian marshes,* Sylwia cursed silently, setting her half-drunk water glass down hard on the tiles and walking straight down the hall into Judge Silberstein's study with her chin up and her Gladstone bag held in front of her like an iron shield.

All four windows stood open behind drawn thin ecru net curtains today to catch faint garden breeze through chestnut leaves; bright golden five-o'clock light flooded across Biedermeier mahogany and bare floorboards without a single shadow left to hide inside.

Ludwig stood at the side filing-table in grey uniform trousers and thin white cotton sleeveless undershirt—heat had stripped even German colonels down to vests behind closed shutters—burning two short teleprinter ribbons over the wire basket with a brass petrol lighter. In sleepless full daylight, tanned across neck and shoulders from two days' open-car inspection out toward Modlin, scarred left clavicle dipping asymmetric above lean corded pectoral muscle, he turned his head as her leather heels stopped three paces inside the open doorway.

Five seconds of dead silence stretched across sunlit parquet while two pairs of eyes measured each other bare in broad five-o'clock light—neither drunk, neither terrified by sirens, every remembered detail of Wednesday night sitting stark and undeniable right on top of the sunlit air between them.

"Close the hall door," Ludwig said quietly, snapping his brass lighter shut and brushing black ash off his fingers. "Draft blows ash onto the rug."

Sylwia kicked the double study door shut without turning her back, walked briskly to the straight wooden chair beside the desk, and didn't sit down.

"Before you ask about my stairs," she said in crisp, clipped Berlin ward-German, "nobody from Section Two came near Lindleya or Koszykowa all week. Have your Szucha typist carbons shown why?"

Ludwig walked round to the front of Judge Silberstein's desk and picked up two narrow pink teleprinter duplicate slips clipped together with a steel pin, handing them across without touching her fingers.

Sylwia read four lines of terse German police shorthand dated *Montag, 5.VII.43, 07:40 Uhr*:

*Polizeirevier XII (Mokotów) meldet Leichenfund ul. Puławska 94, Hinterhof: poln. Zivilingenieur Stanisław Kownacki (geb. 1898), Genickschuss aus Kal. 9mm Parabellum. Keine Papiere entwendet. Referat IV N übernimmt Vorgang unter Sperrvermerk (V-Mann "Uhrzeiger" liquidiert).*

*Stanisław Kownacki — "Inżynier Stach."* Found at seven-forty Monday morning in a Mokotów back courtyard with a single nine-millimeter Parabellum execution round through the base of his skull, wallet untouched—signature sentence of *Armia Krajowa*'s Special Court once London's Thursday Chłodna audit cross-checked Marek Wolski's sudden horrified revolt!

"Your engineer didn't run," Ludwig observed calmly, leaning back against the stout front edge of Judge Silberstein's mahogany desk two feet from where she stood holding the pink slip. "Once you cracked his boss's Dachau nephew open on Wednesday, he checked the Danzig mail dates himself inside forty-eight hours and handed *Inżynier Stach* to your own executioners on Sunday night."

"Which means Kroll knows since seven-forty yesterday morning that *Uhrzeiger* was unmasked from German files," Sylwia said sharply, laying the pink slip back onto green blotter leather. "And Marek himself—"

"—is running underground on false papers from *both* Kroll's vengeance teams and his own frightened committee until they decide how much he leaked unwittingly," Ludwig finished. "He won't touch Lindleya while Stach lies dead with nine-millimeter proof round his neck—unless Kroll catches Marek alive first and squeezes him on Szucha."

Silence fell back across the bright room—out of politics, out of dead traitors, leaving twenty-nine minutes on the brass desk-clock and two feet of sun-warmed July air between her grey linen skirt and his grey wool knees braced wide against mahogany.

"Turn toward the window so I can check whether you tore deep fibers Wednesday night," Sylwia said flatly, reaching toward her Gladstone clasp for clinical armor.

Ludwig didn't turn toward the window.

His right hand reached out slow—giving her three whole seconds to step backward or slap him off if she genuinely wanted twenty minutes of medical theater—and closed warm and steady round her bare wrist before her fingers touched the leather bag clasp, drawing her half a step forward until the hem of her grey linen skirt brushed between his spread knees against the desk edge.

"Nothing tore Wednesday night," Ludwig said low, winter-green eyes looking straight into hers in unsparing golden daylight, zero hiding-place left on earth. "Except whatever ridiculous vow you just spent thirty minutes rehearsing on the walk down Marszałkowska."

Colour flared hot and furious across Sylwia's high cheekbones—hating to death how effortlessly he read the three iron rules behind her forehead before she opened her mouth!

"Let go my wrist, Schoeler. Your corporal comes back with cherries at quarter to six, and it's broad daylight."

"Thirty-eight minutes," he murmured, thumb sliding up her inner forearm across the exact pulse point jumping rapid beneath pale mercury-chapped skin while his left hand came up gently—shockingly gentle for a man built out of wire and spite—to unhook the single hot mother-of-pearl button fastened tight under her chin, parting her linen collar half an inch in warm sun to uncover the fading violet-yellow shadow of his own Wednesday night tooth-mark above her left collarbone.

Sylwia's breath caught dry in her throat; she didn't step back.

Looking down at that fading purple mark on her pale Polish skin in full daylight did something dangerous to Ludwig's face—tightening his jaw white while darkening his eyes to deep river-slate. Slowly he bent his head six inches and pressed his warm, dry mouth—no teeth today, open lips silent and burning hot—directly over the fading bruise he had left on her collarbone, breathing her skin in through soap and summer salt while both hands slid round her waist to lift her sitting onto the cool cleared edge of Judge Silberstein's mahogany desk right over German teleprinter ash.

Rule One, Rule Two, and Rule Three went up like dry straw touched to phosphorous.

Sylwia's hands didn't push his shoulders off—they locked fierce into the damp dark-blond hair at the back of his skull, pulling his mouth straight up from her collarbone onto hers in blinding five-o'clock sun while her knees parted shamelessly round his lean waist on polished mahogany! No blackout sirens covered a sound today: every slick catch of breath, the sharp metallic rasp of his trouser fly-buttons giving under both their hands together in sunlit quiet, her grey linen skirt bunched above bare thighs pale above dark lisle stocking-tops while she tore thin cotton drawers aside rather than waiting ten seconds to step out of them!

Standing braced between her spread thighs at the desk edge in white sleeveless vest, Ludwig gripped both bare undersides of her buttocks in hard surgeon's palms, tilting her pelvis forward off smooth mahogany into broad green-gold afternoon light where both of them saw everything—her dark eyes wide open and black-dilated on his, his heavy flushed length sliding hot through slick folds straight home inside her in one slow, devastatingly deep push while neither looked away by half an inch!

Sylwia bit hard onto the heel of her own hand to muffle the sharp moan rising up her chest—chestnut leaves rustling ten feet away outside net curtains, German boots clicking faintly down Królewska pavement two floors below!—until Ludwig pulled her wrist away from her teeth and covered her mouth with his own, swallowing every ragged sound directly down his throat while moving inside her with long, deep, unhurried strokes twice as lethal in daylight silence as storm frenzy had been in the dark.

Every slow roll of his hips ground rough wool breeches against her bare inner thighs and pressed heavy, inescapable friction square across her clitoris; wrapping both stockinged legs tight round his waist and hooking bare heels behind his belt-loops, Sylwia arched back onto one elbow among Silberstein's inkwells—blouse pulled wide off one bare breast gleaming white in sunlit green reflection—watching Ludwig's eyes drop half-dazed to her breast before his head bent to take her nipple hot into his mouth while his right thumb slid wet between their joined bodies to stroke tight, merciless circles right where he stretched her widest!

She shattered inside ninety seconds in total, suffocating silence—biting clean through the cotton shoulder-strap of his undershirt with locked teeth while tears of pure physical overload stung hot behind her lashes and fierce rhythmic contractions clenched so hard round him that Ludwig's breath tore ragged against her breast, gripping the mahogany desk-lip white-knuckled beside her hips as he drove four deep, shuddering thrusts home and spent hot inside her with his damp forehead dropped heavy against her bare sternum.

For sixty quiet seconds sunlit dust motes drifted lazy gold across books and bare thighs while downstairs in the courtyard someone beat a carpet rhythmically—*whack, whack, whack*—with a wicker beater.

At twenty-four minutes to six—nine whole minutes before Mekelburg's boots touched back stairs with sour cherries—Sylwia stood washed, combed, buttoned neat to the throat in front of the Venetian mirror, every hairpin straight as bayonets, sliding her untouched stethoscope back into her bag.

When she walked out past Ludwig leaning clothed against the pantry lintel, neither pretended this was the last time anymore.

"Friday at five," Ludwig said quietly.

"Bring boiled water and lock the desk," Sylwia answered without smiling, and walked down into baking July sun feeling utterly steady, utterly damned, and sharper awake than she had felt since twenty-two.
