An Inquiry
A death in a dining house belongs, by the city's old and flexible anatomy, to several jurisdictions at once, which means it belongs to whoever moves first with the best Latin. By the second hour of the morning the dead man's uncle the senator has written to the urban prefect; by the third, Antonia's physician has deposited, at her instruction, a sealed account with a notary she has paid for years precisely against the day someone's heart would table a motion. By noon the baths have decided that the man was poisoned by a rival for a singer's favours, cuckolded by a tax farmer and carried out singing, three deaths, all false, all immortal. Rome does not mind an inquiry. Rome minds having nothing to say while it proceeds.

Lentulus receives the news at his clients' hour, in the mouth of a freedman who brings petitions and relishes disaster the way pastry cooks relish saffron: professionally, and more than he admits. He dismisses the man mid-flourish. He stands in his own atrium among the masks with the information arranging itself in him like troops: his committee, her house, a corpse, the city's tongue. He is aware — he will be able to date the awareness later, to this minute, to the quality of light through the compluvium — of a feeling he cannot at first name because it has arrived wearing the wrong uniform. It is not satisfaction. It has the shape of its opposite, hurrying.

Servilia, who has also heard — Servilia hears everything at the speed other people take to decide whether to repeat it — says from the doorway, “Do not go there.”

“I have no intention of going there.”

“You have every intention of being the man Rome watches not going there. It amounts.” She comes into the room. “If you press this now, you will look like a creditor applauding a fire. If you defend her, you will look like—” She stops, not for want of the word.

“Like a man who inspects a house at dawn and eulogises it by noon.”

“Like its patron,” Servilia says, with surgical calm. “The Avidii will read it. The emperor's secretaries will read it. Your daughter, Lentulus, will read it, in the faces at dinner for the rest of her unmarried life.”

“So I am to let the prefect's men establish whether a man died of his heart or of her fish, and my committee is to master the antiquarian skill of silence.”

“You are to let the truth be established by men who do not love or hate in that street. It is a short list. The physician is on it, I understand, because *she* had the foresight to employ him before she needed him. There is a lesson in that for families who arrange their credit after the vintage.”

She goes. The masks remain. Lentulus looks up at his grandfather — Gnaeus, naturally, the dented-mirror Gnaeus, under whose cupboard a sealed tablet has been ripening for nine years — and has the clearest thought of his morning, the kind a man buries standing up: *If I open it, it is because a man has died at her table. Everything in this family requires a death to become sayable.*

He does not open it. He sends, instead, an unsigned note by a slave who is not his usual messenger, which is the most signature-like act available to a Cornelius: *Keep the physician's account sealed until the prefect asks. Answer in the order of the evening, not the order of the questions.*

It is good advice. It is also, and he knows it before the slave has left the street, the first unpaid thing he has done for Antonia Cassia in twenty-one years, and it sits in him for the rest of the day like a coin swallowed in childhood: unnoticed by the world, impossible to forget the swallowing of.

The prefect's men are correct, which Antonia prefers; malice she can price, correctness she can plan around. They view the dining room. They view the kitchen, where the pastry cook, misunderstanding the visit in a way she chooses to believe is genuine, offers them almond cakes of a quality that will enter the record nowhere and influence it everywhere. They take the physician's account. They take Philomela's statement, and Philomela — who has sung for these men's superiors and remembers the programme — gives it in the order of the evening, as a certain unsigned advice has suggested, though Antonia would saw her own lyre in half before admitting that the order was not her own idea an hour before the note arrived.

“You keep a physician on retainer,” the senior man says.

“I keep a house full of rich men eating dormice in a heat they complain of and pay extra to sit in. A physician on retainer is not foresight. It is tableware.”

“And the dead man owed you money.”

“Diomedes.” The tablet comes, the upright hand, the small sum, a dinner in December and a loan against a racehorse, stamped, witnessed, uninteresting as prayer. “He owed me less than his uncle spends on mourning. If I poisoned debtors of this size, officer, Rome would need a second Tiber.”

The man almost smiles, files the smile under conduct unbecoming, and closes the tablet. At the door, with the correctness that has carried them both through the day like a shared umbrella, he says, “The prefect will find a death by misadventure, madam, if misadventure is what the account supports. The prefect is not influenced by committees.”

“Tell the prefect the house appreciates inhabiting the same city as his principles.”

When they have gone, Eirene says, “The note helped.”

“The physician helped. The cakes helped. Notes are just handwriting that has been for a walk.” Antonia puts the note in the box with the tax receipts, beside its predecessor, between the quarters: a small, growing archive of a language neither of them writes in anymore and both of them still read fluently. “Barbatus said the widow asked for me.”

“She asked whether he suffered and whether he was — alone with anyone, at the end. Barbatus said he died laughing at a story about a tax farmer, in a room full of witnesses, with a singer holding his shoulders. She said, 'He would have liked the witnesses.' Then she cried, he said, like a person opening shutters.”

“Send her the December bill,” Antonia says. “Marked settled. And flowers that cost less than the bill, so it doesn't look like arithmetic.”

Eirene goes to arrange the flowers that are not arithmetic. Antonia sits a moment in the emptied dining room under the hanging with the red border, listening to her house's heart resume its ordinary rhythm, and allows herself — the room is empty, the accounts are straight, a man is dead who died laughing and there are worse inscriptions — one unfiled thought, arrived in a familiar hand: *In the order of the evening, not the order of the questions.* It is how you tell any story if the telling is to save you. It is how she has never yet told hers.
