# Ether and Coal Dust

*Warsaw, 10–11 February 1943*

The corporal's fourth and fifth fingers hung by gray ribbons of flexor tendon and dirty skin, black with cordite tattooing where a bullet had smashed the steering wheel rim of the *Kübelwagen* into splinters of vulcanite and steel right inside his grip. His ring-finger metacarpal head was crushed into wet gravel.

"Sit back against the wall if your ears ring," Sylwia told him, drawing up two percent procaine—Polish *Polfa* prewar stock with sediment specks at the bottom of the brown bottle, hoarded for digits and teeth because general anesthesia cost forty grams of ether—into a glass Record syringe whose plunger leaked backward across her thumb unless she held it crooked. "I'm blocking the ulnar and median nerves at the wrist crease. It stings like boiling grease for ten seconds and then goes wooden."

"Kein Äther," the boy muttered. Without his reversible winter hood he looked even younger, ears sticking out red and chapped from aregulation Wehrmacht haircut shaved white above the neck-bone, nose broadened slightly at the bridge where a horse or a sergeant had kicked him years ago. With his good right hand he unbuckled his belt slightly, pulled out his gray metal identity disc only far enough for Bronka to copy the stamped characters onto the yellow Wehrmacht casualty slip—*Mekelburg, Bruno, Gefr. — St.Kp. Nachr.Ers.Abt. 1*, a bland Königsberg signals replacement depot tag that fooled nobody whose officer rode around Ochota burning briefcases at midnight—and tucked the tin oval straight back inside his damp wool shirt. "Ich muss wach bleiben. Bis unsere Leute da sind."

"Your people telephoned twenty minutes ago from Saski Square," Docent Mirecki said from the radiator, where he sat rubbing his blue-veined shins and pretending for dignity's sake that his hands weren't shaking now that the adrenalin had ebbed out of his cherry spirit. "Two military policemen from *Kommandantur* are already smoking in my vestibule. Nobody is going to carry your *Oberstleutnant* off up the chimney while Frau Doktor tidies your stumps."

Mekelburg didn't answer Mirecki. Peasants from East Prussia—Masurian borderlanders half the time, with grandmothers who cursed cows in dialect Polish while voting German in plebiscites and sending grandsons to shoot Poles—knew instantly who ran a kitchen or a slaughter-shed. His pale, lashless eyes stayed fixed on Sylwia's face while she slid the needle point beneath his palmaris longus tendon.

He didn't flinch when the procaine went in, only clamped his back molars until the masseter muscle jumped under his stubbled jaw. While the block took hold, Sylwia scrubbed the shredded left palm mercilessly with warm sterile soap-water and steel forceps, picking five jagged crumbs of black steering-wheel ebonite out of the hypothenar muscle belly.

"What blew the glass in?" she asked conversationally in German, testing the web of his little finger with mouse-tooth forceps until she saw he felt pressure without pain. "Grenade or pistol?"

"Snowbank," Mekelburg said flatly. "Skidded into an iron post."

"Iron posts in Ochota spit nine-millimeter Parabellum jackets now?" She snipped the dead fifth ray cleanly disarticulated at the metacarpophalangeal joint with bone-cutting forceps—*snap*, cartilage gleaming pearly blue-white under the acetylene lamp before dark venous ooze filmed it over—and rongeured the shattered head of the fourth metacarpal smooth on a bevel so when skin flaps healed over the palm edge he wouldn't have a tender bone spur jabbing every shovel handle or rifle stock he gripped for the rest of his life. "Keep that story for your company clerk, *Gefreiter*. If an iron post also shot your commander through the left infraclavicular fossa at downward forty-degree trajectory from an upper window or railway embankment, tell him he was lucky the iron post shot high."

Mekelburg's breath hitched once—not at the bone-nibbler crunching his fourth knuckle away, because the procaine held solid, but at *downward forty-degree trajectory*. Slowly his eyes dropped to her swift hands fashioning a palmar skin flap—saving every scrap of tough, blood-rich volar pad from the ruined ring finger before discarding the dead bone, folding thick hairless palm skin upward over the raw amputation stump so the scar line would lie dorsally out of his grip.

"Why are you turning the skin from underneath like that?" he asked hoarsely.

"Because you drive cars and lift crates with the front of your hand, not the back," Sylwia said, driving curved cutting needles through tough horny dermis with a rhythmic *chirp* of linen thread. "Put a scar in the crease of a working man's palm and it splits open every frost. You have a thumb, an index, and a middle finger untouched. Keep them clean for ten days and you'll still button your own trousers and hold reins. Leave the bandage soaked in axle grease and you'll lose the wrist to tendon-sheath pus by Lent."

For ten seconds the boy watched her knot off the sixth interrupted stitch, clip the tails cleanly to four millimeters, and dust yellow sulfanilamide powder sparingly along the seam before wrapping the ulnar half of his hand in gauze over a boiled wooden tongue-depressor splint. Something cautious and animal shifted behind his pale eyes—not gratitude, nothing so soft or treacherous, merely the slow calculation of a farm dog discovering the vet hadn't drowned the litter.

"Er hat Ihren Namen nicht gesagt," Mekelburg murmured suddenly, too low for Mirecki by the radiator to catch over the hiss of the Primus stove. *He didn't say your name.* "Im Wagen nicht. Nur dass Lindleya vierhundert Meter näher liegt als die Koszykowa-Wache, als mir das Lenkrad wegflog. Und dann auf dem Flur—" He stopped himself short, jaw snapping shut as if he had already given away three pfennigs too much.

Sylwia tied off the roller bandage around his wrist with a sharp double tuck that made him wince despite the nerve block. *He knew Lindleya was four hundred meters closer.* Meaning Ludwig Schoeler had been conscious enough in the back of a shot-up *Kübelwagen*, with two fingers of his own right hand stuffed under a bayonet strap into his spurting collarbone hole, to navigate Warsaw streets by memory in a blizzard—without having the faintest notion she worked inside Pavilion Three. Why should he? Last he knew of Sylwia Kotarska, she was boarding a night train to Poznań in March nineteen-thirty-three with two battered leather suitcases and four Reichsmarks sixty in her purse. Warsaw had a million and a quarter souls before the bombs and nine hospitals left standing. Sheer blind arithmetic of bleeding arteries and street corners had dumped him onto Table One.

She found she hated sheer blind arithmetic almost as much as design.

At half-past seven, when dirty pearl daylight was leaking through the frosted upper panes and city current had come back weakly enough to turn the ceiling bulbs the colour of stagnant tea, Stabsarzt Doktor Kurt Vesper arrived in great state.

Vesper always smelled from six paces away of pink Odol tooth-water,Yardley lavender brilliantine looted from Paris in forty, and last night's cognac sweating sourly through fat white pores around his nostrils. Before the war he had owned a private gynecological sanatorium in Schwabing where Munich brewer's wives went for rest cures and discreet curettages; three years in the Generalgouvernement had thickened his neck over his velvet-edged Wehrmacht collar without teaching him which end of a Thomas femur splint went toward the hip. Behind him marched a tall, humorless Wehrmacht captain wearing *Abwehrstelle* staff tabs who introduced himself to nobody, and two military policemen with MP40 submachine guns slung across their chests.

"Unheard of," Vesper announced in the corridor outside Room Fourteen, slapping soft pigskin gloves against his riding breeches while Mirecki bowed three degrees deeper than strictly required by Generalgouvernement medical ordinance. "Utterly irregular, Herr Kollege Mirecki! An officer of *Oberstleutnant* rank brought into a Polish municipal pavilion without prior clearance from *Sanitätsabteilung Warschau*! My motor-ambulance from *Reserve-Lazarett Eins* is backing up to your ramp as we speak—stretcher crew waiting, heated cabin, German nursing sisters—"

"Nobody moves him today," Sylwia said.

She had not changed out of her operating smock; dried rust-brown spatters stippled the front from breastbone to hem because clean smocks came round on Mondays and today was Wednesday. She stood blocking the half-glass door of Room Fourteen with a tin temperature clipboard crooked under her elbow and dark purple hollows bruised deep beneath both eyes from thirty hours awake.

Vesper puffed his damp upper lip out beneath his clipped toothbrush moustache. He disliked Sylwia intensely—partly because her Berlin German made his Bavarian vowels sound like beer-hall cabaret, partly because last November she had corrected his diagnosis of an "acute appendicular phlegmon" in a Luftwaffe paymaster by pulling a ruptured ectopic pregnancy out of the paymaster's Polish mistress on Table Two while Vesper stood blinking in galoshes.

"Frau Doktor Kotarska," Vesper said stiffly, raising his chin for the benefit of the silent *Abwehr* captain beside him, "your emergency intervention last night is noted and will be entered favorably in the quarterly municipal hospital report. However, post-operative care of senior Wehrmacht officers belongs exclusively under Reich medical supervision on Ujazdowskie—"

"Look at the chart before you sign his death warrant, *Herr Stabsarzt*," Sylwia interrupted, holding out the clipboard without yielding an inch of doorway. "Subclavian-axillary arterial transition severed two-thirds across by jagged steel casing. Primary end-to-end Carrel anastomosis in five-zero silk over a two-centimeter clavicular resection, completed five hours ago without heparin because there isn't a vial of Liquemin between here and Breslau. His systolic pressure is ninety-four over fifty-eight. Do you know how thick the wall of a human axillary artery is after intimal trimming, *Herr Stabsarzt*?"

Vesper's pink eyelids blinked rapidly. Gynecologists tied uterine arteries blind in broad ligament fat with #2 catgut thick as grocer's string; he hadn't touched a major peripheral arterial repair since his state exam under Lexer in nineteen-twenty-six. "Naturally—vascular wall fragility is well understood—"

"One millimeter point two," Sylwia said coldly. "Right now a fresh platelet fibrin cuff less than the thickness of wet cigarette paper is sealing twenty-four silk needle holes while every heartbeat hammers ninety millimeters of mercury against it, without a plaster shoulder spica because plaster hides secondary hemorrhage. Bounce him three kilometers east down Nowogrodzka and across Marszałkowska tram-rails in a springless field ambulance this morning, and stitch six tears through bruised media before you pass the church of St. Alexander. He will dump three liters of blood into the dressing in ninety seconds flat. By the time your orderlies open the rear doors at Ujazdowskie, you'll be lifting out a corpse."

She held out her cheap steel fountain pen, uncapped, nib pointed squarely at Vesper's chest buttons.

"Sign the transfer release right here across the top of my operative note, *Herr Stabsarzt*, accepting sole clinical responsibility over surgeon's contraindication. I'll have two porters bring him down to your ambulance in four minutes."

Silence dropped over the tiled corridor, broken only by water knocking inside the half-warmed steam pipe along the wainscot and a distant coughing fit from the Polish ward upstairs.

Beside Vesper, the tall *Abwehr* captain spoke for the first time. He had thin gray hair combed flat across a narrow Prussian skull and eyes like two tarnished pfennigs. "How many days before an arterial suture of that class withstands transport, Frau Doktor?"

"Ten days minimum if it stays afebrile," Sylwia said directly to the captain, ignoring Vesper altogether. "Fourteen to twenty-one if anaerobic infection flares in the open muscle pocket—and it will flare, because whoever blew your car window in drove dirty overcoat wool down against his brachial plexus. Move him early and either the vessel blows out from bacterial mycotic softening of the suture line, or a mural thrombus shakes loose and plugs his left middle cerebral artery."

The captain looked past her shoulder through the wired glass panel of Room Fourteen, where Mekelburg sat upright on a wooden stool beside the white iron bed, splint resting across his knee, watching the door handle like a mastiff. Actually, keeping a wounded *Abwehr* officer tucked inside a quiet, sealed six-room annex on Lindleya with two sentries at the corridor doors—rather than on an open officers' surgical ward at *Lazarett Ujazdowski* where thirty Wehrmacht doctors, SD liaison clerks, and Red Cross visitors wandered past every bed—appeared to strike the narrow-skulled captain as unexpectedly convenient.

"My chief in Berlin will wish *Oberstleutnant* Schoeler alive and lucid rather than ceremonially housed, *Herr Stabsarzt*," the captain told Vesper softly. "We will post a twenty-four-hour two-man guard at the swing doors of this wing and one inside Room Fourteen. Clear Rooms Twelve, Thirteen, and Fifteen completely. Only Docent Mirecki, Frau Doktor Kotarska, and one vetted scrub nurse you personally vouch for are to enter Room Fourteen until *Oberstleutnant* Schoeler can sit upright in a staff car."

Vesper swallowed hard, rescuing his dignity by puffing out his chest and waving his pigskin glove magnanimously at Mirecki. "Just what I was about to propose on inspection of the pulse curve, Hauptmann! Quite out of the question to jolt fresh Carrel silk over winter potholes—elementary vascular prudence! Docent Mirecki, you answer to me with your head for asepsis and security in this corridor. I shall inspect the wound personally every forty-eight hours."

"Every privilege, Herr Stabsarzt," Mirecki murmured, bowing until his pince-nez swung free on their black ribbon.

Sylwia capped her fountain pen with a sharp click. Behind her back, through four inches of painted hospital pine, she could almost feel the silent geometry of Room Fourteen tightening around Pavilion Three like wire twisted round a parcel: three German MP40s permanently stationed forty feet below the Polish wards where two forged typhus charts and a boy from the Radom railway sabotage ring were currently hiding under false appendectomy scars.

And on the iron bed inside, kept breathing by twenty-four tiny bites of black vaselined silk she had put in with her own steady hand, lay Ludwig.

*Berlin, October 1928*

In the autumn before the big freeze cracked the water mains along Unter den Linden, the dissecting hall of the *Anatomisches Institut* on Philippstraße smelled of phenol, wet loden coats, and cheap *Sturm* cigarettes smoked illegally behind the zinc cadaver vats by boys whose fathers wore duelling scars from Heidelberg and Bonn.

Sylwia was eighteen years and four months old, six weeks out of the Polish diplomatic flat on Kurfürstenstraße since her father had been recalled to Warsaw over the rye-tariff talks and left her eighty marks a month, an iron cot in Widow Pohl's boarding apartment off Savignyplatz, and cold instructions not to disgrace Poznań brains among Prussians. Out of a hundred and ninety second-semester medical students crowded around twenty-four grey slate tables under the skylights, eleven were women and precisely one had a Polish passport stamped across her *Studienbuch*. Whenever she asked for the bone-saw or the red wax pencil, the three *Corpsstudenten* assigned to Table Seven—two Pomeranian landowners' sons with sticking-plaster still taped across fresh *Mensur* cheek-cuts and a fat Saxon pharmacist's heir named Kleist—developed instantaneous deafness, conversing loudly across her crown about rowing sculls at Grünau while leaving her the stripped abdominal mesentery fat nobody wanted to clean.

By the third week of October she stopped asking for instruments. She bought her own second-hand Collin dissecting kit from a pawnbroker behind Alexanderplatz—ebony handles darkened by some dead army surgeon's sweat, steel honed on a barber's Belgian hone until it shaved arm hair dry—arrived at six-fifteen every morning when the old one-armed *Diener* unlocked the zinc tanks, and claimed Cadaver Nineteen at the corner window table after a tubercular Rhineland student dropped out spitting blood.

Cadaver Nineteen had been a coal-barge woman fished out of the Landwehrkanal near Hallesches Tor in August: fifty-odd, broad-boned, calves knotted with purple varicosities fixed blue-gray by carbolic injection, calluses thick as horn along both palms from hauling wet hemp hawsers. Nobody wanted Table Nineteen because the cold draft down the tall north casement numbed your fingers stiff by nine o'clock and Geheimrat Professor von Halden's Wednesday surgical-anatomy spot-checks always started along the sunny center aisle before work took him back to Luisenstraße.

On the last Tuesday of October, Sylwia walked up the wet stone stairs at ten minutes past six in sleet-darkness, shaking rain off her too-long black umbrella, and stopped dead inside the swing doors.

The green-shaded gooseneck lamp above Table Nineteen was already lit.

Someone in a starched white laboratory smock two inches too short at the cuffs—wrists lean and big-boned, damp dark-blond hair raked carelessly straight back off a high forehead with wet fingers—stood bent low over her barge-woman's propped left shoulder, working a fine pair of Graefe iris forceps deep beneath the reflected pectoralis minor with unhurried, proprietary absorption.

Not one of the Corps boys. Corps boys stood spraddle-legged like cavalrymen and hacked fascia as if peeling turnips; besides, this one had an unmarked face—high, clean cheekbones, an arrogant narrow nose, a mouth cut too sharp at the corners for good humor—and a threadbare navy-blue naval officer's pea-coat from ten winters ago flung dry over the radiator pipe behind him, its brass anchor buttons replaced by plain horn.

Sylwia walked down the slate aisle until the wet rubber soles of her overshoes squeaked two feet from his elbow.

"That is Table Nineteen," she said.

He did not startle, and he did not take his forceps off the glistening pale cord of the musculocutaneous nerve until he had separated a delicate spiderweb of injected vasa nervorum intact down to its entry into the coracobrachialis. Only then did he straighten half-way, glancing sidelong down three inches of height at her drenched black felt cloche hat and fierce dark eyes.

Up close his irises were winter river-water—grey-green with a narrow black limbal ring—cool, sleepless, and wholly devoid of apology.

"Table Nineteen was unassigned on the beadle's chalkboard after Moeller hemorrhaged," he said. Not East Elbian Junker bark; crisp Berlin-West mixed with north-coast Kiel salt, fast and dry. "And whoever started reflecting the deltoid flap yesterday afternoon left five millimeters of clavipectoral fascia sloppy along the coracoid process, which would have torn the lateral thoracic artery off cleanly if I hadn't rescued it twenty minutes ago. You can take the right leg if you need femoral triangle practice."

Blood climbed hot and silent behind Sylwia's cheekbones—the dangerous kind that made her voice drop quiet rather than rise. "My name has been chalked in the corner slot of Nineteen's slate frame since Thursday. If you couldn't read four inches below your elbow, wash your eyes out at the tap. And I left that five-millimeter cuff of clavipectoral fascia unstripped deliberately because Nineteen has a high-splitting doubled thoracoacromial trunk piercing five millimeters medial to textbook line, which any second-day butcher hurrying to clear muscle bellies would have sliced off at the root—exactly where your left forceps blade just nicked the venous comitans."

For the space of two breaths his eyes flicked down into the axillary hollow—where, unmistakably, a tiny shred of blue-wax-injected vein wall lay severed beside his clean nerve—and then rose straight back to her face.

Most twenty-year-old men caught in an error by an eighteen-year-old girl with a faint Posen-Polish clip on her *r*'s either blustered red or laughed it off with heavy condescension. He did neither. His nostrils flared half a millimeter; the corner of his mouth tightened white, taking her entire measure—shabby black coat sleeves darned with silk thread at the wrists, pawnshop ebony scalpel box already unlatched in her ungloved hand, furious precision—and filing her instantaneously not as a curiosity or a woman, but as an enemy on narrow ground.

"Schoeler," he said shortly, neither clicking heels nor bowing. "And Geheimrat von Halden's Wednesday demonstration prize this term goes to the cleanest unilateral brachial plexus preparation on the floor. One cadaver cannot turn in two left shoulders."

"Then go find yourself a dead Prussian in the tank, Herr Schoeler," Sylwia said, setting her ebony box down with a sharp snap on the slate lip beside his elbow and stripping off her wet coat without yielding two inches of table margin, "because the right plexus on Nineteen is mine, and by tomorrow morning at nine o'clock von Halden won't bother looking at your left."

They stood shoulder to shoulder across four feet of carbolized human carcass for eleven hours straight that Tuesday without speaking another ten words, sharing neither grease-pencil nor sponge bucket, elbows knocking cold whenever both leaned over the sternum at once to trace the scalene hiatus from opposite sides of the barge-woman's neck. At two in the afternoon Kleist strolled over eating a liverwurst roll to make some heavy joke about the *Polnische Wirtschaft* setting up shop with "Bastard-Schoeler from Charlottenburg," and shriveled backward three steps when both of them looked up from opposite sides of the corpse with identical murderous silence. By seven that evening Sylwia's fingers were numb blue to the second knuckle from phenol water and hunger; she refused to go down to Aschinger's for five-pfennig pea soup with unlimited rolls because Schoeler hadn't gone, and she knew—with visceral, lunatic certainty—that the second she left the hall he would work an hour past lock-up bribing one-armed Otto with cigarettes. So she stayed till nine, chewing half a dry crust of sour rye inside her smock pocket while her eyes blurred under the green glass shade, teasing every finest twig of the medial cutaneous nerve out of grey fat until the right axilla looked like ivory lace suspended over red muscle.

At nine-fifteen Wednesday morning, Geheimrat von Halden swept down the north aisle in his spotless floor-length white coat, trailed by three terrified *Oberärzte*, six *Assistenten*, and forty crowding students crane-necked for blood.

Von Halden paused at Table Nineteen, hooked gold-rimmed spectacles onto his hooked Pomeranian nose, and looked down at the left shoulder first.

"Clean," the great man murmured, touching the tip of a silver probe almost caressingly to Ludwig's preparation. "Subscapular nerves preserved down to the teres major entry. Who prepared the left?"

"Schoeler, Herr Geheimrat," Ludwig said smoothly. Not boastful; quiet, respectful, letting the work speak—and then, with a minute, deliberate half-turn of his shoulder that blocked two *Assistenten*'s view of the right side while sounding helpful as choir-silk: "A rare textbook variation on the left, Herr Geheimrat: complete absence of the lateral root contribution to the median nerve, compensated low in the mid-brachium. The right side appears symmetrical so far as the superficial dissection reached last night."

*So far as the superficial dissection reached last night.*

Sylwia felt cold needle-ice go straight down her spine into her heels. He hadn't sabotaged her side with a blade—that would have been clumsy, detectable, amateurish. Instead, arriving ten minutes before eight, he had draped her finished right shoulder neatly under a heavy phenol-soaked oilcloth sheet as custom required for *unfinished* specimens so drying air wouldn't shrivel half-cleaned fat, ensuring von Halden would inspect the uncovered left showcase, nod at "symmetrical," and sweep down the aisle without ever lifting the cloth!

Already von Halden was turning half a step toward Table Twenty.

Sylwia reached across the barge-woman's breastbone, pinched the corner of the dripping phenol oilcloth between two raw fingers, and whipped it clean off the right shoulder in a single crisp snap that spattered three drops of carbolic water across Ludwig Schoeler's starched cuff.

"Not symmetrical at all, *Herr Geheimrat*," Sylwia said clearly into the horrified hush of forty male throats. "Bilateral asymmetry. On the right side the missing lateral contribution does not wait for the mid-brachium—it pierces coracobrachialis high behind the pectoralis minor tendon and sends a recurrent communicating loop straight back beneath the axillary artery, where Herr Schoeler's left-side dissection stopped two millimeters too shallow to look behind the vessel sheath."

Dead, breathless quiet. Kleist actually opened his mouth round like a carp. Across the slate trough, Ludwig stood dead still, two dark spots of carbolic wet on his white linen wrist, looking not at von Halden—at *her*, grey-green eyes narrowed to bright slits of pure, astonished venom and something sharper underneath that had no polite name in German or Polish.

Von Halden stepped back to Table Nineteen. For ninety eternal seconds the silver probe dipped behind Sylwia's right axillary artery—lifting the hair-fine white communicating loop she had found at half-past eight last night when her eyes were watering—and then crossed straight to Ludwig's left side, hooking Ludwig's intact axillary artery aside to reveal, buried untouched in deep perivascular fascia nobody ever opened unless hunting ghosts, the exact tiny mirror-loop Ludwig had missed because he had been busy admiring his clean nerve trunks.

Von Halden straightened slowly. He looked at the small dark nineteen-year-old girl in the darned black cardigan cuffs; he looked at the tall blond twenty-year-old boy with the horn-buttoned pea-coat behind him. A dry, dangerous crease dented one corner of the old surgeon's mouth.

"Herr Schoeler," von Halden said pleasantly, "knows how to display an anatomy plate so gentlemen clap. Fräulein Kotarska appears to know that corpses lie. Split the book voucher down the middle, Herr Oberarzt—and put them on opposite sides of the same dog table next semester so neither grows lazy."

When the retinue swept onward toward Table Twenty, nobody congratulated her. Across the sunken chest of the drowned barge-woman, Ludwig picked up his chamois cloth and wiped his scalpel blade dry from heel to point with slow, surgical care.

"Don't leave your oilcloth unwatched next time, *Fräulein Kotarska*," he murmured softly, for her ears alone. "Warsaw rules may forgive luck once. I don't."

"Buy deeper spectacles with your half-voucher, *Herr Schoeler*," she whispered back, smiling straight into his teeth while packing her pawnshop ebony case. "You dissect like a window-dresser."

*Warsaw, 11 February 1943*

At eleven o'clock on Thursday night the green-shaded reading bulb beside Bed Fourteen threw a tilted cone of weak yellow light across scrubbed white floorboards and left the high corners of the ceiling drowned in shadow.

Outside the closed oak door, the two *Abwehr* military policemen traded matches in whispers; inside the room, Bruno Mekelburg slept sitting bolt upright on a straight chair jammed into the window corner behind the blackout blind, chin sunk onto his good chest-crossed arm, splinted left hand resting upright against his collarbone like a white wooden paddle. Every twenty minutes the boy woke for three seconds whenever Sylwia moved, checked that it was only her white smock between bed and tray, and dropped straight back under like a sentry trained in freezing trenches.

Ludwig had not slept since seven that evening.

Fever sat at thirty-eight point six—moderate surgical resorption fever so far, not yet the jagged saw-tooth spike of anaerobic sepsis—putting a dry, glassy sheen across his forehead and hollowing the sockets beneath his cheekbones until the skull architecture showed clean and gaunt as an X-ray plate. Forty hours after losing half his blood volume on a snowy floor, a sensible casualty lay quiet under ten milligrams of morphine and thanked God for warm linen. Four hours ago Ludwig had refused Bronka's evening morphine syringe by turning his head flat into the pillow and locking his jaws until Bronka threatened in Wola Polish to sit on his nose and pour castor oil down his ear instead; when Sylwia came on night duty at nine, she had found the morphine ampoule still unbroken on the enamel stand and his grey-green eyes wide open on the ceiling crack.

Now she stood on his left side—the ruined side, where he had to turn his head thirty degrees against stiff sternocleidomastoid pull to watch her—unpinning the outer cellulose pad to check the Carrel irrigation tubes and the distal radial pulse before midnight.

The sweetish, hospital-sharp reek of wet iodoform rose off the warm dressings mixed with old blood serum and the sharp, unwashed animal salt of male sweat. His chest was bare under the folded-back rough linen hospital sheet; somebody—Mekelburg, presumably—had shaved the dark-blond hair off his left pectoral and upper ribs dry when prepping the adhesive tape border, leaving two angry razor nicks near the nipple. Across his right ribs and flat, sunken abdomen, five winters of war had hardened the lean twenty-three-year-old body she remembered only through starched clinic coats into corded, unforgiving sinew, with an older jagged white shell-splinter scar puckered low above his right iliac crest—France in forty, maybe, or nineteen-forty-one in the mud before Moscow.

She laid two cool fingers lightly over his left radial artery at the wrist. Eighty-eight beats per minute, full volume, warm skin down to the fingernails, capillary refill under two seconds when she pinched his thumbnail white. Intima-to-intima everted seam holding without a murmur of thrill.

"No aneurysm thrill yet," a dry, scraped voice said six inches above her bent ear, in Berlin German pitched barely louder than the steam-pipe hiss so Mekelburg in the corner didn't stir. "Five-zero vaselined silk, continuous over-and-over. I could tell by the wrist pulse yesterday afternoon you didn't waste time on interrupted mattress."

Sylwia did not take her fingers off his wrist until she had finished counting a full thirty seconds against her pinned lapel watch. Then she laid his arm precisely back inside the calico Velpeau sling fold and picked up the glass Dakin's syringe to flush five milliliters of fresh sodium hypochlorite down the upper rubber Carrel tube into the open wound bed.

Cold antiseptic hissed softly deep inside raw muscle; every tendon in his throat stood out like wire as the chlorine bite hit bare nerve endings beneath his resected collarbone, and sweat jumped in fine beads along his upper lip, though not a sound passed his teeth.

"Talk less," Sylwia said evenly, eyes on the yellow return fluid weeping cleanly into the kidney basin Bronka had left wedged on waterproof oilcloth under his shoulder. "You tore two centimeters of apical pleura Tuesday night. Pop a catgut knot coughing to show off your pulse diagnostics and I won't bother putting a chest drain in neatly—I'll stick a trocar between your ribs cold."

"You already left the whole deltopectoral triangle wide open like a butcher's stall." His voice sounded as if someone had dragged broken brick through his larynx—dry mouth from fluid restriction and mouth-breathing—yet the old razor-edged cadence sat intact underneath, unblunted by ten years or two liters of spilled blood. "I felt four inches of packed gauze when I shifted at dawn. What did you resect—middle third of the clavicle?"

"Two centimeters. Comminuted into four dirty splinters over the vessel." She capped the upper tube with its sterile glass spigot, flushed the lower tube, checked the return—turbid pink-amber, faintly sour, no gas bubbles, no mouse-nest stink yet—and tucked fresh dry cellulose wadding snugly over the iodoform pad. "Your shoulder girdle will drop six millimeters inward when it calluses. Tailor in Berlin can pad your left tunic board with horsehair so the generals don't weep."

A sound came out of his chest—half silent laugh, half immediate sharp catch of breath as the diaphragm tugged his torn pleura. When she straightened with the soiled kidney basin in her left hand, his eyes locked onto hers across two feet of green-lit air and held.

Ten years.

She had rehearsed seeing Ludwig Schoeler dead a dozen times between September thirty-nine, when German Heinkels dropped incendiaries onto Pavilion Two's roof while she was amputating a tram-conductor's crushed foot in falling plaster dust, and this week—imagining him frozen stiff in Russian snow outside Vyazma, or blown apart in some staff car by mines east of Brest, feeling each time a cold, tidy satisfaction like closing an old ledger column. Never once had she imagined him twenty inches away in white hospital calico with grey stubble shadowing the crescent scar under his right cheekbone, looking straight up through those clear, ruthless winter-water eyes into every line hunger and Warsaw had carved around her mouth since twenty-three.

"Still no gloves, *Kotarska*," he murmured, glancing down at the raw red mercury-burned cracks across her knuckles gripping the enamel rim of the basin. "Von Halden would dock your Sunday theater privileges."

"Von Halden doesn't scrub in ninety-percent methylated wood alcohol after digging German steel out of Polish backsides," she said softly, leaning six inches lower over the bedrail so her shadow fell square across his eyes while Mekelburg snored faintly by the blackout blind. Her voice stayed silk-flat, conversational enough that anyone peeking through the door glass would see a dutiful ward doctor adjusting a pillow corner. "And you aren't in Luisenstraße, *Herr Oberstleutnant*. You are four blocks north of Filtrowa in a city where boys hang from railway bridges with wire round their necks while your posters rot in the sleet."

His gaze didn't flicker away from hers by a hair's breadth. Pale fever-sheen gleamed along his cheekbones; his right hand lay outside the sheet, long fingers curled loose against rough bleached linen, four paces from Mekelburg's holstered pistol and infinitely further from help if she had chosen Tuesday morning at one-fifteen to lift her thumb.

"I know what city I'm in," Ludwig said, very quiet. "And I know how long a transected axillary trunk bleeds before the heart pumps air. Three minutes, maybe four with cold spasm. Nobody in that theater knew an axillary stump from pectoral fascia except you." His eyes narrowed slightly on her mouth. "Why didn't your thumb slip, Sylwia?"

Hearing her given name spoken in that low Berlin register after ten years hit her solar plexus like swallowing crushed ice whole. Not a muscle in her face moved.

"Because it was my table," she said, cold as the zinc slab downstairs, "and nobody dies messily on my table to save himself a walk later. Don't flatter yourself into philosophy, Schoeler. Pigs get sewn up first so they're fat enough for autumn."

For one second—less than a pulse beat—the grim line of his bloodless mouth almost twitched at the corner, not into warmth, never warmth between them, into that old lethal recognition of thrust and parry no decent human being had ever given either of them since nineteen-thirty-three.

Then pain or exhaustion pulled the gray veil back behind his eyes; his jaw set hard against the pillow as the raw chlorine burn deep in his open chest settled into its long two-hour throb without morphine.

"Turn the lamp off on your way out," he breathed, staring back up at the cracked plaster ceiling. "Your hospital stinks of boiled cabbage and thieves."

"Sleep on your back," Sylwia answered at the door, snapping the green shade switch off with dry fingers and plunging him back into winter dark. "Roll left in your dreams and pull my stitches apart, and next time I'll let Docent Mirecki tie your arm off at the stump."
