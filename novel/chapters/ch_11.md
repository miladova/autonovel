# Ash on the Laundry

*Warsaw, 19–24 April 1943*

What woke Sylwia at six minutes past six on the Monday of Holy Week was not the noise itself—four explosions three kilometers north sounded no louder against Koszykowa's windowpanes than coal wagons coupling hard down by Towarowa—it was the rhythm afterward.

Railway shunters banged twice and stopped to curse; German anti-aircraft batteries practiced in crisp four-gun salvos that rattled teacups and echoed away across the Vistula. This was low, flat seventy-five-millimeter field howitzer fire—*thump... crump*—firing point-blank over open sights between brick walls where echoes had nowhere to roll, stitched together instantly by two distinct kinds of automatic chatter: the tearing, thousand-round-a-minute rip of Wehrmacht MG-34s answered three seconds later by slower, sharper pistol cracks and the dull subterranean concussion of homemade grenade tins exploding inside courtyards.

Sylwia swung bare feet onto cold floorboards without putting on slippers, pushed the blackout blind aside two inches, and unlatched Goldfeder's inner casement.

April morning air rushed in sharp and blue, smelling of wet lilac buds from courtyard trees, coal smoke, and—faint at first, drifting high down out of the north over Elektoralna and Grzybowska—cordite mixed with burning kerosene.

Across the narrow courtyard well, two tenement windows snapped open; a bald tailor in striped flannel nightshirt leaned out craning northward, crossed himself rapidly, and pulled his head back inside before his wife grabbed his elbow. Down on Koszykowa cobbles milk-carts rattled uninterrupted; a red Number Nine tram clanged past heading east on schedule.

*Passover tonight,* Sylwia realized with a jolt behind her breastbone, counting calendar days backward from Easter Sunday. *And Tuesday's picric-acid lime cart never goes in again.*

When she reached Pavilion Three at seven-fifteen, half the convalescent fractures on the third floor had limped out onto the iron fire-balcony facing north in grey hospital dressing-gowns, shading their eyes against bright spring sun. From third-floor height, above the slate mansards of Nowogrodzka and Bagno, you saw it plain: a thick, greasy column of brown-black smoke rising straight up out of the Muranów skyline north of Chłodna into windless blue heaven, spreading at a thousand feet into a flat dark anvil cloud while faint dull thuds vibrated up through brick soles every forty seconds.

Down on the ground floor, the stenciled *Reserviert — Wehrmacht-Notdienst* sign was already being unscrewed from the east-wing swing doors by a hospital carpenter while Stabsarzt Vesper strutted up and down the tiled corridor in freshly polished riding boots, slapping his gloves against his thigh and beaming as if he personally had sewn Ludwig Schoeler's subclavian artery together with his teeth.

At eight-forty-five Room Fourteen's door opened for the last time.

Mekelburg came out first carrying his commander's battered leather portmanteau in his intact right hand, three-fingered left palm scarred tough pink without splint now and tucked neatly behind his belt buckle; as he passed Sylwia standing beside Docent Mirecki at the admissions counter, the boy snapped his heels together two degrees sharper than regulations required for civilian doctors, pale country eyes meeting hers for one silent flick before he carried the bag down the steps toward a mud-splashed grey Wanderer staff car idling in the courtyard.

Behind him walked Ludwig.

In tailored field-grey wool greatcoat buttoned up to the silver-piped collar, peaked cap set slightly low over his brow so the black patent visor shadowed his winter-green eyes, he looked ten miles removed from any man who had ever lain shivering under vinegar cloths or spat leather straps onto hospital linen. Only Sylwia—knowing where two centimeters of bone were missing underneath that left shoulder-board—saw how meticulously the Berlin tailor's horsehair pad masked the six-millimeter drop of his left acromion, and how consciously he carried his gloved left hand loose along his coat seam rather than stiff in a sling.

"Splendid functional restoration, *Herr Oberstleutnant*!" Vesper trumpeted, flourishing three carbon copies of the yellow Wehrmacht discharge certificate across Walenty's oak counter. "One hundred and sixty-five degrees painless abduction recorded Saturday—scarcely a five-percent pensionable impairment! Sign the bottom duplicate here for *Ortskommandantur* records, if you please, and Docent Mirecki witnesses municipal release."

Ludwig uncapped his own black Pelikan fountain pen right-handed and bent slightly over the counter to sign *Schoeler, Obstlt.* in small, slantless vertical script sharp as copperplate needles.

Outside through the open courtyard doors, three dull cannon thuds sounded back-to-back from the north, shaking a pinch of dry putty dust off the vestibule transom. Vesper cleared his throat loudly, pretending artillery inside a conquered capital three hundred miles behind the front was merely Tuesday weather: "Sanitary clearing action in the northern quarantine sector—high time those bandit nests were smoked out before warm weather breeds typhus!"

Mirecki stared fixedly at the floor tiles behind his pince-nez. Bronka, standing four feet back with folded arms like salt pork, narrowed her eyes at the back of Vesper's pink neck as if measuring where a cleaver went between third and fourth cervical vertebrae.

Ludwig capped his pen without glancing at Vesper, handed the sheet across to Sylwia—whose countersignature as attending vascular surgeon occupied line three—and stepped half a pace round the corner of the oak desk while she signed so Vesper's fat shoulder stood blocked by Mirecki signing copy two.

Through the open vestibule doorway drifted a tiny grey curl of charred feather-tick ash, spinning lazily down on sunlit draft to settle right onto the green blotter six inches from Sylwia's knuckles.

"Keep your cellar bolted tight this week, *Kotarska*," Ludwig murmured in crisp Berlin German pitched below the scratch of Mirecki's nib, looking down at her bent dark head rather than her face so Vesper saw nothing except an officer waiting for his copy. "SS-Brigadeführer Stroop took command over Hahn's head at six this morning with Lemberg pioneer battalions and flamethrowers. Anyone caught near northern manhole covers gets shot on the asphalt without *Szucha* paperwork."

Sylwia finished the final tail of *Dr. med. S. Kotarska*, blotted the wet black ink calmly with wooden rocker-blotter, and lifted her eyes straight into his under the black cap visor.

"Tuesdays and Fridays," he added a fraction louder—loud enough now for Vesper's benefit, cool and official as ice—"five o'clock sharp per *Herr Stabsarzt*'s outpatient order, Frau Doktor. Don't make my clerk telephone your hospital."

"Tuesdays and Fridays, *Herr Oberstleutnant*," Sylwia said without expression.

He touched two leather-gloved fingers to his visor—one salute covering Mirecki, Vesper, and six Polish orderlies without distinguishing her by half an inch—walked down the stone steps into blinding April sunlight, folded his long frame into the rear seat of the grey Wanderer without favoring his left shoulder, and was gone through Lindleya gate before the gatehouse clock finished striking nine.

Behind him Room Fourteen stood wide open to ward maids sloshing lysol water across empty pine boards. All morning, whenever Sylwia walked past the ground-floor east corridor on her way between Theater One and the stairs, the missing click of military-police boots outside those swing doors felt not like liberation, like having a tooth pulled out by the roots and leaving a raw, bleeding socket her tongue couldn't stop touching—while thirty blocks north the cannon fire grew heavier every hour.

* * *

By Good Friday the sun over Warsaw turned the colour of a dirty copper five-grosz piece seen through smoked glass.

Not clouds—blue sky still burned cloudless above Praga and Mokotów—a vast, slanting river of greasy brown-violet smoke pouring continuously south-southeast off burning Muranów, Nalewki, Zamenhofa, and Świętojerska as SS demolition engineers Stopped storming stairwells where Jewish pistol teams shot them off landings and began systematically torching tenement block after tenement block from cellar to roof with incendiary charges and crude-oil sprayers.

Up on Pavilion Three's flat tarred gravel roof at two in the afternoon, sixty boiled hospital sheets hung flapping on galvanized wire lines strung between brick chimney-stacks. Every sheet Bronka had hung wet-white at eight a.m. was speckled gray-black by noon with greasy soot flakes the size of fingernails—charred ledger paper carrying Hebrew and Polish print still legible until you touched it and it crumbled to black tallow on your skin, burnt horsehair mattress stuffing, scorched pigeon feathers, and a sweetish, roasting fat-and-lime stench no hospital surgeon who had ever smelled cautery on human muscle could mistake for pinewood.

"Three tubs of lye wasted," Bronka muttered savagely, yanking clothes-pegs off wire with cold red knuckles and stuffing ruined grey-streaked sheets into wicker laundry hampers while wind whipped her white head-cloth sideways. She paused with an armful of linen crushed against her apron, staring north over a mile of sunlit slate roofs toward the rolling black wall where orange flame-tongues licked visible even at midday whenever a tenement roof collapsed inward.

Down below along Marszałkowska and Aleje Jerozolimskie, church bells were silent since Maundy Thursday *Gloria*—wooden clappers rattling dry for Good Friday liturgy—yet carried clear down a freak north gust past Saski Garden came the tinny, cheerful oompah-oompah of the steam calliope on the Easter fairground carousel erected thirty yards outside the Ghetto wall on Krasiński Square, turning round and round with painted wooden horses while crowds in spring coats licked watery fruit-ice cones and watched dive-bombers or artillery shells burst two hundred feet away behind brick!

"Swine," Bronka spat straight onto the tarred roof gravel—not specifying German swine or Polish carousel-riders, cursing the whole bipedal species under heaven in one mouthful—and crossed herself hard with her left thumb still holding three wooden pegs. "Even beasts in Wola stockyards bellow when the shed next door burns. Up by Krasiński they buy sunflower seeds and lift children on their shoulders to watch roofs fall in."

"Take that hamper down to soak cold before the grease sets," Sylwia said, pulling a charred scrap of printed Yiddish prayer-book margin gently off a pillowcase before folding it into her pocket—not out of piety, she hadn't knelt sincerely in church since her mother died pickled in morphine at nineteen-twenty-nine, only because throwing letters burned off living rooms into a hospital slop-tub felt obscene. "I'm going down to check Halina before three."

Down in the *Fleckfieber* basement tunnel, zero daylight reached; yet even down there, drawn through the courtyard intake flue feeding the agar stove, hung that unmistakable sweet-greasy char.

When Sylwia rapped three-and-one on the iron-sheathed door, nobody slid the bolt for twenty seconds. She rapped harder; finally the upper iron bolt scraped back slow, and Halina stood there without her grey cardigan, sleeves rolled past white forearms covered in fresh purple louse-bite grids—she had strapped twelve Weigl cages instead of ten onto her thighs *and* forearms today as if trying to drain half a pint out of herself on purpose—holding a broken glass Pasteur pipette in her right fist with bright red blood dripping steadily off her cut thumb pad onto the stone floor.

Sylwia stepped inside, kicked the iron door bolted behind her, took Halina's bleeding right wrist without a word, and steered her to the slate laboratory sink.

The telephone junction box behind the horse-serum rack—where two hair-fine copper wires spliced clandestinely into the municipal sanitary sewer cable had carried ninety-second whispers from Bonifraterska twice a week since winter—hung open with both copper leads cut clean off and wrapped back round porcelain insulators.

"Melted short at four yesterday afternoon when they blew the brushmakers' block on Świętojerska," Halina said in a voice so flat and conversational it raised gooseflesh down Sylwia's arms. She didn't look down while Sylwia picked a three-millimeter needle-sliver of soda-glass out of her thumb pulp with iris forceps and poured raw iodine straight into the cut. Zero flinch, zero hiss. "Municipal linesman came down the manhole at dawn to clear fault alarms before German post-engineers traced the bridge back here. Said the cast-iron sewer cover at Świętojerska corner was red-hot enough to fry spit, and water running down the storm main toward the Vistula was forty degrees warm and full of dead people boiled in cellars."

Sylwia pressed sterile gauze tight over Halina's thumb and wrapped adhesive tape twice round it, keeping hold of the younger woman's icy hand between both her own raw palms when she finished.

"Brushmakers' block had deep brick vaults connecting into the main four-foot storm collector toward Bonifraterska and Długa," Sylwia said steadily, refusing past tense until someone showed her Dawid Różański's body on zinc. "If twenty boys in Dawid's courtyard bought Belgian pistols and petrol jelly in February, they didn't sit upstairs waiting for roofs to fall—they went down into the bunkers and sewers when the walls caught."

Halina's dark eyes—huge, sunken black coals below that ridiculous strip of brassy yellow peroxide hair—lifted straight to Sylwia's face. Not a drop of moisture swam in them; everything wet in Halina seemed burned dry down to white salt.

"Give me that bottle behind the agar tins," Halina said.

Sylwia reached behind the horse-meat agar canisters, pulled out the quarter-full liter bottle of ninety-five-percent laboratory absolute ethanol Halina used for Gram decolorizing, poured two fingers into two clean fifty-milliliter Pyrex beakers, diluted both splash-for-splash with distilled water so it wouldn't strip pharyngeal mucosa raw, and handed one over.

They drank standing against the slate microscope bench while eight thousand typhus lice rustled dryly inside dark thirty-degree wooden drawers two yards left.

Halina set her empty Pyrex beaker down with a sharp click that nearly cracked the rim. "Two sewer workers from the municipal water board—Poles who took gold twenty-dollar pieces in January to map the storm-main grates under Krasiński Gardens and Długa—came to my landlady's cousin on Tamka at midnight last night. They want ten thousand złoty more by Monday before they lift a single manhole iron outside the wall, because Stroop's pioneers started dropping smoke grenades and gas candles down the inspection shafts yesterday."

"Have you got ten thousand?" Sylwia asked immediately.

"I have four thousand two hundred sewed inside my mattress hem and my mother's gold wedding ring worth two thousand on Marszałkowska," Halina said starkly. "Which leaves three thousand eight hundred short before Monday night—and if anyone *does* crawl out under Długa or Bonifraterska next week, half of them will have third-degree benzine and phosphorus burns, smoke-blinded corneas, and sewer-rat Weil's disease with nowhere to lie except coffins."

Sylwia didn't hesitate half a second. Upstairs in Koszykowa's hollowed soap-bar sat nine hundred złoty left from winter; Stefan Bielski owed eighteen hundred for Easter insulin come tomorrow afternoon; and locked inside Docent Mirecki's private emergency safe sat twelve gram vials of Swiss *Ciba* morphine tartrate worth four hundred złoty apiece to any society physician on Mokotowska whose rich nervous patients couldn't sleep for the smoke!

"Keep your mother's ring in your mattress," Sylwia said coldly. "You'll have four thousand cash in your coat pocket Monday noon before the water-board shift changes. And starting tonight Bronka and I pack six sterile burn-and-eye kits inside zinc autopsy specimen drums in the mortuary annex—tannic acid jelly, silver nitrate one-percent drops, atropine eye ointment, anti-tetanus horse serum, twenty-gram sulfanilamide packets. Anyone your sewer men pull up alive after dark comes in the Nowogrodzka hearse wagon under coffins to our contagious morgue vestibule."

For the first time Halina's chin quivered one millimeter—fierce, ugly threat of human cracking she killed instantly by biting down hard on her own lip until blood showed red against pale teeth.

"If Kroll or Vesper catches one burned Jew from Muranów inside your contagious morgue, Kotarska," Halina whispered harshly, "they don't give you Pawiak—they shoot you right against the coal bin beside me."

"Then don't drop your pipette on the floor before Monday," Sylwia said tartly, rinsing both Pyrex beakers clean under the cold tap so not a trace of alcohol smell lingered on laboratory glass. "And dye your roots tonight with strong chamomile tea mixed into half-peroxide—somebody upstairs with good eyes remarked last month that Silesian bleach goes orange over agar steam."

Halina blinked once in sheer, stunned disbelief—realizing *which* German upstairs had noticed her hair roots for five weeks and walked out Monday morning without opening his mouth to Aleja Szucha—and let out a single short, barked laugh darker than pitch. "What a charming century we picked to learn medicine in, Sylwia."

* * *

On Holy Saturday afternoon the twenty-fourth of April, Wanda Bielska's dining-room table on Filtrowa gleamed snowy white under starched damask ironed twice with borax water, centered round a crystal bowl of hard-boiled eggs dyed russet-mahogany in boiled onion skins, scraped white horseradish root, green boxwood sprigs, a sugar lamb with a tiny red paper resurrection banner, and two coils of real pale pork *biała kiełbasa* glistening with marjoram fat.

"Shut the balcony window, Stefan, for heaven's sake!" Wanda cried from the sideboard, dabbing furiously with a damp dishcloth at three greasy black soot-smudges that had drifted straight onto her lace runner while Stefan aired his cigar smoke. "Every ten minutes another filthy flake blows down from town—I've washed the kitchen sill thrice since breakfast! Why can't they put water hoses on it if buildings are burning?"

"Because nobody wastes municipal fire brigades inside a closed bandit perimeter, my dear," Stefan Bielski said reasonably, latching the brass casement handle tight against the copper-haze April sky and turning back toward the table rubbing soft pink hands. Today Stefan wore a new charcoal-grey suit cut from *Bezugsschein* textile coupons issued to senior *Ostbahn* and municipal tramway engineers. "Sit down, Sylwia, take three slices of white sausage before Staś eats the whole ring—real country garlic sausage from Łowicz, my foreman's brother-in-law brought two kilos in yesterday! Honestly, whatever one thinks privately as a Christian—and nobody pities misled women and children more than I do—launching armed anarchy with machine-guns inside the middle of a crowded city during Holy Week is sheer criminal madness. All it accomplishes is ruining everybody's Easter air and provoking tighter police curfews on hardworking Poles."

Sylwia stood behind Staś's chair with both hands resting lightly on the boy's narrow flannel shoulders.

Two hours ago she had finished packing six zinc autopsy drums in Pavilion Three's dead-house with tannic burn-jelly and silver-nitrate eye drops for people crawling blind through four feet of human excrement beneath Bonifraterska; on her walk down Chałubińskiego ten minutes ago a warm gray flake of charred feather-mattress had landed right in her hair, smelling of roasted lard and lime. Looking down at two coils of pale garlic pork sausage nested in green Easter boxwood while Stefan Bielski mourned his window-lace made something cold and bright snap clean behind her eyes.

"Eighteen hundred złoty turned into three thousand eight hundred this morning, Stefan," Sylwia said pleasantly, not sitting down and not touching her plate.

Stefan paused with his carving fork poised half an inch above the sausage skin. "Excuse me?"

"Staś's May insulin," Sylwia lied with crystalline, glacial calm, looking straight across the sugar resurrection lamb into her brother-in-law's plump face. "Danish refrigerator wagons got held up behind troop-trains from Lemberg—probably carrying flamethrower oil for that 'sanitary nuisance' spoiling Wanda's lace curtains. Railway pharmacists raised forty-unit Novo vials to nineteen hundred apiece cash before Monday noon. Two vials makes thirty-eight hundred. Plus eight hundred left from March if you want June covered before summer heat spoils unrefrigerated stock. Four thousand six hundred total."

Wanda gasped audibly, clutching her dishcloth to her bosom. "Four thousand six—Sylwia, have railway people no fear of God on Holy Saturday?! Extorting four thousand złoty out of a sick child's veins!"

Stefan flushed dull brick-red from collar to ears—not stupid enough for five seconds to believe insulin had jumped two hundred percent since Palm Sunday, yet trapped dead flat in front of his wife and diabetic nine-year-old son! Question Sylwia's black-market price aloud, ask for receipts, refuse to pay—and watch nine-year-old Staś stare up with huge dark eyes wondering why Papa bought Łowicz garlic sausages and new charcoal wool suits while begrudging insulin money; pay up, and watch four thousand *Volksliste* złoty walk straight out his front door into whatever dark Polish gutter Sylwia chose to pour it down!

"Outrageous profiteering," Stefan choked out through tight teeth, yanking his fat calfskin wallet out of his breast pocket so jerkily two ration coupons fluttered onto the carpet. "Bandits at every railway siding—no wonder order takes an iron hand in this country!"

One, two, three, four—forty crisp hundred-złoty Bank Emisyjny notes slapped onto the corner of the damask cloth two inches from the horseradish dish, plus six fifties and three twenties until four thousand six hundred sat stacked neat.

Sylwia picked up the stack bare-handed, counted it deliberately leaf by leaf right in front of Stefan's bulging eyes—letting eight hundred reserve go toward actual May insulin from Mekelburg's cheap *Sanitätspark* clerk and clearing *three thousand eight hundred exact* for Halina's sewer workers Monday morning!—and slipped the roll into her worn black handbag with a crisp brass snap.

"Enjoy your sausage, Stefan," Sylwia said mildly. "Chew the gristle well—we lost a Volksdeutscher miller on Ward A last Tuesday when pork fat impacted above a pyloric spasm, and nobody likes having a rubber stomach-tube shoved down his nose on Easter Sunday."

"Sylwia, you aren't leaving without touching an egg!" Wanda wailed, tears springing instantly into round china-blue eyes. "On Holy Saturday! Mother in heaven—you come in smelling of ether like an executioner, empty Stefan's pockets, insult our table, and walk out without sharing *jajko*!"

Sylwia took one quarter-wedge of brown onion-dyed boiled egg off the saucer edge, touched half to Staś's solemn outstretched piece—*"Health and quiet nights, Stasiu"*—ate her sliver standing up in two chews so hospital protein wasn't wasted, kissed the top of Wanda's powdered cheek once out of sheer pity for soft creatures born without armor, and turned to the hall.

Staś slipped down off his chair without asking permission and ran into the dark vestibule to unbolt the heavy front door for her before Stefan could follow.

Out on the cold stone stair-landing, with the front door pulled almost shut behind his skinny shoulder-blades, the nine-year-old boy looked up at her with huge, sleepless eyes that missed nothing—neither green *Kennkarte* shame nor hospital lies.

"Ciocia," he whispered so low Wanda inside couldn't hear. "At recess behind the courtyard pump yesterday, Janek the janitor's boy said the black snow falling on the roofs isn't coal soot—it's people burning alive behind the wall on Nalewki. Papa told Mama Janek's father listens to London lies."

Every tidy maternal lie ever invented for nine-year-olds rose to Sylwia's tongue and died before passing her teeth. Lie to this child today while he smelled the grease himself through cracked stair-putty, and by fifteen he either grew into Stefan Bielski or hated her for treating him like cattle.

She dropped to one knee on the stone landing so her dark eyes sat level with his thin face, buttoning the top horn button of his grey cardigan under his chin with gentle, mercury-scarred fingers.

"Janek's father isn't lying, Stasiu," Sylwia said very quietly. "Men and women behind that wall chose to die fighting with pistols in their hands rather than get onto cattle trains, and the Germans are burning the houses down over them because they're afraid to walk up the stairs. Don't repeat that at school, and don't argue with your father at supper—your job this year is to measure your Benedict's tubes straight, eat every spoonful of buckwheat, and grow tall enough to outlive them. Understand?"

Staś looked straight back at her for three solemn seconds, chin firming into Kazimierz Kotarski's narrow Poznań jaw, and nodded once without crying.

When Sylwia stepped out onto Filtrowa pavement five minutes later—three thousand eight hundred złoty of collaborator's blood-money warm inside her purse toward lifting manhole irons under Długa, and Ludwig Schoeler's green *Sonderausweis* waiting in her pocket for Tuesday at five o'clock—a single grey ash-flake drifted down out of the copper sky onto the back of her bare wrist, weightless as breath, and vanished when she closed her fist.
