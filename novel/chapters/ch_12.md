# Nur für Deutsche

*Warsaw, 27 April – 4 May 1943*

Two painted wooden sawhorses and fifty yards of barbed-wire concertina separated Marszałkowska mud from the swept granite flags of ulica Królewska, and crossing between them at ten minutes to five felt like walking out of a typhus ward straight onto the stage of a Viennese operetta performed by butchers.

Outside the barrier, women in cardboard-soled shoes queued three deep in blowing Muranów ash for four hundred grams of gluey clay bread; forty paces inside the barrier, beneath green-and-white striped canvas awnings opposite the colonnade of Saski Palace, two bareheaded Wehrmacht Signals auxiliaries in sheer rayon stockings sat laughing over tall glasses of real raspberry *Eisbecher* topped with whipped cream while a waiter in a white jacket swept fallen chestnut blossoms off the kerb with a twig broom.

Sylwia walked up to the sentry box wearing her best prewar charcoal-grey tailored suit—pressed at midnight under a damp towel with a flatiron heated on two handfuls of precious peat, skirt let in two inches at the waist since thirty-nine—carrying her scuffed black Gladstone bag in her left hand and her grey *Kennkarte* tucked inside Ludwig's green Wehrmacht *Sonderausweis* in her right.

The Bavarian *Schutzpolizei* corporal leaning against the striped barrier pole didn't bother lowering his cigarette until her shoe-tips stopped two feet from his boots. One lazy flick of small blue eyes took in the Polish cut of her collar, the absence of any Reich swastika pin or *Ostbahn* badge on her lapel, and the dark hair coiled severe under a plain black felt hat.

"Runter vom Bürgersteig," he grunted automatically around his cigarette butt, jerking his thumb toward the wet cobbles of the carriageway reserved for delivery handcarts and Poles with work-gang passes. "Pavement's German."

Sylwia didn't step down an inch, and she didn't raise her voice—simply flipped open the stiff green *Wehrmachtkommandantur* card six inches under his nose so the violet eagle of *Abwehrstelle Warschau* and Stabsarzt Vesper's counter-stamp filled his vision.

"Ärztlicher Außendienst auf Befehl des Oberkommandos," she said in clipped Charlottenburg German cold enough to frost brass. "Królewska sechzehn, Wohnung vier. Call the duty officer inside Saski Palace gate if you have trouble spelling *Oberstleutnant*, *Herr Wachtmeister*."

Nothing cowed minor German policemen faster than pure Westend Berlin syntax delivered as if their boots were dusty. Out came the cigarette from between his teeth; heels came half-together before his brain caught the Polish surname *Kotarska* on line two, leaving him marooned halfway between a salute and a scowl as he waved her sullenly past the sawhorse along the clean granite sidewalk.

Number Sixteen Królewska had been built around nineteen-twelve for wealthy advocates and bank directors who wanted chestnuts outside their tall French windows and five minutes' walk to the Stock Exchange: cream limestone facade undamaged by thirty-nine's shells save for pockmarks round the cornice, wrought-iron vestibule gates polished black, brass plate beside the front bell reading *Quartieramt der Wehrmacht — Hausverwaltung*.

Remembering Ludwig's dry instruction in the splint room, Sylwia bypassed the marble front portico altogether, turning down the narrow vaulted carriage-passage into a quiet graveled rear courtyard where lilac bushes were just breaking purple bud despite the faint brown haze still staining the upper sky toward the north.

Service Stair B smelled not of cabbage-water and cat urine like Koszykowa—it smelled of turpentine floor-wax, real coal smoke, and roasting meat drifting out of somebody's second-floor kitchen vent.

On the second-floor back landing, beside a tall narrower pantry door painted cream over older mahogany, Sylwia raised her gloved hand to press the porcelain bell-button—and stopped with her knuckle half an inch from the china disk.

Right beside her eye on the painted wooden doorpost, tilted at the unmistakable thirty-degree inward slant no carpenter ever used for bell-wires or nameplate screws, ran a faint four-inch oblong scar beneath one coat of German cream paint where an older brass or silver case had been wrenched off with a chisel blade, leaving two plugged nail-holes top and bottom.

Even painted over twice by *Quartieramt* painters in forty-one, wood remembered. Before the war, half the senior appellate barristers along Królewska had been assimilated Polish-Jewish jurists whose grandfathers wore silk top hats in eighteen-eighty and still nailed a tiny silver *mezuzah* to the back service doorpost for old mothers from Kazimierz.

Before she touched the bell, the pantry door clicked open from inside.

Bruno Mekelburg stood there in clean shirtsleeves and grey uniform braces, polishing a black riding boot with a horsehair brush held deftly in his good right hand while the boot-tree sat wedged between his knees. His scarred three-fingered left stump looked pink, tough, and entirely uninflamed.

"Afternoon, *Frau Doktor*," Mekelburg said low, stepping wide to let her into a tiled butler's pantry lined with glass-fronted oak china cupboards. No military policemen here; quiet apartment thick-walled enough to muffle cannon three miles off down to distant summer thunder. Before hanging her charcoal jacket on the pantry peg, the boy reached behind two stacked soup tureens, pulled out a small square parcel wrapped in brown Wehrmacht ration-butcher paper tied with hemp string, and slid it straight down into her open Gladstone bag alongside her stethoscope without rattling a bottle. "Three vials. Forty-unit Novo, good till March forty-four. Twenty Reichsmarks—forty złoty official, wait, zero złoty to you: my skat clerk took one small bottle of hospital spirit Bronka gave me last month and called us square."

Three ten-milliliter vials of forty-unit Danish insulin—seventy-five days of nine-year-old Staś Bielski's life, worth nearly six thousand złoty at Holy Saturday railway extortion prices—tucked into her bag for half a pint of stolen rectified alcohol!

Sylwia looked sharply into Mekelburg's turnip-blond country face, checking whether Ludwig stood behind that skat story.

"Did your *Oberstleutnant* pay twenty Reichsmarks for those?" she asked straight out.

"Didn't tell him," Mekelburg muttered stolidly, going back to buffing toe-leather until it shone like black glass. "What officers don't sign for, officers don't answer for. He's down the hall in the corner study—second door left."

Down a wide parquet corridor stripped of carpets—pale oblongs on oak herringbones showing where Persian runners had lain for twenty years before some *Quartieramt* wife rolled them up for Munich—every piece of remaining heavy furniture bore a tiny white pasted paper square under its rim stamped in purple ink: *Wehrmacht-Unterkunftverwaltung Warschau — Inv. Nr. 4412*.

Inside the corner study, tall double casements stood latched against spring ash, looking sideways into the green tops of Saski Garden's horse-chestnuts. Books still lined two walls ceiling-high behind leaded glass—not German military manuals, rows of gilt-stamped *Orzecznictwo Sądu Najwyższego* (Decisions of the Polish Supreme Court), French civil codes, leather-bound Mickiewicz and Heine—because nobody bothered carting Polish law books to Berlin when requisitioning a warm flat for staff colonels.

And curling up off a small silver tray set on the Biedermeier side-table came a smell so shockingly rich, roasted, bitter-sweet, and extinct that Sylwia's parotid glands contracted with actual sharp pain beneath both ears before her brain named it.

Real coffee.

Not burnt barley, not bitter chicory-and-sugar-beet *plujka*, not roasted acorns: genuine dark oil-roasted Santos or Guatemala beans ground ten minutes ago in a brass hand-mill, steaming black inside two eggshell-thin Rosenthal cups with two white cubes of real beet sugar sitting on each saucer.

Ludwig stood beside the tall porcelain corner stove in white linen shirtsleeves and uniform trousers without tunic, reading a typed blue flimsy held in his left hand—actually holding paper steady between left thumb and index finger now without tremor—while his right leaned casually on the warm tiles. Eight days outside hospital walls had browned his gaunt cheekbones slightly in spring wind; without ward calico round him, standing tall among stolen Polish law books with real coffee steaming at his elbow, he belonged suddenly and intolerably to the winning side of the barbed wire outside.

"Five minutes early," Ludwig observed, tossing the blue flimsy into an iron wire basket containing cold grey paper-ash—he burned notes right at his elbow as he read them—and nodding toward the silver tray. "Drink it while it's hot. Came off a Lisbon courier bag via Madrid last week; even Vesper hasn't smelled beans like that since forty."

Sylwia set her Gladstone bag down on a straight wooden chair—not the leather armchair—walked two steps to the glass-fronted bookcase beside the tray, and tilted her head slightly to read the small engraved bookplate visible inside an open volume of *Prawo Cywilne* lying face-up on the blotter corner:

*Ex Libris Dr. jur. Maurycy Silberstein, Sędzia Sądu Apelacyjnego w Warszawie.*

Maurycy Silberstein. Sylwia remembered her father speaking that name across dinner linen in twenty-six with dry grudging respect: sharpest commercial-treaty jurist in Warsaw, seventy years old by forty-two if typhus or Treblinka cattle-cars hadn't finished him eight months ago, leaving behind three thousand law volumes, two plugged nail-holes on his pantry doorpost, and thin Rosenthal porcelain for Wehrmacht officers to pour Lisbon courier coffee into while Muranów burned three kilometers north.

Without saying a single word, Sylwia picked up the thin porcelain cup poured for her—hot black fragrance rising dizzying straight into four years of caffeine-starved brain vessels—carried it six paces back across the parquet to the open pantry sink-alcove visible from the study threshold, tipped the cup upside down into the brass drain-hole while Mekelburg blinked over his boot-brush, rinsed the brown stain down with two splashes of cold tap water, and walked back into the study to set the empty white cup neatly onto its saucer.

Ludwig hadn't moved an inch beside the porcelain stove.

His winter-green eyes tracked the empty cup down onto china, then lifted straight to her face—zero flush, zero bluster, zero cheap German defensive excuse (*"If I hadn't moved in here some SS captain would have"*—Stefan Bielski's line!). Instead his mouth tightened by one hard millimeter at the corner, taking the slap clean across the jaw in dead silence.

"Take your shirt off and sit under the window light," Sylwia said coldly in German, unstrapping her leather bag. "Twenty minutes—that's what your green pass buys."

He unbuttoned his white linen shirt from throat to belt without haste, pulled the left arm free last, and sat sideways on the straight wooden chair facing the tall French window rather than Silberstein's deep leather armchair.

Green-gold reflection off Saski Garden's young chestnut leaves washed sideways across bare bone and muscle. Ten days since she had broken his shoulder adhesions raw in the splint room. Every trace of open raw granulation trough had finally epithelialized shut save one dry pinhead scab at the medial end: in its place stretched a dense, contracted, glossy purple-crimson keloid star five centimeters wide, fused deep to pectoralis fascia over the two-centimeter gap in his collarbone, pulsing faintly at its lower border with every beat of her five-zero silk artery repair buried half an inch beneath.

Sylwia did not put on gloves, and she did not warm her fingers at the stove first.

Standing at his left shoulder, smelling turpentine floor-wax, faint roast coffee she had refused to drink, and clean warm skin without hospital iodoform for the first time since February, she pressed two cool thumbs firmly into the thick purple scar-cord to test deep gliding mobility over the subclavius bed.

Beneath her cold thumbs his pectoral muscle twitched once—involuntary skin-reflex—and went rock-still.

"No deep tenderness over the vessel?" she asked, sliding firm cross-fiber friction strokes across the keloid border with a dab of plain lanolin out of her jar so tight new collagen wouldn't strangle the lateral pectoral nerve twigs.

"Only when cold wind hits it off the river," Ludwig said quietly, staring straight ahead at glass rather than up at her chin while her thumbs worked slow deep circles half an inch below his bare collarbone. "Tingling down thumb and index finger mornings for ten minutes until I hang from a door-lintel—lateral cord traction in scar tissue. Clears once blood warms it."

"Door-lintel hanging's idiotic before week twelve—stretch against a wall corner with elbow bent ninety degrees, or you'll tear what I sewed." She lifted his left wrist to test radial pulse under maximal overhead abduction—raising his bare arm high until pectoralis stretched drum-tight beneath her fingers, checking whether scar compression pinched the repaired axillary trunk when overhead (*Adson-type positional occlusion*). Radial pulse stayed full, strong, seventy-two beats per minute without diminishing a hair.

Scientifically, medically, honestly, she could sign him completely cured today and never walk up Judge Silberstein's stairs again.

As she lowered his arm slowly back toward his knee, Ludwig spoke into the quiet room without turning his head.

"On Saturday the seventeenth of April at eleven-forty, Reinhart Kroll's *Referat IV N* surveillance log booked an urgent street-check on *Elektoralna fourteen, courtyard right*."

Sylwia's fingers stayed resting lightly round his bare left wrist for two frozen heartbeats before letting go cleanly.

"Nothing was found in the bombed glazier's shed," Ludwig continued in that same quiet, level tone while reaching for his white shirt. "Whereupon Kroll annoyedly docked two hundred Swiss francs off Friday's Danzig courier disbursement to source *Uhrzeiger*—code-entered on Szucha's Polish desk as *Zegar*—for passing unverified hospital gossip."

Even knowing it already from Pelagia's lace curtain, hearing the cold archival confirmation out of Aleja Szucha's own books landed like iron dropping down a shaft.

"How did you get inside Kroll's disbursement log?" she asked softly.

"One of Kroll's Baltic-German typists on Szucha has a brother serving in a penal sapper company outside Velikiye Luki whose transfer papers cross *Abwehr* East desks once a quarter," Ludwig said dryly, slipping his arms back into white linen without buttoning the front yet, turning on the wooden chair so he looked straight up at her standing two feet away between him and the window. "Who did you feed *Elektoralna fourteen* to on Thursday the fifteenth, Sylwia?"

Every instinct drilled into underground cells screamed *never name a Polish operative to a German uniform under torture, let alone standing upright in daylight!* And Ludwig didn't wait for a name—raising two fingers half an inch to stop her before her lips even parted.

"Don't tell me his name," he said sharply. "I don't want his name in my head. Just listen carefully to the mechanics before you get yourself killed out of loyalty to rotten wood. Whoever you handed *Chłodna twenty-two* and *Elektoralna fourteen* to sits inside twenty-four hours of Kroll's wire—either directly taking Swiss francs himself, or handing every scrap straight to a superior who does. Cut him off abruptly tomorrow without excuse, and if *he* is *Zegar*, he realizes instantly why you went dry and hands your *Koszykowa* address to Kroll inside twelve hours to scrub the witness before your own underground court-martial catches him."

Sylwia's eyes narrowed cold on his face. *Your Koszykowa address.* "When did you look up my home street?"

"Ten February at noon, when Hauptmann von Berg ran security checks on every pair of Polish hands allowed inside Room Fourteen," Ludwig answered without a flicker of apology. "Koszykowa thirty-three, fourth floor rear, partitioned library. Which means if my clerk found it inside six hours on municipal medical rosters, Kroll's dogs find it in ten minutes once somebody points. Keep meeting your handler once a fortnight when summoned, feed him harmless Stabsarzt Vesper gossip—cognac crates, hospital coal thefts, routine troop-train frostbite numbers—and zero names, zero hours, zero streets."

Silence hummed inside Judge Silberstein's study. Outside in Saski Garden a blackbird whistled liquid gold notes in a chestnut tree while half a league north another heavy demolition charge—*crump*—shook window putty faint under Sylwia's fingertips.

"Why?" Sylwia asked flatly, looking down at him sitting bare-chested under unbuttoned linen with her purple scar-star gleaming between collarbones. "Our ledger sat even at St. Charles Borromeo on Chłodna twelve days ago. You got your pelure warning out to London's circle; I got an eighteen-year-old boy out of a bath-closet and three warnings out of your mouth. Why drag me across barbed wire twice a week on a forged medical excuse to coach me how not to get shot by Aleja Szucha?"

Ludwig looked up at her for five long seconds without speaking.

In afternoon chestnut-shadow his face had none of the boyish swagger from Wannsee and none of the fever-vulnerability from Lindleya—just thirty-four years of hard bone, sleepless intelligence, and an honesty uglier than any compliment because it stripped away the last rag of noble pretense between them.

"Because Tirpitzufer is losing its footing in Berlin week by week since Oster's people stumbled," Ludwig said very quietly—meaning nothing specific to her yet on *Oster*, everything on tone—"and inside six months either Kroll's black-coats swallow Saski Square whole, or Warsaw blows sky-high under everybody's boots. Until one of those happens, you are the single human being within four hundred miles who doesn't lie to me like a subordinate or lick boots like Stefan Bielski's tramway clerks—and if Reinhart Kroll hangs you off a balcony on Koszykowa because some fool in a trenchcoat sold you for two hundred Swiss francs, I'll do something stupid enough on Aleja Szucha to get shot before autumn."

Sylwia's breath caught—not soft, sharp as swallowing a needle whole—at hearing *Stefan Bielski's* name and *if Kroll hangs you I'll do something stupid* spoken in the exact dry, furious voice in which at twenty-one he had said *Want me to break Kleist's collarbone tomorrow?*

"Leave my sister's husband out of your files," she whispered fiercely, snatching her lanolin jar off the side-table and dropping it clattering into her leather bag.

"Tell your sister's husband to stop drinking beer at the *Deutsches Haus* annex with Direktor Krehl's bookkeeper, then, and his name won't cross counter-sabotage tramway summaries," Ludwig replied coolly, buttoning his white shirt right-handed from bottom to collar without standing up to block her exit. "Friday at five, *Frau Doktor*. Next time I won't insult your politics with coffee—we have boiled tap water in the pantry."

* * *

By the fourth of May—her third visit across the Królewska sawhorses—the giant black smoke-column over Muranów had thinned to a low, ragged grey pall smelling more of pulverized brick, carbolic lime, and blown-out sewer gas than fresh burning timber, though sporadic pistol cracks still echoed stubbornly after dark out of deep cellar bunkers six feet below glowing rubble.

Something far worse than hostility began settling into those twenty-five minutes every Tuesday and Friday between five and five-twenty-five inside *Wohnung 4*: familiarity.

Not peace—peace never survived ten sentences between them—a taut, silent bodily truce inside four high-ceilinged rooms where nobody else entered once Mekelburg vanished down to polish staff-car fenders in the courtyard.

On Tuesday the fourth of May a sudden violet cloudburst swept off the Vistula at four-fifty, drenching Sylwia from knees to shoulders in warm sheet-rain during the last hundred yards down Królewska after lightning struck a tram-wire on Marszałkowska. When Mekelburg let her up the pantry stair, water ran in dark streams off her felt hat-brim onto waxed tiles.

Before entering the study, while peeling off her sodden charcoal jacket in the unlit pantry passage, Sylwia's eyes snagged on something low along the unpainted inside edge of the kitchen pantry doorframe—missed last week because the door had stood open against the cupboards.

Five horizontal pencil marks notched into varnished pine between three feet and four feet eight inches off the floor, dated in tiny neat script beside a child's name:

*Róża — VI.1935*
*Róża — VI.1936*
*Róża — VI.1937*
*Róża — VI.1938*
*Róża — VI.1939*

No *VI.1940*. Judge Silberstein's granddaughter, nine or ten years old when the pencil stopped—Staś's exact age right now on Filtrowa.

Sylwia stood motionless in her wet white cotton blouse—thin prewar batiste clinging damp to her shoulder blades and black brassiere straps—with one fingertip resting feather-light on *VI.1939* without touching graphite hard enough to smudge it.

Behind her on dry parquet, shod in the soft indoor leather slippers he wore when pacing over maps, Ludwig stopped in the pantry doorway carrying a dry folded hand-towel he had fetched without being asked when he heard rain hammering the courtyard zinc.

He saw where her finger rested on the doorframe.

Neither of them spoke. Not *terrible times*, not *they weren't here when I took the billet*—quartermaster inventories told dates plain enough: two Wehrmacht transport colonels had occupied *Wohnung 4* between forty-one and this January before Ludwig ever unpacked a razor here, and neither dead transport colonels nor grammar changed where little Róża Silberstein's shoes ended up in forty-two.

Noticeably, however, nobody in two years had painted over the inside edge of the pantry frame, and when Mekelburg scrubbed woodwork every Saturday with soda-water, the boy scrubbed *around* those five pencil lines down to an eighth of an inch.

Ludwig held out the dry linen towel without stepping across the pantry sill.

Sylwia took her finger off *1939*, accepted the rough towel without brushing his hand, dried her rain-wet throat, face, and coiled dark hair—pins loosening until one damp black strand fell curling past her collarbone—and walked past him into the study.

Because rain drowned daylight dark green through the chestnut trees outside, Ludwig hadn't turned on the chandelier—only the green-shaded brass banker's lamp on Silberstein's desk, throwing the exact submerged emerald-and-amber aquarium glow across books and leather sofa that had burned above Slate Table Nineteen and Room Fourteen's night-table.

"Lie flat on the sofa today so I can stretch the posterior axillary fold past horizontal without your spine arching," Sylwia said, voice slightly husky from rain-chill, kneeling on one knee beside the long dark-green leather sofa rather than pulling over chairs.

Ludwig lay back flat in unbuttoned shirt-fronts pushed wide off his left chest, head resting directly on cool green leather without bolster, letting her take his left wrist and elbow from behind his crown.

Ten minutes of slow passive stretching—pushing scarred pectoralis major and latissimus fibers millimeter by millimeter into full overhead extension while her bare right hand pressed steady counter-weight down across his warm lower ribs to keep his thoracic cage flat against the leather.

Rain drummed thick and hypnotic against tall glass six feet away. Beneath her right palm his ribs rose and fell slow, deep, eight seconds to a cycle; thin damp batiste of her rain-wet blouse cooled rapidly against her own breasts until nipples tightened hard and treacherous against worn cotton—and when she leaned forward over his shoulder to test internal rotation behind his scapula, her fallen dark damp curl brushed bare across his chin and throat.

Ludwig's eyes stayed wide open two inches beneath her collarbone, grey-green gone nearly black in green banker's-lamp shadow.

He didn't grab her wrist today, and he didn't make a single Berlin crack. Only his right hand—lying loose along the outer edge of the leather cushion two inches from her bent knee on the parquet—closed slowly into a tight, silent fist against smooth calfskin until the leather creaked faintly under his knuckles, waiting out the last sixty seconds of her twenty-minute clock without touching her by a hair.

When Sylwia stood up abruptly at five-twenty-two, blood drumming hot and furious in her ears at nothing that had even happened, her damp blouse had picked up dry warmth off the air six inches above his chest.

"Once a week is ample from mid-May," she said too fast toward her open bag, snapping steel latches shut without looking round. "Your range is ninety-five percent normal and the nerve-tingling's dropping."

From the sofa behind her, buttoning linen slow in green shadow, came his quiet voice:

"Keep Tuesdays *and* Fridays on the card, *Kotarska*. May's going to get uglier before June, and Stabsarzt Vesper never reads range percentages past page one."

She grabbed her half-dry charcoal jacket off the pantry hook and went fast down the waxed service stairs into warm spring rain without touching the doorframe on her way out, breathing deep gulps of wet lilac air in the courtyard before putting her mask back on for Marszałkowska.
