# Four

## The Fire

On the ninth of June 1944 the air was so still that the smoke from the mill went straight up, and the swallows flew low, and the old women said there would be a storm.

There was no storm. There was, instead, a wounded Russian in the forge cellar.

They brought him on the night of the tenth, three men from the forest and Tomasz, on a cart with the boards taken out and straw in the bottom, and he was unconscious and had been for two days, and the wound in his thigh had gone the colour of a plum and smelled of something sweet. He was perhaps twenty-five. He had a name, which was Dmitri, and a photograph in his tunic of a woman with a wide face, and a hole in the left thigh where a shell fragment had gone in and had not come out.

"He'll die," her father said, when they had him on the floor of the forge with the lamp turned low.

"He might not," Tomasz said.

"Look at it. He'll die by Friday and then we'll have a German in the yard asking what the smell is." Her father knelt down with a grunt and put his hand on the man's forehead. "And if he doesn't die, he'll talk in his fever, and the whole village will hear it, and then we'll all be in the ground. Where did you get him?"

"From the swamp. There was a group of them, four, and two were dead, and the others went east. He was in the reeds."

"And you carried him here."

"On the cart. Yes."

Her father looked up at him. "You're a fool, Tomasz Wierzbicki."

"I know."

"Get me hot water and the good knife and all the clean linen in the house, and tell your father to pray, because I can't do both." He rolled up his sleeves. "And then go away. You're not to be seen here."

They kept him four days. The fragment came out on the first night, and her father sewed the wound and packed it with a paste of honey and rye flour that his own mother had used, and Zofia held the lamp and then held the man's shoulders while he thrashed, and on the third day the fever broke and he opened his eyes and said something in Russian, and began to cry, and her father gave him water and said, "There, there, soldier," in Polish, and the man fell asleep again.

On the twelfth the Frydmans moved from the barn to the new hole under the orchard, because her father did not want them in the same building as a Russian who might shout in his sleep. It took an hour, in the dark. Rywka Frydman had to be carried. Sara walked between Zofia and her father with the doll held against her chest, and she was ten now, and thin as a stick, and she had not been out of doors in daylight since 1942. When they got to the orchard she stopped and looked up at the sky, which was full of stars, and she said, "Oh," very quietly, and stood there, and Zofia had to make her move.

On the morning of the fourteenth Zofia was sent with a message to the mill.

It was a stupid errand and she knew it was stupid. The message was in a loaf, and the loaf was in a basket, and the basket had eggs on top of it, and she went by the field path because the road had a checkpoint. The message was from the unit to Pani Zaleska, and it was about flour, and it was written in pencil on a piece of paper the size of a hand, and it was in Tomasz's writing, which she did not know until she was already in the meadow and had stopped to check that the bread was not broken, and saw the shape of his letters, and smiled.

The patrol came out of the birch wood.

There were three of them and one was Vogel, the young one, and one was the Ukrainian they called Iwan, and she did not know the third. They had a dog. She ran, which was the worst thing she could have done, and she got perhaps forty metres before Iwan caught her by the hair and put her on the ground.

They found the paper. Of course they found it. They took her basket apart with the butts of their rifles and shook out the eggs and the loaf onto the grass, and Vogel picked up the paper and read it and laughed, and said something to Iwan in German, and Iwan hit her across the face with the back of his hand, twice, and then they took her to Krasnystaw in the lorry.

The gendarmerie post was in a house on the Lublin road that had belonged to a doctor. There was a yard with a pump. There was a room at the back with a table and two chairs and a strip of carpet, and on the wall a picture of the Führer that had been hung too high, and a bucket in the corner, and a window with the shutters closed and a bar across them, and a wasp working at the window all the time she was in there.

Kliemann came at noon. He was fat and had a pleasant face and he brought a bottle of beer with him and set it on the table and did not offer her any.

"You are Zofia Krawiec," he said in Polish, which he spoke well and with enjoyment. "From Brzozówka. Your father is the smith. Yes?"

"Yes."

"You are seventeen. My God, you are a child." He sat down opposite her and folded his hands on the table. "Do you know what happens to children who carry messages for bandits?"

She said nothing.

"They are shot," Kliemann said pleasantly, "and their families are shot, and their village is burned. This is not my idea. This is the order of the Reich. I am a policeman, I carry out orders." He took a drink of the beer. "But it is a hot day and I am not a monster. You are going to tell me where the bandits are, and then you will go home to your mother, and we will forget the eggs."

She said nothing.

He put the paper on the table and turned it round with one finger. "This is a boy's hand. Look at it. A boy, not a man. Someone from your village, yes? Someone you know. Someone you like, perhaps." He smiled. "You are seventeen. There is always someone."

She looked at the paper. The flour. The signature was a mark, not a name, but the letters were his, and she thought: they will find him. They will go to the mill and Pani Zaleska will tell them nothing, and they will go to the village and they will ask, and someone will say, the organist's boy, he writes like that, everyone knows he writes like that, and they will take his father. And she thought: if I say nothing they will shoot me, and then they will take his father anyway.

Kliemann waited. He was patient in a way that was worse than violence, because it suggested that he had done this many times and had never yet needed to hurry.

At four in the afternoon Vogel came in and spoke to him in German, quickly, and Kliemann's face changed, and he said something back, and Vogel went out.

"There is a wounded man in your village," Kliemann said. "A Russian. My sergeant has been watching your father's forge since Tuesday. Also there are Jews." He leaned forward. "Now, girl. I am going to be honest with you, because I have no time. In one hour I am going to send a company to Brzozówka, and they are going to search the village, house by house, and if they find a Russian in your father's cellar, or Jews, or weapons, then the village will be burned, and the men will be shot, and the women will be sent to the camp, and you will have caused it. If you tell me now, this afternoon, then I will send my sergeant and four men and we will take the Russian and the Jews and it will be a police matter, and the village will be spared, and you will go home." He spread his hands. "That is the whole of it. One hour."

She was seventeen. She had been hit twice in the face and her left eye was closing, and she had been in that room for five hours with a wasp, and she was so frightened that she could taste it, a metallic taste at the back of her mouth, and she was thinking not in sentences but in pictures: her mother at the tub with the church linen; Piotrek asleep; her father's hands.

"The forge," she said.

Kliemann did not move. "What about the forge?"

"Under the forge. There's a cellar. The coal cellar." Her voice came out steady, which afterwards seemed to her the most terrible thing of all. "He's there. The Russian. He's been there since Monday."

Kliemann wrote it down. He wrote slowly, in a small neat hand, and while he wrote the wasp went up and down the window.

"And the Jews?"

"I don't know about any Jews."

"Girl."

"I don't know about any Jews." She heard her own voice go up at the end, like a question. "There are no Jews in Brzozówka. They took them in forty-two."

Kliemann looked at her for a while. Then he shrugged, and wrote something else, and blotted it, and said, "Wait here."

She waited two hours. The wasp died or went away. At six a policeman brought her a piece of bread and a cup of water and did not speak. At half past six Kliemann came back with the paper and put it in front of her, and it was two pages, written in German, and he read it aloud to her in Polish, translating as he went, and it said that she had been detained in the meadow with a message, and that she had stated that a wounded Soviet soldier was concealed in the cellar beneath the smithy of Józef Krawiec, and that there were persons of Jewish origin concealed in the same dwelling, and that she had signed it of her own free will.

"That last part isn't true," she said.

"It is true now," Kliemann said. "Sign."

She signed. Her hand was shaking, and the Z came out with a round loop, careful, a girl's signature, and she looked at it after she had done it and thought, that is my hand, that is my hand on this paper, and then Kliemann took the paper away and put it in a folder and said, "Go home."

"Am I—"

"Go home," he said, and held the door. "Walk. Do not run. If you run, my men will shoot you, and I will have to fill out another paper."

She walked out through the yard, past the pump, past the lorry, and the sentry at the gate looked at her and did not move, and she went down the Lublin road with her arms at her sides, and the sun was going down behind the town and the sky over the rooftops was the colour of a peach.

She walked for half a kilometre. Then she heard the shooting, a long way off, in the direction of the village, and she began to run.

The first thing she saw was the smoke, and it was not the colour she expected. She had expected black. It was white and grey, a great slow column going up and bending east, and under it, as she came over the rise by the birch wood, the village was burning in a line, from the mill end, house by house, in the still air, and she could hear the fire, which was a sound like canvas tearing, and men shouting, and a horse screaming, and she could not see any people at all.

She came down the hill into the smoke.

The mill was gone. The Frydmans' shop was gone. The school was burning. The church was not burning; they had not fired the church, and the wooden spire stood up out of the smoke with the bell gone, and the door was open, and there was a man on the steps with his hands over his head.

She ran to the crossroads. The forge was burning, and her father's house behind it was burning, and the roof had fallen in at one end, and there were flames coming out of the windows in a way that was almost quiet, almost domestic, like a stove on a winter evening.

"Matka!"

Nobody answered.

She got as far as the gate. The heat came off the house like a wall and she could not go further, and she stood at the gate and shouted, and her voice went into the fire and came back to her, and then a man caught her round the waist from behind and carried her backwards, and it was Antoni Szeliga, the deputy sołtys, whose face was black with soot, and he was shouting something she could not hear.

"The orchard!" he was shouting. "Zofia! The orchard!"

She did not understand. Then she did.

She broke away from him and ran along the fence to the orchard, and the orchard was not burning, and the woodpile was there, and the hatch under it was open.

The hole was empty.

There was a chair knocked over at the bottom of the ladder. There was Sara's doll on the earth floor, face up, the china face cracked across one cheek. There was a book. There was the smell.

She stood at the edge of the hole and looked down at it for a long time. Then she climbed down and picked up the doll and the book, and climbed out, and sat down on the grass under the apple tree with the doll in her lap, and that is where they found her, half an hour later, when the fire had come down to embers and the Germans had gone.

They had taken her father. They had taken him alive, with his hands tied, and put him in the lorry with four other men and the young man from the mill, and driven away. Her mother and Piotrek had been in the house. The neighbours had got them out, or had got out what there was. The Frydmans had been taken out of the orchard and shot at the edge of the birch wood with the Russian, and they were lying there in a row, four of them, and someone had put a coat over Rywka's face, and Sara was between her mother and her father with her hand out, and Zofia, when she saw it, made a sound that she had not known she could make, and Antoni Szeliga took her by the shoulders and turned her round and held her against his chest while she screamed into his coat.

Thirty-one people died at Brzozówka on the fourteenth of June 1944. Eleven houses burned, and the mill, and the school, and the shop, and the forge, and the barns with the winter grain in them. The church stood. It stood for another year, and then in 1945 a shell from the front took the roof, and in 1957 the parish rebuilt it in brick, with the money of the parish and a grant from the Office for Religious Affairs which was signed, in the end, on a Tuesday, by a deputy director who had never been to Brzozówka and who did not know, when she signed it, that she had.

Tomasz came at dawn.

He came up the road from the forest with two others and his rifle on his back, and he stopped at the edge of the village and looked at it, and then he came on, and he found her sitting by the church wall with her hands in her lap.

"Zosia."

She looked up at him. He was grey with fatigue and his eyes were red, and he had come fifteen kilometres on foot, and she could see that he had been running.

"Your mother?" he said.

She shook her head.

"And Piotrek?"

She shook her head.

He knelt down in front of her. "Your father?"

"They took him. To Lublin, they said. To the castle."

He was silent. Then he said, "The frydmans?"

She could not say it. She looked towards the birch wood and he followed her look, and he got up, and stood there for a moment with his hand over his mouth, and then he went off towards the trees, and she watched him go, and she thought: now. Tell him now. Tell him now, before he gets there.

He came back in twenty minutes. His face was different. He sat down on the ground beside her with his back against the wall, and neither of them spoke for a long time.

"The Russian too," he said at last.

"Yes."

"I brought him here." He put his hands over his face. "I brought him here."

"Tomasz—"

"I brought him here," he said, from behind his hands, "and they found him, and they burned the village. That's what happened. That's what happened, isn't it."

She looked at him. The sun was up over the birch wood and the smoke had thinned to a haze, and somewhere behind them a woman was keening over a body, a long rising note that went on and on and then stopped.

"Yes," she said. "That's what happened."

She meant, at that moment, to tell him everything. She had it in her mouth. She had the exact words. But the words were: *I told them where he was. Kliemann asked me and I told him and they burned the village anyway,* and every time she got to the end of that sentence, in her mind, there was nothing on the other side of it — no face that she could bear to see, no life after it — and so she said what she said, and then it was too late, and then it was years too late, and then it was 1958.

They buried them on the sixteenth, in the churchyard, in one grave for the ones who had no family, and separate graves for the Frydmans, because Father Zaremba said that they were of the old covenant and should be buried by their own law, and there was no one to do it, so he did it himself, with his own hands, in his vestments, reading the Hebrew he did not know from a book he borrowed from a doctor in Krasnystaw, and he was sick afterwards, and he did it anyway.

The village looked for someone to blame. It looked, first, at the partisans, because the partisans had brought the Russian to the forge, and the men in the forest were not popular that summer; then it looked at the miller, because the miller was a Protestant and had a cousin in the Gestapo, which was not true; then it looked at Antoni Szeliga, who had been seen on the road at Krasnystaw on the twelfth talking to the gendarmes about a permit for the mill.

Szeliga had been talking to the gendarmes about a permit for the mill. He said so, at the meeting in the church porch, in front of eighty people, and he said it again, and nobody believed him, and a man called Bednarek hit him in the face and he did not hit back, and after that no one in Brzozówka spoke to Antoni Szeliga again for nine years, and his wife would not sit beside him in church, and in March 1953 he hanged himself in his barn.

Zofia was in the church porch at that meeting. She was seventeen. She stood at the back with her hands folded and let a man be condemned for what she had done, and she did not say a word, and afterwards she went home to the house that was not there any more and lay down on the floor of the neighbour's kitchen and slept for fourteen hours.

On the twenty-second of July the radio said that a Polish Committee of National Liberation had been established at Chełm, and that the Red Army had crossed the Bug. On the twenty-fourth it said that Lublin was free. On the twenty-third the Soviet tanks came through Brzozówka on the Krasnystaw road, and the village stood at the edge of the road and watched them, and this time nobody waved.

On the twenty-fifth a lorry came up from Krasnystaw with four men in it, in Polish uniform with the eagle on their caps but not the eagle that anyone in Brzozówka recognized, and one of them got down, and he was a man of forty in a captain's tunic with the left sleeve pinned up, and he walked into the middle of the village and stood in the ashes of the forge and looked around him and said, "Where is Józef Krawiec?"

"He was taken," said someone. "In June."

The man turned. He had a broad, ugly, clever face, and grey eyes, and he looked at the burned houses one after another as though he were counting them.

"And the family?"

"His wife and son are dead. In the churchyard." She was standing ten paces away, and she said it herself, in a voice that surprised her. "I'm his daughter."

The man looked at her for a long time. Then he came over and took off his cap, which nobody had done, and said, "I knew your father. In 1937, at Krasnystaw, when they broke up the strike at the mill. He gave me a horse." He put his cap back on. "I am Wiktor Sroka. I am with the Polish Army, and also with the Party, and I have come to see what can be done here."

"There's nothing here," Zofia said.

"There is always something," said Wiktor Sroka. "That is the first thing you learn."

He came back in August, and in September, and by October she was working for him. Not for the Party, at first — she was seventeen and did not know what the Party was, except that her father had hated it and that the men who belonged to it had come in the lorries and had not been afraid. She worked for Sroka because he was the only person in the district who talked about the future as if it were a real place, and because he had lost an arm at the Ebro fighting for something he could explain, and because he looked at her without pity, which after the summer was a relief.

"You are carrying something," he said to her once, in October, in the office they had made in the mill that had been rebuilt as a cooperative. "I can see it. You walk like a woman carrying a bucket."

"I'm not carrying anything."

"Everyone in this district is carrying something. The ones who say they are not are carrying the most." He lit a cigarette, one-handed, with a matchbox held under his chin. "I will tell you a thing, Zofia Krawiec, and you will not believe it for ten years. Guilt is a bourgeois luxury. It is the most expensive thing in Poland and it does no one any good. What happened in June happened because of the Germans and because of the war and because this country is a road between two empires that both like to march down it. If you want to do something about it, then do something. Build. Teach. Join. But do not sit in a corner with your bucket."

She looked at him. "And if it was partly my fault?"

"Then it was partly your fault," Sroka said, "and you will do something anyway, and in twenty years you will look back and see that the something you did was worth more than the fault. Or you will not. But you will not know until you have done it." He put the matchbox in his pocket. "There is a school at Krasnystaw. We need someone to teach the children to read, because the Germans did not, and the nuns are too frightened, and the peasants are too busy. You can read. Pan Wróbel taught you. Go and teach."

She went.

In August, on the ninth, Tomasz was arrested at Krasnystaw.

She did not know for three years. She was at a meeting in Chełm that day, learning how to organize a cooperative, and when she came back they told her that the organist's son had been taken by the NKVD with eleven others from the district and put on a train, and that the charge was service in the Home Army, and that they had been sent east, and that no one knew where.

She went to Sroka. She stood in front of his desk and said, "Get him out."

Sroka looked at her for a while. "Who?"

"Tomasz Wierzbicki. The organist's son. You know who."

"I know who he is." Sroka put down his pen. "I cannot get him out. Nobody can get him out. He was in the AK, and the AK shot at the Red Army in Vilnius, and the NKVD does not ask my opinion about such things." He paused. "Why does it matter to you?"

She opened her mouth, and then closed it.

"Ah," said Sroka. And then, more quietly, "Zofia. Listen to me. The war is not over. It is going to go on for a long time, in this country, in another form. There will be people on one side and people on the other, and you will have to choose, and the choosing will cost you things you cannot imagine now. Do not choose with your heart. The heart is a bad commissar. Choose with your head, and then make your heart follow, and in ten years you will find that it has."

She stood there. Outside, the mill wheel turned, and someone was singing in the yard.

"I don't know what that means," she said.

"You will," said Wiktor Sroka.
