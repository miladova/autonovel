Transtiberim

Diomedes goes to Transtiberim to buy thread. He buys, in the course of a slow and scented morning among the fullers' vats — urine and earth and the clean, assertive smell of other people's garments being forgiven — a great deal of thread. He buys red, which the house does not need, and undyed linen thread, which it does, and he buys, with the unhurried negligence of a man spending someone else's money on someone else's errand, the following: that Vibius the fuller was freed twenty-two years ago by a master in the tanning trade; that his wife, dead two winters, was a Baiae woman, a laundress, who came to Rome in the household of a dealer; that the couple, childless through the first years of their freedom, took in a child in the winter of the year the old emperor died — a girl, days old, brought by a midwife whose nails, an old woman at the vat remembers unprompted and then wishes she hadn't, were hennaed; that money came with the child, not much, regularly, for some years, then stopped, as money does when the hand that sends it dies or decides the account is closed; that the girl was raised as born to them — “and whose business is it, Greek?” says the old woman, suddenly, with the fuller's wife's whole defensive city in her voice — “she was fed, she was taught, she's betrothed to a baker with a mother like a customs office, she is *theirs* in every way the gods inspect—” and Diomedes, who has what he came for and more than he wants, buys thread from the old woman at her price, which is also a way of saying: this conversation is a transaction; I was never an inquiry.

He does not go straight back across the river. A man who has bought information at a vat the way he would buy thread — casually, at the seller's price, without informing their fathers — owes his employer, and himself, a second column. Diomedes has kept other people's books too long to carry a single entry up the Caelian and call it an audit. So he continues, thread parcels accumulating on his arm like a tradesman's alibi, down the fullers' street to where the tanners have theirs, the smell changing its argument as he crosses the invisible border between two of Rome's necessary stenches.

The tanner who freed Vibius is long dead, but the trade keeps widows the way the Senate keeps masks: for the record, and so that somebody in the family can still answer questions in a voice with authority in it. The widow is eighty, deaf in the ear she presents to creditors, sharp in the other. She sells Diomedes a strap he does not need and tells him, for the strap's sake, that Vibius was freed with money in the transaction that the family — *her* family, she says, arranging her hands like tools on a bench — did not supply entire.

“Someone put up the rest?” Diomedes asks, examining the strap as if straps were the errand.

“Someone's *steward* put up the rest. Twenty-two years ago come the vintage. Quarterly after, whilst the child was little. It was called a debt my man owed in Campania, only my man, God keep him, never owed a debt in Campania in his life except a mule, and we ate the mule's replacement in the hard year.” She looks at him with the one sharp ear's worth of hearing. “You are from a house that buys thread.”

“I am from a house that pays on the day.”

“Then pay for the strap, Greek, and I'll throw in what my man said at the end, which was that the money was clean even if the errand wasn't, and that Vibius would have taken the girl for nothing by the second winter. Fullers,” she says, with a tanner's whole professional contempt and, underneath it, the respect of one river trade for another, “keep what they're given to keep. It's the earth in their hands. Everything sticks.”

At the guild office — a room over a wine shop where the fullers keep their common chest, their funeral lists and a clerk with a cough like a small dog announcing strangers — Diomedes buys no thread at all, the guild not selling any, and is therefore obliged to be himself in his second-best capacity: a respectable freedman making enquiries for a mistress who commissions work across the river and wishes to place further work with a household *of good standing*. Standing is a word the guild understands in all its denominations. The clerk produces, from a chest that smells of cedar and other men's funerals, the household's entry: manumission registered, dues paid without interruption, a wife's death two winters ago with the guild's contribution to the pyre recorded, a daughter of the house listed — the clerk's stylus moves past the line at the speed of men who have written such lines before — *in the year after the old emperor's death*, in the column where the guild, which is wiser in these matters than the Senate, records not births but *arrivals in the household*.

Diomedes looks at the line for exactly as long as a man verifying standing should look, and no longer. He has, in the course of eleven years, read most of the important sentences of his working life upside down, in passage, or by the light of other people's intentions. This one he reads right way up, and it is worse.

“The dues are never late,” the clerk says, apologising for the house in the way of guilds, which is to say recommending it to history. “Vibius pays in the bad winters first and eats after. The daughter sews for half the bridal trade this side. If your mistress wants standing, Greek, there it stands.”

“My mistress,” Diomedes says, “wants thread,” and buys, from the wine shop below because the morning requires ballast for the story he is not telling, a flask he will present to Barbatus as the expedition's entire official yield.

By then it is past noon and the river light is coming up off the water in the hard, interrogative way it does in winter, making everyone's errands look like testimony. He makes one more call, and it is the one he has been scheduling for last since the vats, the way men schedule the dentist they call a physician in Rome and the word is the same in Greek. The dealer's household that brought the Baiae laundress to Rome has dwindled, in twenty years, to a freedwoman keeping a cookshop by the island bridge, where she sells a lentil dish with an imperial reputation among bargees. Diomedes eats it standing. It is very good. He says so. In the freemasonry of freedpeople who keep books in one form or another, a compliment about lentils, paid on the day, will open almost as much as money.

“Claudia,” she says, when he asks, in the third person of a man ordering thread, after old servants of the house. “The Baiae woman. She married across to a fuller, tanner's man Vibius. Hands like yours — counting hands. She could carry a house down ninety stairs and tell you the tally at the bottom without spilling.” The cook wipes a bowl in the way of women finishing a subject. “She came up from Baiae the winter I was still in the house. Brought nothing with her that I ever saw, except the counting. Then a year or so on there's a baby in the fuller’s house and money coming through hands, and Claudia — I went to the naming, Greek, I ate there — Claudia looks at that child the way our mistress used to look at a table she'd set herself. Like a thing she'd *arranged* to keep. There's women have children and women keep them, and sometimes the gods file them under different names. You want more lentils?”

He wants, suddenly, to be on the bridge with the river under him doing its old work of carrying things away in pieces too small to invoice. He pays for lentils he has not ordered a second bowl of, which the cook accepts as the conversation's true currency. Three columns now agree, in the only book this item will ever be entered in, which is his memory, in the hand he keeps for facts that must survive fashion.

He walks home across the island bridge slowly, a freedman in early middle age carrying thread and an undetonated century. He has served Antonia eleven years. He knows her books' three handwritings, her policy on glass jewellery, the exact depth of the silence she keeps about Baiae; he has never, in eleven years, seen her face do what he is now obliged to walk toward and cause. He composes, as all good secretaries compose, the order of the evening. He decides against the order of the questions. She taught him that herself, years ago, over a disputed invoice: *Tell me what happened, Diomedes. I will supply the cross-examination internally, at my usual rates.*

She listens with a stylus in her hand. She does not write. At the hennaed nails the stylus stops its small, unconscious turning, and at the money stopping it resumes, and these are the only two entries her body makes in the record.

“The year,” she says.

“The winter the old emperor died.”

“Nineteen years.” She counts it aloud at last, in the counting voice, the coins-in-the-dark voice. “Nineteen. The midwife said — not strong. A girl, not strong.” The stylus turns. “Who sent the money?”

“The vats do not know. It came through the dealer's people at first. Then through a steward. A Quirinal household steward, the old woman thinks, before she decided she thought nothing.”

“A steward.” Antonia looks toward the courtyard, where the damp spot has dried in the week's sun to the faintest map of Spain. “My mother's master's steward was called Philodorus. He had a way of settling things at a distance and charging the house for the mileage. He died — Eirene heard it from a laundress — nine years ago. The same winter as old Gnaeus Cornelius.” She sets the stylus down in exact alignment with the edge of the tablet, an act of government over the only province presently obeying her. “So the money stopped when the men who were paying to forget had both finished dying.”

“Philodorus,” Antonia says again. The name does what names do in her memory: it opens a room with furniture in it. A steward's office off a Quirinal corridor, oak galls and other people's weather, and a man of forty with a pen-knife he used the way other men use doctrine, trimming, trimming. He settled the rental rolls. He settled the young master's mishaps. In the winter everything in that house changed its address, he settled — but the room will keep. She has housekeeping to do in this one.

“Show me the years,” she says.

Diomedes takes a fresh tablet — not any of the three books' wax; a practice tablet, the kind the kitchen boy learns on, which will be smoothed by supper. It is the most serious gesture available in the room and both of them know it. He writes in his own hand for once, the hand nobody sees, quick and ugly and hers by long hire. A winter. A muster morning she has not named and he does not either; he writes around it the way stewards write around a stair. A dealer's contract: *with increase*. She watches those two words arrive on a child's practice tablet in her own office, in a clerk's fast hand, nineteen years after she read them upside down standing very still with her hands folded, and the stylus in her own hand — she has taken it up again without deciding to — resumes its turning at a speed she would fine a guest for.

Baiae. Stairs. The laundress Claudia in the same household, counting linen like a liturgy. A midwife — his stylus hesitates, a toll-gate, and writes *nails*, because even in his own hand there are things a man writes the short way. Money in a sleeve. A report.

“The report,” Antonia says. “Write it as it was said.”

“Madam.”

“Write it, Diomedes. We are auditing. Upside down and at their prices is how I have always read it.”

He writes: *a girl, not strong*.

The room goes on being a room — courtyard light, the ghost-map drying, somewhere Ammonius instructing a sauce in the difference between Alexandria and Rome — and Antonia looks at the four words that bought her ability to stand up and climb stairs again within the fortnight, and finds that at thirty-eight, with a house around her and three sets of books, the words have lost their power to be the end of the account. They are an entry. Entries can be audited. It is a poorer comfort than the one they replaced, and better, the way medicine is better than honey.

“On,” she says.

The river crossing. The tanner's manumission completed with other people's money, the guild chest, the cookshop by the bridge: *arrivals in the household*. Quarterly sums through a steward until the winter both men died. Then nothing — the nothing that is, in Diomedes' rendering, its own eloquent column, because the household continued, the dues were paid in bad winters first and the girl was taught, counted, kept. He draws a line under nothing, as if it were a sum.

“That line,” Antonia says, “is the first honest item anyone has entered in this account in nineteen years. A fuller and a laundress, paying silence off by instalments long after the men who bought it stopped funding it.” Her voice is doing its duties. Her hands have stopped asking permission so successfully that they have stopped altogether, folded now, the way they were folded once for a contract reading, in front of a dealer. She notices this. She un-folds them, deliberately, an act of government over the province she actually owns. “What is she called in the guild book?”

“Prima,” Diomedes says. “Vibia Prima. In the column for arrivals.”

“First,” Antonia says. The stylus turns. “They called the kept one *first*. Write it down — no.” The correction crosses the room like the laugh in the dining room changed key. “Don't write that down anywhere, Diomedes. That word is hers and theirs. We are not registering her in this office. We are learning, at my usual rates, what the city did with my instructions and my money and my—” She stops, at the exact edge of the noun, the way she stops at the edge of a table she has set herself. “With the goods,” she finishes, in the dealer's own Latin, savagely. “His word. Let it stand in the minutes next to his name, wherever dead men keep them.”

He smooths the practice tablet as he writes, paragraph by paragraph, so that the facts go out of the world in the order they came into the room: in the order of the evening, though neither of them says the phrase, because phrases, like letters, arrive at addresses their authors never wrote, and this one is not due on the Caelian in a slave's hand yet. What remains when the wax is smooth is a man and a woman in an office with nothing on paper at all and everything in the second column.

“One more column, madam,” Diomedes says quietly. “The tanner's widow said a thing for the price of a strap. That Vibius would have taken the child for nothing by the second winter. I entered it nowhere, but I bring it as it was given: it has a face.”

Antonia looks toward the courtyard, where Eirene is presently crossing with lamps, and where, on Tuesdays, a needlewoman counts with her mouth open and refuses the kitchen. “Yes,” she says at last. “That one has a face. That is how I know it is not paper.”

Diomedes says nothing. It is the most expensive service he renders her, and he renders it now at the full rate.

“I will not have her told by a vat,” Antonia says at last. “Not by a Greek buying thread, not by a committee, not by the baths. If there is a telling, it will be in this order: I will be certain; she will be told by someone she already trusts with a needle; and no one — Diomedes, write this in whichever book governs the end of the world — no one will use her, meanwhile, as an instrument against any living Cornelius. She is not paper. I have paper for that.”

“Thread,” Diomedes says, and bows, and goes to put eleven years of discretion around a morning's purchases like linen around glass.

Across the river, at the same hour, in an office smelling of cedar and old evenings, a practice tablet is being smoothed for the third time by a man putting his day's discretion around it like linen around glass. The smoothing takes longer than the writing took. That is also professional. Diomedes has smoothed money-laundering, a senator's ring, two elopements that the books record as *staff, transferred*, and once — in the two-table years, in a lodging over a fuller's whose vats now do the house's linen at a family rate neither party will itemise — a letter Antonia dictated standing up and then stood watching him unwax, word by word, with the expression of a woman supervising the carrying of her own furniture.

He brings the clean tablet back to the desk where she is still, absurdly, working. “The thread account is closed, madam,” he says. It is their word, after today, for an entire department of government. “The house is owed a hanging's delivery on Thursday. Barbatus will walk it over himself. He has views about the bridge — that it is watched, and that watching is to be answered with lamp oil and courtesy.”

“Then Thursday,” Antonia says, and does not take up a stylus, and the lamps go down by sections across the courtyard, the theatre lit from the back going home. “Diomedes. In whichever book governs — yes. That one. Enter this: *nothing was used today. Nobody was spent.* Make it look boring enough to survive.”

He bows, and writes nothing down, which in his system, as the whole city will one day have cause to learn, is the most serious form of record.

Prima knows nothing of any of this, which is the last night of not-knowing she will ever sleep in whole, though she has no way to mark it. Her day is fully occupied by the ordinary treacheries: the baker's mother, who has discovered that Prima reads and is consulting her own mother-in-law scars about whether reading in a wife is a dowry or a defect; her father Vibius, whose cough has taken up residence for the winter and is redecorating; the hanging's second border, complete now, paid on the day — with a sum for thread, itemised, which made her father look at her over his soup with the expression of a man who has always known his daughter was worth more than the world agreed and is experiencing vindication as a kind of grief.

The baker's mother has chosen this morning, from a calendar of inspections that the neighbourhood keeps the way the Senate keeps its committees, to find out whether reading in a wife is a dowry or a defect. She has devised a test. It arrives in the form of Titus's yeast account, delivered by Titus himself, broad-handed and apologetic, with flour on one forearm like a senator's stripe and a story about the yeast man's widow that is clearly intended to be the reason the account is in Prima's hands at all.

“Mother says will you cast it, since you're here with nothing but the hanging done.”

“I am here,” Prima says, “with the hanging done. It took a month. Rome was built slower, Titus, but with more help.”

She casts it at the fullers' table, with her father supervising the house's noises from the doorway and coughing disapproval of all secondhand arithmetic. The yeast man's bill is wrong by eleven sesterces in the yeast man's favour. It is in the second column. It is always in the second column. She shows Titus where his own hand, in a hurry at the oven door, has let a figure be carried like a tray — seen, applauded, set down in the wrong room.

“Eleven,” Titus says, with the injured respect of a man watching his future being competently itemised. “That's a day's buns.”

“That's a day's buns a month, which is a roof in ten years.” Prima sands the tablet with the edge of her thumb, a trick she has lately acquired from watching better clerks than Titus, in houses he does not ask about. “Tell your mother reading is a dowry. In my case it comes with tools.”

Titus laughs, and the laugh does the thing it does in small rooms over vats: it recruits the father, the doorway and a neighbour carrying a garment past the window into brief, unreasonable citizenship. This is what her betrothal looks like from inside, on a good winter morning: not the contract read once, quickly, with her dowry of tools stumbled over; not Mother Livia's hems; but a boy with flour up his arm holding a ladder steady while she climbs into a column and comes down with eleven sesterces and the neighbourhood's opinion, revised by half a degree, in her favour.

Mother Livia herself arrives at noon with soup, which in that family is a flag of truce with vegetables in it. She has heard about the eleven sesterces at the speed Servilia hears things in another tax bracket. She sets the soup down, inspects the hanging — finished now, folded face inward over the back of the good chair, two hundred flowers sleeping on each other like a cohort at rest — and runs her thumb, the customs officer's thumb, along the border's edge.

“The mistress on the Caelian paid on the day?”

“On the day, Mother Livia. Thread itemised.”

“Itemised,” the older woman repeats, tasting the foreign coin. “Your mother would have liked a woman who itemises. She kept that book of hers — the leaves — stairs and owings. A body would think she meant to present it in the next world as evidence.” She says it without looking at Vibius, and Vibius, in the doorway, looks with great attention at the soup, and Prima, between them, understands that a conversation she is not in has just taken place over her finished work, in the dialect adults use where children used to stand. She files it where she files all such exchanges: under her needles, where the leaves themselves live, weighing nothing.

The guild women come in the afternoon, in the plural that in Transtiberim constitutes an institution. They have come about Vibius's cough — a syrup, a visitation rota, the serious business of whether the guild physician is to be asked for a second time at the guild's expense or whether the fuller will be allowed, as one of them puts it, to *die at his own rates like a contractor* — and they stay, as the plural always stays, for the unfolding of the hanging. Prima shakes it out over the table. The red flowers march. A silence performs the neighbourhood's true review.

“Put your name in it,” says the oldest of them, the woman from the vats, who has lately been looking at Prima the way Mother Livia looks at a hem. “In the border. Where it won't show unless you're looking for who held the work.”

“Tradeswomen don't sign table linen,” Prima says.

“Tradeswomen sign whatever will be read after they're filed.” The old woman's hands stay in her sleeves. She has lately sold a great deal of thread to a Greek from the right bank, at her price, and the transaction has completed itself in her like a sum coming right at the second count. “Your mother signed stairs, in Baiae, with her feet. Ninety of them, twice a day, with her name in no book the house kept.” She looks at the flowers as if they were a procession she has decided to let pass. “Sign yours, girl. It is a new fashion. I have it on the best authority across the river.”

Prima does not sign. Not yet. She folds the hanging face inward, so that whatever the world does to it in transit happens to the back of the flowers, and the guild women go out into the winter street with the syrup administered, the rota arranged, and the neighbourhood's whole unwritten archive adjusted by one entry, somewhere between the quarters.

“Eat at the house?” he asks.

“Never. As instructed.” She pushes his cup toward him; the cough medicine is mostly honey and authority. “The mistress asked after my mother's town. Baiae. I said she talked about stairs. The mistress went quiet the way rich women go quiet at a price.”

Vibius looks into his cup for a long time. He is a man who has carried other people's garments through urine and fuller's earth and out the other side clean, and he believes, in his bones, that most things can be got washable if you do not rush the soak. “Your mother,” he says, “talked about stairs because in Baiae she worked for a house with ninety of them and a mistress who timed her. She said a person should count stairs so the stairs don't count them.” He coughs. “She was the countingest woman I ever knew, your mother. Counting and keeping. Keeping especially.”

“Keeping what?”

“Us,” Vibius says, and drinks, and is discovered by his daughter, watching him, to be looking at her with the full apparatus of his love arranged on his face like tools on a bench, which frightens her more than any hem-inspection, because her father is a man who keeps his feelings the way he keeps his accounts: accurately, and at the back.

After supper — soup at the front of it, in the position of honour, so that Mother Livia's truce is properly entered in the house's own books — Prima does the thing she has been not doing since the vats woman left. She takes out the leaves.

They live under her needles in the work-box, folded into a square of undyed linen, and they smell, after two years, of the box's cedar and faintly of the vats, which is to say of every true thing in her life. They are not a book. They will never survive an editor, a fire, or a winter in a damp room, and her mother, who understood material better than any woman Prima will ever meet on either bank, clearly intended exactly that frailty: these are accounts meant to be presented, not preserved; evidence for a hearing in a jurisdiction that keeps no copies.

The hand is the counting hand, learned late and written large. *House at Baiae, the widow's, ninety stairs.* Under it, columns that are not columns yet: names, with the work beside them, the way the guild book writes arrivals. *Aemilia, linen. Paid festival only.* *Io, lamps. Gone north with the sale.* *Girl from Puteoli, name not asked, carried water the hard winter, owed the summer.* Page by leaf-leaf they go, through houses whose owners Rome has filed under furniture, until the hand changes: smaller, later, the letters coming the way steps come when the basket is full. A new column, headed with a word Prima has read a hundred times without its ever having explained itself, the way household gods don't: *Kept.*

Under *Kept*, names again. Hers is not among them. She has noticed this before, in the noticing way of girls who read other people's news held steady: by expecting the omission so completely it has never had a shape. Tonight, with the old woman's voice still in the room — *sign yours, girl; I have it on the best authority across the river* — the omission stands up off the leaf like a figure mis-carried into the second column. Her mother kept a ledger of the unpaid and the kept, of stairs climbed with nobody's name in the house's books, and of the women the houses owed when they died. In all those leaves there is no entry for a daughter arrived. There is a blank space, wider than the others, after the last *Kept* name, with a stitch-mark beside it — Prima puts her thumb on it, the customs officer's granddaughter by trades rather than blood — a little cross, carefully, like a count being taken of something that would be finished later.

“Father,” she says. The room's noises arrange themselves the way troops arrange in other houses' atria. Vibius is in the doorway of the sleeping room with his honey, supervising. “In Mother's leaves. Under Kept. There's a space.”

He looks at her for a long time, and she understands that she has walked a question back to the wrong end of an evening that has not happened yet, and that her father — who keeps his feelings the way he keeps accounts, accurately and at the back — is deciding, at a pace the cough sets, what can be carried and what must be set down in this doorway, tonight, between the soup and the honey.

“Your mother,” he says at last, “left spaces where the count wasn't finished. She said a completed book is a dead one.” He coughs, and drinks, and goes through to his bed with the competence of a man banking a fire he will not see lit. “Sign nothing till the count comes right, Prima. She'd have said that. It'll be among the sayings.”

She folds the leaves face inward, the way she folds her best work, so that whatever the world does to them in the night happens to the back of the flowers. Then she takes the day's last job off the good chair — her own hem, as it happens, parting at the ankle like a sentence deciding to be continued elsewhere — and sews it in the light going blue at the shutter, counting stitches with her mouth open, unaware, as her mother once put it, that the stairs or the centuries are counting her back.

That night she dreams — she will remember this later, and disbelieve the remembering, and be right both times — of a courtyard with roses and a woman counting coins in the dark, getting it wrong, beginning again. She wakes before the count comes right. The winter light is at the shutter. Her father's cough is counting itself in the next room. She gets up to heat the honey, an ordinary daughter in Transtiberim, last morning of the world as filed, and puts a pot on a small fire with the competence of women in her family going back, though she could not name them, through laundries and dressing rooms, through ninety stairs in Baiae and a mirror held steady at seventeen, all the way down the long female ledger of Romans who kept other people's houses standing and called it, because the language offered nothing better, nothing at all.
