Every Favour a Debt
A year is not an ending. Rome disapproves of endings; it has aqueducts instead. Things arrive where the maps claimed they did, late, by gravity and maintenance, and the city drinks without inquiring into the small unglamorous lettering. But a house keeps its own calendar. A house counts from the morning the damp came through a wall in the shape of a country it had never been to, and from an inspection notice left before dawn, and from the sound — still the truest minute Antonia owns, more reliable than any portent the colleges sell — of a needle going in, and coming out, and the flowers along a border learning how to be the same and not the same.

So let the record, in whichever hand survives the quarter, close the books on the following. The child across the river is born by harvest, a girl, in the lean-to room above the vats where frankly so much of the century was laundered. She is called, after business in the family that the baker's mother calls respect and Daphne calls orchestration, Lucia Vibia — the river first, the light before it, paperwork after a decent interval — and she has a thumbprint, if a person were fanciful, on the inside of one wrist, and she will be taught letters before thread by women competing on the point like rival firms. The bakehouse flourishes at the rate of honest weight. The hanging with the red flowers goes back and forth across the bridges on feast days until the neighbourhood begins to date things by it, which is how traditions, like debts, acquire the patina of having been inevitable.

Marcus goes, at summer's end, as a junior officer to an eastern staff, not his father's war, which has gone to safer hands, but a real one, with baskets. He writes home in Latin of steadily improving plainness. From Antioch he writes once to Transtiberim: three lines, no Greek beyond a place-name, and an unsuitable present of writing tablets for a niece not yet born when he sent them, which Prima puts with her tools, evidence admissible at last. Cornelia, from a house that no longer leaks into the dining room, becomes the Avidii's connection to every singer in Rome worth paying properly, and corresponds — through the house's careful channels, at the usual rates of sororal paperwork — with a needlewoman of remarkable hems whose trousseau work her household commissions above the market. The letter in which she first guesses why is never sent. Servilia, who has scheduled everyone else's acts of this drama for nineteen years, sends instead, on the suitable feast, a recipe request. It comes back copied in Diomedes second hand, the one things live in. The pastry cook, learning that Servilia has made his almond cakes for a committee of aqueduct widows, takes to calling himself the pastry cook to two empires, and has to be sat on, kindly and specifically, by both.

Lentulus grows older the way his family goes into decline: with great dignity and improved water pressure. His name is spoken in baths, as steam; it is carved, also, in small letters where things work. Hadrian comes home from Athens and the future clanks gently up the Palatine in Greek dress; the emperor goes east to his war at last, to the drum-rolls of the stone yards, and the old men who stayed behind to mind the city's plumbing find themselves, to their discreet surprise, in something resembling favour. It lifts nobody to a column. It keeps the fountains running. Lentulus, inspecting an arcade in the second spring, is heard by an engineer to say that history is mostly maintenance and the rest is scaffolding. The engineer, who wears foreign trousers and has read exactly the legible part of the frieze, reports this in the baths as philosophy. It does the rounds. It reaches the Caelian in the door-book version by noon.

And the house. Let the record show the house. Registered, taxed, inspected by appointment, its women signing their contracts with a clause in their own hands at the foot — Daphne first, in letters big enough for auditors, Philomela with a flourish praetors would call distinguishing. The library of *names* the city is convinced it keeps, it keeps exactly as it ever kept them: in Antonia's memory, unminuted, ageing like wine rather than wax. What has changed, if the aediles cared to measure a doorway they no longer measure, is that the house has begun to be described at dinners as respectable. Antonia treats the word the way her house treats glass jewellery: she acknowledges the intention and returns it beautifully. Respectability is a lane that opens. She has spent twenty-one years learning how lanes close. She will take the peace and keep the paperwork.

On the last evening this record keeps — for houses, like columns, must declare a dedication date somewhere, or the scaffolding never comes down — the lamps are being trimmed, Diomedes is failing, on purpose, to notice Eirene correcting his Greek by ear, Barbatus is telling the night porter a story whose dishonest preamble has achieved its final, perfected length, and Antonia Cassia, who was born with nothing worth the law's remembering, stands in her own courtyard with her hand flat to a wall that has stayed dry a full year along a drain whose lettering nobody will ever read. From the great room, the sound of women counting — stitches, bars, coins, stairs — comes as it has always come in this house, under the music, like the true plumbing.

In Rome, every favour creates a debt. She has made a life, and a house, and — in the third set of books, the real one, filed between the quarters among unsigned notes and one folded message about ribbons — a family, out of knowing to the last figure what the debts were. Every secret carries a price: she has paid hers in the only coin that kept its value in the getting, which was work, done on the ground, in season, by name. And the past, however carefully buried —— the past came back across a bridge one Tuesday at a time with a work-box, and sat in the courtyard, and counted with its mouth open, and asked for nothing but thread at its own prices.

She takes her hand off the wall. The plaster is warm now, evening-warmed, hers. Somewhere across the river a child is being counted to sleep. Somewhere on the Palatine an old man is choosing, nightly, at compound interest, better. In the Forum, above them all, a stone war climbs toward a pot of gold ashes and the one perfect view; and at its foot, unregarded, the men with baskets hold up, as they always have, the legible beginning of everything.

“The books are straight,” Diomedes says from the doorway, in the second hand, the one for things that must look boring to survive.

“Then we are even, nearly,” Antonia said. “In this city that's a fortune. Put the lamps up. Rome is coming to dinner.”
