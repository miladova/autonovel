Every Favour a Debt

A year is not an ending. Rome disapproves of endings; it has aqueducts instead. Things arrive where the maps claimed they did, late, by gravity and maintenance, and the city drinks without inquiring into the small unglamorous lettering. But a house keeps its own calendar. A house counts from the morning the damp came through a wall in the shape of a country it had never been to, and from an inspection notice left before dawn, and from the sound — still the truest minute Antonia owns, more reliable than any portent the colleges sell — of a needle going in, and coming out, and the flowers along a border learning how to be the same and not the same.

So let the record, in whichever hand survives the quarter, close the books on the following. The child across the river is born by harvest, a girl, in the lean-to room above the vats where frankly so much of the century was laundered. She is called, after business in the family that the baker's mother calls respect and Daphne calls orchestration, Lucia Vibia — the river first, the light before it, paperwork after a decent interval — and she has a thumbprint, if a person were fanciful, on the inside of one wrist, and she will be taught letters before thread by women competing on the point like rival firms. The bakehouse flourishes at the rate of honest weight. The hanging with the red flowers goes back and forth across the bridges on feast days until the neighbourhood begins to date things by it, which is how traditions, like debts, acquire the patina of having been inevitable.

The birth, when it comes, has the decency to come after the guild feast and before the worst of harvest weather. This is entered by Mother Livia as the child’s first act of household cooperation and therefore as a promising sign. The hanging has been brought across in a cart with Barbatus walking beside it like an honour guard who has seen too much history to be trusted with perspective. For one day the red flowers have framed a bakery door through which half of Transtiberim has passed carrying baskets, babies, toolboxes and opinions. The Caelian house sent no representative more controversial than Barbatus and a quantity of almond cakes listed as samples. The samples were inspected like hems and destroyed by acclamation.

By the time Lucia arrives, her curriculum has already been settled by four generations of women in two postal districts. Letters before thread. It is Prima’s sentence, but Claudia’s handwriting is all over its ancestry and Antonia’s tenderly withheld applause all over her restraint. Titus, faced with a daughter whose thumb rests during sleep in a position suspiciously like someone testing pressure, announces that she will also learn weights. “Letters, thread and whether a man is shorting the flour,” he says. “After that, poetry can queue.”

The name takes longer. Names do, in families that know how much damage a tidy inscription can do. Lucia for the light, say some. Vibia for the house that already knows how to keep her. Nothing from the Palatine, no ancestral stone unloaded onto a person currently engaged chiefly in appetite and volume. Marcus, informed by a channel so indirect it might legally qualify as weather, sends no name. He sends later, from far away, writing tablets of a quality Prima inspects silently and puts away because a gift need not be accepted at its sender’s pace to be retained.

Antonia does not attend in any capacity the neighbours could be asked to describe. She sends what is asked for, which turns out to be linen whose seams Eirene has pronounced too good for any infant likely to respect them, and a small hoard of coin entered through the guild as a customer’s customary present for feast work. Every instinct trained by a life of surviving rooms wants to make the gift larger. She does not. Prima has not asked to be carried. She has asked—by every sentence she has ever put into Antonia’s office—to be counted correctly.

On the third day, a note arrives at the Caelian not through a courier but with the bread order, folded between an invoice and a list of cushions for repair:

*Born Tuesday. Girl. Loud. Titus says defective silence encouraging. Letters before thread agreed across all departments. The child has your way of looking at a room before deciding whether to pay for it.*

There is no signature. The hand is enough.

Diomedes reads it because Antonia gives it to him to read, which is a new category in their working lives: documents surrendered by choice before they can leak. “A favourable report,” he says.

“File it with the hanging loan.”

“The third book suggests a more durable heading.”

“The third book,” Antonia says, “is between quarters.”

“So is the item, madam.”

It goes there. Some histories melt. This one is given a slightly better room.

Marcus goes, at summer's end, as a junior officer to an eastern staff, not his father's war, which has gone to safer hands, but a real one, with baskets. He writes home in Latin of steadily improving plainness. From Antioch he writes once to Transtiberim: three lines, no Greek beyond a place-name, and an unsuitable present of writing tablets for a niece not yet born when he sent them, which Prima puts with her tools, evidence admissible at last. Cornelia, from a house that no longer leaks into the dining room, becomes the Avidii's connection to every singer in Rome worth paying properly, and corresponds — through the house's careful channels, at the usual rates of sororal paperwork — with a needlewoman of remarkable hems whose trousseau work her household commissions above the market. The letter in which she first guesses why is never sent. Servilia, who has scheduled everyone else's acts of this drama for nineteen years, sends instead, on the suitable feast, a recipe request. It comes back copied in Diomedes second hand, the one things live in. The pastry cook, learning that Servilia has made his almond cakes for a committee of aqueduct widows, takes to calling himself the pastry cook to two empires, and has to be sat on, kindly and specifically, by both.

Marcus’s departure is the first family ceremony at which no one tries to improve the cast by leaving somebody out of the reported version. He goes east not to a governorship wrapped in ancestral expectation but to a staff appointment where his duties include supply returns, interpreters and the mapping of roads that exist mostly as opinions held by tired men. Servilia sees to his equipment as if outfitting a province she may need to repossess. Lentulus sees to his debts, which is more loving and less photogenic.

Onesimus packs a spare cloak strap without being told. It has been mended by a needlewoman across the river at trade rates and finished during a series of transactions nobody at the Palatine is expected to declaim at breakfast.

“You will write plainly,” Servilia instructs him at the gate. “Greek is for showing Athens you were educated. Latin is for telling your mother whether your socks correspond to the manifest.”

“Mother, the army has quartermasters.”

“The army has quartermasters in the way this family has clerks. Their existence is not a substitute for accuracy.”

Lentulus gives him no deathless family sentence. The cupboard under his grandfather’s mask contains a letter in Latin with four lines in it and a sealed tablet whose terrible imperative has lately stopped shouting. He says, “Do nothing where other men’s names are spoken that you would not enter in your own hand.”

Marcus hears the riverine arithmetic in it and does not disfigure the moment by attribution. “Noisily,” he says.

“Preferably not during a night march.”

The letters improve as promised. They begin full of roads. They become, over months, interested in the men who move baskets along them. At Antioch, in a room where the heat enters through every architectural boast, Marcus writes to Transtiberim with the third-person discipline of a man who has learned the price of putting himself at the head of other people’s weather. He sends tablets “for accounts when required” and does not mention an unborn child because he does not know, yet, in a form he is entitled to act on. Fate, receiving this, registers a small protest at the loss of its usual office.

Servilia reads his home letters and annotates the supply figures. Lentulus reads them and says less each time, which in their household is becoming the sound of a man contributing accurately. In one, Marcus writes: *The general is much praised for the river crossing. The river was crossed by engineers who had been up since the fourth hour and a Syrian woman who told us which landing would not eat the mules. We put the praise where the despatch could reach it. I am learning.*

Lentulus takes this letter into the atrium and stands for a while among the masks, not seeking permission. He puts it, after a considered moment, not in the cupboard, but in the ordinary family archive where a clerk can retrieve it during business hours. Progress, in the Cornelii, has begun to look like filing.

Cornelia’s first letter to Prima is not, properly speaking, a letter. It is an order, routed through a Secretary, for two lengths of work suitable for a west-facing room: measurements, light, the warning that red can be reduced. Prima’s estimate comes back in her firm hand with thread separately costed and an unimpeachable sentence about hanging in strong sun. They correspond for some months as women in Rome often do, by making a commercial instrument carry more weather than its face declares.

Only later does a second enclosure begin to appear, on smaller paper: a question about a song’s refrain, a note that the music floor still speaks under the stairs, a request—phrased as household interest—whether a certain stitch can be made to disappear while still holding. Prima answers at invoice length. Sometimes shorter. The household gods of Transtiberim are not consulted on fashion, and Mother Livia reads nothing she has not been given with intent.

Cornelia’s unsent letter lives in her box for most of a season. She takes it out after evenings when the family silence has taken a particular shape across a table. She has written, on the first attempt, an appalling amount of Greek, because strong feeling in her class comes with metre pre-installed. On the second she has written: *I think I know why my mother’s house goes quiet when I sing a line about accounts.* On the third, simply: *May I ask the order of the questions?*

She does not send any of them. Not from cowardice—Cornelia has begun paying musicians in advance and growing a backbone under management—but from a sudden, mature suspicion that ignorance can also be other people’s room. Perhaps Prima has not been waiting to be solved by a sister in a west-facing house. Perhaps a question, sent at the wrong rate, is a demand dressed for dinner.

Servilia sees the page of questions while helping her daughter select which papers should travel on an Avidian estate visit. She reads the top line because it is there and because mothers, even in the fourth ledger, retain vast powers of accidental reconnaissance.

“Keep them at the front of the box,” she says.

“Mother.”

“I am not answering. I am approving storage.” She closes the lid. “When a thing is told for your benefit, Cornelia, it will not be delivered by musicians.”

“Is that a promise of a date?”

“It is a promise of subject matter.”

Cornelia almost laughs. “You are becoming less governed.”

“I am governing smaller territories. It looks like softness from the provinces.”

That week Servilia sends, through correct channels and at a respectable hour, a request for the recipe for almond cakes. It is addressed to a house in the abstract, as if recipes arose spontaneously among institutions. The reply comes copied clearly, with quantities, by Diomedes in the hand that allows things to live. There is also, in Eirene’s inimitable afterthought delivered verbally through Barbatus, an instruction to let the committee wife beat the mixture until it changes its politics.

Servilia makes the cakes herself—supervised by a kitchen staff browsing catastrophe—for an assembly of widows concerned with fountain repairs. They are pronounced excellent by women whose husbands used to complain about taxes and are now, through widowhood and improved pressure, allocating them. She sends no acknowledgement. A week later a second request arrives at the Caelian for the name of the almond supplier.

Diomedes enters it under Correspondence.

“Not family?” Eirene asks.

“Not between the quarters.”

Antonia hears, and for once makes no suggestion about the accounts.

Lentulus grows older the way his family goes into decline: with great dignity and improved water pressure. His name is spoken in baths, as steam; it is carved, also, in small letters where things work. Hadrian comes home from Athens and the future clanks gently up the Palatine in Greek dress; the emperor goes east to his war at last, to the drum-rolls of the stone yards, and the old men who stayed behind to mind the city's plumbing find themselves, to their discreet surprise, in something resembling favour. It lifts nobody to a column. It keeps the fountains running. Lentulus, inspecting an arcade in the second spring, is heard by an engineer to say that history is mostly maintenance and the rest is scaffolding. The engineer, who wears foreign trousers and has read exactly the legible part of the frieze, reports this in the baths as philosophy. It does the rounds. It reaches the Caelian in the door-book version by noon.

The following spring finds Lentulus on the Praeneste road with mud drying into pale maps on his cloak and a work party informing him that the arcade he has come to inspect has decided, in his absence, to become two arcades separated by an aspiration. This is the engineer’s phrase. The foreman’s is shorter and involves gods with weak ankles.

Servilia has travelled out with him by carriage because the widows’ fountain committee—an institution created accidentally out of almond cakes and municipal despair—requires an inspection of its own. She sits in the shade of a market gardener’s awning with three widow-administrators and a bowl of early fruit, extracting from the district representative a timetable so exact that the man begins to take notes on his own excuses.

Lentulus watches from a distance with the cautious awe of a husband who has seen his wife annex a province by catering. “She has a staff now,” he tells the engineer.

“They appear to have been elected by complaints,” the engineer says. “It is the soundest franchise on the road.”

The leak, when found, is not where the maps indicate. It is where a woman who sells onions under the arcade says the ground “thinks wet” before rain. They dig. She is right. Her name is entered on the work return because Lentulus orders it and because the clerk has by now learned that an order from this curator, where names are concerned, is written in the mood of a man who has seen cupboards.

Servilia, informed at lunch, says, “We shall make an office of you yet.”

“I am an office.”

“No, Gnaeus. You are becoming a clerk with ancestry. It is a more useful creature.”

The onion seller’s name is later misspelled on an inscription plaque. Lentulus has it corrected. The correction costs more than sentiment and less than an arch. It is the kind of arithmetic nobody in the baths will ever quote in verse.

Hadrian’s return brings the usual re-sorting of invitations. A dinner now arrives for Lentulus wearing a warmer seal. He accepts some, refuses those that coincide with inspection days, and is reported by an old colleague to have become “difficult to flatter on an empty stomach”. Whether this indicates favour in the new climate or a private revolution in digestion, Rome debates and files under Health.

At one of these dinners a younger courtier asks, too smilingly, whether the famous clause was intended as law or autobiography. The table becomes aware of knives.

Lentulus says, “It was intended as a sentence. Women made it legible. The rest is maintenance.”

The phrase travels, as we know. It reaches Barbatus by noon in the simplified form *history is drainage*, which he delivers while changing a lamp seal. Antonia, hearing it in the correct full version from Diomedes at evening, makes no correction to the public record. Some intellectual property is best allowed to leak.

Across the river, the child acquires a calendar of women. There is the baker’s mother, who regards sleep as a municipal resource and has ideas about its allocation. There are guild women with practical views on swaddling and subversive views on priests. There is a widowed neighbour whose contribution is chiefly to sit near the cradle looking severe at any man who imagines a baby in a working house should be made compatible with his notion of silence.

Prima goes back to work by instalments: estimates at the table, fine work when Lucia permits, teaching a younger fuller’s girl how to make stitch disappear without making the argument look dishonest. She prices the teaching. This scandalises exactly one guild official, who is bought off by the discovery that his own daughter wishes to enrol.

“Education,” Mother Livia says at the hearing, “has become a tradeswoman. I hope the city is paying attention to what arrives at its name.”

Letters arrive too, at intervals no one mistakes for an invasion. Cornelia’s commissions carry a marginal household weather. Marcus’s tablets are now used for Lucia’s accounts—receipts, weights, a fastidious record of which relations by bakery, guild and invoice have brought useful objects during the first quarter of a life. Prima signs nothing on her daughter’s behalf except the facts. Even facts, she has reason to know, need supervision.

One Tuesday the work-box comes to the Caelian with a tiny woollen cap in it awaiting a seam. Lucia does not. The separation is deliberate and, Antonia thinks, educational for everybody queuing inside her own ribs. Prima mends the cap while they talk of strong sun and reduced red. At the door she says, “She has discovered counting.”

“At five months?”

“She watches fingers as if a customs officer is doing something unreliable.”

“An inherited scepticism.”

“Taught,” Prima says. “We are a great family for acquiring characters by watching the room.”

Antonia gives her the revised estimate and no surplus wisdom. Barbatus carries the work to the street cart. The cap goes home in the box, completed, with no hidden flower at all. Sometimes a seam is merely a seam. The house is learning the difference between underwriting a life and leaving a signature in its lining.

There is a will now. There has always been a will—Antonia is not a woman who would leave Rome to compose her posthumous intentions in its own hand—but it has been revised by the Greek in a room so small that death itself would have to sit correctly. Eirene receives money and the right to choose her own address, which she announces will remain wherever the soup is properly seasoned. Diomedes receives an annuity and the care of archive categories no one else can be trusted to pronounce. Barbatus receives enough to retire into a door of his own, an idea he treats as a security risk.

The women’s savings remain unambiguously theirs, with a sentence inserted at Antonia’s insistence after the banker suggested that clarity could sometimes be detrimental to flexibility. “Flexibility,” she said, “is what a hinge does after other people have installed the lock. Write it straight.”

The house itself goes into an arrangement of shares, stewards and conditions too dull for any poet to steal. Daphne, if she wishes, will have an interest in the music side. Philomela’s funeral harmonies are to be encouraged by capital, within rates. The office copies are to survive whichever tax regime thinks itself eternal.

“Is there,” the Greek asks, with his face wiped to its banking level, “a family acknowledgment I should expect in another document?”

“There are acknowledgments in every document she has signed this year,” Antonia says. “None requires your office to hold its breath.”

He accepts this. Prima, by the name that has done the work,has her own papers. If an additional bequest ever travels, it will do so under instructions that survive public explanation. No child of either bank is to be surprised into dynasty by a seal after somebody’s funeral.

“Your late instructions,” the Greek says, “are structurally unromantic.”

“They are load-bearing.”

“A distinction history underrates.”

That night Antonia tells Eirene only the part concerning soup tenure. Eirene, who has known since the Subura when Antonia is consulting death as another difficult guest, says: “Are you ill, love?”

“I am in my forties and own property near drains.”

“So: mortal, with paperwork.”

“At the full rate.”

“Then set another cover in the dining room. I am not having us mourn your legibility before dinner.”

There are things the record cannot close, and the accountant in Antonia is learning to distinguish an open column from an unfinished wound. Cornelia still does not know in the first hand. She knows in the way a room knows a floorboard complains: by shifts in weight, by subjects that take a side door, by a mother who says *partly* and a brother whose letters grow plainer when certain names are absent. If a conversation waits for her in a music room of her own, it is not this book’s appointment to force the hour. Prima, too, has not asked for the estate paper sealed among poetry-proof items. The seal remains. It has become almost polite.

Marcus is still across a moving map. Letters cross the empire faster than justice and slower than appetite. Trajan’s war will find its own outcome; men with baskets will pay at whatever rate glory is currently demanding. One day he may come home with a Syrian woman’s accurate sense of landing-places in his sentences and ask a question of his sister in Latin. Or not. The empty space at the end of his correspondence is currently occupied, correctly, by discipline.

Lentulus will inspect more water. It is not a redemption pageant. Some mornings he is merely an ageing senator with a cough in damp weather and opinions about contractors. Some evenings Servilia finds him in the study with the plain notebook open to *persons to whom precision is owed*, adding a clerk, an onion seller, a son-in-law’s father who was right about tides. The list is growing less patrician and more administratively sound. She corrects the spelling and says nothing about furniture.

And in Transtiberim a first daughter of the new order will be argued over by rival firms of literacy: guild women with ink, a baker with weights, a mother with a needle, a grandmother by invoices who has learned the usefulness of not arriving. At some point a child will be able to read a contract before she is expected to be improved by other people’s summaries. If Rome notices, it will call it a decline in manners. If Rome survives it, it will call it tradition before the child is twenty.

Houses, unlike empires, do not have to be completed to be their own records. They leak, they are maintained, they change what counts as an interior. The books are therefore left, after all, not closed but straight—and the distinction, filed between quarters in the second hand, will have to serve.

By the second spring the clause has acquired what all successful Roman innovations acquire: a nickname, three wrong attributions and a stationer’s edition with commentary nobody admits to needing. Saufia comes to the Caelian at the trades entrance with her cousin’s daughter and a bundle of papers tied this time in red thread. The cousin is taking work in a house whose proprietor speaks of savings as if he invented the weather around them.

“I am not asking for charity,” Saufia says, before soup can be deployed in her direction. “I am asking for Diomedes at reading speed.”

Diomedes reads. The girl—twenty, sharp about cloth measurements and raw about documents—stops him at *retention against contingencies*. “Meaning he keeps it if anything happens?”

“Meaning anything he can name afterwards,” Saufia says. “We have had that rain.”

Antonia, present because a proprietor’s presence at this stage in her life can shorten some negotiations and lengthen the right ones, says nothing until the girl asks whether a copy can be left with someone outside the house. “It can,” she says. “It should. Bring back the version with the wording changed.”

The girl returns three days later with *retention* amended, quantities named, and a rhythm about her that Eirene classifies as a family trait of the recently documented. She does not take the work in the end. A fuller’s guild place opens, less glamorous, better lit. Saufia tells the story in advance of other versions: no torch, just weatherproof paper and a woman who had been told where to get it read.

That is the clause as history. Not a column. A route women tell one another between market stalls, an address in a song’s second hearing, a sentence forwarded at half price and eventually, in the great Roman manner, copied without anyone needing to remember which Cornelius was once afraid of it.

In Antonia books it is entered under Educational one last time. Diomedes considers the heading. “The category has outgrown its room.”

“Then let it be infrastructure,” she says. “Rome will read it better in small letters.”

And the house. Let the record show the house. Registered, taxed, inspected by appointment, its women signing their contracts with a clause in their own hands at the foot — Daphne first, in letters big enough for auditors, Philomela with a flourish praetors would call distinguishing. The library of *names* the city is convinced it keeps, it keeps exactly as it ever kept them: in Antonia's memory, unminuted, ageing like wine rather than wax. What has changed, if the aediles cared to measure a doorway they no longer measure, is that the house has begun to be described at dinners as respectable. Antonia treats the word the way her house treats glass jewellery: she acknowledges the intention and returns it beautifully. Respectability is a lane that opens. She has spent twenty-one years learning how lanes close. She will take the peace and keep the paperwork.

The house has acquired other, smaller revolutions under cover of becoming respectable. Daphne is teaching two mornings a week now: a banker’s niece with a stepmother problem and the daughter of a freed silk merchant whose Latin is excellent and whose respectability can be purchased by the half-hour. Her fees are paid into the strongbox in amounts she reports to Eirene as if filing dispatches from a successfully defended border.

“I am becoming capital,” she says.

“You are becoming less available for tragedy,” Eirene says. “Capital is a side effect.”

Philomela, whose flourish on her renewed contract would have caused a praetor to use the word *distinguishing* beyond his usual workload, has begun arranging harmonies for a group of women who sing at private funerals. “Grief pays promptly,” she explains. “And nobody asks for an encore in the atrium.”

A new musician arrives in spring with references, talent and an immediate instinct for the house’s filing of men according to how they behave around closed doors. She is twenty-six, from Tarentum, and wants her savings held where a husband cannot give them a tour of his debts. Antonia lays out the contract. The clause is cited. The musician reads it twice and asks the question that has become, in the Caelian admissions office, more reliable than an audition: “Who besides me has a copy?”

“The Greek. The registry. Diomedes in triplicate, which is a small country.”

“And if I leave in weather?”

“Settle promised work where people have paid for it. Take what is yours.”

“Do many leave?”

“Not since the front door started appearing in the documents.”

She signs, then plays something so cleanly that Ammonius comes out of the kitchen against all pastry discipline and stands with flour on one forearm like a sailor sighting home. “Hire her before the sauce notices,” he says.

She is hired. Her first public appearance is at a dinner where an aedile’s inspector is present as a private brother-in-law and tries, from force of administrative habit, to look official during the fish. Barbatus serves him wine with enough courtesy to disable a border post.

Later, in the accounts, Diomedes enters the evening under Ordinary Extraordinary. Antonia questions the category.

“It is where the house keeps events,” he says, “that are no longer news and not yet routine.”

“Peace, in working dress.”

“Peace does not itemise, madam. We remain its Secretary.”

The last evening, before the courtyard minute, has its own small fullness of incidents requiring no committee. A Spanish olive merchant arrives with a cousin and a grievance against shipping. A senator’s widowed sister, new to eating in complex houses since the word *respectable* began doing her family’s thinking for it, listens to Daphne’s loom song and sends a message to the kitchen for Diomedes. She wants, not the recipe, but the name of a good banker who will explain a statement without patting it.

Diomedes gives her the Greek and warns that the room is smaller than expected.

“Most useful rooms are,” she says. It goes into the door-book as an unattributed advance in civilisation.

Ammonius launches a sauce that causes two guests to propose marriages to separate relatives of his and one to propose a trading partnership. He declines all offers on the grounds of artistic solvency. Eirene removes an arena-grade drunk from the doorway by addressing him as a former colleague of Barbatus, which is historically inaccurate and operationally perfect.

At the main table the conversation turns, inevitably, to the emperor in the east. The war is discussed in the new style: with maps, confidence and an occasional uncharacteristic pause when somebody says *supply*. A young officer home on leave says the men marching out of Antioch now ask after letters “in plain Latin” from their families because quick meanings travel better in heat. This reaches Antonia through two courses and a pantry door. She says nothing. The house has learned not to make itself the footnote to its own evening.

Barbatus, at the street, encounters a girl with a tray who is running plates between two houses because her employer’s dinner ambitions have exceeded his domestic furniture. She almost drops a polished platter at the sight of a chair procession. He steadies it—not the girl, the tray—and tells her to hold it by the weight, not the shine.

Eirene sees this from the doorway and says, later, in the kitchen where all of Rome is revised: “That is the whole curriculum.”

“Enter it under Educational,” says Diomedes.

“Under Housekeeping,” says Antonia. “We are respectable now. We can let the truth in by the trade entrance first.”

On the last evening this record keeps — for houses, like columns, must declare a dedication date somewhere, or the scaffolding never comes down — the lamps are being trimmed, Diomedes is failing, on purpose, to notice Eirene correcting his Greek by ear, Barbatus is telling the night porter a story whose dishonest preamble has achieved its final, perfected length, and Antonia Cassia, who was born with nothing worth the law's remembering, stands in her own courtyard with her hand flat to a wall that has stayed dry a full year along a drain whose lettering nobody will ever read. From the great room, the sound of women counting — stitches, bars, coins, stairs — comes as it has always come in this house, under the music, like the true plumbing.

She does not, that evening, make a speech. The house would have minuted it in three hands and ruined the fish. But in the passage between courtyard and dining rooms she passes the place where damp once came through in the shape of an unvisited country and remembers January’s arithmetic: inspection before dawn; Barbatus in the doorway; the Greek’s rooms arranged like truths; a girl with a border and a singer with a laugh; a man in a chair smelling of ashes; all the ordinary fear of a woman who had built one life at full price and did not intend to have it surveyed by amateurs.

The wall is dry. It will not be dry forever. That is not pessimism, it is building management. Roofs go. Drains silt. Young men discover new geometries and have to be fed through them. A house is not a monument delivered from time; it is a local arrangement with time, serviced by people who know which stair complains and what a name looks like when it is being kept by its owner.

Diomedes is at the office table, closing one book and not the others. “The inspector has sent next quarter’s date,” he says. “An appointment so firm it amounts to flirtation.”

“Give him the almond cakes.”

“Already minuted.”

“And no doorway measuring unless he requests it as entertainment.”

“The schedule has a column.”

Eirene, whose hair has taken another colour toward iron this year and whose authority has compensated by darkening, comes through with a tray that is absolutely not a mirror. “Are we looking at walls for our morale now?”

“I am inspecting an asset.”

“Inspect one that can be eaten. There is a lamb developing strategy.”

From the great room Daphne begins the loom song, not the whole thing—three bars, a cap placed on the evening, for people who know. Somebody laughs before the accounting verse because some guests have become regular enough to have anticipations. It is, Antonia thinks, the most harmless form of tradition: a joke arriving in a place that has learned to hold its silences correctly.

She goes in to dinner by the front of the room, not the trades side. A small geography. Hers.

In Rome, every favour creates a debt. She has made a life, and a house, and — in the third set of books, the real one, filed between the quarters among unsigned notes and one folded message about ribbons — a family, out of knowing to the last figure what the debts were. Every secret carries a price: she has paid hers in the only coin that kept its value in the getting, which was work, done on the ground, in season, by name. And the past, however carefully buried —— the past came back across a bridge one Tuesday at a time with a work-box, and sat in the courtyard, and counted with its mouth open, and asked for nothing but thread at its own prices.

She takes her hand off the wall. The plaster is warm now, evening-warmed, hers. Somewhere across the river a child is being counted to sleep. Somewhere on the Palatine an old man is choosing, nightly, at compound interest, better. In the Forum, above them all, a stone war climbs toward a pot of gold ashes and the one perfect view; and at its foot, unregarded, the men with baskets hold up, as they always have, the legible beginning of everything.

“The books are straight,” Diomedes says from the doorway, in the second hand, the one for things that must look boring to survive.

“Then we are even, nearly,” Antonia said. “In this city that's a fortune. Put the lamps up. Rome is coming to dinner.”
