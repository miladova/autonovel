What the Column Shows

They dedicate the column the following May, and Rome, which has watched it climb for two years inside its scaffolding, receives the reveal the way it receives everything imperial: by first applauding the emperor and then, having discharged glory, getting on with the serious business of trying to read it. The frieze goes up and up in its spiral: bridges, camps, battles, surrenders, the emperor addressing, the emperor sacrificing, the emperor — carved at intervals close enough that a man at ground level can keep finding him like a heartbeat — while all around him the war happens to other, smaller people, who carry and build and burn and are carried. From the courtyard you can read the beginning and the high middle blurs. For the rest you would need to be a bird, or the architect, or dead and put up there in a pot of gold at the top, as they say the emperor's ashes will be: the one reader of his own war with a perfect view being history, which in Rome means property.

The scaffolding has been coming down for days in a reverse siege, plank by plank, until the city has become used to fragments: a bridge here, a deified shoulder there, a horse’s cannon bone doing more for morale in the market than several speeches. On the dedication morning the last screens come away under the supervision of men whose names will not be carved anywhere, and the crowd makes the sound crowds make when a veil and a building are removed in the same gesture.

It is, everyone agrees before they have seen half of it, magnificent. It is also impossible. Human necks were not designed by Apollodorus.

Vendors have positioned themselves with the strategic genius of barbarian kings. Water is being sold at column prices. Little clay spirals are available for children, inaccurate in every detail except cost. A guide with one eye and absolute pitch in misinformation is explaining to a party from Narbonensis that the emperor appears fifty-eight times, or fifty-nine if you count the sacrifice twice, a theological nuance he is charging extra to settle.

“Does he?” someone behind Antonia asks.

“Count,” says his wife, with the air of a woman who has been asking him to do so in other departments for years.

The priests do what priests do with imperial stone: make it less lonely at the top. Offerings smoke in the officially fragranced direction. A flute player in new sandals watches the conductor as if music were a series of doors opening at public expense. Somewhere on the opposite side, a soldier who served in one of the armies being commemorated is describing a river crossing to his grandson and pointing confidently at the wrong bridge. Neither child nor history appears damaged.

Antonia’s margin is arranged by the purple-dealer with the authority of a man who has kept a viewing privilege alive through four changes of fashion. He has brought a stool for a wife who declines it, cushions nobody admits to needing and shade at a price he waves away because debts, between their houses, have learned to arrive in more useful denominations.

“You chose the morning light,” Antonia says.

“I chose where rich men become silhouettes and ordinary people can see their shoes.” He considers the column with a tradesman’s eye for repetition. “Nice work at the bottom. After the tenth turn it becomes a rumour with masonry.”

“Rome is committed to optimism at altitude.”

“Rome is committed to selling hats. Half this crowd will leave with a neck like a question.”

Eirene, who has brought provisions against the possibility that empire may run long, unwraps bread with the secrecy of a minor cult. “If a god desired us to stand through dedications unfed,” she says, “he would have put kitchens in the Forum and charged admission.”

Antonia stands in the same margin she stood in for the Forum's dedication, veiled respectably, the purple-dealer still making room out of an old debt now refinanced by gratitude. But the company has reorganised itself around her the way water reorganises around a stone it has decided to keep. Eirene is at her elbow. Prima — married since the winter, in a baker's wife's good wool with her tools' dowry half converted to a share in the bakehouse oven — stands at the other, at a distance that family keeps in a crowd full of aediles: close enough for history, far enough for paperwork. Marcus, who has taken to being civil in public forums with the concentration of a man defusing his own youth, inclines his head to the party from across the contractor's wives with a discretion that costs him visibly and is therefore, by every moral the city runs on, nearly worth something. The baker Titus holds Prima's elbow the way men hold ladders, and looks up at the imperial spiral with his mouth open, counting.

Prima’s marriage, when it came after mourning, had been a model of the small town Mother Livia designed: benches in the right streets, guild witnesses fed before witnesses in the wrong mood could form a faction, papers read at a volume suitable for enemies. Titus had held a ladder for garlands and then stood under them looking as if the ceremony had been especially arranged to make use of his one reliable talent. Prima wore no saffron that could be described as a banner. She wore her best blue, a colour that deepened in low light and had at the hem, where nobody could inspect it without exposing their own intentions, one small flower with its petal count privately amended.

Now she wears the tailored plainness of a woman whose work is being commissioned by people who used to spell her name from a guild label. Her share in the oven is no longer a novelty other women discuss in her presence. It has become something much more satisfactory: uninteresting to say aloud and difficult to take away in practice. The tools are still hers. The order book is partly hers. The house has her mark in seams nobody sees unless the seams fail, which they have not yet been given cause to do.

Titus is calculating how many men with baskets it would take to feed the crowd if the dedication went wrong. It is his form of historical scholarship. “The army part I follow,” he says. “All those bridges. But they never carve who made the bread before the bridge. A man doesn’t cross a river on glory before breakfast.”

“They carve sacks,” Prima says. “Look third turn up. You can just see the carrying.”

“Sacks are not men.”

“They have men under them.”

“So has the empire.”

Marcus, across the wives and contractors, hears at least the shape of this because laughter from their little group crosses the formal lane. He does not turn too far. He has been taught this year—by letters, doorways and a mother with a fourth ledger in her voice—how to be present without arranging other people’s papers. He raises a cup of watered wine in the general direction of tradespeople, a gesture large enough to be civility and small enough not to be a claim. Prima inclines her head exactly the distance a customer is owed.

Afterwards Titus says, “He has improved.”

“He is learning to do nothing at public volume.”

“It sounds tiring.”

“It is his first trade.”

Antonia hears, and does not let her face make an entry. This is how it will be in crowds: daughter with husband and bread on her breath, son by another woman in the lane where names are expected, herself between them in a veil that says respectability and a position that says nothing history can read from the ground. Closeness, far enough for paperwork.

It should hurt more. Perhaps at other hours it does. In the morning light, with the column taking all available symbolism and Eirene distributing bread like a ration officer of the private empire, it feels strangely like a sum that knows its own columns.

“What can you see?” Prima asks him.

“The beginning. And men with baskets. There's always the men with baskets, if you look where the emperor isn't.”

Antonia looks where the emperor isn't. She is good at it; it has been her civic religion's other service. In the carved crowd at the column's base the stone has them all: auxilia in their foreign trousers, engineers, women with bundles at the edges of camps, the whole freight of an empire moving on the backs the inscription will not name. She thinks of the names in Diomedes third book, wax renewed quarterly so some histories melt; of a fuller and his wife keeping a child after the paying stopped being the reason; of a woman in the Subura reading a Claudian clause aloud to a lodging-house door; of every *unpaid* person holding the mirror steady at seventeen while the carving was done elsewhere. Rome will read this column the way it reads everything: from the bottom, getting tired. She does not hold it against the stone. Stone is only as honest as the height you let people read it from.

For Lentulus, the dedication is an exercise in attending his own century at a new altitude. He stands where senior men stand, hears the words for the emperor, feels in his shoulder the older war stirring like a clerk called by a wrong name. Around him the Syria conversation has finally exhausted its diminishing returns; the man who got the province has done nothing in public more exciting than depart competently, which history may reward with the inattention it usually reserves for functioning government.

Acilius leans towards him as the procession regroups. “Your water is mentioned with approval,” he murmurs, “by a man who never approves liquids before noon.”

“Hadrian?”

“Athens.”

“The city has become a person in your correspondence.”

“It saves postage.”

Servilia, on his other side, is looking not at the frieze but at the crowd margin where veils, children and provision baskets are doing a fuller empire. Lentulus knows the angle. He does not follow it with his eyes. That, too, is a discipline and—whatever the house across the hills may enter in its third book—an act of care.

“You are not seeking your column,” Servilia says, so low that only marriage can hear.

“I know where the hands are.”

She gives him the faintest of edits. “Do not make it literature.”

“I shall make it aqueducts.”

Postumus, ahead of them, is already making it literature for a priest whose chin-strap is doing the listening. The tale includes a bridge, a barbarian king and a remark by Trajan that improves every time Postumus crosses the same water. Servilia watches with the expression of a woman who has heard the Republic fall at dinner so often she could provide seating.

At one point Lentulus catches Marcus across the forum—son, heir, a young man who has gone in eighteen months from a doorway nuisance to someone other people now ask for when a matter requires the appearance of not being handled. Their eyes meet. Marcus does not nod toward Prima. Lentulus does not look. Father and son perform the same informed nothing with a public synchrony no dancing master could have afforded.

A stone-carver is standing two veils to Antonia’s left with his whole family arranged around him as if by a frieze designer who understood sandwiches. He is pointing out his portion to a son too young to be corrupted by perspective. “There. The mules. I did the ears.”

“All of them?” the child asks.

“The ears. Lucius did the eyes because his father knew the foreman. Don’t let it go to the family head.”

The wife says, “Your grandfather did a cornucopia on the Basilica and still charged us for repairs. Art runs in the blood, along with invoicing.”

Antonia looks at the mules. The ears are excellent. It seems important, suddenly, that excellence can be local—an ear here, a stair counted there, a woman’s initials under a mark for payment purposes—and remain not less historical for declining to be visible from the top.

Near the base, women are doing what women do at imperial dedications: holding children, property, sunshades and the social knowledge of who in the neighbouring rows is likely to faint before the flutes reach the second sacrifice. A freedwoman in a green cloak is explaining the frieze to a younger woman with a basket by beginning not with Trajan but with the first bridge. “You start with how they got across,” she says. “The speeches are later and wash off.”

Antonia thinks, unbidden, of Saufia holding paper out of the rain; of the press-house woman writing initials learned from a son; of Daphne explaining money held *for* and money held *from* in her speaking voice; of Prima with copies folded for aprons. Rome is spiralling upward in low relief, but down here, at ankle height, another medium is doing the work: hands passing bread, documents, children, warnings, prices.

Eirene follows her glance with the accuracy of a woman who has been reading faces beside her since the Subura. “Don’t get civic, love. Not before the bread.”

“I am observing the freight.”

“Observe it fed.”

The purple-dealer, who has been pretending not to listen on principle, says: “If they’d carved the women who packed the armies, they’d have needed two more columns and a warehouse.”

“They carve sacks,” Prima says, from her station.

“Madam, you are ahead of the official guide.”

“It is a rule with monuments. Look where the man isn’t being worshipped.”

It gets a local laugh, brief and unattributable. On the official side the flutes proceed. At ground level, a fuller’s daughter by either bank gives instruction in how to read empire, and the stone does not fall.

Above them, bridged and battling, the narrative climbs into blur and godhood. Below, the carver’s wife is telling her son that his father’s mule-ears will outlast three dynasties unless a prefect decides stone is a tax in waiting. The child, reassured, goes back to his bread with the seriousness of a veteran.

Across the courtyard, in the lane that opens for them, the senators pass: Acilius, consular, benign; Postumus, telling a story to a priest's chin-strap; and Lentulus, in wool that still costs more than a horse, with Servilia beside him in grey that costs more than the horse's opinions. He does not look for Antonia. It is still a discipline. But as the lane closes he does something that only three people in the crowd can read — one of them baked this morning, one of them keeps three sets of books, and the third counts stitches with her mouth open — he touches, briefly, two fingers to the edge of his toga where the old scar's mirror-image would sit, under the left ear: not a sign, not a signal, only a man feeling for a fact on his own skin in a public place. Felix always leaves the scar. Some men like to think the decision is fresh every morning. He is not one of them anymore. The lane closes.

When the ceremony discharges them at last, the crowd does not disperse so much as change trades. Tourists become diners. Soldiers become grandfathers with improved geography. The stone-carver’s child is carried home asleep with bread still in one hand, one shoe retaining a suspicious quantity of forum dust that his mother says will be entered as an heirloom unless it gets on the good tunic.

Prima and Titus do not linger for the second stage of public admiration, which consists of men explaining to one another what they would have carved. The bakehouse has an afternoon order; history, in their household, is permitted but not allowed to spoil rising times. Prima pauses beside a stall selling copies of the dedication programme and buys none.

“You could keep one,” Titus says.

“I was there. I have the feet to prove it.”

“You keep other papers.”

“I keep papers people can act on.” She looks back up, once, to the men with baskets entering immortality at ground level. “I shall remember the sacks.”

At the margin, Eirene is folding the provisions cloth with the briskness of a retreat by an undefeated army. “You’ll be across the river by the guild hour?”

“If the bridge isn’t full of historians,” Titus says.

“The bridge is always full of historians. They just call the tolls different things.”

Prima turns—not towards Antonia directly; the crowd has thinned and directness now would be a public claim—but into the general accounting shade where conversation can be held. “We have the feast coming,” she says. “The guild wants the bakehouse to make more than bread this year. There may be an order for hangings beyond my household throne.”

“Throne?” Eirene says.

“Mother Livia has corrected my vocabulary. I have a share, a room and a say in the benches.”

“It qualifies,” Antonia says.

“The house might lend the big hanging for the bakery door.” Prima says it while watching a child lose a clay spiral to a drain. “If a loan can be entered respectably.”

There it is: not the written message yet, the idea in advance of it, offered in a crowd where an answer can be anything and nothing. Antonia gives it the courtesy of no immediate gratitude. “The office has forms for respectable weather.”

“Then I will write.”

Titus, who has rescued the clay spiral by the expedient of a baker’s reach, returns it to its owner and receives a stare of naked wonder. “Men with baskets,” he says. “Practical at all altitudes.”

Prima takes his elbow—not held by him this time, a small redrawing of force that only families and ladders notice—and they go with the river-bound crowd into a city returning, under its newest immortal spiral, to dinner.

By the afternoon the interior stair has already become Rome’s newest way of telling people what kind of person they are. Those admitted by influence describe the climb as an honour. Those not admitted describe it as narrow. Stone-carvers describe it, accurately, as 185 steps and a nice piece of work in the dark.

Lentulus is offered access through the water board, whose engineers are being shown every new structure as if empire were a continuing professional seminar. Marcus says he should go. “It is our column now, whether we commissioned it or were upstaged by it.”

“You wish to come out above the war.”

“I wish to see whether the men with baskets are clearer from there.”

“They won’t be.”

“Then it is a fact I should collect at first hand. The family archive is short on collected facts.”

They go at a curated hour, with an engineer, a priest who dislikes stairs and a clerk whose lamp is probably the most honest expression of the state present. Inside, the spiral vanishes. The story becomes treads. Breath. The small windows cut for light giving ground-level Rome back in slices: a roof, a street where a mule is being unreasonable, a flash of river, a hill with houses whose names will not be read from any monument but are known in duplicate by the people who pay for their drainage.

Halfway up the priest asks whether the emperor’s genius is not most visible in forcing the narrative to turn around the reader.

Marcus says, from two steps above and in the new moderate voice that has destabilised three bathhouses, “It is visible in making us climb for it.”

Lentulus looks at the back of his son’s head in lamplight and thinks of a girl across the river counting red flowers so they can be read at the plane where another person’s hands will touch them. Stone wants altitude. Cloth wants eyes near enough to check the join. He does not say this. Ministerial silence, for once, is carrying load.

At the top Rome unrolls in the official direction and, less loyally, in all the others: hills, smoke, the Tiber taking light like a tray in the wrong hands. The clerk points out landmarks. The engineer points out the arcade lines instead, unglamorous and necessary, coming in over the gardens.

“There,” he says. “Your small lettering, senator. From up here it looks like water.”

It is the finest compliment an aqueduct can receive. Lentulus stays no longer than decorum requires. The city is beautiful from a height, but it is not, he has learned at a late course fee, thereby more truthful.

Cornelia watches the latter part of the dedication from the Avidian enclosure, where the harbour interest has arranged itself with better cushions than the nobility and a much more realistic supply of water. Her husband is explaining the frieze to a younger cousin by translating every military feature into tonnage. “That is not a siege tower,” he says, “that is a warehouse that has made a decision.”

She has been married long enough now to recognise which of his jokes are educational policy. This one is both.

Her music room floor has been repaired but retains, as he promised, a limited right of complaint under the stairs. The master is paid in advance, quarterly, in a schedule so irreproachable that even Servilia has run out of improvements to suggest at breakfast. Cornelia sings there most mornings. She does not sing today. A dedication is not a music room, and a young matron of two houses has learned which rooms are hers because other people are standing in them applauding a column.

And yet, as the flutes take a phrase she knows, she feels the Greek song enter her breath without knocking. Her mother is across an official distance in grey. Their eyes do not meet; Servilia has never wasted alliance-scale emotion on line of sight. Still, Cornelia puts both feet flat on the stone, the way Daphne once instructed her in a sentence delivered through three intermediaries and one argument about lemons.

Her husband notices. “The floor is not complaining here.”

“It is municipal. It has other management.”

“You look as if you might sing at it.”

“I am saving myself for rooms where the accounts are opened.”

He considers this with the care of a man who knows her sentences now sometimes have sisters behind them. “Should I be alarmed?”

“Only if your invoices are late.”

The joke goes through their marriage at the proper small scale, unrecorded. Below them Marcus is doing his public nothing. Her father is doing his older nothing with more expense of spirit. Her mother is being an institution in a veil. The whole family, Cornelia thinks, is being carved in a medium less permanent than stone and much harder to read from any single side.

She does not know yet what will be told her, or at whose table, or under which heading of family information arriving in the wrong century. She knows a box with copies in a room she can reach. She knows a husband who pays musicians on time and a mother who says *partly* as if it were a province to be governed with respect. She has begun to keep, in the box, a page of questions for which no adult has provided the order.

At the top, in her own hand, it says: *Why does the house go quiet when the Caelian singer rests?* Underneath, later and smaller: *Why do I know the Greek tune in my breath before I have decided?*

Questions are cheap to store. That is why families prefer them to answers. Cornelia folds the dedication programme around nothing in particular and keeps it anyway. Paper improves with the bearer.

That evening the house on the Caelian is full in its ordinary extraordinary way. The column's shadow, two hills away, is at that hour complimenting everything. Daphne sings a new song — Greek verses, a Roman setting, about a woman at a loom — and in the last stanza the faithful wife does the thing the poets have never yet permitted her: she presents her husband, home from the war at last, with the accounts. It gets the laugh and then it gets the silence, which in Antonia's room is the more expensive response. Men of business look into their cups. Someone says it will never play in the theatres, and Philomela, who has taken to harmony the way praetors take to *distinguishing* things, says that is because the theatres only sing the first frieze of everything, darling, the part where the emperor is still legible.

The song has been rehearsed all spring in the way dangerous material is rehearsed at the Caelian: first as technique, then as expense, then—when Eirene pronounced it “too true to survive before the fish”—as an after-course licence. Daphne found the Greek verses in a copy sold to her by a musician who had performed for sailors and widows and consequently understood both ends of the vowel. The Roman setting is hers and partly Philomela’s, who now sings harmony with the intellectual arrogance of a woman who has discovered that a second line can change a first one’s government.

“The wife waits,” Daphne explained at rehearsal, while Ammonius pretended not to be emotionally available in the doorway. “War, travels, storms, sirens—all the expensive weather. Then he comes home expecting an embrace with catering. She gives him the books.”

“Do the books balance?” Diomedes asked.

“That is the seduction scene.”

“Then it will play.”

In performance the joke lands because Daphne does not lean on it. She sings the faithful wife’s patience straight for three verses, giving the room time to admire its own mythology. Only in the last does the loom give way to household accounts: wool bought, debts refused, a suitor turned away by the simple expedient of presenting him with the cost of the roof. The husband’s homecoming is not diminished. It is audited.

The silence after the laugh has texture. Men look at cups, as noted by history. Women do not. A banker’s wife asks, too clearly, who wrote the last verse. Daphne says “a household”, which in the Caelian can mean a committee of survivors, and Philomela adds the harmony’s final note from behind the screen where she has been fulfilling a singer’s ancient right to appear at the most interpretive moment.

Later, when the guests have moved from culture back to finance, Daphne is offered money for the song by a man who wants it performed at his villa “without the accounts part.”

“Then it is a different song,” she says. “You may buy that one from tradition. It is heavily discounted and requires no thought after midnight.”

Diomedes, who is collecting respects in small change by the door, observes to Barbatus: “Our clause has acquired music.”

“It had lawyers before,” Barbatus says. “Music is cheaper and people remember the tune at doors.”

Antonia does not commission an encore. Some things should be allowed to go home in guests’ heads without a house receipt. She only tells Daphne, in passing, to add the song’s last verse to the copy kept with the renewed contracts.

“Under Educational?”

“Under Household Administration. We are an institution, not a college.”

Daphne, twenty-two and free three years and possessed of modest real stones in the Greek’s strongbox, considers the phrase with professional delight. “Then my accounts will be sung in the archive.”

“They are already the tune,” Philomela says. “Some of us merely harmonised.”

Earlier that evening, before the message, there is the ordinary crisis of a soup course delayed by a senator’s guest who has brought his own philosopher and expects the evening to be seasoned accordingly. Ammonius refuses to let philosophy near his stock. “It has been clarified,” he says. “It needs no worldview.”

Diomedes manages the delay by giving the philosopher a small room in the conversation, Greek only, and charging him in the older sense: attention from everyone he had hoped merely to dazzle. By the second course the man is asking Daphne why the loom song makes the room go quiet at its funniest line.

“Because an account is a love letter people can act on,” she says.

The philosopher writes this down. Diomedes writes down the fact that he has written it down. Civilisation proceeds by citation.

At the door, a tax inspector who is not on duty has come as somebody’s brother-in-law and keeps catching himself admiring the door-book’s categories. “Tradeswomen related by commission,” he reads, under his breath, while Barbatus holds the lamp at a professional angle. “Elastic.”

“Durable,” says Barbatus. “It gets woven by the piece.”

Only later does Antonia learn that the random censorious guest from last winter—the one who predicted the house would be closed by a committee and then attempted to dine at a discount on the strength of his error—has returned with his wife, having heard of Daphne’s song from three separate outraged dinner parties. He applauds in the right places and leaves money under a napkin “for the singer’s education.” Diomedes returns half and keeps the correct fee. “Overpayment,” he explains, “is an incomplete apology.”

The young inspector who takes almond cakes as a matter of administrative routine is there too, on family business and off-duty enough to call the new stove by a pet name in front of witnesses. He asks Eirene whether institutions alter their paperwork as they age.

“They alter their memories first, dear,” she says. “Paper only becomes honest when it notices.”

It is into this fabric—music, stone dust still on the shoes of half the guests, philosophy demoted by soup, paperwork enjoying a social life—that the bakery message eventually finds its way like a bright thread through a sober ground.

Antonia moves through the rooms. The damp has not come through the courtyard wall this spring: a new drain, Cornelian-supervised, aqueduct-adjacent, invoice impeccable, sees to it. Diomedes books, in the second hand now more often than the first, total themselves with less arguing. The house will be registered, taxed, inspected by appointment, and let alone, which in Rome is the compound noun *peace*. At the hour when the guests begin to be steered, waterproofed, toward litters and back streets, Barbatus brings a folded message with no seal, and stands looking at it in his fist like a man who has finally been handed the last of a kind.

It is from Prima. — *The shop has the guild feast. We will want the big room's hanging for the bakery door that day, if the house can lend what I sewed it. The child will be born by harvest, Titus says, who knows about rising. If it's a girl she will be taught letters before thread. If it's a boy, thread first, then letters, so he knows what the letters cost.* At the foot, in the writing that Barbatus taught no one and Daphne taught her half of: *I count everything so it doesn't count me. We're even, nearly. Which in the family, I'm told, is a kind of fortune.*

Antonia stands in the doorway of the great dining room, where the hanging in question draws its red border round the evening. Eirene, passing with the lamps, says nothing, having spent thirty years learning the precise weight of that currency. Antonia folds the message once — precisely, as she has folded others, between the quarters of the tax receipts where all the important correspondence of her life has been filed since a young man taught her, among other thefts, the letter that begins her name. “Tell Barbatus,” she says, “the house will bring the hanging itself on the morning. By the front street. In a cart with ribbons, if the pastry cook's nephew can be trusted with colours.”

The message has come across the river in the late afternoon by a bakery boy who was paid in hot rolls at both ends and regards himself, not unreasonably, as an imperial courier in flour. Barbatus has kept it until the house reached the point in the evening when a mistress could receive family news without guests mistaking it for wine.

Antonia reads it the first time for logistics. Hanging. Bakery door. Guild feast. She is already instructing Diomedes silently about a cart when the sentence about the child crosses the room without asking admission.

She reads it the second time more slowly. *If it’s a girl she will be taught letters before thread. If it’s a boy, thread first, then letters, so he knows what the letters cost.* There is an entire education policy in it, also a joke at every man who ever held a mirror or a stylus over a woman’s life and called the accompanying invoice nature.

Eirene goes by with lamps and says nothing. Then, at the far end of the corridor, comes back without the lamps and says, “Well?”

“She is going to have a child by harvest.”

“Then harvest,” Eirene says, “will have to watch its manners.” She takes the folded paper from Antonia’s hand only to give it back in a better fold, a form of midwifery in any office. “Do not send money in a panic. Send the hanging. She asked for the hanging.”

“I know.”

“You have your knowing face. It worries the younger staff.”

Antonia looks into the great dining room where the red border is holding an evening of other people’s dynasties in a frame. The hanging has been worked by a woman who counted with her mouth open and stopped a room’s history in a doorway. It has belonged to a house that sells arrival as an experience. For one feast day it will go across a river and hang in a bakery door, where flour dust and guild weather will perform their own criticism.

“She wants it lent.”

“Then lend it like you mean it.” Eirene begins mentally marshalling carts. “Ribbons might frighten the flour, love.”

“The pastry cook’s nephew can be trusted with colours up to municipal level.”

“Can Barbatus?”

“He can be trusted to describe it later as a naval engagement.”

The answer Antonia will send is still forming. She does not write *grandmother*, a word whose paperwork Prima never promised to publish. She does not write *daughter*. The family has learned, painfully, to let nouns arrive at their own rates. What she writes that night, after the guests, in her own hand and with Diomedes refusing on principle to improve it, is an acceptance of the loan, terms for anchoring the hanging against bakery steam, and one line that costs her an administrative hour to keep this short: *Tell the child, either model, the house keeps copies.*

Diomedes reads it and makes no correction. “Entered under correspondence?”

“Under family,” Antonia says. “Between the quarters.”

“That heading is becoming untidy.”

“It is becoming a room.”

The lamps go up. The city does its night shift: bakers and fullers and clerks and column-carvers sleeping the sleep of the unnamed. Tomorrow there will be registrations, favourable winds for Avidian grain, a leaking arcade on the Praeneste road acquiring small unglamorous lettering, a wedding or a funeral or a girl with a mirror learning to read over somebody's shoulder the exact words that will one day be the beginning of her. Rome is the mistress of the known world, says the inscription programme. It is also, says the house, settling its accounts by lamplight, quarterly, in three hands, a fuller’s daughter at either end of it, holding.
