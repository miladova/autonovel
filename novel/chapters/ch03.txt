Clients in the Morning
A great house in Rome begins the day by being besieged politely. From first light the atrium fills: clients in clean togas they cannot afford to clean often, freedmen with petitions folded to the size of embarrassment, a poet with an epic in a bucket, two cousins from Arpinum who believe the Cornelii owe their grandfather a magistracy. Servilia moves among the preparations like a quartermaster who has read the campaign plan and found it sentimental. Lentulus receives, remembers names, distinguishes the man who needs a loan from the man who needs the mention of a loan in front of witnesses, and keeps, beneath the surface of the morning, a steady count of what each greeting will cost and in what currency it can be repaid.

It is not cynicism. Or rather, he would say, it is not only cynicism. Patronage is the city's true aqueduct: favour flows downhill, obligation climbs back up, and everyone is wet. His father taught him to receive clients standing, so that no man could say the Cornelii sat while Rome asked. His father also taught him — not in words; in the disposition of estates, in marriages proposed over maps — that a family survives by converting land into loyalty and loyalty back into land before either currency fails.

The man from the Campanian agent is third, and he is the morning's true weather. The harvest loans have been rolled over twice. The interest, compounded through a banker whose name Lentulus prefers not to write in his own hand, now stands against the olive groves above Capua and, behind a veil of legal Latin, against the house on the Palatine itself.

“How long?” Lentulus asks.

“The banker is patient, dominus.”

“Bankers are patient the way rivers are patient. I asked how long.”

“Until the autumn, certainly. Beyond the autumn he will want a payment that signifies intention.”

“A payment always signifies intention. That is its function.”

The agent bows over the blow and withdraws to be given breakfast by a household that feeds its creditors' messengers better than some families feed their sons, because hunger talks.

Servilia, when the atrium has thinned, says, “Cornelia has been asked to sing at the house of the Acilii. She will go, she will sing one song, she will not sing the Greek one. Her voice is an asset and I will not have it spent like loose change.” Cornelia, sixteen, stands in the doorway of her mother's room with a scroll crushed slightly in one hand, being discussed in the third person in the manner of daughters and provinces. She has learnt — Lentulus sees it and is, obscurely, both proud and sorry — not to let her face bid in an auction she is not permitted to enter.

“And the Avidii?” he asks.

“The Avidii have money from shipping and a son with all his teeth. Their name was made by a man who unloaded grain. In two generations it will be an old name, because their grandchildren will pay historians.” Servilia folds a cloth that does not need folding. “We need money that does not smell of the docks, Lentulus, and we need it without being seen to need it. That is the whole of my position. It has been the whole of my position since the Campanian accounts came home in November, and I notice it becomes no larger for being restated.”

Cornelia says, “May I go to my lesson, Mother?”

“You may. Do not let the music master touch your wrist to correct it. He corrects what does not require correction.”

When the girl has gone, Lentulus says, “You think I should take the Avidii.”

“I think you should know the price of refusing them. You have always known the price of everything except refusal. Refusal you treat as free, because your family has been able to afford it for three hundred years.” She looks toward the atrium, where a slave is sweeping the morning's footprints toward the street door in long, even strokes. “Three hundred years is not a sum of money, husband. It is only a story we tell the money.”

Antonia's morning clients are not called clients. They are called the wine merchant wanting a longer credit, the mother of a girl in the Subura who has heard that Antonia pays for midwives without asking whose child it is, the ageing actor who once played kings and now plays, with great dignity, a man asking for the loan of a dining room for his benefit recital. She receives them sitting, because a woman who stands to receive in her own house looks like staff. She receives them quickly, because slowness in others is a tax she declines to pay twice.

Daphne comes last, which is strategy, and not hers alone: Eirene has held her back until Antonia has eaten. Daphne is twenty-two, freed three years, with a voice that makes men believe they have finer feelings than their conduct supports. She is wearing, at an hour when no one is performing anything, a necklace of green stones that Antonia has not seen before.

“Sit down,” Antonia says. “Take it off first. I want to talk to you, not to it.”

Daphne takes it off with the languor she uses on stage when a heroine removes a crown. In her palm the stones look smaller. Most gifts do, Antonia reflects, when they come off the body and into the light of an office.

“He says they are emeralds.”

“He lies professionally or he lies as a hobby?”

“He is a senator's son. It may be both.”

“Marcus Lentulus,” Antonia says. It is not a question, because Diomedes keeps the door-book better than the Senate keeps its minutes.

Daphne, who is not a fool, stops arranging her face. “He hears me sing. He talks about his father the way men talk about weather in a country they mean to emigrate from. He has given me glass stones and a great deal of true information, and I think he believes the exchange was the other way round.”

“What information?”

“That the father means to press the committee. That there is talk of the Syrian appointment and that the father needs to be seen as a man who cleans stables. That the sister is to be sold — his word is married, but he is twenty and drunk on being the only honest man in his family — to shipping money. That the Cornelii owe—” she pauses “—everyone.”

Antonia is very still. In the courtyard someone laughs, a real laugh, the pastry cook's, and a dove answers from the roof tiles with its soft, stupid music. “You will give the necklace back,” she says.

“Antonia—”

“You will give it back with the message that you admired it too much to risk it among musicians. He will understand that he has been caught lying and forgiven beautifully, and he will be yours for another month and truthful for at least a week. Glass accepted is a price agreed, Daphne. I did not free you — I did not pay your former owner a sum I still resent remembering — so that a Lentulus could buy you again wholesale.”

“You say his name,” Daphne says quietly, “the way other people say a street they used to live in.”

“I say it the way I say the tax office. With respect for its power to inconvenience me.” Antonia stands, which ends it. But at the door she stops, because the girl — the young woman, the freedwoman with glass in her palm — is looking at the stones as if they might yet argue their case. “You want things that are real,” Antonia says, more gently. “So did I. The city will offer you a great many shining substitutes and call your refusal ingratitude. Refuse anyway. A real stone can be sold in a bad winter. Remember which of the two you are collecting.”

That night the house fills in its ordinary, extraordinary way: a grain contractor celebrating a contract he cannot yet announce, two equestrians negotiating a partnership over courses designed to slow them down, an elderly advocate who comes for the music and stays, chastely and entirely, for the pleasure of being listened to by women who are paid to find the law interesting and have become, over the years, genuinely formidable on the subject of inheritance. Antonia moves between rooms as she always moves, present everywhere the way a key signature is present in music. She tastes the sauce for the dormice and sends it back. She settles, with four words and a hand on an elbow, a quarrel between cousins over a wager on the races. She notes, without writing, that the grain contractor's partner drinks only water after the fish course, which means he is either ill, devout, or negotiating in earnest — and files it under all three.

At the second hour of the night Diomedes brings her, among the ordinary messages, a folded note with no seal, in a hand she knows before she opens it the way one knows a step on a stair.

It says: *Withdraw the young men and I withdraw the committee. We need not meet to arrange what we both understand.*

There is no signature. Lentulus never learned that an unsigned note is still a signature if the handwriting is the one that taught you, at seventeen, how to shape the letter that begins your name.

Antonia holds it a moment over the lamp — not to burn it. She never burns paper that could be evidence or leverage or, on a night when the accounts are done and the house is loud with other people's appetites, company. She folds it once, precisely, and puts it in the box where she keeps the tax receipts, between the quarters.

“Tell the messenger,” she says to Diomedes, “that the house thanks his master for his interest in its guest list, and that the guest list, like the tax receipts, is filed quarterly.”

Diomedes, who has translated her meaning into survival for eleven years, bows and goes to find the exact tone in which a non-answer can be made to sound like a door closing in a well-built house.
