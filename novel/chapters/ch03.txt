Clients in the Morning

A great house in Rome begins the day by being besieged politely. From first light the atrium fills: clients in clean togas they cannot afford to clean often, freedmen with petitions folded to the size of embarrassment, a poet with an epic in a bucket, two cousins from Arpinum who believe the Cornelii owe their grandfather a magistracy. Servilia moves among the preparations like a quartermaster who has read the campaign plan and found it sentimental. Lentulus receives, remembers names, distinguishes the man who needs a loan from the man who needs the mention of a loan in front of witnesses, and keeps, beneath the surface of the morning, a steady count of what each greeting will cost and in what currency it can be repaid.

It is not cynicism. Or rather, he would say, it is not only cynicism. Patronage is the city's true aqueduct: favour flows downhill, obligation climbs back up, and everyone is wet. His father taught him to receive clients standing, so that no man could say the Cornelii sat while Rome asked. His father also taught him — not in words; in the disposition of estates, in marriages proposed over maps — that a family survives by converting land into loyalty and loyalty back into land before either currency fails.

The first client is the poet, because poets are always first, having the least to do after. He is called Quintus Valerius Soranus, after three men who would have paid to stop him, and he carries his epic in a bucket because the book-boxes are full — twelve books on the Dacian wars, in which the emperor appears, by the poet's own proud census, two hundred and forty times, and the Cornelii, by a creative interpretation of the campaign rolls, four. He does not want money, which is the first thing poets say in atria. He wants the work *heard by a person whose hearing signifies*, which is money in a laurel wreath. Lentulus hears the proem standing, as he hears everything at this hour, and finds, to his own mild disgust, that it is not contemptible. The man can do a river crossing. Rivers are his metre's home address.

“The passage where the bridge goes over at night,” Lentulus says. “Cut the catalogue of timbers by half.”

Soranus clutches the bucket like a hostage. “The timbers are documentary.”

“So is a tax return. We do not recite them at dinner.” Lentulus takes, from the tray his steward holds at the correct angle of invisibility, a small purse that the household prepares each morning in three sizes — Servilia calls them *encouragement, dismissal,* and *danger* — and gives the poet the middle one. “Read the bridge, and only the bridge, at Acilius' next evening. I will see that you are invited. If Acilius' guests are still awake at the far bank, we will discuss the remaining eleven books.”

The poet bows so low the bucket tips. Somewhere in the arithmetic of patronage a bridge has just been funded, and a poet's winter, and a small lien placed on the memory of the Dacian wars, where the Cornelii now appear four times, by purchase. This is how history is made in Rome before it is carved. The carving comes later, when the embarrassing parts have been paid for and can be omitted respectably.

The second client is not one man but a delegation: three freedmen of a Cornelian grain depot at Ostia, come up to Rome with a lawsuit in one satchel and, in another, smoked fish for the household, which Servilia's steward accepts with the gravity due to tribute between nations. Their case is a warehouse boundary, a neighbour's wall, a measurement disputed to the width of a man's hand — matters on which, as Lentulus listens, some hundreds of people's dinners actually depend, though no epic will ever spend a river on them. He asks the one question their advocate has evidently forbidden them to answer — who owns the wall's other side — and watches them decide, in a three-way flicker of eyes, to trust the atrium instead.

“The Avidii,” says the eldest, in the voice of a man confessing weather. “The new depot. Since the autumn.”

Lentulus looks at the wax masks, who look back with three centuries of practice at receiving this kind of news before breakfast. His daughter's possible marriage has just acquired a party wall. Rome is a village with aqueducts; he has known it since he was twenty and it has never once been convenient.

“You will not sue,” he says. “You will survey — a surveyor of my choosing, paid from this house, so that his findings are a favour and not a missile — and the Avidii will be shown his rods at a dinner, not a tribunal. A hand's width, gentlemen, is a negotiating instrument. In court it becomes a principle, and principles eat warehouses.”

They bow over the solution, relieved in the way of men who came for a war and are being given a boundary stone and a hot breakfast. The steward moves them toward the kitchens. On their way out they pass the two cousins from Arpinum, standing in togas of a whiteness that argues long storage and short funds, rehearsing, without moving their lips, the case that the Cornelii owe their grandfather a magistracy. Lentulus can see the case's whole future from where he stands: ten minutes of genealogy, produced like a sword from a walking stick; his own reply, in the formula his father used — *the family's gratitude is a matter of record* — which is Latin for a door; and then Servilia, later, telling him the Arpinum cousins have a cousin of their own in the harbour dues office, and that gratitude ought therefore to be put on the record in a more spendable denomination. She will be right. She is the quartermaster of gratitude. He merely issues it at parades.

Between clients, in the breath the atrium takes, he is aware of the household performing the morning as his wife has scored it: the door slave admitting no one whose name the steward has not tasted first; the boy with the guest-towels; the clerk at the side table writing, in a book that is the family's true epic, who came, what they asked, what they were given, what it will cost when it comes back — and everything comes back; that is the aqueduct. Cornelia crosses the far end of the atrium on her way to her lesson with her scroll held like something she is rescuing rather than carrying, and does not look at the clients, who must not be given a daughter to look at in business hours; the rule is Servilia's, older than the girl, part of the house's fortifications. Lentulus watches his daughter not look at his morning and feels, obscurely, audited.

A veteran is waiting too, further back, in the place men put themselves when they have been clients before and know the tariff of patience. He has Moesia in his face and one hand that the years since have taught to do the work of the other one. Lentulus served — *served* is the household word; his own, privately, is *attended* — with the man's legion in a winter he does not recount at dinners. He will keep the veteran last, because what the man needs is not a purse-size but a recommendation to a land commission, which takes the time it takes, and because the Arpinum cousins must be seen to be received before the veteran is *seen to matter more than they do*, which everyone in the atrium already knows and the order of the morning must conspire not to say. Patronage, he thinks, for the thousandth time, in the exact tone of a man reading his own inscription as someone else carves it: it is not a system for distributing help. It is a system for distributing the appearance of help in an order nobody can afford to dispute. His father would have called that a provincial sentiment too, and then received his clients in the same order, standing, for fifty years.

The man from the Campanian agent is third, and he is the morning's true weather. The harvest loans have been rolled over twice. The interest, compounded through a banker whose name Lentulus prefers not to write in his own hand, now stands against the olive groves above Capua and, behind a veil of legal Latin, against the house on the Palatine itself.

“How long?” Lentulus asks.

“The banker is patient, dominus.”

“Bankers are patient the way rivers are patient. I asked how long.”

“Until the autumn, certainly. Beyond the autumn he will want a payment that signifies intention.”

“A payment always signifies intention. That is its function.”

The agent bows over the blow and withdraws to be given breakfast by a household that feeds its creditors' messengers better than some families feed their sons, because hunger talks.

Servilia, when the atrium has thinned, says, “Cornelia has been asked to sing at the house of the Acilii. She will go, she will sing one song, she will not sing the Greek one. Her voice is an asset and I will not have it spent like loose change.” Cornelia, sixteen, stands in the doorway of her mother's room with a scroll crushed slightly in one hand, being discussed in the third person in the manner of daughters and provinces. She has learnt — Lentulus sees it and is, obscurely, both proud and sorry — not to let her face bid in an auction she is not permitted to enter.

“And the Avidii?” he asks.

“The Avidii have money from shipping and a son with all his teeth. Their name was made by a man who unloaded grain. In two generations it will be an old name, because their grandchildren will pay historians.” Servilia folds a cloth that does not need folding. “We need money that does not smell of the docks, Lentulus, and we need it without being seen to need it. That is the whole of my position. It has been the whole of my position since the Campanian accounts came home in November, and I notice it becomes no larger for being restated.”

Cornelia says, “May I go to my lesson, Mother?”

“You may. Do not let the music master touch your wrist to correct it. He corrects what does not require correction.”

When the girl has gone, Lentulus says, “You think I should take the Avidii.”

“I think you should know the price of refusing them. You have always known the price of everything except refusal. Refusal you treat as free, because your family has been able to afford it for three hundred years.” She looks toward the atrium, where a slave is sweeping the morning's footprints toward the street door in long, even strokes. “Three hundred years is not a sum of money, husband. It is only a story we tell the money.”

When she has gone, the atrium settles its morning accounts without her: clients to the street, breakfast to the clients who missed it standing, the clerk's book to the steward, the steward to the kitchens with the day's small change of favour itemised into meals. Lentulus dictates letters in his study with the windows open to the winter garden, where nothing is flowering and everything is being cut back by a gardener who conducts pruning as a branch of political theory. To the Campanian agent: patience, arrangements, the vintage invoked like a god with a good memory. To a fellow senator whose vote he needs on the harbour dues and whose son he has just, at ruinous expense of dignity, praised in the Senate toilets — the true committee rooms of Rome, where men stand in a row and tell each other the truth at a fixed distance — a letter of such warmth that his secretary, who is paid to believe dictation, raises an eyebrow he is not paid for.

“Read it back,” Lentulus says.

The secretary reads. The warmth, read back, sounds like upholstery.

“Send it,” Lentulus says. “Upholstery is what coalitions sit on.”

From the far side of the garden comes Cornelia's lyre, and the music master's voice, and the particular silence where a correction by hand would go if this household permitted what half of Rome permits and calls tuition. The master is a Greek of forty with a reputation he keeps bandaged, and Servilia's instruction about the wrist has clearly reorganised his entire method into metaphor: *as if the string were a thread, domina; as if the hand were coming home from a journey*. Cornelia plays. She plays the figure correctly, and then — Lentulus hears it because a father who has sat through committees develops an ear for the inserted clause — she plays it again with something added, a turn of her own, small and unlicensed and unmistakably hers, and the master's voice, after a pause in which a career is weighed against an ear, says, “Again, domina. The second way.”

Lentulus stands at his window with a letter about harbour dues going cold in the secretary's hand and listens to his daughter insert herself, note by note, into music older than the family. He thinks: this is what we are selling to the Avidii, and the word *selling*, which Marcus used at a dinner table and was rebuked for, stands in his own mind unrebuked, in a locked room, at his usual rates. Not the girl — the form insists, and the form is a kind of truth. The name. The thing the name will do for a shipping family's grandchildren in rooms this girl will decorate with a voice her mother issues like a ration: one song, not the Greek. He thinks of the Greek at the Basilica, in whose quiet office this family's name is presently sitting in a file, being patient like a river. He thinks — the garden, the pruning, the note again, the second way — of the other file, in the cupboard under his grandfather's mask, where a different accounting of his name has been sitting for nine years being patient like something worse than a river. Like a debt in a currency bankers fear to touch.

“Dominus?” the secretary says.

“Nothing. A correction.” Lentulus turns back to the letters. “In the letter to the agent. Where I say the family will make a payment signifying intention — strike *signifying intention*. Put: *the family will pay*. If we are to be hauled up our own aqueduct by our creditors, we may as well arrive with clean Latin.”

The secretary, who has written *signifying intention* for this family for eleven years, strikes it with a stylus stroke so deep it goes through the wax to the wood. Outside, the gardener cuts something back to a knuckle and stands looking at it with the satisfaction of a man who believes, professionally, in return growth. The lyre plays the figure a third time. The second way. It is better. Everyone in earshot of the garden, including the truth, knows it is better.

Morning is when the house performs its most delicate sorting. Night has its reputation, but morning has the information. Diomedes lays the day’s requests out in order of solvency, discretion and the probability that a man’s idea of secrecy involves arriving with three friends and a recognisable cloak. Eirene reads over his shoulder while eating bread, a combination that has prevented more disasters than the city watch.

“This one asks for Daphne exclusively,” she says. “Exclusively. As if she were a hunting dog with ambitions.”

“He means music at his sister’s dinner,” Diomedes says. “The wording is unfortunate.”

“Wording is the whole confession in this town. Send Philomela as well and charge him for harmony. Men behave better when outnumbered by lyrics.”

Antonia, at the other side of the table, is going through a list of clients whose credit requires translation. A grain factor can be trusted to pay after the fleet. A senator’s nephew can be trusted to intend to pay, which is not a currency she accepts. A widow from the Aventine wants a room for six women, music, no men except the one paid to play flute and leave before dessert. Diomedes has marked this request with a star.

“Is she respectable?” Eirene asks.

“She is punctual,” Antonia says. “It is the form respectability should take before lunch.”

“She will want names hypothetical.”

“Then we will give her first names and good water. Rome can survive a number of administrations under those conditions.”

A client arrives before the doors are properly awake. He is not drunk—morning clients have the dignity of business about even their vices—and asks to speak to the mistress about a debt from a deceased friend. It transpires that he wants the debt to die with the friend and the evening’s entertainment to be resurrected at his own table. Antonia hears him out with the expression she reserves for tax receipts and unsatisfactory weather.

“Your friend is dead,” she says. “His account is not. It has relatives.”

“I thought, madam, in the spirit of—”

“My house does not provide spirits.”

Barbatus, in the doorway, becomes the size of a conclusion. The client pays half now and arranges the rest before the next moon, which Eirene calls “a funeral with instalments”. Daphne, passing to rehearsal with her hair still negotiating its terms, hums a phrase so pure it makes the morning office briefly feel underdressed.

“You see?” Antonia tells Diomedes. “Exclusive.”

“Madam, I shall phrase the reply with diplomacy.”

“Phrase it with fees. Diplomacy can harmonise.”

Antonia's morning clients are not called clients. They are called the wine merchant wanting a longer credit, the mother of a girl in the Subura who has heard that Antonia pays for midwives without asking whose child it is, the ageing actor who once played kings and now plays, with great dignity, a man asking for the loan of a dining room for his benefit recital. She receives them sitting, because a woman who stands to receive in her own house looks like staff. She receives them quickly, because slowness in others is a tax she declines to pay twice.

Daphne comes last, which is strategy, and not hers alone: Eirene has held her back until Antonia has eaten. Daphne is twenty-two, freed three years, with a voice that makes men believe they have finer feelings than their conduct supports. She is wearing, at an hour when no one is performing anything, a necklace of green stones that Antonia has not seen before.

“Sit down,” Antonia says. “Take it off first. I want to talk to you, not to it.”

Daphne takes it off with the languor she uses on stage when a heroine removes a crown. In her palm the stones look smaller. Most gifts do, Antonia reflects, when they come off the body and into the light of an office.

“He says they are emeralds.”

“He lies professionally or he lies as a hobby?”

“He is a senator's son. It may be both.”

“Marcus Lentulus,” Antonia says. It is not a question, because Diomedes keeps the door-book better than the Senate keeps its minutes.

Daphne, who is not a fool, stops arranging her face. “He hears me sing. He talks about his father the way men talk about weather in a country they mean to emigrate from. He has given me glass stones and a great deal of true information, and I think he believes the exchange was the other way round.”

“What information?”

“That the father means to press the committee. That there is talk of the Syrian appointment and that the father needs to be seen as a man who cleans stables. That the sister is to be sold — his word is married, but he is twenty and drunk on being the only honest man in his family — to shipping money. That the Cornelii owe—” she pauses “—everyone.”

Antonia is very still. In the courtyard someone laughs, a real laugh, the pastry cook's, and a dove answers from the roof tiles with its soft, stupid music. “You will give the necklace back,” she says.

“Antonia—”

“You will give it back with the message that you admired it too much to risk it among musicians. He will understand that he has been caught lying and forgiven beautifully, and he will be yours for another month and truthful for at least a week. Glass accepted is a price agreed, Daphne. I did not free you — I did not pay your former owner a sum I still resent remembering — so that a Lentulus could buy you again wholesale.”

“You say his name,” Daphne says quietly, “the way other people say a street they used to live in.”

“I say it the way I say the tax office. With respect for its power to inconvenience me.” Antonia stands, which ends it. But at the door she stops, because the girl — the young woman, the freedwoman with glass in her palm — is looking at the stones as if they might yet argue their case. “You want things that are real,” Antonia says, more gently. “So did I. The city will offer you a great many shining substitutes and call your refusal ingratitude. Refuse anyway. A real stone can be sold in a bad winter. Remember which of the two you are collecting.”

Daphne stays in the small room when Antonia has gone, with the glass stones cooling in her palm like a story cooling into evidence. She has been free for three years, and freedom, she is learning in instalments, is mostly the right to decide which instructions to be angry about. Antonia's instruction is good. That is what makes it galling. A bad order can be disobeyed with a clear conscience and a song; a good one must be followed in full view of one's own judgment, nodding.

Eirene comes in with the day's flowers for the small room — winter roses, forced and faintly resentful — and looks at the necklace the way she looks at everything expensive and unearned in this house: as a management problem with a clasp.

“He'll bring a real one next time,” Daphne says.

“He'll bring a worse lie and a better dinner story. They learn in that order, those boys; it is a family curriculum.” Eirene sets the flowers down and begins, out of thirty years of habit, to sort them by the life left in them. “I was given a necklace once by a man who owned the house I worked in, the street it stood in and, as the law then filed it, me. Real stones. Wonderful stones. I wore them at his table for two years and when he died his son's steward took them off my neck with a receipt. *Household property*, he said. He wasn't even unkind. Kindness wasn't in the inventory either.”

Daphne closes her hand over the glass. “Antonia never tells that story.”

“Antonia tells nobody's story, including Antonia's. It is why this house can charge what it charges.” Eirene holds a rose up to the light, finds it acceptable, forgives it. “Give the glass back beautifully, as instructed. Keep the information, as instructed. And when you are my age, Daphne, buy your own stones with money that has your name in a Greek's book, and let some senator's son explain to the room what they are. There is no music like it. I have heard Philomela. I know whereof I speak.”

That night the house fills in its ordinary, extraordinary way: a grain contractor celebrating a contract he cannot yet announce, two equestrians negotiating a partnership over courses designed to slow them down, an elderly advocate who comes for the music and stays, chastely and entirely, for the pleasure of being listened to by women who are paid to find the law interesting and have become, over the years, genuinely formidable on the subject of inheritance. Antonia moves between rooms as she always moves, present everywhere the way a key signature is present in music. She tastes the sauce for the dormice and sends it back. She settles, with four words and a hand on an elbow, a quarrel between cousins over a wager on the races. She notes, without writing, that the grain contractor's partner drinks only water after the fish course, which means he is either ill, devout, or negotiating in earnest — and files it under all three.

Consider the fish. Ammonius does, at the length other men give to mistresses. It has come up from Ostia in wet straw on a mule that is now, by house policy, being fed in the yard at a standard the muleteer describes to the kitchen boy as *senatorial*. The grain contractor, whose unannounced triumph the evening exists to celebrate without announcing, is a man from Puteoli with a face like a harbour wall and manners he has had built for him by a succession of wives, each better born than the last. He knows fish the way bankers know weather. He tastes Ammonius' sauce — the sauce has been sent back once tonight already, by Antonia, with a message consisting of the word *again* — and closes his eyes.

“My ships,” he says, “should carry this instead of grain.”

“Your ships,” says his partner, who drinks water after the fish course and is therefore, as Antonia has already filed, negotiating in earnest, “would arrive empty. The crews have votes too.”

It gets the laugh — from the table, from the corner where Philomela is resting between songs with a cup she is not drinking, for the voice — and into the laugh's warmth the partner slides the actual evening: tonnage, winter sailings, a clause about spoilage that has clearly been the subject of a war in somebody's office. Antonia, passing with the unhurried visibility of a hostess who is also the management, hears the clause resolve itself between the fish and the boar, in the presence of witnesses too well fed to be bought cheaply later. This is the house's true menu. Men book her rooms the way they book notaries: so that agreements acquire flavour and cannot afterwards be said to have happened in an atmosphere of duress. She charges for the food. The peace of mind is itemised as music.

The elderly advocate — he is called Mamercus, he is seventy-one, he has outlived two emperors, three wives and the fashion for his best courtroom manner, which was irony before irony was taxed — holds his usual court in the small room, and his court, as usual, is women. Tonight it is Daphne, between songs, and a widow of equestrian family who comes to the house, chaperoned by a freedwoman of ferocious respectability, to hear music her own household calls unsuitable for a mourning that has entered its fourth year and shows signs of being redecorated. Mamercus is explaining, with the patience of a man who has explained it in basilicas for money, why a will made in a fit of gratitude is the most dangerous document in Roman law.

“Gratitude,” he says, “is an intoxicant. Worse. Wine wears off by morning and a man may plead it. Gratitude *improves* overnight, like your mistress's stock, and by the time the witnesses are called he believes he owes his life to a person who has merely returned his seal-ring from a dining-room floor.” He looks sideways at Antonia, who is passing and has never, in eleven years, returned anything without first ensuring its owner fully experiences having lost it. “Present company professionally excepted.”

“You lost a stylus here in November, Mamercus,” Antonia says. “Shall I make a will about it?”

“You returned the stylus with a lecture on the price of ivory. I have left it to my grandson as a moral instrument.”

The widow laughs — startles herself with it, checks the freedwoman, finds no tribunal there — and laughs again, properly. Antonia marks it. There is a tariff of grief in Rome, precisely observed by the women it is levied on and quoted back at them by everyone else, and part of what her house sells, unitemised anywhere, is a room where a respectable widow may discover at seventy — no, at fifty, the widow is fifty and has been filed by her family under furniture — that her laugh still works at its original specification. She will book again. She will bring, in time, a niece. Empires of Antonia's kind are not built on the appetites Rome confesses to in the baths. They are built on the ones it doesn't: to be listened to, to be well fed without owing a marriage for it, to sit in a room where the women are professionally not shocked and the men are contractually not owners, and hear a very old advocate explain inheritance law like a love song.

In the great room the two equestrians have arrived, by way of Ammonius' boar, at the brink of their partnership, and are toasting it in a Falernian whose route to this table — a carter, a lesson, a cellar book — they will never know. One of them waylays Antonia by the serving door with the confidence of a man who believes charm should be volume-discounted.

“We want the big room in April,” he says. “For the signing feast. Forty covers. Can the house do forty?”

“The house has done sixty for men who sign bigger,” Antonia says. “April is three months away. Rome may fall, your partner may sober up, the price of boar—”

“Then it's booked?”

“It is *pencilled*,” she says, “which in this house means Diomedes has it in wax, and wax, young man, has carried the whole of Roman law for a thousand years. Come in February with a deposit and a list of who must not sit beside whom. That list is the feast. The food is what we serve while the list works.”

He laughs, delighted to be handled; men of his age and order pay extra for the sensation, which Antonia's day-book records without comment under *room hire, premium*. She moves on. The musicians change; Daphne rises to sing, and the room does the thing rooms do for her now, settling with a small, collective adjustment of cushions and standards. She sings a Greek air first, for form, and then a Roman street tune she has slowed down until it sounds like a hymn that has seen things, and the grain contractor — harbour wall, three wives, fleet impounded in a sauce — is seen by Philomela, and reported to the kitchen, to wipe his eyes with the back of his hand and immediately appoint the gesture to the pepper.

This is the second hour's approach. Antonia clocks it by the lamps: the boy has trimmed them once, will trim them again before the steward's hour; the second trimming is her signal to check the door-book and the street, because after it the evening belongs to whoever has stayed, and staying late in her house is a category of person she likes to choose personally. She is at the door-book, with Barbatus' thumb blacking the margin where he has checked a seal, when Diomedes comes up the corridor with the evening's messages on a tray he uses for the purpose — a nice distinction, his, between paper and food, which he maintains the whole of Roman administration fails to. Bills, a booking from a Spaniard with silver interests, a note from a lady whose handwriting Diomedes has placed, correctly, before unfolding it. And underneath, where a man who keeps three books in three hands puts the item a day will be audited by: a folded sheet with no seal at all.

At the second hour of the night Diomedes brings her, among the ordinary messages, a folded note with no seal, in a hand she knows before she opens it the way one knows a step on a stair.

It says: *Withdraw the young men and I withdraw the committee. We need not meet to arrange what we both understand.*

There is no signature. Lentulus never learned that an unsigned note is still a signature if the handwriting is the one that taught you, at seventeen, how to shape the letter that begins your name.

Antonia holds it a moment over the lamp — not to burn it. She never burns paper that could be evidence or leverage or, on a night when the accounts are done and the house is loud with other people's appetites, company. She folds it once, precisely, and puts it in the box where she keeps the tax receipts, between the quarters.

“Tell the messenger,” she says to Diomedes, “that the house thanks his master for his interest in its guest list, and that the guest list, like the tax receipts, is filed quarterly.”

Diomedes, who has translated her meaning into survival for eleven years, bows and goes to find the exact tone in which a non-answer can be made to sound like a door closing in a well-built house.
