The Dedication

She is early, because to be late in this city is to be read as careless, and careless women are charged more for everything. The morning is still blue at the edges. Below the Caelian the streets are already loud with handcarts and muleteers swearing at the gradient, with slaves carrying water up from the fountains in jars that knock against their hips. Antonia Cassia stands in her own courtyard and puts her hand, quite flat, to the painted plaster to feel whether the damp has come through again in the night. It has. A bloom of cold under the red. She makes a note of it in the part of her mind where repairs live, between the price of Falernian and the name of the fuller who does not steal.

Diomedes is waiting with the tablets. He does not look at her face first. He looks at her hands, as clerks do, to see if she is ready to hold a stylus or only to listen. “The dedication crowds will close the Forum by the third hour,” he says. “If the wine is to come up from the warehouses today it must come now.”

“Then now,” she says. “And pay the carter before he unloads, not after. I am tired of his face at the kitchen door.”

He marks it. The wax takes the point with a small, neat resistance that she has always liked. Everything in this house should resist a little before it yields. The doors. The accounts. The women, when a guest mistakes payment for ownership. Herself, most of all, when she is asked a question whose answer would be useful to the asker.

The house wakes in sections, like a theatre being lit from the back. First the kitchen yard, where the night porter hands his keys to the morning boy with a recital of grievances against the weather, the watch, and a cat that has adopted the woodpile and is raising, by the sound of it, a dynasty. Then the kitchen itself: fires coaxed, yesterday's ash raked out and saved in a bucket for the fuller, because nothing in this house is thrown away until two people have agreed it cannot be sold, fed to something, or used to clean something else. Then water. A house on the Caelian drinks uphill. Every jar in it has come up the street on a human back, and Antonia, who carried water professionally in her youth, in a town built of stairs, has opinions about waste that she expresses without raising her voice and without repetition. The household can recite them. Guests, who are charged for waste in ways they never identify on the bill, are spared the recital.

Barbatus does the doors at first light, not because the street is dangerous at dawn — the street at dawn is full of bakers' boys and old women, Rome's true garrison — but because a door opened carelessly sets the tone of a day. He stands in the entrance in his vast, weathered calm and looks up and down the street the way he once looked across sand: not for enemies, exactly; for intentions. Tradesmen read him correctly and moderate theirs. A fishmonger's boy who arrived in November intending to short-weight the house on mullet now brings the mullet up to the threshold holding the scales like a votive offering. Barbatus has never threatened him. Barbatus, who spent eleven years being professionally threatened for the entertainment of thousands, has retired from the genre.

“The fish is better than the boy,” he reports to the kitchen, which is his way of passing an invoice.

Antonia does her first round while the house is still honest. Later, with the lamps lit and the hangings down and musicians tuning in the small room, the place will put on the face it is paid for; but at this hour it shows her what it is: a machine for converting money into ease, with all the belts and buckets visible. She checks the great dining room where last night's Alexandrians argued about harbour dues until the lamps drowned in their own oil. The floor has been washed; a couch has a wine stain blooming on its cover like a small purple province; somebody — a guest, a servant, history will not be told — has left a ring on the window ledge, gold, with a stone the colour of weak tea. She picks it up and holds it to the light. In her first years in Rome a ring like that, lost in her rooms, would have gone to the Greek by noon and come back as rent. Now she puts it in the box in Diomedes' office labelled, in his upright hand, *Guests' Property, Forgotten*, where it will wait to be claimed by a man who will arrive at dusk in a panic of discretion, having told his wife one story and his steward another. He will overpay the house for its silence in some future transaction, never knowing he has done it, and the ring will have earned more in the box than it ever would at the Greek's. This is what Antonia means when she tells the younger women that honesty is a long position. They think she is joking. Eirene, who has seen the box's contents turn over the years like a small, glittering tide — rings, a senator's seal-stone, once, memorably, a set of false teeth in a cedar case — knows she is not.

In the kitchen the pastry cook is already at war. He is from Alexandria, he is called Ammonius, and he regards Roman cooking the way Roman generals regard other people's countries: as raw material awaiting improvement. This morning's enemy is the price of pine nuts, which has risen, and the quality of the honey, which in his formulation has *fallen morally*. Antonia listens to the whole dispatch while tasting yesterday's stock. It is a ritual. He requires an audience with the power to fund him; she requires him to believe the funding is in doubt, because a cook who feels secure begins to cook for other cooks, and other cooks do not pay her bills.

“You may have the pine nuts,” she says at last, “at the old price, from the man in the Forum Boarium. Tell him the house remembers the short measure at Saturnalia. He will remember being remembered.”

“And the honey?”

“The honey is from our own man outside the Colline Gate. If it has fallen morally, Ammonius, it has done so in your storeroom, among your jars, under your seal.” She rinses the spoon herself — a hairdresser's daughter does not leave spoons; it is the same religion as the hair — and puts it back in its place. “Reform it.”

Ammonius bows with the grandeur of a man accepting a province and turns back to his fires, already composing the menu for a night still twelve hours away, while the kitchen boy — eleven years old, freeborn, apprenticed by a mother who came to Antonia first for a midwife's fee and stayed, over the years, for arithmetic lessons she pretends are recipes — mouths the words *fallen morally* over the onions with the deep satisfaction of a person stocking a phrase for later use. Antonia sees him do it. She lets him keep it. A house survives on what its people steal from it in language.

Upstairs, the women are waking in the order of their engagements, which is to say that Philomela, who sang until the second watch, is not waking at all, and has hung a strip of red wool on her door, the house's flag for *a voice is sleeping; knock and be billed*. Daphne is awake and practising scales at a volume calculated to annoy nobody who matters. There are rules here too, arrived at over eleven years and written nowhere, which is why they work: no woman in this house descends to the dining rooms before she has eaten; no woman entertains a guest who has struck a servant, once, anywhere, on any provocation — Rome calls this an eccentricity and pays a premium for it; no woman's earnings pass through any hand but Diomedes', and his pass through Antonia's every quarter, in her presence, with the tablets open. Twice a year a new arrival, hired from some lodging house by the theatres where the rules were different, tests the ones about money. Twice a year Antonia explains, sitting down, in the small room, that she did not climb out of Baiae on stairs of other women's wages and does not intend to be carried up fresh ones. The explanation takes four minutes. It has not yet needed repeating to the same woman.

She is on the stairs herself when the carter arrives early, contrary to instruction, and with only half the wine. She hears Barbatus admit him to the yard and hears, in the quality of the cart's complaining axle, a story preparing itself: the warehouses, the dedication crowds, a mule with opinions. By the time she reaches the yard the man has got as far as the mule.

“The other half,” Antonia says, “is where?”

“Coming, mistress. On the second cart. By the third hour, the fourth at the outside. The Forum—”

“The Forum was built over twelve years. It did not surprise the warehouses this morning.” She looks at the amphorae he has brought: sealed, stamped, the Falernian she ordered, a cheaper Campanian she did not, riding among them like a poor relation in a good cloak. She rests her hand, quite gently, on the shoulder of one of the impostors. “And these?”

The carter looks at Barbatus. Barbatus looks at the street. It is a conversation men have in this yard about twice a year, usually at the point where someone's arithmetic meets hers.

“A mistake at loading,” the carter says. “It being dark.”

“Then it can be unloaded in the light. Take the Campanian back, bring the rest of the Falernian by the third hour, and I will pay the full contract, including today's cartage, as agreed. Bring it by the fourth, and I will pay for the wine and not the cartage, because the afternoon of an imperial dedication is not when I conduct my cellar down a public street. Bring me another mistake in the dark at any hour you choose—” she takes her hand off the amphora “—and you may explain the darkness to the Greek at the Basilica, who holds your note for the mule. He holds most people's notes. It is why his office is so quiet.”

The carter begins unloading with the energy of a man who has just remembered an appointment with honesty. Diomedes, arriving with a stylus, records the amended delivery without expression. He has a category for this in the day-book. Every house in Rome has, whether it writes it down or not: *attempted, at dawn, in the dark*. Antonia watches a moment longer, to give the lesson its full weight of witness, and it is while she is standing in the yard among the amphorae, with the January light coming up over the roofs and the whole city beginning to make its dedication-day noise below the hill, that she hears the courtyard door, and Eirene's step on the stones.

Eirene comes across the courtyard with a bowl of warm water and a cloth over her arm, her hair still pinned for sleep. She is Antonia's age and has the particular beauty of women who have stopped arranging their faces for other people before noon. “You have ink on your jaw,” Eirene says.

“Then I was working in my sleep.”

“You were working instead of it.” Eirene wipes the mark away. Her fingers are cool. “The new hanging is here. The embroiderer herself brought it. She would not give it to the porter.”

“Good for her. Give her breakfast and keep her from Barbatus. He thinks courtesy is a kind of wrestling.”

Diomedes turns a tablet over. “There is also a notice. From the aediles' office. It was left before dawn, which is either diligence or a wish not to be seen leaving it.”

Antonia takes it. The seal is correct. The hand is a clerk's hand, trained to make threats look like administration. An inspection. A review of contributions, of registrations, of the uses to which rooms in this insula are put after dark. Words that have been washed so often they no longer smell of anything: order, decency, precedent. At the foot, a name cited as proposing the review to the Senate's committee. She reads it twice, not because she doubts her eyes but because her eyes, at thirty-eight, prefer close work in better light, and because she wants the small delay in which a feeling can be put down somewhere it will not be seen.

Gnaeus Cornelius Lentulus.

Eirene is watching her. Diomedes is very interested in the courtyard roses, which are overwatered and know it.

“Well,” Antonia says. “The emperor dedicates a forum today, and Lentulus dedicates himself to my drains. We all build what we can.”

She hands the notice back. “File it with the tax receipts. Answer nothing today. If a man sends a clerk before dawn he wants a reply before noon, and I have never yet let Lentulus set the hour in my house.”

He is being shaved when his son comes in, which means he cannot turn his head, which means his son must say whatever he has come to say to a profile held still by another man's hand. Gnaeus Cornelius Lentulus has found, over twenty years of public life, that a razor at the throat improves the quality of other people's sentences. They become shorter. They become, if not honest, at least economical.

“The Forum today,” the boy says. He is twenty and calls this an opening.

“Yes.”

“Shall I ride with you or with Uncle Postumus?”

“With me. Postumus will talk through the sacrifices and then claim he foresaw the omens.”

The barber's thumb pulls the skin taut under his jaw. In the mirror — polished bronze, his grandfather's, the rim dented where a slave dropped it the winter the Tiber froze at the edges — Lentulus can see his son shift from foot to foot in a way that his tutors were paid to correct and did not. The boy has his mother's height and, Lentulus thinks, with the clarity that comes when one may not move, none of his mother's patience and all of his own appetite for being elsewhere.

“There is talk,” the boy says, “about your proposal. The inspections.”

“There is always talk. It is the city's second water supply.”

“They say it is aimed. At one house in particular.”

The razor pauses. The barber, who has shaved three consuls and buried two, knows when a household has stepped on its own hem.

“Who are they, Marcus?” Lentulus asks.

“Men at the baths. Men who owe me money at dice and pay in information because it is cheaper.”

“A proposal on registrations is aimed at everyone who has failed to register. If one house feels it more than others, that may be because one house has grown very large on very small paperwork.” He can hear how well this will sound in the Senate, and how thin it sounds here, with soap on his neck. “You will not repeat bath-house talk at the dedication. You will stand where you are put, you will be seen to attend to the emperor, and you will remember that your sister's negotiations require this family to look like a wall, not a window.”

Marcus bows, correctly, which in this family is what young men do instead of slamming doors the slaves would have to repair.

When he has gone, the barber resumes. “Shall I leave the scar, dominus?”

The scar is small, under the left ear, older than the boy who just left the room. Lentulus says, “You always leave the scar, Felix. It is not a decision I need to make fresh each morning.”

“No, dominus.” Felix wipes the blade. “Some men like to think it is.”

Out in the atrium the ancestral masks look down from their cupboards, wax faces darkened by smoke and time, each with its painted inscription: offices held, triumphs voted, provinces governed. Lentulus passes them as he passes them every morning, with the brief, involuntary arithmetic of the living: what he has added, what he has merely maintained, what the debts secured against the Campanian estates would look like if debts were inscribed under a man's name as offices are. He touches nothing. A Cornelius does not touch the masks for luck. He behaves as if luck were a vulgarity other families require.

His wife is at the household shrine, making the morning offering with the household gathered in the order she has arranged and rearranged over nineteen years until it looks like nature. Servilia does not look up when he joins her. She pours, she speaks the words, she sets the cup down exactly within the ring its predecessors have left on the stone. Only when the slaves have withdrawn does she say, “You sent the notice before dawn.”

“The aediles sent it. I proposed a review. These are distinct actions.”

“In the Senate they will be. At dinner they will not.” She straightens the fold of his toga where it crosses his chest. Her hands are expert and entirely without tenderness, which is not the same as without care. “If you mean to make war on that woman, Lentulus, do it in daylight and win it quickly. This family has a daughter to marry. We cannot afford a siege that everyone in Rome watches while eating.”

“I do not make war on women,” he says.

“No,” Servilia says. “You make proposals. Rome will supply the war.”

Servilia leaves him to the dressing, which in this house is a liturgy with a staff. The toga is not put on a senator; it is built around him, fold by negotiated fold, by a slave whose grandfather dressed a Cornelius for a triumph and whose professional opinion, delivered through his hands, is that the modern chest is narrower and the modern occasion fatter. Lentulus stands for it the way he stands for clients: as an act of office performed by other people's competence. In the bronze mirror his reflection is assembled by degrees out of the room's grey light — first a shape, then a senator, then, if you know where to look, which Felix does and the dresser does and Servilia has for nineteen years, the man inside the arrangement, holding still.

“Your father,” the dresser says — he is old enough to be allowed uncommissioned sentences, a liberty the household grants by age the way Antonia's grants by invoice — “wore his to the left an inch further. For the sword arm, he said. There was no sword, in the Forum, in my memory.”

“There was always a sword in my father's memory,” Lentulus says. “It is what the memory was *for*.”

He does not say — the room is not one of his locked ones, it is full of hands — that he has started, this winter, to hear his father's sentences arriving in his own voice at committee pitch: *order, decency, precedent*, the washed words, the ones that have been through the fullers so often they have lost their original colour and can be worn to anything. At twenty-four he believed a man chose his vocabulary the way he chose his friends. At forty-five he knows a man's vocabulary is issued to him, like the toga's folds, by people who dressed his grandfather, and that the freedom lies, if it lies anywhere, in the inch to the left or right that nobody else notices.

Cornelia is in the corridor when he comes out, which means she has timed it, which means Servilia knows she has timed it and has permitted the corridor as a theatre. She is dressed for the dedication in the white of a senator's unmarried daughter — a colour that is also a locked room, and better guarded than most in this house — and she has her mother's way of asking a question by arranging to be present when it answers itself.

“Will the emperor speak?” she asks.

“He will dedicate. Speaking is what the inscriptions do afterwards, at greater length and with fewer interruptions.”

“Marcus says the column will have the whole war on it, going round and round.”

“Marcus says a great many things going round and round.” But he says it without the edge he keeps for the boy in public, and his daughter receives the distinction the way she receives everything in this family: filed, accurately, for a future she is not yet charging interest on. “The war will go round and round, yes. Wars do, on stone. It saves the survivors the trouble of describing the parts no one can see from the ground.”

She thinks about this with her whole forehead. At sixteen her thinking is still visible, a condition her education is designed to cure by the vintage. “Who is it for, then? The part nobody can see.”

“The gods,” her father says, because it is the answer the dedication requires, and then — the inch to the left, unnoticed by anyone except the girl it was always going to matter to — “and the people who carried the baskets. Someday somebody will climb up inside it and read it all. Historians,” he says, “are just clients who arrive very late and cannot be turned away.”

Cornelia nods as if a purse of the middle size has been placed, correctly, in her hands. Servilia, appearing at the corridor's end with the household deploying behind her like a fleet in line, takes in father, daughter, and the exact quality of the moment she has scheduled, and says only, “The litters,” which in her Latin covers a multitude of approvals she would regard it as wasteful to itemise.

By the third hour the new Forum is a press of white wool and oiled hair and January sun striking gilding fit to blind a man into piety. Trajan moves through his own magnificence like a surveyor who happens to own the land: a soldier-emperor among columns, basilicas, libraries rising in Greek and Latin on either side of a courtyard where a column will stand when the carvers have finished telling the Dacian wars in a spiral no one at ground level will ever fully read. Antonia, who has no business being seen at an imperial dedication and every business seeing it, stands at the margin with Eirene and a veil drawn in the respectable style, in the company of contractors' wives and a dealer in purple dye who owes her money and is therefore delighted to make room.

She does not look for Lentulus. This is a discipline, like not touching a loose tooth. She looks instead at the stone-cutters' marks still visible on a base course, at a secretary of the emperor's household she knows to be short-sighted and vain about it, at the way the crowd makes a lane for men it believes important and closes, just as instinctively, against men it has finished with. Power in Rome is mostly this: a lane opening.

The dealer in purple is called Gaius Septimius, he dyes for half the bridal trade of Rome, and he owes Antonia nine hundred sesterces on a note she bought from his landlord in the autumn, at a discount, for reasons that were partly business and partly that the landlord's son had beaten a servant in her house two years ago and she collects such accounts in whatever currency presents itself. Septimius does not know she holds the note. He knows she is a woman who once found him, at two days' notice, a buyer for a consignment of cloth ruined — *improved*, he said at the time, sweating — by an apprentice's error with the mordant. Gratitude of that vintage does not thin. He makes room for her and Eirene beside his wife, a gaunt, handsome woman with dyer's hands she hides in public inside her sleeves, and launches, by way of rent on the standing space, into the cost of everything in sight.

“You see the gilding on the basilica entablature? Gold leaf, Antonia. Not wash. Leaf. My cousin had the contract for the glue alone and bought a farm at Tibur.”

“Then the empire is in good health,” Antonia says. “When the glue men are buying farms, the world is sticking together.”

Septimius' wife makes a sound inside her veil that is either a laugh or a cough being strangled for respectability. Eirene, who has the stillness of a woman listening with her whole skin, says, “Tell her about the library steps. He has been telling the street all morning. It improves with an audience that pays by the piece.”

But Antonia is not listening to Septimius, not with the front of her mind. She is doing what she does in every crowd: reading it as a ledger. There — a contractor who supplied lime for the Forum vaults, gone rich and strange with it, wearing a ring on every finger as if his hands were a manifesto; his wife assessing other wives' jewellery with the care of a money-changer at a doubtful coin. There — two young equestrians she dined last month, deep in an attempt to look senatorial, an aspiration their tailors have funded and their posture betrays. There — very still, in the grey that costs more than colour — Servilia. Antonia has seen her perhaps a dozen times in twenty years, always at this distance, which is the distance Rome keeps between the women in its arrangements: close enough to evaluate, too far to require a greeting. Servilia's veil is arranged as if by engineers. She is listening to an older woman — an aunt, an Acilia, somebody with a priesthood in the family — with an attention so complete it must be costing her something, and Antonia, who prices attention professionally, thinks: *that is a woman being told the cost of a marriage. It takes one to itemise one.*

She does not permit the thought a second sentence. Servilia is filed, has been filed for nineteen years, under *the settlement*, and Antonia does not open other women's files in public; she has a house that lives by the principle.

The crowd has its own weather. It thickens toward the imperial party, thins at the altars where the smoke will be, eddies around a man selling, with an entrepreneur's instinct for a captive Senate, little clay columns no taller than a thumb, already spiralled, already triumphant. A child is crying somewhere in the particular key that carries. A legionary veteran on a crutch is being helped, by two strangers and a third man's elbow, up onto a plinth for a better view, and the crowd makes that small, satisfied noise cities make when they have done a decency within sight of each other. Somewhere a rope gives way at a barrier and there is a surge and a counter-surge and a voice raised in official Latin, and a pickpocket — Antonia watches his whole career: the drift, the gather, the repaired innocence of the hands — decides, feeling Barbatus's attention from twenty paces like weather on his neck, to become a spectator instead. Barbatus is not beside her today; Barbatus is never beside her at dedications, it would be a statement, and statements are for clients. But a man who has worked a door for eleven years radiates. She has seen his attention work from across a courtyard full of wine. A Forum is merely a courtyard with an emperor in it.

“What do you suppose it cost?” Eirene asks, meaning the whole thing: forum, basilica, libraries, the column still in its scaffold of timber like a tree not yet allowed to be stone.

“The Dacians,” Antonia says. “Personally. That is the official answer and for once it is also the accounts. Gold in the mountains, men in the mines, marble in Rome.” She watches a team of oxen, garlanded to the edge of dignity, being walked in a wide, patient circuit to keep them calm before the sacrifice. Their hides shine. Handlers talk to them in the low, continuous murmur men use for animals they respect and intend to kill, a tone not unknown in the Senate. “Everything here has been somewhere else first, Eirene. That's the dedication they're actually performing.”

“You should write speeches.”

“I should charge for them. Septimius — your wife's sleeve is better dyed than the priest's border, and if you tell her I said so she will charge you for the compliment in kind.”

She says it because Septimius' wife has taken her hands out of her sleeves to settle her veil and the purple under her nails is the deep, true, sea-snail purple that money cannot quite buy and work cannot wash out. The woman hears. Under the veil something changes — not a smile, which would need witnesses; an acknowledgement, tradeswoman to tradeswoman across a dyer's husband, that the hands have been seen and priced correctly. It is the kind of transaction Antonia values: exact, unrecorded, impossible to tax. Rome runs on them underneath everything it inscribes.

Now the procession of office is forming on the far side: lictors, priests, the colleges in their order, boys with incense swinging their arms wide the way boys have swung incense since Romulus, half devotion, half showing off. The emperor is visible for whole seconds at a time between shoulders: a soldier's head, grey now, the famous plainness of dress that costs more to maintain than purple, since it must never look maintained. Antonia has fed half the men walking behind him. She catalogues them without appetite: who is up, who is being carried by his wife's money, who has begun, in the last year, to have his dinners at home and his truths told by others, which is the first symptom. Power in Rome is mostly this, she thinks again — and then the crowd shifts, in that organic, prescient way it has, a lane beginning to open before the reason for it is visible, the way water finds out about the stone before the stone arrives.

It opens, inevitably, not far from her. Senators in their broad stripes. A priest with his cap tied under his chin. And Lentulus, in the fold and fall of wool that costs more than a good horse, listening to the emperor with his head inclined at the angle that, in their youth — she puts the thought down, hard, like a cup that might spill — that men of his family are taught before they are taught Greek. He is forty-five. There is grey now at his temples that the barber cannot shave away. It suits him, which she notes as she would note a competitor's new cook: professionally, and with resentment held at a distance where it can be of use.

He sees her. Of course he sees her. Twenty years of not speaking in public requires a highly developed awareness of where the other person is standing.

Neither of them inclines a head. The crowd shifts. A child on his father's shoulders points at the equestrian statue and shrieks with the pure, unpatronised joy of a person who does not yet know what anything costs. Somewhere a trumpet tests the air and the pigeons rise off the new marble in a single grey applause.

Eirene says, very quietly, “The embroiderer is still at the house. She asked whether the mistress pays by the piece or by the day, and when Diomedes said by the piece she said, good, because her days belong to her father. I liked her for it.”

“Pay her by the piece,” Antonia says, still looking at the Forum, at the lane closing now behind the senators. “And then add a day's wage, and write it in the book as thread. If her days belong to her father, we will not be the ones to inform him that her work is worth more than he thinks. Let her tell him herself, when she is ready.”

She turns before the sacrifices begin. She has a house to run, an inspection to outlive, and a long-practised conviction that the gods, like senators, prefer to be addressed by people who have first attended to their drains.
