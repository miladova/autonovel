The Dedication
She is early, because to be late in this city is to be read as careless, and careless women are charged more for everything. The morning is still blue at the edges. Below the Caelian the streets are already loud with handcarts and muleteers swearing at the gradient, with slaves carrying water up from the fountains in jars that knock against their hips. Antonia Cassia stands in her own courtyard and puts her hand, quite flat, to the painted plaster to feel whether the damp has come through again in the night. It has. A bloom of cold under the red. She makes a note of it in the part of her mind where repairs live, between the price of Falernian and the name of the fuller who does not steal.

Diomedes is waiting with the tablets. He does not look at her face first. He looks at her hands, as clerks do, to see if she is ready to hold a stylus or only to listen. “The dedication crowds will close the Forum by the third hour,” he says. “If the wine is to come up from the warehouses today it must come now.”

“Then now,” she says. “And pay the carter before he unloads, not after. I am tired of his face at the kitchen door.”

He marks it. The wax takes the point with a small, neat resistance that she has always liked. Everything in this house should resist a little before it yields. The doors. The accounts. The women, when a guest mistakes payment for ownership. Herself, most of all, when she is asked a question whose answer would be useful to the asker.

Eirene comes across the courtyard with a bowl of warm water and a cloth over her arm, her hair still pinned for sleep. She is Antonia's age and has the particular beauty of women who have stopped arranging their faces for other people before noon. “You have ink on your jaw,” Eirene says.

“Then I was working in my sleep.”

“You were working instead of it.” Eirene wipes the mark away. Her fingers are cool. “The new hanging is here. The embroiderer herself brought it. She would not give it to the porter.”

“Good for her. Give her breakfast and keep her from Barbatus. He thinks courtesy is a kind of wrestling.”

Diomedes turns a tablet over. “There is also a notice. From the aediles' office. It was left before dawn, which is either diligence or a wish not to be seen leaving it.”

Antonia takes it. The seal is correct. The hand is a clerk's hand, trained to make threats look like administration. An inspection. A review of contributions, of registrations, of the uses to which rooms in this insula are put after dark. Words that have been washed so often they no longer smell of anything: order, decency, precedent. At the foot, a name cited as proposing the review to the Senate's committee. She reads it twice, not because she doubts her eyes but because her eyes, at thirty-eight, prefer close work in better light, and because she wants the small delay in which a feeling can be put down somewhere it will not be seen.

Gnaeus Cornelius Lentulus.

Eirene is watching her. Diomedes is very interested in the courtyard roses, which are overwatered and know it.

“Well,” Antonia says. “The emperor dedicates a forum today, and Lentulus dedicates himself to my drains. We all build what we can.”

She hands the notice back. “File it with the tax receipts. Answer nothing today. If a man sends a clerk before dawn he wants a reply before noon, and I have never yet let Lentulus set the hour in my house.”

He is being shaved when his son comes in, which means he cannot turn his head, which means his son must say whatever he has come to say to a profile held still by another man's hand. Gnaeus Cornelius Lentulus has found, over twenty years of public life, that a razor at the throat improves the quality of other people's sentences. They become shorter. They become, if not honest, at least economical.

“The Forum today,” the boy says. He is twenty and calls this an opening.

“Yes.”

“Shall I ride with you or with Uncle Postumus?”

“With me. Postumus will talk through the sacrifices and then claim he foresaw the omens.”

The barber's thumb pulls the skin taut under his jaw. In the mirror — polished bronze, his grandfather's, the rim dented where a slave dropped it the winter the Tiber froze at the edges — Lentulus can see his son shift from foot to foot in a way that his tutors were paid to correct and did not. The boy has his mother's height and, Lentulus thinks, with the clarity that comes when one may not move, none of his mother's patience and all of his own appetite for being elsewhere.

“There is talk,” the boy says, “about your proposal. The inspections.”

“There is always talk. It is the city's second water supply.”

“They say it is aimed. At one house in particular.”

The razor pauses. The barber, who has shaved three consuls and buried two, knows when a household has stepped on its own hem.

“Who are they, Marcus?” Lentulus asks.

“Men at the baths. Men who owe me money at dice and pay in information because it is cheaper.”

“A proposal on registrations is aimed at everyone who has failed to register. If one house feels it more than others, that may be because one house has grown very large on very small paperwork.” He can hear how well this will sound in the Senate, and how thin it sounds here, with soap on his neck. “You will not repeat bath-house talk at the dedication. You will stand where you are put, you will be seen to attend to the emperor, and you will remember that your sister's negotiations require this family to look like a wall, not a window.”

Marcus bows, correctly, which in this family is what young men do instead of slamming doors the slaves would have to repair.

When he has gone, the barber resumes. “Shall I leave the scar, dominus?”

The scar is small, under the left ear, older than the boy who just left the room. Lentulus says, “You always leave the scar, Felix. It is not a decision I need to make fresh each morning.”

“No, dominus.” Felix wipes the blade. “Some men like to think it is.”

Out in the atrium the ancestral masks look down from their cupboards, wax faces darkened by smoke and time, each with its painted inscription: offices held, triumphs voted, provinces governed. Lentulus passes them as he passes them every morning, with the brief, involuntary arithmetic of the living: what he has added, what he has merely maintained, what the debts secured against the Campanian estates would look like if debts were inscribed under a man's name as offices are. He touches nothing. A Cornelius does not touch the masks for luck. He behaves as if luck were a vulgarity other families require.

His wife is at the household shrine, making the morning offering with the household gathered in the order she has arranged and rearranged over nineteen years until it looks like nature. Servilia does not look up when he joins her. She pours, she speaks the words, she sets the cup down exactly within the ring its predecessors have left on the stone. Only when the slaves have withdrawn does she say, “You sent the notice before dawn.”

“The aediles sent it. I proposed a review. These are distinct actions.”

“In the Senate they will be. At dinner they will not.” She straightens the fold of his toga where it crosses his chest. Her hands are expert and entirely without tenderness, which is not the same as without care. “If you mean to make war on that woman, Lentulus, do it in daylight and win it quickly. This family has a daughter to marry. We cannot afford a siege that everyone in Rome watches while eating.”

“I do not make war on women,” he says.

“No,” Servilia says. “You make proposals. Rome will supply the war.”

By the third hour the new Forum is a press of white wool and oiled hair and January sun striking gilding fit to blind a man into piety. Trajan moves through his own magnificence like a surveyor who happens to own the land: a soldier-emperor among columns, basilicas, libraries rising in Greek and Latin on either side of a courtyard where a column will stand when the carvers have finished telling the Dacian wars in a spiral no one at ground level will ever fully read. Antonia, who has no business being seen at an imperial dedication and every business seeing it, stands at the margin with Eirene and a veil drawn in the respectable style, in the company of contractors' wives and a dealer in purple dye who owes her money and is therefore delighted to make room.

She does not look for Lentulus. This is a discipline, like not touching a loose tooth. She looks instead at the stone-cutters' marks still visible on a base course, at a secretary of the emperor's household she knows to be short-sighted and vain about it, at the way the crowd makes a lane for men it believes important and closes, just as instinctively, against men it has finished with. Power in Rome is mostly this: a lane opening.

It opens, inevitably, not far from her. Senators in their broad stripes. A priest with his cap tied under his chin. And Lentulus, in the fold and fall of wool that costs more than a good horse, listening to the emperor with his head inclined at the angle that, in their youth — she puts the thought down, hard, like a cup that might spill — that men of his family are taught before they are taught Greek. He is forty-five. There is grey now at his temples that the barber cannot shave away. It suits him, which she notes as she would note a competitor's new cook: professionally, and with resentment held at a distance where it can be of use.

He sees her. Of course he sees her. Twenty years of not speaking in public requires a highly developed awareness of where the other person is standing.

Neither of them inclines a head. The crowd shifts. A child on his father's shoulders points at the equestrian statue and shrieks with the pure, unpatronised joy of a person who does not yet know what anything costs. Somewhere a trumpet tests the air and the pigeons rise off the new marble in a single grey applause.

Eirene says, very quietly, “The embroiderer is still at the house. She asked whether the mistress pays by the piece or by the day, and when Diomedes said by the piece she said, good, because her days belong to her father. I liked her for it.”

“Pay her by the piece,” Antonia says, still looking at the Forum, at the lane closing now behind the senators. “And then add a day's wage, and write it in the book as thread. If her days belong to her father, we will not be the ones to inform him that her work is worth more than he thinks. Let her tell him herself, when she is ready.”

She turns before the sacrifices begin. She has a house to run, an inspection to outlive, and a long-practised conviction that the gods, like senators, prefer to be addressed by people who have first attended to their drains.
