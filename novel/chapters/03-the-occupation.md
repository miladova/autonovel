# Three

## The Occupation

The Germans did not arrive in Brzozówka all at once. They arrived in pieces over two years, each piece small enough to be borne, and by 1942 the village had a new shape and had stopped noticing that it had one.

First came the papers. In the spring of 1940 everyone over fourteen was registered at the gmina office and given a Kennkarte, a grey fold of card with a fingerprint and a photograph, and Zofia, who was twelve, was measured and photographed with her hair pulled back and told to look at the wall. Then came the quotas: grain, potatoes, meat, milk, eggs, a list nailed to the door of the gmina office, with the name of every household and what it owed. Then came the Arbeitsamt, which took the young people for work in Germany, and the first three boys went in June and one of them wrote twice and then never again. Then came the order that the school was to be used for the storage of grain, which it already was. Then came the order that no one was to be out of doors after eight in the evening. Then came the gendarmes, four of them, at the post in Krasnystaw, who came through the village on Thursdays in a lorry and sometimes on foot and sometimes not at all, and whose names, once learned, were never forgotten: Kliemann, who was fat and had a pleasant face and had killed a man in the market at Krasnystaw with the butt of a rifle for not taking off his cap; Bauer, who was thin and silent and collected watches; a Ukrainian from Lublin they called Iwan; and a young one, Vogel, who was nineteen and had been a bank clerk and who gave sweets to children and once wept in the street, and who was the worst of them in the end.

The village did what villages do. It lied about its grain. It hid its pigs in the birch wood and its best tools under the floor of the barn. It sent its boys to the forest when the lorries came. It bribed the Polish policeman, who was a man from the next parish and who took the bribes and passed on what he could. It kept its mouth shut, and it kept its accounts in its head, and it survived, in the way that a stone survives a river, by not being soluble.

And it went on teaching its children.

Pan Wróbel had been dismissed from the school in November 1939 for having been a member of the peasant party and for having a son in Vilnius. He stayed in the village, because he had nowhere else, and in January 1940 he began to teach again, four evenings a week, in the kitchen of Franciszek Wierzbicki's house, to eleven children between the ages of nine and seventeen.

It was dangerous. The penalty for clandestine teaching was not always death, but it was camp, and everyone knew it, and the parents who sent their children knew it and sent them anyway, because a Pole without Polish is a peasant and a peasant without letters is a serf and this had been understood in Brzozówka for a hundred years. The lessons were held after dark with the shutters closed and a lamp turned low and a lookout in the yard. The book was one book, a history of Poland, in three volumes, which Pan Wróbel had wrapped in oilcloth and buried in the priest's garden in October 1939 and dug up again in the spring. The children learned the dates by singing them, and they learned the poetry by heart, and when the lookout knocked twice on the window frame, the book went into the space behind the stove and the eleven children sat in silence with their hands folded while Pan Wróbel told them, in a level voice, about the beet harvest.

Zofia went to those lessons for three years. She learned the history of her country in a kitchen, by lamplight, in a house that smelled of wood shavings and cabbage, sitting on a bench beside Tomasz Wierzbicki, who had already read the book twice and who corrected Pan Wróbel when he got a date wrong, which Pan Wróbel permitted, because the boy was right.

***

In the autumn of 1942 the Germans took the Frydmans away.

There had been a ghetto in Krasnystaw since the winter of 1940, and the Jews of the district had been moved into it, and the shop in Brzozówka had been closed and its windows broken and its stock taken by the Kreishauptmann's office and sold, and Icek Frydman had been seen in the market at Krasnystaw that winter with a yellow star on his coat, selling cigarettes from a tray. Then in October 1942 the lorries came to the ghetto at night, and in the morning there were no Jews in Krasnystaw, or very few, and the ones who remained were in the camp at Trawniki, and the ones who remained after that were nowhere.

But Frydman had got out. In October of 1942, in the middle of a night of rain, he had come to the door of the smithy with his wife and his daughter and a bundle, and her father had opened the door in his shirt and looked at them for a long moment, and then he had said, "Come in, Icek," and closed the door behind them, and that was the whole of the discussion.

They lived for twenty months in a space under the floor of the barn, which Józef Krawiec had dug out himself over four nights, with a hatch under the straw and a pipe for air that came up behind the pigsty. They were not to make a sound during the day. They were not to come out except at night, and not every night, and only into the barn. Rywka Frydman, who had been a large woman, became thin, and then became something else, something that sat in the dark with its hands in its lap. Sara, who had been eight, was nine, and then ten; she had her doll with the china face, and she had a piece of chalk and a board, and Zofia taught her the alphabet from a primer she had stolen from the school storeroom, in the dark, by touch, whispering, and Sara learned to read in the summer of 1943, which is to say she learned to read in a hole in the ground under a barn in occupied Poland at the age of nine, and she was very proud of it, and she could not tell anyone.

Her father never spoke of it. Not once, in all the years after. He carried the hay down to them and carried the slops up and put his ear to the ground when there were footsteps in the yard, and he did it for twenty months, and he did not mention it, and if anyone had asked him why he would have said that a man does not let his neighbour be taken out of his house, and he would have thought the question stupid.

The war came into the village in other ways. In November 1942 the Germans began the expulsions in the Zamość region, which was sixty kilometres east, and the roads filled with people. They came through Brzozówka in wagons and on foot, families from villages that no longer existed, with their bedding and their pots and whatever they had been able to carry, and the Germans put them in transit camps and then in trains, and the trains went west, and some of the people were Poles from good farms who had been there since the fourteenth century and were now being moved to make room for German settlers from Bessarabia and the Baltic.

Some of them did not go on. They stopped in Brzozówka, in the barns and the school storeroom and the church porch, and the village fed them, which it could not afford, and hid some of them, which it could not afford at all. There were children among them who had been separated from their parents, and there was a great deal of talk about the children, because the Germans were taking the children too, not for labour but for something else, and no one knew what, and everyone knew.

***

Zofia was fifteen in the winter of 1942. She was tall for her age and strong from the yoke and the water and the hay, and she had begun to be looked at by men, which she disliked and used. She had stopped going to the secret lessons in the autumn, because Pan Wróbel had been arrested in September and sent to Majdanek, and had died there in January, and there was no one to teach. She could read now, well, and she read everything in the priest's study that he would lend her, which was not much, and everything in the Wierzbickis' house, which was more. She had a notebook in which she wrote, in a careful hand, the things she did not say: the dates from Pan Wróbel's history, the poems, the names of the dead.

Tomasz was eighteen in the winter of 1942 and was no longer in the village most nights.

She did not know at first. He was there in the daytime, in the carpenter's shop, planing boards, or in the church, pumping the organ, or at the forge, helping her father with a hinge. And then he would be gone, and his father would say he had gone to Krasnystaw for an order, and Zofia would see the old man's hands, and the way he did not look at her.

***

In March she found out. She had gone up to the church to sweep the aisle — Father Zaremba paid her four złoty a month and a loaf — and Tomasz was in the organ loft with the little door open, and he had taken off his shirt, and there was a bandage around his ribs, badly done, and blood coming through it in a line the width of a finger.

He heard her on the stair and turned, and for a moment his face was not his face.

"Go down."

"You're bleeding."

"It's a scratch. Go down, Zosia."

She came up the last three steps, and he did not stop her, and she looked at the bandage, which had been put on by someone who did not know how, and she said, "Sit on the bench. I'll do it. I've done my father's hands."

"Zofia."

"Sit on the bench."

He sat. She fetched water from the jug by the organ and a clean cloth from the chest where the vestments were kept, and she unwound the bandage, and the wound underneath was a groove along the lowest rib, about four inches long, not deep, and beginning, she thought, to be angry at the edges. She cleaned it. He held the bench with both hands and did not make a sound, and she put on the salve from the tin in the vestment chest, and bound it with a strip of the linen that was kept for the altar, which was a sin, and which she did not mention to him, and which he did not mention either.

When she had finished he said, "You won't tell your father."

"I won't tell my father."

"You won't tell anyone."

"I know what to do, Tomasz."

He looked at her. He had grown into his face at last that winter; he was going to be a handsome man, and she had noticed it in the way one notices a change in the weather, without deciding anything. There was a bruise coming up along his jaw.

"Since when?" she said.

"Last spring."

"The forest?"

He did not answer, which was an answer.

"Since last spring," she said, "and you never said."

"I couldn't."

"You could have. Not the names. Just — that you were. I thought you were going to Krasnystaw for orders." Her voice had gone thin. "I thought you were — I don't know what I thought."

"Zosia."

"You could have told me that much."

He was silent for a moment. Then he said, "Do you know what they do to a man's family? If he's taken and they find out who knew? They take the family. Not the man, he's in the forest, they can't get him. The family." He looked at his hands. "My father is sixty. Yours has the frydmans under the barn." He said it flatly, without emphasis, and she understood that he was telling her the most dangerous thing he knew in order to explain himself, and that he was trusting her with it, and that it was the only apology he had.

"All right," she said.

"All right?"

"All right." She put the stopper in the salve. "But you will eat. You're thin. You'll come to the house on Thursday and my mother will feed you and she will think it's for the church."

He laughed — one breath of it, and then he winced and put a hand to his ribs. "You're a terrible girl, Zofia Krawiec."

"I know," she said. "Lie down for ten minutes and then go down the back way. There's a hole in the fence by the orchard."

In the summer of 1943 she became a courier.

It was Tomasz's doing, and she never knew whether to be grateful. A unit in the forest needed people who could carry a message and not be looked at twice: girls of fifteen and sixteen, boys too young for the army, old women with baskets. Zofia was sixteen, she could walk fifteen kilometres and back in a day, she had a good memory, and she had a face that people trusted, which she had already begun to understand was a resource.

Her first message was a folded paper in the false bottom of a basket of eggs, from the mill to a farm beyond the birch wood. Her second was spoken: eleven words, which she repeated to herself all the way there and all the way back, and which she could still recite in 1958, though she had long since forgotten what they meant. Her third was a warning.

She was not brave, she said afterward, when people asked, and people did ask, in the years when it was still possible to ask. She was frightened the whole time. She was frightened in a way that made her hands cold and her hearing unnaturally good, and she went on doing it because doing nothing was worse, and because there was a boy in the forest who would know.

***

The summer of 1943 was the last good summer of her life, and she knew it while it was happening, which is a rare and terrible thing.

The rye was high that year. The Germans were busy in the east; the front had moved and the troops had gone with it, and the gendarmes were fewer, and there was a kind of breathing space in the valley, and in that space the ordinary life of the village went on with a strange intensity, as if it knew it had been given a reprieve and meant to use it. The hay was cut. The bees swarmed and were caught. There was a wedding at the mill in July and they danced, and Zofia danced with Tomasz, and the band was two fiddles and a drum, and her mother cried and pretended it was the onions.

It happened in August, at the river, on a hot night after the Assumption.

They had walked down to the bend, as they had a thousand times, and the water was low and warm and the alders made a dark place, and the moon was up. He had been telling her something about the unit, nothing important, an argument about a horse, and then he stopped talking, and she knew, in the way that one knows, that the evening had turned, and she sat very still.

"Zosia."

"Mm."

"I'm going to say something and you mustn't laugh."

"I won't laugh."

"I've thought about it for a long time. Since — I don't know. Since the well."

"The well?"

"The bucket. When you were nine." He was looking at the water. "I thought, that girl is going to be somebody. And then I thought, I would like to be there when she is. And then it was years later and I was thinking it still."

She could not speak. Her heart was going in a way that was not entirely pleasant.

"Say something," he said.

"I can't."

"Say anything. Insult me. That would be better than—"

She kissed him.

It was a bad kiss, the first one; she had no idea what she was doing, and neither, she discovered, did he, and there was a moment when their noses were in the way and she started to laugh against his mouth, and then it stopped being funny. Afterward they sat on the fallen willow, holding hands, and neither of them said anything for a long time, and the river went past, and a heron got up out of the reeds and went away downstream, and she thought: I will remember this. I will remember the exact temperature of the air. And she did, for the rest of her life.

"There's a war on," he said at last.

"I know."

"I might be killed."

"Don't."

"I have to say it. I might be killed, and you might—"

"Tomasz." She turned and took his face in her hands, which she had never done, which she would not do again for fifteen years. "I know there's a war on. I know you might be killed. I am not a child. But if you say it again I will push you in the river, and you will deserve it, and the fish will eat you, and I will tell everyone you were a Protestant."

He laughed, and then he was quiet, and then he said, "When it's over."

"When it's over."

"The house at the bend. Two rooms and a porch."

"And a stove better than my father's."

"A stove better than your father's," he said gravely, "and he will be insulted, and I will invite him to dinner, and he will come, and he will say the stove is not bad, which from him is a compliment, and then we will be all right."

She put her head on his shoulder and looked at the water. "What will you do? After. For work."

He was silent a moment. Then he said, "I think I'm going to be a priest."

She sat up. "What?"

"I've thought about it since I was a boy. My father knows. I've not — I've never said it to anyone else." He turned to look at her, and his face in the moonlight was anxious and very young. "It doesn't change anything. It doesn't change this. I don't know how to explain it. It's not that I don't want—" He stopped. "I want you. I have wanted you since the well. But there is also this other thing, and it has been there longer, and I don't know what to do with it, and I thought you should know before—"

"You thought I should know," she said, "before I kissed you?"

"Before I asked you to wait for me."

She looked at him for a long moment. The heron had gone. Somewhere up at the mill a dog was barking, and then it stopped.

"Then I'll wait," she said.

***

He made her the ring in October. It was a bicycle spoke, cut and filed and bent round a mandrel in his father's shop, and it was not beautiful, and it left a green mark on her finger, and she wore it on a string around her neck because a ring on the hand would be seen. He gave it to her behind the church after the rosary, in the dark, with the smell of the cold coming off the fields.

"It's not gold," he said.

"It's better than gold. Gold is soft."

"That's not how it works."

"That's how it works for me," she said, and put it in her blouse, against her skin, and felt it cold and then warm.

The winter of 1943 was hard. The Germans took the potatoes and then took the pigs and then took the men for labour, thirty-one from the gmina in January, and one of them was the miller's son, who was twenty and had been at the wedding. The partisans in the forest were being hunted; there were sweeps in December and January, and twice Tomasz did not come home for three weeks, and his father played the organ at Mass with his face like a wall. The front was coming. Everyone said so. The Reds were at the Dnieper, and then the Bug, and it was said they would be in Lublin by the summer, and what would happen then no one knew, though everyone had an opinion.

***

In April 1944 the Germans put up a poster in the market square at Krasnystaw. It was printed in Polish and German, and it announced that the population of the district was to be resettled for reasons of military necessity, and that anyone who sheltered partisans or Jews would be punished with death, and that the punishment would be carried out on the whole village.

Her father read it and folded his hands behind his back and looked at it for a while, and then he said, "They will come here."

"When?"

"When they come." He turned away. "We should dig another place. Under the orchard. Not for us — for them. If they come at night the barn is the first place they'll look."

They dug it over three nights, in the dark, with the soil going into the pigsty so that the ground would not show. It was a hole four paces by three, lined with boards, with a hatch under the woodpile and a pipe for air, and it took them six nights in the end, and on the last night her father stood at the edge of it with the spade and said, "If I am taken, you will take them to Pani Zaleska at the mill. She will not refuse. She owes me."

"She won't be asked."

"She will be asked," he said, "and she will not refuse. You will go at night, by the river, not the road."

"Tata."

"Say it back to me."

"At night, by the river, to Pani Zaleska at the mill. She will not refuse."

"Good." He put the spade over his shoulder. "And Zofia. If they take me, you will not come after me, and you will not go to the gendarmes, and you will not do anything at all. You will stay in the house with your mother and the boy. Do you understand?"

"Yes."

"You are lying," her father said, without heat, "but say it anyway."

In May the front came closer and the nights were full of aircraft going east, a low continuous sound like a river in the sky. Tomasz came home for four days in the middle of the month, thinner, with a cough, and on the last evening they walked down to the bend and sat on the willow and did not talk much. The water was high. The alders were in leaf.

"In June," he said, "the unit is going to move. There's a mobilization. After that — I don't know. Maybe it will be over. They say the Red Army will be in Lublin by July."

"And then?"

"And then the war will be over and I will come back and I will go to the seminary and you will wait." He took her hand. "Unless you've changed your mind."

"I haven't changed my mind."

"You might. You're seventeen."

"I'm seventeen and I have made a ring out of a bicycle spoke and I am not going to change my mind." She held up her hand with the string of it around her neck, and he caught the ring and turned it in his fingers. "When?"

"August. September. The latest." He put the ring back. "Zosia, if I don't come back—"

"Then I'll come and find you. I'll walk to wherever you are."

"You won't know where I am."

"I'll find out. I'm a courier," she said. "I'm very good."

He laughed. Then he said, quietly, "I know you are."

They went up the path at midnight with their arms around each other, and at the orchard gate he kissed her and said, "In August," and she said, "In August," and watched him go down the road between the two rows of lilacs, which were in flower, and which filled the whole valley with their smell, so that for the rest of her life she could not smell lilac without being twenty again, and seventeen, and standing at a gate in the dark, believing a thing that was not true.
