# Turnip Soup and Insulin

*Warsaw, 21 February 1943*

On Filtrowa Street the window glass was all whole, and when Wanda opened the front door a gust of warm cabbage-fat and beeswax floor polish rolled out onto the landing thick enough to make Sylwia's empty stomach contract like a fist.

"Take your galoshes off on the mat, Sylwunia, Stefan had the parquet scraped last month," Wanda said before she said hello, kissing the air half an inch beside Sylwia's cold cheek so she wouldn't catch hospital carbolic on her Sunday cardigan. Wanda was thirty-seven, four years older than Sylwia and born to Kazimierz Kotarski's first wife from Kalisz, who had left Wanda round china-blue eyes, soft sloping shoulders, and an unshakable conviction that history was primarily an rudeness aimed at respectable housekeeping. "Goodness, look at your wrists, haven't they even lard-soap left at Dzieciątka Jezus? Give me your coat—mind the sleeve lining, it's trailing."

"Leave the coat on the hook," Sylwia said, lifting her leather medical bag clear before Wanda could set it near the radiator pipe. "Insulin stays cold. Where's Staś?"

"In the dining room doing long division so he doesn't run about before his injection. Come see—Stefan got three real lemons through the Ostbahn canteen on Friday, actual Messina lemons wrapped in tissue paper with Italian print on it!"

Everything inside Number Sixty-Eight Filtrowa sat two inches off plumb with the rest of Warsaw, like a stage set carried intact out of nineteen-thirty-eight and bolted down inside a slaughterhouse. The brass pendulum clock still chimed quarters; the rubber plant in the bay window still had glossy leaves wiped every Saturday with skimmed milk; nobody had sawed the legs off the walnut sideboard to feed the stove because Inżynier Stefan Bielski's tramway-workshop coal allotment arrived on the first of every month in plump hundred-kilo jute sacks delivered by municipal lorry. All it had cost Stefan in March forty-one was three signatures at the *Abteilung Bevölkerungswesen und Fürsorge* attesting that his late mother, born Hedwig Krawczyk in Tarnowitz, Upper Silesia, had raised him in "partially German domestic cultural consciousness"—whereupon the occupation authorities handed him a green *Kennkarte* of *Deutsche Volksliste* Group Three, revocable on ten years' probation, freeing his flat from requisition, doubling his butter coupons, and ensuring that every former Polytechnic classmate who passed him on Marszałkowska crossed silently into the slush on the far pavement rather than spit.

In the dining room Staś scrambled down off his plush-seated chair the second Sylwia appeared, thin knees knocking beneath grey flannel shorts outgrown by three inches—not because the Bielskis lacked cloth coupons, but because diabetic children since forty-one burned their own muscle fat whenever black-market insulin ran short for three days and grew upward like cellar potato-sprouts without thickening.

"Ciocia!" He flung both bony arms round her waist, pressing his warm, cropped dark head hard against her sternum before remembering himself and stepping back straight-backed as Sylwia had taught him. "Sixty-two milligrams minimal glycosuria all week—Mama boiled Benedict's solution Tuesday and Friday, stayed green-blue both times, zero brick-red precipitate!"

Sylwia's mouth softened—the solitary square yard of Warsaw where it ever did without irony—and she cupped two cold fingers beneath the boy's pointed chin, tilting his face toward the bay window to check his sclerae, tongue moisture, and breath. No fruity acetone smell of ketosis; mucous membranes decent pink; just violet smudges carved deep beneath long lashes too big for his face.

"Show me your injection map before you brag, panie profesorze," she said. "If you stuck the left outer middle quadrant three days running because your right hand's lazy, I'll make you recite the cranial nerves backward in Latin."

"Alternated like clockwork," Stefan Bielski announced genially, emerging from his study wiping tobacco crumbs off a trim brown moustache and extending two damp fingers Sylwia touched for half a second and dropped. Stefan had put on four kilos since autumn—a soft pad under the chin, waist straining the third button of a good prewar Bielsko worsted vest—which in February forty-three looked almost obscene on a Polish frame, like rouge on a sexton. "Afternoon, Sylwia. Sit, sit, soup's cooling, and Wanda's been up since six skimming the stock."

"Injection first," Sylwia said, picking up her bag. "Fifteen minutes before carbohydrate load, Stefan, same as every month since forty. Come into the bathroom, Stasiu."

The tiled bathroom smelled of real Palmolive soap—another *Nur für Deutsche* shop miracle—and wet flannel. While her small nickel spirit-burner brought distilled water bubbling around the glass Record syringe and fine twenty-six-gauge needle, Staś sat perched on the closed wooden toilet lid with his right woollen stocking rolled down to the ankle, trying desperately not to stare at the fresh brown glass ten-milliliter vial Sylwia lifted out of cotton wool: *Insulin Leo — Nordisk Insulinlaboratorium, København — 40 International Units per ml*.

Nine-year-olds in Warsaw knew prices adults in Zurich didn't know. One ten-milliliter vial of forty-unit Danish insulin—four hundred units, barely twenty-five days' life at sixteen units daily even on strict turnip-and-buckwheat rationing—cost two thousand four hundred *młynarki* on the black corridor behind Świętokrzyska when a crooked Wehrmacht medical orderly happened to steal one off a hospital train from Berlin, and four thousand when none came through for three weeks.

"Did you get two bottles, Ciocia?" the boy whispered, watching her swab the rubber cap with spirit.

"Three," Sylwia lied smoothly without batting an eyelash—she had got one and a half, the half salvaged from a sixty-year-old Volksdeutscher butcher who died of uremia in Pavilion One on Thursday before his family claimed his washbag, and the whole vial bought by trading Bronka's best pair of stolen curved Krupp intestinal clamps to a railway pharmacist from Poznań. "Enough till Easter if your mother keeps her hands off the white flour. Pinch up—two inches above last Wednesday's dot."

Staś pinched a pitifully thin fold of pale thigh skin between thumb and index finger—almost zero subcutaneous fat pad left over vastus lateralis, so she had to slide the needle in at twenty-five degrees shallow not to hit muscle fascia where insulin absorbed too fast and dropped him into sweating afternoon hypoglycemia. He didn't even blink when the steel pricked home, counting steadily under his breath: "*Jeden, dwa, trzy, cztery, pięć...*"

"Good soldier," Sylwia said, pressing dry sterile cotton briefly over the puncture before rolling his stocking up and ruffling his hair once. "Go eat your soup slowly. Don't gulp the barley."

When she came out drying her syringe parts in linen, Stefan was waiting alone in the hall doorway between bathroom and dining room, blocking the runner carpet with his broad worsted shoulders while Wanda clattered soup plates inside.

"How much do I owe you for the Danish bottle?" Stefan asked low, already pulling a thick calfskin wallet bloated with crisp hundred-złoty *młynarki* notes out of his breast pocket.

"Twelve hundred," Sylwia said, naming half what the Poznań pharmacist's markup cost in hard barter because taking Stefan's *Volksliste* paper money always left grease on her fingers, yet refusing it outright drove Wanda into three-hour weeping fits about pride.

Stefan peeled off fifteen hundreds anyway and pushed them into the outer pocket of her bag with heavy brother-in-law magnanimity. "Take fifteen—buy yourself fifty grams of lard before your skin cracks off your bones, Sylwia. Honestly, looking at you across the table makes Wanda cry all Sunday night. Why you insist on starving in that freezing municipal pest-house on Polish ration cards when one signature from Docent Mirecki certifying your six Berlin semesters puts you on *Ostbahn* contract medical scales—"

"Don't start, Stefan."

"—I'm speaking practically, as family!" His voice dropped into that aggrieved, oily whisper she had heard in twenty Warsaw drawing rooms where men tried scrubbing green ink off their souls with arithmetic. "Do you think I enjoy saying *Guten Morgen* to Herr Direktor Krehl from Essen every morning at the Wola sheds while boys chalk *Świnia* on my garden gate in the dark? Somebody has to keep seventy tramcars rolling or three hundred thousand Poles walk ten kilometers in slush to work! If I hadn't signed Group Three in forty-one, some Baltic German drunk from Riga would be sitting in my chair sending half my fitters to Reich factories, and Staś would have been dead in six weeks without coal or currency!"

Sylwia snapped the brass clasp of her medical bag shut. In the dim hall mirror behind Stefan's ear, her own face looked stripped down to grey bone and dark eyes—thirty-three going on fifty, Helena's cold mouth inherited whole without Helena's morphine lullaby.

"Keep telling the mirror that when you shave, Stefan," she said quietly. "Just check your Wola workshop absence sheets twice before you sign them on Mondays. Word down on Towarowa is Direktor Krehl started forwarding three-day sick-leave names straight to the Skaryszewska labor-camp trucks last week—and when the boys who chalk gates get tired of chalk, they don't ask whose mother came from Tarnowitz."

All the healthy butter-pink drained out of Stefan's cheeks down into his collar. Before he could find breath to bluster, Wanda poked her head round the sliding oak doors calling brightly that the turnip broth was growing cold, and for forty-five minutes they sat beneath Kazimierz Kotarski's oil portrait eating yellow rutabaga soup boiled with a single goose neck while talking with grotesque, suffocating politeness about double-glazing putty and Staś's geography marks.

At ten minutes past three Sylwia kissed Staś's forehead, left half her slice of ersatz lemon-rind cake untouched beside her saucer because sweet saccharin made her throat gag, and walked back out into the sharp February wind toward Lindleya with twenty minutes to spare before SS-Hauptsturmführer Kroll came calling.

* * *

You could always tell when Aleja Szucha was inside a Polish hospital before you saw a single black collar tab, because every wooden clog on the ground floor stopped clattering.

Usually at four o'clock on Sunday afternoon Pavilion Three buzzed like a wet beehive: relatives smuggling jars of pickled beets under shawls up the back stairs, ward maids slopping grey lysol water out of galvanized buckets, convalescents with bandaged heads smoking split cigarettes in twos behind the radiator pipes. When Sylwia pushed through the courtyard side door at ten to four, the vestibule smelled solely of floor wax and cold iron. Behind the glass admissions partition old Walenty sat upright on his high stool with both hands flat on the green blotter while two young SD *Rottenführer* in field-grey SS tunics with silver *SD* sleeve diamonds stood beside the radiator smoking Turkish *Nil* cigarettes and flicking ash straight onto Bronka's scrubbed black-and-white tiles.

Inside Docent Mirecki's ground-floor consulting room, the porcelain stove had been stoked too hot.

SS-Hauptsturmführer Reinhart Kroll sat at Mirecki's own mahogany desk—not sprawling across it like cinema bullies, sitting very straight on the front half of the leather cushion with his knees together, his peaked cap resting upside-down on a clean handkerchief beside the inkwell so warehouse dust wouldn't touch the silver skull. He was perhaps thirty-one: slender, narrow-chested, smooth-shaven to baby pinkness, hair the pale sandy-ash of wet sawdust parted neatly with water on the left. Beneath immaculate French-cuffed linen shirt-sleeves protruding half an inch past his grey wool cuffs, he wore thin white cotton gloves even indoors; every two or three minutes, without looking down, the gloved index finger of his left hand stroked back and forth across the inner aspect of his right wrist, where something dry and inflamed rasped audibly beneath the cotton.

"Ah—Frau Doktor Kotarska," Mirecki said too fast from where he stood stranded beside his own bookcase, sweat shining high on his temples despite winter light. "Herr *Hauptsturmführer* Kroll was just reviewing our emergency theater register from Tuesday week."

"A model of legibility," Kroll said, rising two polite inches off the chair leather as Sylwia closed the door behind her. His German was educated Dresden Saxon rubbed nearly clean at Leipzig or Göttingen law school—soft consonants, gentle pitch, the voice of a notary explaining probate fees to an orphan. "One enters municipal establishments in the Generalgouvernement expecting administrative chaos, and instead encounters Prussian book-keeping. You studied in Berlin, Frau Doktor?"

"Friedrich-Wilhelms-Universität, *Herr Hauptsturmführer*," Sylwia said, standing still with her hands loose at her sides so he could see neither tension nor deference. "Nineteen twenty-eight to thirty-three. Surgical state examination under Geheimrat von Halden."

"Luisenstraße," Kroll murmured appreciatively, white cotton fingertip whispering *scritch-scritch* across his right wrist seam. "My uncle held the chair of administrative jurisprudence two streets over on Unter den Linden until his retirement in thirty-seven. Small world, even out here in the east. Sit down, please—no? Surgeons prefer standing; quite right." He glanced down at Walenty's heavy canvas-bound night register lying open across Mirecki's blotter. "I am merely tidying an untidy file for the *Kommandeur der Sicherheitspolizei*. On the night of nine February, between midnight and one a.m., unidentified bandits discharged automatic weapons near the Szczęśliwice railway water-tower in Ochota, killing a Polish track-watchman? No, a civilian driver of uncertain papers, and burning a stolen passenger vehicle. Roughly forty minutes later—at 01:16 by your gate clerk's clock—*Oberstleutnant* Schoeler arrives here with gunshot and fragmentation injuries."

Sylwia kept her eyes calmly on the silver-braided edge of Kroll's upturned cap visor. Forty minutes. From Szczęśliwice water-tower to Lindleya Number Four, even skidding in second gear through snow on three good tires, took *nine* minutes—twelve at worst if Towarowa crossing gate was down. Where had Ludwig Schoeler and a bleeding corporal with two blown-off fingers spent twenty-eight missing minutes in a blizzard with a severed axillary artery?

*Der Koffer ist im Feuer, ich hab ihn verbrannt wie befohlen.* Burning wet leather and paper packets in February snow without petrol took twenty minutes if you waited till every cipher sheet turned black ash before driving to a hospital!

Except if Kroll proved twenty-eight missing minutes, Kroll had cause to strip *Abwehrstelle*'s jurisdiction away on grounds of destroyed criminal evidence—and drag Mekelburg (and anybody else near Room Fourteen) down to Aleja Szucha's basement corridor before supper.

"Gate clerk's clock runs off the wall pendulum in the unheated porter's lodge, *Herr Hauptsturmführer*," Sylwia said smoothly, as if reciting blood-count decimals at morning rounds. "Every frost freezes the oil in its escapement until it loses ten minutes, and old Walenty enters admissions *after* stretchers clear the vestibule—when the corporal stopped holding a Walther pistol at his ribs. By my lapel watch inside Operating Theater One, my Lister shears cut *Oberstleutnant* Schoeler's tunic at twelve minutes to one."

Beside the bookcase, Docent Mirecki didn't breathe; behind his thick pince-nez one eyelid gave a microscopic flutter at hearing Sylwia calmly move the German clock back twenty-eight minutes out of thin air without knowing what Schoeler himself had told—or was about to tell—Kroll down the hall!

Kroll tilted his sandy head five degrees, pale eyes mild and interested. "Twelve minutes to one. You checked your watch before cutting an exsanguinating man's collar open, Frau Doktor?"

"Every vascular surgeon checks clamping time to the minute before occluding a subclavian-axillary trunk, *Herr Hauptsturmführer*," Sylwia replied coolly. "Exceed warm ischemia limits or lose track of shock duration, and you don't know whether limb paralysis afterward comes from nerve severance or ischemic necrosis. Clamping began at 00:51; anastomosis flow restored at 01:14; Walenty copied the theater transfer down at 01:16 when the porter came out for hot bricks."

For four seconds the only sound in Mirecki's heated office was *scritch-scritch*, cotton across dry wrist-scabs.

"Admirable precision," Kroll said softly. "Then you won't mind accompanying me down the hall for three minutes while I pay my respects to *Oberstleutnant* Schoeler and receive any recollection he may wish to contribute toward catching the scoundrels who shot him."

"Three minutes maximum," Sylwia said, stepping aside to open the door herself before Mirecki's shaking knees had to lead. "His wound was debrided raw down to pectoral periosteum five days ago and he runs evening resorption spikes. Stabsarzt Vesper's chart instructions are posted on the door."

Outside Room Fourteen the two *Abwehr* military policemen did not snap heels quite so smartly for an SS *Hauptsturmführer* as ordinary infantrymen did; the senior sergeant—a grizzled forty-year-old Pomeranian from Saski Square—deliberately took twenty full seconds reading Kroll's silver warrant disc and logging Kroll's badge number in pencil on his wall clipboard before unlocking the oak door. Twenty seconds of loud brass key-rattling and heavy throat-clearing: ample warning for anyone awake behind four inches of pine.

Inside, Room Fourteen smelled sharply of Dakin's chlorine and fresh cold air from two inches of open top casement.

Ludwig lay propped on one bolster instead of two—looking calculatedly ten percent greyer and weaker than he had forty minutes ago when Bronka brought his afternoon barley water, eyelids half-mast over winter-water irises, left shoulder bulky under white calico bindings, uninjured right hand resting slack atop the grey Wehrmacht blanket. In the corner by the radiator stood Mekelburg at rigid Prussian attention, wooden hand-splint gleaming white against his tunic buttons.

"*Herr Oberstleutnant*," Kroll said pleasantly, halting at the foot rail rather than approaching the right side of the bed. "Hauptsturmführer Kroll, *Kommandeur der Sicherheitspolizei und des SD Warschau*, Referat IV A. Permit me to express my commander's sincere wishes for your rapid convalescence."

Ludwig looked at Kroll down the high, gaunt bridge of his nose for two unhurried seconds before inclining his chin three millimeters. Even half-naked in coarse Polish hospital linen with twelve days' surgical pallor hollowing his cheeks, he managed to make a healthy man in pressed grey wool with two armed guards outside look like an insurance clerk calling without an appointment.

"Give *Standartenführer* Hahn my thanks, Kroll," Ludwig said, voice dry and slightly raspy, conserving breath with theatrical economy Sylwia recognized instantly from ten oral exams where he had milked examiners dry. "Though I understood *Abwehrstelle* liaison already forwarded our preliminary motor-incident report to Aleja Szucha on Thursday."

"A four-line summary from Hauptmann von Berg, yes," Kroll agreed gently, white cotton thumb stroking his right wrist cuff. "Inspecting Towarowa perimeter approaches prior to night courier dispatch; ambushed from darkness near Szczęśliwice embankment; vehicle disabled; immediate withdrawal to nearest surgical emergency post. Only two small technical puzzles remain for my ballistics people, *Herr Oberstleutnant*, before we close the street sweep."

"Ask them inside Frau Doktor's three minutes, then," Ludwig murmured, flicking a cool, utterly impersonal glance past Sylwia's white shoulder as if she were a radiator valve he barely knew by sight. "Her chlorine tubes make breathing tedious."

*Watch out now,* Sylwia's nerves sang, catching the exact cadence—now had to come the clock trap! How could she give him *twelve minutes to one* without opening her mouth out of turn?

Kroll stepped two paces round the foot rail. "First: municipal tramway night-watchmen near the Ochota water-tower logged gunfire bursts at twelve-twenty-eight a.m. Yet according to the hospital gate-book outside, your car struck the courtyard gate here at *one-sixteen*—nearly fifty minutes later, for a drive of three kilometers. My chief naturally wondered whether your vehicle was forced down elsewhere along Grójecka in a second engagement, or whether you attempted pursuit on foot before collapsing."

Sylwia didn't look at Ludwig; she stepped crisply to the chart bracket hanging at the iron foot-rail right beside Kroll's elbow, unhooking the yellow Wehrmacht clinical sheet with a sharp tin rattle and holding the inked red temperature-and-clamp graph directly between Kroll and the bed as if corroborating an annoying bureaucratic point already settled.

"As I just explained in Docent Mirecki's office, *Herr Hauptsturmführer*," she said tartly in her briskest Luisenstraße ward voice, tapping the top left margin inked on February tenth, "the gatehouse pendulum runs slow and logs post-theater stretcher returns. Here is the operative anesthesia-and-hemostasis sheet signed by Docent Mirecki and myself on the morning of the tenth: *Clamping arteria axillaris sinistra 00:51; reperfusion 01:14*. If gunfire occurred at 00:28 in snow blackout with two shredded left fingers on the driver and a bayonet tourniquet to twist before driving through three closed barriers, twenty minutes barely got him through the gate alive."

Across the white cotton sheet, Ludwig's grey-green eyes met hers for one quarter of a second—stone-still, zero blink, absorbing *00:51* and the entire false timeline whole without moving an eyebrow.

"Twenty-three minutes," Ludwig corrected her irritably, speaking straight over the tin chart at Kroll with splendid, ill-tempered officer's pedantry. "Towarowa railway crossing barrier was frozen shut with nobody in the hut, so Mekelburg had to back fifty meters down a drifted coal alley on one rim and ram through the wooden cattle-fence behind Platynowa. My watch crystal smashed against the dashboard at twenty-nine past twelve when the burst took out our windshield. Look in the washstand drawer if your ballistics boys collect broken Junghans hands, Kroll."

Kroll did not open the washstand drawer. Instead he smiled—a small, thin, polite Saxon smile that didn't touch his sandy eyelashes—and moved seamlessly to trap number two.

"Most clarifying, *Herr Oberstleutnant*. Then the second detail is mere metallurgy. Near the water-tower ditch at dawn our patrol recovered nine-millimeter Parabellum casings of standard Austrian Steyr manufacture—*and* five deformed fragments of black vulcanite and brass matching neither Wehrmacht *Kübelwagen* fittings nor Polish underground Sten guns, alongside a burned civilian Steyr 220 sedan fifty meters down the siding. Did you happen to observe a second vehicle at the embankment before the firing began?"

Every molecule of air in Room Fourteen seemed to freeze solid.

Sylwia remembered the five jagged black crumbs of ebonite she had picked out of Bruno Mekelburg's torn hypothenar muscle at two in the morning on February tenth and dropped straight into the zinc slop-pail with bloody gauze—burned in the furnace boiler by seven a.m.! Wait: *Kübelwagen* Type 82 steering wheels were painted pressed steel spoked with thin hard-rubber coating—or early bakelite—wait: had Kroll's men found fragments from the *other* car, or was Kroll bluffing to see if Schoeler admitted another car was parked right bumper-to-bumper with his?!

In the corner by the radiator, young Mekelburg stared straight ahead at the plaster wall above Kroll's shoulder, motionless as pine wood.

"Snow was blowing horizontally across our headlights at ten meters' visibility," Ludwig said dryly. "If a civilian Steyr was parked fifty meters down an unlit coal siding, whoever owned it didn't blow his horn to introduce himself before shooting out my glass. As for black vulcanite—ask Frau Doktor what she dug out of my driver's hand along with half my steering-wheel rim and radio handset casing. Our FuG field-set transmitter mounted under the dash took three rounds square before the grease caught fire."

Kroll's pale eyes slid sideways to Sylwia. "You recovered radio-handset bakelite from the corporal's palm, Frau Doktor?"

"Five angular fragments of black phenolic resin and one twisted brass contact-pin embedded in the fourth metacarpal web, *Herr Hauptsturmführer*," Sylwia lied without a ripple in her voice, inventing the brass contact-pin out of pure surgical detail so clean you could have mounted it in a glass jar on Philippstraße. "Discarded with amputated necrotic digits into the septic incinerator bucket per typhus-and-gangrene ordinance. Had *Sicherheitspolizei* requested foreign-body preservation on Tuesday morning, I would gladly have saved you the fingers in formalin."

For three silent beats Kroll looked from the crisp, dark-eyed Polish woman in her scrubbed white smock to the gaunt Wehrmacht intelligence officer propped against hospital bolsters. White cotton rasped once—*scritch*—across Kroll's right wrist.

"Formalin will scarcely be necessary, Frau Doktor," Kroll said softly. "You have both been admirably thorough. Pray do not let me tax your suture line another sixty seconds, *Herr Oberstleutnant*. When you return to Saski Square, perhaps you will dine with us on Szucha so we can compare notes on Towarowa night traffic."

"Delighted," Ludwig said flatly. "As soon as I can cut meat with two hands."

Kroll bowed two millimeters from the waist to Ludwig, inclined his sandy head politely to Sylwia, and stepped out into the corridor, where Mirecki stood waiting like a condemned vestryman to escort him back toward the courtyard gates.

Sylwia waited at the wired glass door-panel until Kroll's grey wool back and his two Turkish-cigarette *Rottenführer* disappeared past the vestibule swing doors. Then she turned slowly back into Room Fourteen.

"Step outside by the MP bench for two minutes and smoke, *Gefreiter*," Ludwig told Mekelburg quietly without taking his eyes off Sylwia.

Mekelburg slipped out and pulled the heavy oak door latched behind him.

Silence slammed down inside the white room—hotter and ten times uglier than when the Gestapo had been standing at the bed-foot.

Sylwia walked straight to the left rail, snatching up Ludwig's wrist not gently as on Thursday night, gripping two cold fingers hard over his radial artery while furious colour burned two sharp flags across her cheekbones. Pulse ninety-six—up twelve beats, steady as iron.

"Don't *ever* lean on my chart in front of Aleja Szucha without warning me what lie you've parked in your washstand drawer," she hissed in Berlin German barely above breath, low enough to die three feet short of the door crack. "Twenty-nine past twelve on a smashed watch crystal! What if I had told him one-fifteen upstairs in Mirecki's office?"

"You didn't," Ludwig said just as low, eyes glittering winter-green and wide awake now—every trace of theatrical invalid droop gone clean out of his jaw. "Because you knew forty-eight minutes between Szczęśliwice and Lindleya looked like an elephant in snow, and you never missed basic subtraction in your life. *Brass contact-pin from a FuG handset*, though—that was inspired, Sylwia. We didn't even carry a transmitter in the car."

Her fingers dropped his wrist as if the skin had blistered her. "Then what *did* burn for twenty-eight minutes in the snow while you bled two liters through your collarbone onto floorboards—before your boy drove here?"

He looked straight up at her, close enough that she could smell dry iron salt on his skin under the chlorine dressing and see the tiny pulse kicking below the angle of his shaved jaw. No amusement left now; cold, lethal calculation stripped every civil mask off his bone structure.

"Something Kroll's people murdered two men at a water-tower trying to put their white cotton gloves on," Ludwig said softly. "And something that keeps your Docent Mirecki and half this hospital off Pawiak straw as long as Kroll goes home empty-handed. Who else asked you about Szczęśliwice before four o'clock today, *Kotarska*?"

Sylwia froze inside her white smock—not a flicker in her eyelids, not a millimeter of breath drawn early, yet she saw instantly in the razor-thin narrowing of his eyes that *he didn't even need her to answer*. Nobody innocent knew enough about Szczęśliwice railway ditches to invent FuG radio pins on a half-second cue unless she already knew Kroll had found a burned second car there!

For five seconds they stared straight into each other's eyes across twenty inches of hospital sheet—Polish underground asset and German *Abwehr* counter-espionage chief, neither speaking the fatal word aloud because speaking a word aloud inside four plastered walls turned suspicion into carcass-weight—locked in a mutual stranglehold where either one could hang the other before midnight and neither dared let go first.

"Drink your barley water," Sylwia said through stiff lips, stepping back from the iron rail and hooking the yellow chart back onto its tin peg with a metallic snap. "And if your fever crosses thirty-nine tonight because you played parlor games with a Dresden hangman, you'll sweat it out without aspirin."

"Lock the top casement catch before you go," Ludwig answered quietly against the pillow. "Wind's turning east again."

* * *

At half-past six Mirecki caught her in the ground-floor dispensary unlocking the poison cupboard for Tuesday's surgical morphine tally, shut the frosted glass door behind his heels, and poured forty milliliters of ninety-six-percent rectified spirit straight out of the pharmacy stock carboy into two porcelain ointment-measuring cups without bothering to add cherry syrup.

"Drink," he ordered, shoving one cup across the zinc counter until clear alcohol sloshed over his blotter.

Sylwia tossed half of hers back raw—it burned down her esophagus like kerosene and lit an immediate small coal pit behind her ribs where Wanda's watery turnip broth had already vanished—and left the other half for Mirecki, who emptied his own cup first and eyed hers mournfully before looking away on principle.

"Where on earth did you get *zero-zero-fifty-one clamping time*?" Mirecki whispered, wiping his damp moustache with two trembling fingers. "On Wednesday morning you yourself wrote *circa 01:20* on the preliminary theatre scrap before Bronka burned the bloody paper!"

"And at ten minutes to four today Kroll sat in your chair trying to hang twenty-eight missing minutes round our gatehouse clock so he could arrest Schoeler's corporal for burning criminal evidence and haul Walenty down to Aleja Szucha to count his teeth," Sylwia said calmly, entering three morphine ampoules in purple indelible pencil across the narcotics ledger. "Once Walenty reaches Szucha, panie docencie, how many hours do you think an eighty-kilo gate porter aged sixty-four lasts on wet concrete before he mentions that young men with bandaged thighs sometimes leave Pavilion Three at five in the morning in vegetable carts?"

Mirecki sat down heavily on the tall dispensary stool, shoulders caving inside his threadbare English tweed until his collar stood half an inch away from his scrawny neck. Outside in the courtyard snow had started sifting down again off black roofs, whispering soft against the wired dispensary window.

"You noticed Kroll's wrists?" Mirecki asked dully.

"Neurodermatitis chronica," Sylwia said. "Weeping lichenified plaques under boiled cotton gloves. Scratches whenever somebody tells him a tidy story he doesn't quite believe."

"My cousin Bronisław—the advocate from Radom—was questioned by a Saxon Referent in white cotton gloves last October before they sent the autumn transport to Majdanek," Mirecki said, staring down into his empty porcelain measure. "Bronisław wrote one postcard from Lublin before Christmas saying the polite ones who offer chairs never shout; they merely cross out three lines in pencil after you leave the room, and next Tuesday the truck stops at your door." He looked up sharply through his pince-nez. "Did Schoeler confirm your 00:51?"

"Down to the smashed watch crystal in his washstand drawer."

Instead of looking relieved, Mirecki looked sicker than ever. "Which means an *Abwehr* lieutenant-colonel lied in his teeth to the Security Police inside my ward, using your clinical chart as an alibi! Sylwia, for the love of Christ—get that wound closed or clean enough to sign out by Friday! Let Saski Square and Aleja Szucha strangle each other inside the Brühl Hotel where Polish doctors don't have to hold the basin!"

"Look at his temperature sheet tomorrow morning if you think we're signing him out Friday," Sylwia said, corking the spirit carboy tight and locking the narcotics cupboard with a double turn of the heavy iron ward-key. "Deep pectoral pockets full of street grit don't ask permission from Aleja Szucha before they suppurate. Good night, panie docencie. Don't drink out of the methanol jar by mistake—Bronka switched the shelves."

She walked back toward Koszykowa before the eight o'clock curfew bell with Stefan's fifteen hundred crisp *Volksliste* złoty folded inside her bag like greasy butcher's paper and the remaining half-vial of dead Volksdeutscher uremic insulin wrapped in flannel inside her inner pocket where snow-cold kept it stable without freezing it solid.

Along Nowogrodzka the wind off Ochota carried coal smoke mixed with burnt horse-hoof from the glue vat down by Towarowa. At the corner of Emilii Plater two boys no older than Staś squatted over an iron sewer grate trying to hook dropped coke cinders up through the bars with bent wire coat-hangers before a Blue Policeman in a dark navy greatcoat kicked their tin bucket clattering across the slush and sent them scattering like sparrows.

Back up four dark flights at Koszykowa 33, Sylwia wedged the precious half-vial of insulin between the outer and inner window-sashes behind two packed snowballs—nature's two-degree icebox until March—then boiled three potatoes in their skins over a handful of compressed peat dust and ate them salted straight off the point of a kitchen knife standing by the stove.

Across the room on her washstand lay her pawnshop ebony Collin dissecting box from Philippstraße, nineteen-twenty-eight, brass corner-plates greened with fifteen winters of carbolic steam. Without intending to open it, she unlatched the little brass hook with her thumb.

Six slots in faded purple velvet: four worn scalpel handles honed thin down the belly like willow leaves, one silver probe, one pair of fine Graefe iris forceps whose serrated tips still closed true on a single human eyelash.

*Who else asked you about Szczęśliwice before four o'clock today, Kotarska?*

Ten years had turned Ludwig Schoeler from a brilliant, penniless twenty-three-year-old bastard fighting dockers under Schöneberg elevated rails into a thirty-four-year-old Wehrmacht intelligence hunter who spoke guttural Warsaw Polish, burned briefcases in snowy railway ditches while bleeding to death, and saw clean through her cover inside four sentences without moving his head off a hospital pillow. Had he wanted her dead or broken on Aleja Szucha's cellar tiles, one dry correction in front of Reinhart Kroll at four-fifteen today—*Frau Doktor must be misreading her watch, Hauptsturmführer; my car didn't reach Lindleya until past one*—would have put Kroll's white cotton gloves round her throat before dark while clearing Ludwig himself of colluding with Polish staff.

He hadn't done it. Not out of mercy—mercy didn't live inside fifty miles of Ludwig Schoeler's ribs—because he needed Lindleya's four quiet walls around him while his shoulder healed, and because if anybody in Warsaw was going to hold a knife over Sylwia Kotarska's neck or let her go, his insufferable Berlin arrogance would never hand the privilege to a Saxon lawyer in cotton gloves.

Sylwia snapped the ebony lid shut, blew out her tallow stub, and lay down fully dressed under two coats on Goldfeder's narrow daybed, listening to sleet hiss against blackout paper while four blocks north on Lindleya twenty-four stitches of her black silk pulsed eighty-eight times a minute in the dark.
