# The Sentence on Filtrowa

*Warsaw, 19–20 August 1943*

Wanda had buttoned her blue polka-dot crêpe dress one hole crooked from the collar all the way to the waist, so the left hem drooped two inches lower than the right above dusty white kid shoes put on without stockings, and she smelled from six feet off of sour bile and *4711* lavender water splashed frantically over unwashed neck-skin.

At ten minutes past six on Thursday morning—when night orderlies were still sloshing grey lysol suds across Pavilion Three's vestibule tiles and Sylwia had just come down from her third-floor dormer cubby buttoning a fresh ward smock over dark circles—Wanda stumbled straight past old Walenty's desk without answering his *Dzień dobry*, spotted Sylwia by the staircase arch, and grabbed both her wrists with icy, clawing fingers whose pink nail-varnish was chipped half off.

"Not in the hall," Sylwia said instantly, pulling her older half-sister sideways through the narrow pine door of the ground-floor splint-and-linen closet before any probationer nurse or early German courier saw Kazimierz Kotarski's elder daughter shaking like a wet dog.

Before Sylwia even shot the inner bolt, Wanda's plump knees folded clean out from under her; she went down hard onto the scrubbed boards among stacked canvas stretcher-slings, wrapping both bare white arms tight round Sylwia's knees and pressing her damp, powder-streaked cheek flat against Sylwia's white hospital skirt while dry hiccuping sobs tore out of her chest!

"Get off the floor, Wanda," Sylwia said sharply, hooking both hands under Wanda's soft perspiring armpits and hauling sixty-eight kilos of dead sister-weight bodily up onto an upturned crate of plaster bandages. "Stop choking before somebody hears through the transom. Is it Staś? Did his morning sugar crash?"

"Not Staś—*Stefan*!" Wanda gasped, fumbling wildly inside her beaded handbag with trembling thumbs until she tore the rayon lining and pulled out a single four-inch square of thin grey manifold tissue folded twice. "Under the front brass mat at five-thirty when the milk-woman knocked—Stefan went out in his dressing-gown for the bottle, read two lines in the hall, and threw up his coffee all over the parquet! Oh, Mother of Mercy, Sylwia, they're going to shoot him on the stairs in front of Staś—!"

Sylwia smoothed the damp grey manifold square flat across her palm beneath the single bare forty-watt closet bulb.

Typed cleanly through fresh purple carbon without a single spelling error, headed at top left with the crisp hand-stamped black anchor-P emblem (*Kotwica*) of Underground Civil Resistance:

*KIEROWNICTWO WALKI PODZIEMNEJ — OKRĘG WARSZAWA*
*Do: Inż. Stefana Bielskiego, ul. Filtrowa 68 (DVL Gr. III).*
*Stwierdzono współudział w przekazywaniu wykazów robotników Warsztatów Tramwajowych Wola do obozu przejściowego przy ul. Skaryszewskiej w okresie IV–VII 1943 r. Wzywa się niniejszym do natychmiastowego zaprzestania czynności administracyjnych na szkodę Narodu Polskiego w terminie 48 godzin. Niezastosowanie się pociągnie wykonanie wyroku śmierci.*

*Forty-eight hours to cease administrative acts harmful to the Polish Nation; non-compliance brings execution of sentence of death.*

Sylwia folded the grey square once along its crease, feeling zero surprise—only cold, grim recognition of those two silent forty-year-old Wola toolmakers leaning against Turret Lathe Three behind Stefan's sweaty white dust-coat on the fourth of July. Six weeks it had taken Wola's shop-floor cell to verify the Majdanek death postcards of the four April absentees against Stefan's counter-signed payroll sheets, send the file upstairs to Civil Resistance judges, and slip a polite forty-eight-hour final warning under Filtrowa's brass mat instead of shooting him blind through his coat on Monday morning.

"He got off lucky with a warning sheet," Sylwia said coldly, looking down at Wanda dabbing streaming china-blue eyes with a crumpled lace handkerchief. "Real Gestapo informers don't get forty-eight hours' notice—they get nine-millimeter lead in Mokotów courtyards before breakfast."

Wanda stared up at her in horrified disbelief, round mouth trembling open. "Lucky?! Sylwia, your own sister's husband! Stefan never hurt a fly in his life—Direktor Krehl puts thirty payroll sheets in front of him at five p.m. Fridays and screams about sabotage if Stefan doesn't initial the bottom margin! If Stefan walks into Krehl's office tomorrow morning and resigns his post without medical cause, Krehl revokes our *Volksliste* card inside two hours, hands Stefan straight to Skaryszewska himself for economic sabotage, and throws Staś and me onto the street without coal or rations! You *know* those underground boys—everyone on Filtrowa knows doctors at *Dzieciątka Jezus* hide shot boys in cellars! Go tell their commander today that Stefan only signed under German duress and keeps our child alive!"

Every word Wanda cried in that cramped linen closet smelled of turnip soup boiled with collaborator's goose-necks and closed shutters while black human ash blew onto Easter lace.

Sylwia braced one raw palm against the shelf beside Wanda's ear and leaned down until her dark eyes sat eight inches from her sister's tear-swollen face.

"Listen to me carefully, Wanda, and stop blubbering about what everyone on Filtrowa knows before you hang twenty innocent nurses," Sylwia said in a voice quiet enough to freeze boiling fat. "First: I command zero underground courts, and even if I drank tea with General Komorowski every Tuesday, nobody on earth cancels a Wola shop-floor warning while four Polish fitters' widows hold Majdanek ash-cards bearing Stefan's payroll initials. Second: if Stefan keeps sitting at that desk past Saturday morning—or worse, asks German police for armed guards—two nineteen-year-old boys in railway caps will shoot him through the stomach on Filtrowa's steps next week while Staś carries his schoolbag two paces behind him."

Wanda clapped both hands over her mouth, letting out a muffled animal keen behind her rings.

"Does Stefan know you came here?" Sylwia demanded.

"No—he locked himself in his study telephoning some *Volksdeutsche* lawyer cousin from Łódź about applying for emergency railway transfer to Breslau!"

*Breslau.* There sat the true measure of Stefan Bielski's spine: inside forty minutes of reading an underground warning, he wasn't figuring out how to stop signing Polish workers onto death lorries—he was trying to bolt four hundred kilometers west into the Reich, dragging nine-year-old diabetic Staś out of reach of Sylwia's syringes into bombed German cities, or leaving wife and sick boy behind to face empty cupboards!

"Go straight home before he telephones Direktor Krehl and signs his own death warrant," Sylwia ordered, pulling Wanda upright off the plaster crate and buttoning her sister's crooked polka-dot dress straight from neck to waist with swift, pitiless surgeon's fingers as if dressing a six-year-old child. "Unplug your hall telephone wire at the baseboard junction—pull one copper screw loose with nail-scissors and tell Stefan the street line's dead—and keep him inside Number Sixty-Eight until noon. I'll come after morning amputations with my bag."

* * *

At twelve-twenty Filtrowa baked white and shadeless under August noon glare; tar smelled thick along tram-rails, and inside Number Sixty-Eight every heavy plush curtain had been drawn tight across the bay windows by Stefan lest snipers sit perched in linden trees across the street.

In the stifling green gloom of his mahogany study, Stefan Bielski sat behind his desk with an unlit prewar *Havana* cigar chewed wet and shredded at the butt between his teeth, an open leather Gladstone valise half-stuffed with starched shirts on the sofa, and a tiny nickel-plated 6.35-millimeter ladies' Browning vest-pistol—some forgotten nineteen-thirty relic incapable of stopping an angry terrier at ten paces—lying absurdly beside his blotter!

Out in the hallway behind the closed frosted-glass sliding doors of the dining room, Sylwia had glimpsed a small dark-haired shadow sitting motionless on a plush chair beside the rubber plant as she walked in: nine-year-old Staś, pretending to paste postage stamps into an album while straining both ears through cracked oak veneer to hear why Mama had vomited at six and Papa held a toy pistol in the dark.

"Unplugged!" Stefan barked hoarsely the instant Sylwia shut the study door behind her, gray-jowled face glistening oily under the shaded desk-lamp, waistcoat hanging loose because inside six hours terror had already shrunk his belly two inches. "Whole block's telephone exchange cut since seven-thirty—bandits cutting wires before an attack, I tell you! If Wanda hadn't hidden my hat I'd have walked straight down to the *Schutzpolizei* post at Narutowicz Square by nine and demanded two gendarmes on our gate!"

Sylwia set her black leather surgical bag calmly down on the corner of his desk right beside the ridiculous nickel vest-pistol, picked up the tiny gun by two fingers, clicked its safety on—it had been lying cocked off-safe pointed straight at Stefan's own groin!—and dropped it contemptuously into his pen-tray.

"Ask Narutowicz *Schutzpolizei* for two gendarmes on a *Volksliste Three* probationer's gate," Sylwia said conversationally, "and first they laugh in your face because German police don't guard Silesian half-breeds round the clock; second, Wola's liquidation team shoots you climbing onto a tram three mornings later instead of at home—plus adds Wanda to the collaborator list for calling German rifles into a Polish stairwell."

"Then Breslau!" Stefan slammed a damp palm onto the half-packed leather valise. "Krehl's head office in Breslau offered two experienced rolling-stock engineers permanent Reich municipal transfer last month—housing guaranteed, Reich fat cards, German clinic insulin for Staś—"

"Group Three *Volksliste* poles transferred into the Reich since Stalingrad don't sit behind desks in Breslau for six weeks before Wehrmacht replacement boards reclassify men under forty-five *kv*—fit for Eastern Front infantry trenches," Sylwia cut across him like cold wire. "Within ninety days you're freezing outside Vitebsk in grey wool while Wanda cleans barracks latrines in Lower Silesia and Staś dies of diabetic ketoacidosis the first week Allied bombers hit Breslau's pharmacy depot."

Stefan sank heavily down into his swivel chair, chewing the cold wet shred of cigar leaf until brown juice stained his lip. All bluster collapsed out of his fat shoulders at once, leaving bare forty-two-year-old terror staring up at the sister-in-law he had patronized over goose-neck soup since forty-one.

"Then what in Christ's name am I supposed to do?!" he whispered raggedly. "Walk into Krehl's shed tomorrow saying *I quit*—and go to Auschwitz by Friday night for work-refusal?!"

Sylwia unlatched her black Gladstone bag and took out two small objects she had compounded down in Pavilion Three's pharmacy mortar at eleven-thirty while Bronka watched the door:

Two fat white gelatin veterinary-size cachet capsules packed solid with **one hundred and eighty milligrams each of pure, dry crystalline picric acid**—trinitrophenol, peeled straight off Halina's histology fixing shelf and Bronka's burn-ointment stock—alongside one stamped, official pale-green *Sanitätsabteilung Warschau* hospital quarantine requisition bearing **Stabsarzt Dr. Kurt Vesper's** genuine round violet eagle seal and signature from July twenty-fourth!

She laid both white capsules on a clean prescription blank under Stefan's nose.

"Swallow one capsule right now with warm water, and the second at midnight," Sylwia said calmly.

Stefan stared down at the two big white capsules as if she had laid two live hornets on his blotter. "What's inside them?"

"Three hundred and sixty milligrams total of crystalline picric acid," Sylwia said without softening a syllable. "Old nineteen-sixteen Austrian army trick from the Isonzo front—we autopsied two cases at Charité when reservists tried dodging winter maneuvers. Inside eight hours it binds reversibly to serum albumin and skin elastin, turning the whites of your eyes, every square inch of your skin from scalp to toenails, and every drop of your urine brilliant canary-chrome yellow—completely indistinguishable on bedside inspection from severe acute epidemic *icterus catarrhalis* or Weil's spirochetal jaundice."

Stefan's jaw dropped slack. "Poison?!"

"At two grams picric acid destroys red cells and kidneys; at zero point three-six grams it gives you twelve hours of authentic stomach cramps, brisk green diarrhea, mild headache, and three weeks of glorious yellow skin without damaging a single liver lobule," Sylwia said crisply. "Tomorrow morning at eight o'clock, when your eyeballs look like two boiled egg-yolks, an infectious-disease ambulance summoned on *Stabsarzt* Vesper's own Wehrmacht sanitary warrant collects you publicly from Filtrowa under diagnosis *Hepatitis epidemica contagiosa gravis — Verdacht auf Morbus Weilii*. Direktor Krehl is terrified out of his wits of infectious diseases since July's typhus scare—the second Vesper's stamped certificate lands on his desk tomorrow noon certifying minimum six-to-eight weeks' contagious isolation and permanent disqualification from crowded workshop floors due to hepatic insufficiency, Krehl strikes you off active Wola personnel rolls himself and appoints a German foreman while leaving your *Ostbahn* sick-pay card intact for sixty days!"

Silence hung thick inside the dark green room except for the brass pendulum clock ticking steady quarters in the hall.

"And—and the people who put that grey paper under my mat?" Stefan whispered, wiping cold sweat off his forehead.

"By two o'clock tomorrow afternoon the Wola shop-committee sees Vesper's copy confirming Inżynier Bielski has been hauled away yellow as saffron into contagious isolation and permanently replaced at the sheds," Sylwia said, voice dropping flat and lethal as slate. "Recover conveniently in October into some quiet warehouse coal-tally clerkship where not one single Polish workman's name ever crosses your pen again, Stefan—because I have two capsules left in that bottle, and if you ever set foot inside Wola's sheds or touch another absentee list while this war lasts, I won't bring picric acid next time. Swallow."

She poured half a tumbler of tepid carafe water and pushed it across the mahogany.

For five seconds Stefan stared at the huge white gelatin capsule—terrified half to death that his icy-eyed sister-in-law might genuinely have packed cyanide or corrosive sublimate inside to make Wanda a respectable widow!—then remembered nine-millimeter bullets on front stairs, shut both eyes tight, shoved Capsule One onto the back of his furry tongue, and gulped half the tumbler down with a loud, convulsive *glug* that made his Adam's apple bounce like a cork.

Out in the sunny hallway five minutes later, ignoring Wanda's tearful attempts to kiss her hands by the coat-rack, Sylwia slid open the frosted-glass dining-room door three inches.

Nine-year-old Staś sat bolt upright beside his stamp album, pale knuckles locked white round his knees.

"Papa has bad liver-jaundice coming on from bad workshop water, Stasiu," Sylwia told him quietly, winking zero eyes—speaking straight dignified clinic cover a clever nine-year-old could repeat to schoolmates without shame while knowing inside his calm dark Kotarski bones that Aunt Sylwia had just nailed the roof back onto the house. "Ambulance takes him to Pavilion One for rest tomorrow morning. Check your afternoon urine sugar twice on Fridays while Mama fusses, and save me the Brazilian airmail stamps."

"Yes, Ciocia," Staś said steadily, shoulders dropping two full inches out of his ears as he let go his knees.

* * *

By nine o'clock Friday morning Stefan Bielski looked as if someone had dipped him up to the hair-roots into a vat of Easter turmeric dye—sclerae glaring bright chrome-lemon, plump jowls stained deep saffron-gold down into his striped pajama collar, groaning with completely genuine abdominal misery over an enamel slop-basin inside Pavilion One's ground-floor contagious jaundice cubicle.

Direktor Krehl, however, had not stayed fifty years in Silesian railway machine-shops by trusting Polish hospital paper sight unseen.

At half-past ten Walenty hurried across the sunny courtyard holding his cap to warn Sylwia that **Oberwerkmeister Hübner**—Krehl's bull-necked German shop superintendent from Wola, accompanied by a skeptical *Ostbahn* personnel clerk carrying absentee disciplinary forms—had just marched into Pavilion One's admissions vestibule demanding to see "this overnight liver-inflammation of Herr Ingenieur Bielski with my own two eyes before I report malingering to Railway Security!"

Sylwia didn't send a junior intern; buttoning a stiff, unbleached canvas contagious-isolation smock up to her chin and tying a carbolic-reeking gauze mask round her hair, she walked briskly out into Pavilion One's tiled vestibule carrying a freshly filled glass urinal flask glowing luminescent greenish-canary in the morning sun!

"Herr Oberwerkmeister Hübner?" Sylwia stopped three paces upwind of the red-faced German shop-boss, holding out a dripping pair of rubberized isolation overshoes and a wet carbolic mask by iron tongs. "Splendid—kindly step into rubber galoshes and sign our mandatory *Morbus Weilii* contagious-exposure waiver before entering Cubicle Three. Since Tuesday morning we have lost two municipal sewer-orderlies from Wola to acute spirochetal hemorrhagic jaundice transmitted through workshop rat-urine droplets, and *Stabsarzt* Doktor Vesper requires complete residential contact-addresses for fourteen-day military quarantine of anyone breathing within two meters of an active shed case."

Hübner's thick red hand froze halfway toward the rubber overshoes; behind him the *Ostbahn* clerk backed hastily into a potted fern.

"Rat-urine hemorrhagic—two dead since Tuesday?!" Hübner barked, squinting distrustfully past Sylwia's shoulder down the dim green-painted corridor toward Cubicle Three's small wired-glass observation pane. "Look through the glass only—five seconds from the corridor!"

"By all means—hold your breath near the transom," Sylwia invited smoothly, stepping aside so Hübner craned his thick neck twelve inches from the sealed glass pane just as Bronka inside Cubicle Three—timed down to the second on Sylwia's heel-click!—propped poor groaning Stefan upright off damp pillows into full south window-glare!

Even through thick wired glass Stefan looked horrifying: bright chrome-yellow eyeballs rolling wretchedly inside sunken saffron sockets, greenish sweat beading across a turmeric forehead before he doubled forward retching half a pint of bitter yellow water noisily into Bronka's basin!

Hübner recoiled three full paces backward off the glass as if Stefan had spat live cobras through the putty, clapping a checkered handkerchief tight over nose and mouth.

"God in heaven—he's yellower than brass!" Hübner wheezed through cotton, snatching his fountain pen out of his breast pocket and slapping the *Ostbahn* clerk's clipboard flat against his own thigh twenty yards out in open courtyard sunshine to scrawl **Dauernd abgelöst wegen Seuchengefahr — Ersatzmann sofort eingesetzt** (*Permanently relieved due to epidemic hazard — replacement assigned immediately*) across all three copies of Vesper's violet-stamped certificate before bolting out Lindleya's gate to gargle schnapps!

At quarter past two that afternoon, Sylwia got off a Number Nine tram on Młynarska in scorching white dust and stepped inside Kubiak's dark, fly-buzzing corner beer-bar opposite the tramway repair gates, ordering one small glass of sour watery *piwo* at the zinc counter two stools down from the older of the two blue-capped Wola turret-lathe toolmakers eating dry bread and lard on his shift break.

She didn't look at him, didn't speak a code-word, didn't know his name from Adam.

Taking half a sip of warm beer, she laid the carbon third-copy of Pavilion One's stamped municipal infectious admission slip—*Bielski, Stefan, Inż. — Icterus infectiosus gravis, Isolierstation I, dauernd dienstunfähig für Werkstättenbetrieb Wola*—face-up beside the salt-cellar while tying her handbag strap, left it lying accidentally behind on dry zinc when she turned out into the glare, and saw reflected in the fly-spotted mirror behind vodka bottles one scarred lathe-turner's thumb slide smoothly sideways over the paper before her shoe cleared the doorsill.

Nobody died on Filtrowa's steps this week.

Yet when she climbed Service Stair B at Królewska Sixteen three hours later at five-ten—still carrying two faint canary-yellow pinpricks of picric acid stain deeply fixed into the horny cuticle of her right index finger where mortar dust had touched wet skin yesterday—the quiet air inside *Wohnung 4* felt tauter than violin wire before her bag touched wood.

Mekelburg wasn't polishing boots today; the boy sat cleaning the disassembled bolt and extractor spring of his Walther P38 AND a heavy Sauer rifle on oiled canvas across the kitchen table—field-kit maintenance, not garrison spit-and-polish!

Inside the study Ludwig wore full buttoned field-grey tunic even in twenty-nine-degree August heat, standing over an open dispatch-cipher box beside the cold porcelain stove while burning four thick sheaves of Tirpitzufer blue flimsies so fast grey smoke curled lazily past the flue damper.

When the double doors clicked shut behind Sylwia, he walked straight across the parquet, took her raw right hand in his, glanced once with faint dark humor at the two indestructible bright-yellow picric dots beside her nail—

"Austrian nineteen-sixteen jaundice?" he guessed instantly inside half a beat, before all humor vanished clean off his bone structure. "Good. Keep your sister's household indoors after dusk next month. Have you still got that altered black silk dress from your mother's trunk?"

Sylwia stared up at him, every nerve tightening cold. Wait: when had she ever mentioned Helena's altered nineteen-twenty-nine black silk concert dress? Once—lying half-asleep in June's air-raid dark when he asked what four things fit inside two suitcases leaving Berlin!

"Why would I wear silk in Warsaw?" she asked sharply.

"Because twenty minutes ago *SS-Hauptsturmführer* Reinhart Kroll delivered gilded pasteboard invitations through General Stahel's protocol office for next Sunday evening's quarterly German-and-Municipal Epidemic Medical Reception at the *Palais Brühl* Casino annex," Ludwig said quietly, releasing her hand to pick up a stiff cream card embossed with the Generalgouvernement eagle off Judge Silberstein's blotter. "Mandatory attendance for Stabsarzt Vesper, Acting Surgical Chief *Dr. med. Sylwia Kotarska* representing *Dzieciątka Jezus* in Docent Mirecki's illness, and *Abwehrstelle* liaison officers."

Cold slid straight down Sylwia's spine despite August sweat.

"Oster's deputy Dohnanyi has been sitting in a Wehrmacht remand cell in Berlin since spring while Gestapo accountants pick Tirpitzufer's safe-locks open one drawer at a time," Ludwig said very low, stepping close enough that his silver-piped tunic shoulder brushed hers while his winter-green eyes cut straight down to rock-bottom truth. "And Frame Six of that Berlin nineteen-thirty-three file you lifted out of Vesper's folder on Bastille Day—"

Sylwia's breath stopped cold in her throat! *He knew she had lifted the duplicate microfilm strip off Vesper's desk five weeks ago—and hadn't said one single word at Świder while waiting for her to speak first!*

"—reaches Kroll's Warsaw desk in physical original paper by courier next week," Ludwig finished without blinking, ignoring her shock for later because combat survival came first. "Sunday night at the Brühl Casino, Reinhart Kroll intends sitting both of us under one chandelier with white cotton gloves and three glasses of Rhine wine to watch whose eyelids twitch when Berlin nineteen-thirty-three enters polite conversation. Miss that supper, and Kroll signs detention warrants Monday morning. Walk in beside Vesper wearing black silk, treat me like an obnoxious Prussian acquaintance you endured five years on Luisenstraße and six weeks in a hospital bed—and let Kroll break his teeth on bare ice."

For five silent seconds Sylwia stared straight into his cool, sleepless eyes—realizing he had known all through that golden afternoon under Świder pines that she held March eighth nineteen-thirty-three in her pocket, and had still laid himself bare under her hands without asking quarter.

"Eight o'clock Sunday?" she asked evenly, chin lifting sharp as scalpel steel.

"Eight-thirty," Ludwig said softly. "Don't wear perfume—and leave your knives at home; Brühl sentries search handbags."
