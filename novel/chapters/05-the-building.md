# Five

## The Building

They put her in a room on the ground floor with a table and six chairs and a portrait of the Cardinal Primate, and a young priest brought a glass of water she did not touch.

"Please sit down, Father."

"I'll stand, thank you." He was looking at her with a kind of careful astonishment, as though she were a piece of furniture that had been reported lost. "Zosia."

"Comrade Director," she said. "Or Pani Dyrektor, if you prefer. In this building."

"Of course." He inclined his head. "Pani Dyrektor."

"Sit down."

He sat. He folded his hands on the table. He was thirty-four and looked it, and he was tired, and there was a small blue mark at the corner of his mouth where he had cut himself shaving. His cassock was good wool and had been turned once at the cuffs. His shoes were polished and had been resoled at the toe.

"You're in the Office," he said.

"I am deputy director."

"I know. I have known for two years." He said it without any particular weight and watched what it did to her. "The grant for the church at Brzozówka. It came through your office in 1956, and again in 1957 for the roof. Your signature is on the second one. I have looked at it a good many times."

She had known this and had arranged not to know it. The application had come up through the ordinary channel, from the Lublin voivodeship office, with a recommendation, and she had read it and had seen the parish name and had signed it because there was no reason not to sign it, and because to refuse to sign it would have been a fact that someone might notice, and because a roof is a roof.

"You might have written," she said.

"To what address?"

There was a knock and the young priest put his head in and said that Monsignor Lubieński was waiting, and Tomasz said, without looking away from her, "Tell the monsignor that I am with the Office and will come when I am finished," and the door closed.

"The nomination," Zofia said. She opened her folder. She had brought the summary, not the file, and the summary was four pages and she knew it by heart. "His Excellency Bishop Krynicki has submitted a terna to the Council of State, through this Office, in accordance with the understanding of 1956. The Office is required to give its opinion within thirty days. It is my task to prepare that opinion. In order to do so I am directed to speak with the candidate."

"Directed by whom?"

"By my director."

"And your director is directed by whom?"

"The Presidium."

"Which is directed by the Politburo, which is directed by Moscow, which is directed by—" He stopped himself, and made a small apologetic gesture with one hand. "I'm sorry. That was a speech. I have been giving a great many speeches lately, and I find they come more easily than they used to, which frightens me."

"It's a chain of authority," Zofia said. "Every institution has one. Yours has one. I have read your file, so I know about yours."

His eyes were steady. "Then you know that I have been obedient, and that obedience has cost me, and that I am not inclined to be lectured about chains."

She closed the folder. "Father. I am going to speak to you as one official to another, and then I am going to speak to you as myself, and I would be grateful if you would let me do the first before the second."

He sat back. "Go on."

"The Office has no objection in principle to your nomination." She heard her own voice, flat and competent, and was relieved by it. "The Bishop's choice is the Bishop's choice. But this Office has a legitimate interest in the public order, and there are three matters on which it requires assurances before it will transmit a favourable opinion."

"Three."

"First. The Millennium preparations. The episcopate has announced a program of pastoral work leading to 1966. This Office has no wish to interfere with prayer. It does have an interest in ensuring that the program is not used for political ends, and in particular that no bishop is deployed in a manner that will bring the Church into conflict with the state. The Curia will be asked to give a written undertaking that the candidate will take no part in the Millennium program without the prior knowledge of this Office."

He did not move.

"Second. The question of religious instruction in the schools. You have spoken on this subject in Lublin, twice, in April and in June, and both sermons were reported. You are aware that the matter is under review. The Office requires an undertaking that the candidate will not speak publicly on the question of catechism in the schools while the review is pending."

"And third?"

Zofia looked at the folder. "Third. The candidate will make himself available for an informative conversation with the fourth department of the Security Service, at a time and place of its choosing, and will confirm in writing that the conversation took place and that he has no matters to conceal."

The room was quiet. Somewhere in the building a door banged and a woman laughed, and the laugh went up the stairwell and faded.

"Ah," said Tomasz.

"That is the position."

"That is not a position. That is a purchase." He said it mildly. "The first is a gag. The second is a gag. The third is a rope." He leaned forward. "Pani Dyrektor. Let us be accurate with each other, since we are being official. In 1952 I signed a declaration in the Mokotów prison, and I named two men, and one of them lost his post and one of them was in Rakowiecka for a year. I have spent seven years since then being a priest, which is what I am, and doing it as well as I know how, and I have not signed anything else. I have not informed. I have not reported. Not once. And now you come to me with a paper that says: confirm in writing that you have no matters to conceal." He shook his head slowly. "Do you know what that paper is? It is the beginning. That is all. The first one is always a formality. The second one is a name. The third one is a friend."

"Nobody is asking you for names."

"Not yet."

"Father—"

"You said you would speak to me as yourself," he said. "I would like that now. Because I have not seen you since I was twenty and you were seventeen, and in that time I have thought about you every day, and I would like to know what happened to you, and instead you are reading me a list." His voice had not risen, but something in it had changed, and the change was worse than shouting. "What happened to you, Zosia?"

She stood up. She went to the window, which looked out on the yard and the wall and a plane tree with three leaves left on it, and she stood there with her back to him and her arms folded.

"The village was burned," she said. "My mother and my brother were killed. My father was taken to Lublin and then to Majdanek and he came out in August and he was never the same. You were taken by the NKVD and I did not know for three years whether you were alive. I was seventeen years old and I had nothing and no one and a man came with a lorry and told me that the future was a real place and that I could help build it. So I did. I have been building it for fourteen years. Whatever you think of the building, I helped put it up, and I am not ashamed of it, and I am not going to be lectured about it by a priest who spent the war in the forest and the peace in a seminary."

She turned round.

He was looking at her with an expression she could not read at all.

"That is a good answer," he said. "It is almost true. It is true in the way that a wall is true — the bricks are real, and the wall is in the wrong place." He stood. "I'll take the first two."

"What?"

"The first two conditions. I will give the Curia my word that I will not use the Millennium for politics, and I will not speak on the schools while the review is pending. I will keep my word; I always keep my word; it is my only accomplishment. The third I will not do." He picked up his hat from the table. "And I would like you to write that in your opinion. Write: the candidate declines. He declines in the following words. 'I have signed one paper in my life and I will not sign another.' You may quote me."

"That will end your nomination."

"Then it will end." He put on his hat and took it off again, a habit of his father's. "Pani Dyrektor. May I say one more thing, not as a candidate?"

"Yes."

"You have read my file. I have read yours." He said it evenly. "Not the Security Service file. The other one. The one that everybody in this country carries, the one that is written on their faces. I have been looking at yours for six years, from a distance, in the newspapers, at the Congress of the Polish-Soviet Friendship Society in 1955, where you sat in the fourth row." He paused. "You are not a happy woman."

"Don't."

"I am not trying to wound you."

"You are doing it very well for a man who isn't trying."

"I know," he said. "I'm sorry."

They stood there. The plane tree moved in the wind, and one of the three leaves came off it and went down the length of the window and out of sight.

"They will send me again," she said. "To ask."

"I know."

"And you will refuse again."

"I will refuse again." He turned to go, and then stopped with his hand on the door. "Zosia. The church at Brzozówka. The roof. Did you know it was for the roof?"

"Yes."

"Thank you for the roof."

"Go away, Tomasz."

He went. She stood at the window for a long time, and then she sat down at the table and wrote, in her small careful hand, on the back of the summary: *Declines third condition. Quotes: I have signed one paper in my life and I will not sign another. Advises opinion will end nomination. — 4:20 p.m.* And then she sat looking at it, and then she tore it into four pieces and put them in her handbag, because Hanna would type it and Hanna would file it and Wrona would read it before the ink was dry.

She left the office at seven. She did not take the car. She walked up Bagatela to the corner and then, without deciding to, turned into the park.

Łazienki in November is a place for people who have nowhere to be. The trees were bare and the water was the colour of lead, and there were three old men on a bench with a newspaper between them, and a woman with a pram, and a boy throwing stones at the ducks, and a couple standing close together under the monument to Chopin with their backs to the path.

She walked down to the water and stood at the rail.

"Pani Dyrektor."

She turned. He was five paces behind her, hat in his hand, and he had been following her, or he had come here too, and she found that she did not care which.

"You followed me."

"I followed you," he said. "I am sorry. It was not dignified. I stood at the gate for ten minutes deciding, and then I decided badly."

"You should go home, Father."

"I have a room at the seminary and a bed and a lamp and a book, and I have been going home to it for five years, and I find tonight that I do not want to." He came and stood beside her at the rail, at a proper distance, and looked at the water. "May I walk with you? Only walk. I will not ask you anything."

"All right."

They walked. Past the water, up the avenue, under the bare trees, with the lamps just coming on and the gravel loud under their feet. It was cold enough to see breath. He put his hat on, then took it off, then put it on again.

"Ostashkov," he said at last. "You will want to know. Everyone wants to know, and no one asks, and I have got into the habit of telling people so that they do not have to ask."

"You don't have to."

"It's easier than not telling." He put his hands in his pockets. "It was a monastery, before. On an island in the Seliger, a wall and a church and a lot of buildings, and they put us in the buildings. There were about five thousand of us in the end, Poles, mostly officers, but also a good many boys like me, and some civilians. We had a roll call twice a day in all weathers. The food was a bowl of soup and two hundred grams of bread, and in the winter the bread came frozen and you thawed it under your arm. The worst was the first winter, forty-four to forty-five. I was twenty. I had never been ill in my life." He paused. "I got scurvy. My gums bled and my teeth loosened, and I could not eat the bread because my mouth was sores, and a man from Poznań taught me to chew pine needles, which was vile, and which saved me, and which is why I can tell you the taste of a pine needle in the same way I can tell you the taste of an apple."

They came to the Belweder and turned back. The guards at the gate watched them go by without interest.

"There was a priest in the camp," he said. "A Jesuit from Kraków, Father Bąk, and he was sixty and he did not last the first winter, but before he died he heard confessions in the latrine, because it was the only place where a man could speak without being heard, and I served his Mass on a suitcase with a tin of sardines for a paten. He had a stole that he kept inside his shirt, and once a week he put it on and we stood around him in the cold and he said the words, and it was illegal, and if they had found it he would have been shot, and he did it anyway, and it was the only real thing that happened to me in three years." He glanced at her. "I have never told anyone that."

"Why me?"

"Because you are the only person I know who will not be impressed by it," he said.

She stopped walking. They were on the path by the water again, and the lamps were reflected in it in long broken lines.

"I wrote about you," she said.

He was silent.

"In 1949. There was a commission, a verification commission, for the voivodeship committee, and they asked me about my wartime contacts, and they had a list of names, and I was asked about everyone I had known in the Home Army. And I told them the truth, which was that I had known you, and that you had been in the unit, and I said—" She stopped. "I said that you were clever and that you should not be allowed to teach young people."

He looked at the water.

"It was in the file," she said. "It went with you to Mokotów in 1951. That is what I do now, Tomasz. I write sentences that go into files, and men read them in rooms, and other men go and knock on doors. I have been doing it for nine years, and I am very good at it, and I have never once been asked to look at the face of the man in the file until today."

He was quiet for so long that she began to be afraid.

"Say something," she said.

"When they showed me the deposition," he said, "in 1951, the German one — they showed me yours first. The commission paper. The officer put it on the table and said, 'A comrade from your village has given us a full account of you.' And I read it, and I saw the sentence about teaching young people, and it was not signed, it was a summary, and I did not know whose it was. I thought it was Szeliga. Or the miller." He turned to her. "And then they showed me the German, and I knew."

She could not speak.

"I have known since 1951," he said. "Both things. Since 1951."

The wind came off the water. Somewhere across the park a tram went along the street beyond the trees.

"And you never—" She stopped. "You never wrote. You never told anyone. You could have destroyed me. Six years ago, when the thaw came, you could have written one letter to *Tygodnik* and I would have been finished."

"Yes," he said.

"Why?"

He put his hat on, and this time he left it on.

"Because I am a priest," he said, "and because you are not the only person in this story who has signed a paper." He turned and began to walk, slowly, and she went with him, because there was nothing else to do. "In the prison they put a statement in front of me at four in the morning, after nine days, and it named two men I had studied with, and one of them was a good man and a fool and the other was neither, and I signed it, and I have been told since that the good man lost his post and the other was in Rakowiecka for eleven months, and I do not know to this day what became of him." He looked at her. "So you see. We are the same. We are two people who signed, and who have spent ten years being very careful about what we do with our hands. I have no stones to throw. I have never had any stones. That is the whole of it."

They had come to the gate on Agrykola. The street beyond was full of people going home, and the lights of a tram, and a man selling something hot from a brazier with a good red glow.

"Will you come again?" he said.

"I'll be sent."

"That is not what I asked."

She looked at him. In the lamplight he was a thin man in a black coat with his hat in his hand again and a cut on his lip, and she was thirty-one years old and the deputy director of an office of the Polish state, and she had not been this frightened since she was seventeen.

"Yes," she said. "I'll come."

She walked home. It was three kilometres and she did it in forty minutes and she did not feel the cold. At the flat in the MDM the light was on in the kitchen and Jerzy was at the table with his papers and a glass of tea, and he looked up and said, "You're late. There's cabbage."

"I'm not hungry."

"You're never hungry. Sit down and have some cabbage." He pushed a chair out with his foot. He was a big, slow, kind man with a bald patch and spectacles, and he had been at the Planning Commission since 1950, and he loved her in a way that she had accepted at the time as sufficient and had never examined since. "How was the Curia?"

"Difficult."

"They're all difficult." He turned a page. "There's a thing in the paper about the film. Wajda. *Popiół i diament*. It's at the Palladium. Everyone at the Commission has seen it and they all say it's a masterpiece and nobody will tell me what it's about."

"It's about the Home Army."

"Ah," said Jerzy. "Then they won't tell you what it's about." He took off his spectacles and rubbed his eyes. "Zofia. Are you all right?"

"Yes."

"You've been standing in the hall for a minute and a half."

She came and sat down and ate the cabbage, which was very good, and he told her about a dispute over the allocation of cement, and she listened and asked two questions, and at half past ten she went into the bedroom and took the tin of buttons down from the shelf.

It was a good tin, from before the war, with a picture of a Dutch girl on the lid. Inside were buttons, a thimble, a brass key, and a ring made from a bicycle spoke, which left a green mark on the finger and which she had not looked at for four years. She took it out and held it in her palm.

Then she put it back, and put the tin on the shelf, and lay down beside her husband in the dark and listened to him breathe, and did not sleep until three.
