Cornelia's Voice

Cornelia sings at the house of the Acilii with her mother's instructions arranged inside her like furniture in a room she is permitted to use but not to own: one song, not the Greek; bow to the empress's freedwoman first, because power sits in odd chairs; if the Avidii are there, they are to be greeted as old friends of the family, which by the vintage, the gods and the Greek banker's other rooms willing, they will be able to pretend to have been for years.

The preparations take the whole of the morning, which is the point of them. A girl who is to sing one song at the Acilii must be got ready as though for a small, decorous campaign: the hair dressed and then, at her mother's instruction, undressed by half a degree, so that it reads as nature improved by money rather than money improved by nature; the dress chosen from three whose differences are invisible to men and deafening to women; the music copied fair by the master, although Cornelia knows both songs the way she knows the stairs in the dark, because a girl seen to need no copy is a girl advertising a memory, and a memory, in a marriageable daughter, is only an asset if it can be mistaken for obedience.

“That one,” Servilia says, of the middle dress.

“It is the one I wore to the Fabii.”

“Then the Fabii will think we own one good dress and the Acilii will think we own three. Both impressions have their uses.” Her mother is not looking at the dress. She is looking at Cornelia's hands, which have been crushing the edge of the music roll since the second hour. “Hands.”

Cornelia puts them flat on her knees, where hands in this family go to be governed. Once, when she was nine, she asked why the women in the house were always being told about their hands — hold still, hold steady, do not let the master touch the wrist — and Servilia said, “Because hands confess. Faces in this family have advocates. Hands say what the person costs before the person has agreed to the price.” Nine is young to be given the whole constitution. Cornelia has been legislating for her hands ever since.

The music master arrives with his metaphors in good order. He is to warm the voice without spending it, a distinction he explains by reference to racehorses, kilns, and — finding his range at last — lamps: trimmed, not lit. “The Roman song sits here,” he says, not touching her, describing a place in the air somewhere below her collarbone as if it were a province he governs by map. “The Greek sits lower. It is why men prefer it and mothers ration it. A voice that comes from lower down sounds as though it had travelled.”

“Hasn't it?”

“It has travelled from Ionia, dominus's daughter, by way of every dining room between Smyrna and the Subura. That is the difficulty. Rome likes its imports taxed and its daughters domestic.” He hears himself and bows to the door where Servilia is not, quite, standing. “One song. The Roman.”

She warms the voice. The house goes about its hearing of her in the way it has since she was small: a pause in the corridor traffic that nobody minutes, a slave with a tray taking the long way past the music room, Marcus in the garden pretending to be reading accounts he has been specifically forbidden to find entertaining. When she passes his bench with the roll correctly held — not crushed; the hands have learned something this month, though not in time for this morning's inspection — he says, without looking up, “Sing the Greek one and blame the wine.”

“There is no wine for girls at the Acilii.”

“Blame the architecture. It's Greek.” He looks up then. Brother and sister in this family are allies in the limited, heavily taxed way of people who share parents, ancestors, and a growing suspicion that the shares were distributed unequally. “Will the ship be there?”

“Young Avidius—”

“The ship. With ballast.”

“He is expected. I am to greet his family as old friends. We have been old friends, by next vintage, for several years.” She sits, unasked, on the other end of the bench, which puts three feet of wet stone and the entire Roman law of guardianship between them, an intimacy. “Marcus. When you go to the house on the Caelian —”

“I don't.”

“— when Rome goes to the house on the Caelian, then: do the women there sing the Greek ones?”

Marcus considers the accounts with the seriousness of a man discovering a fourth set of books. “They sing what the room can afford,” he says. “Daphne sings a street song slowed down until it sounds like the truth at a funeral. Philomela sings Greek for people who'd know. It's her phrase.” A pause in which he is visibly deciding how much of the plumbing to show a sister the house is scheduled to launch like a ship herself. “They're paid, Cornelia. That's the part father can't get read into the minutes, for or against. A woman who is paid can stop a song. Have you ever seen one stop a song at a dinner here?”

She has seen songs stopped by a cough, by a slipped string, by her mother's glance arriving like weather. She has never seen a singer at the Palatine house stop a song because she had finished the amount of herself the fee had purchased. She files the thought under the things she is not to repeat at the Acilii, which by the fourth hour is the most crowded room in her.

The litter is called. The household deploys: mother, daughter, master at a professional distance, music in its case like an infant in state. Servilia, arranging herself with the economy of a general who has already won the war in the supply lines, says, “The empress's freedwoman's name is Epicharis. She was Plotina's hairdresser and is now, in effect, a ministry. You will bow to her before the Acilia because Acilia will expect it of us and Epicharis will not, which is why it must be done. Power, Cornelia, sits in odd chairs, and it remembers who saw the chair.”

“Will she ask for the Greek one?”

“She will ask for nothing. Ministries don't. Rooms ask, on their behalf, later.”

Cornelia watches the Palatine go past the litter's curtain in bright slices, like light through shutters: a client hurrying home with his morning still on his face, a girl her own age carrying a basket twice her courage up a lane, a fuller’s boy with a garment over his arm picking his way through the traffic like a man escorting a relative. Somewhere behind her, in the house she is leaving for the afternoon, a father is telling a committee what women are for in law. Somewhere ahead, in a room where the furniture of instructions is already arranged inside her, she is to be evidence in one song, not the Greek. She puts her hands flat on her knees, where hands go to be governed, and thinks, with her grandfather's ear and her mother's discipline, about the exact note on which a kept house, in the Roman song, survives its war. She holds it, soundlessly, all the way there.

The room receives them in its tiers. Cornelia has been reading rooms all her life the way other girls read music — and she is good at music; let the two literacies be minuted together. Nearest the windows, where the winter light comes in hard and truthful, the older women sit with the thorough nothing on their faces that her own family practises at moments of unminuted honesty. They are the assessors. No one has appointed them; Rome does not appoint its real committees. Below them, in the room's softer middle, the girls of this season are arranged like a border of flowers, each the same, each slightly alive with difference: two Fabiae who have been taught to play as one instrument, a little Acilia with a cough she is trying to convert into delicacy, a foreign heiress from Narbo whose Latin has an accent her mother describes as *weather* and the room prices as acreage.

Epicharis the empress's freedwoman is sitting in the odd chair. It is, in fact, too low for the dignity she has acquired since the hairdressing years, and she conquers it by ignoring it, a technique Cornelia has lately seen applied to Greek chairs in bankers' offices, though she does not know she has seen it, not in so many images. She is fifty, unpainted, dressed in the grey that costs more than colour, and when Cornelia bows to her first — before the Acilia, as instructed, while the room's temperature changes by the half degree her mother predicted — Epicharis acknowledges it with the precision of a woman who has spent twenty years close enough to an empress's head to hear power think.

“You sing,” she says. It is not a question. Ministries don't.

“I am to sing one song, domina.”

“How many do you *have*?”

The assessors' faces do their thorough nothing at a higher rate of interest. Servilia, three paces away and apparently examining a hanging with a connoisseur's outrage, becomes absolutely still in the place where other people listen.

“I have the Greek ones for my lesson,” Cornelia says, “and the Roman ones for company. It is a family registry.”

Epicharis looks at her for a moment with the expression of a woman pricing a room she might one day have to dress. “Then sing the registered one beautifully, child. Plotina says half of Rome's government is knowing which of your assets to minute.” She turns back to the Acilia with the question that releases the room, and the afternoon is saved, and Cornelia — who has been educated well above the safety limit, and who heard, under the sentence, its understudy waiting in the wings — files *assets to minute* in the crowded room of things she is not to repeat. It has, by now, a waiting list.

The other girls sing first because the Acilii, like Antonia's house and the Senate, understand programme as ballast. The Fabiae perform a duet about sisters awaiting a fleet, in close harmony and matching dresses, which the assessors receive as a prospectus: two dowries, one management, no sudden movements. The little Acilia sings to her cough and is applauded for the effort, which in this market is a way of saying her family's name will carry what her chest cannot. The Narbo heiress sings in a Latin that keeps being surprised by Gaul, and an elderly senator's widow two seats from Epicharis says, at the volume women use for remarks they intend to be overheard by history, “Acreage. It comes out in the vowels.”

Cornelia, in her place in the border, watches the room do its real work and finds she can suddenly see the joins in it — the way Prima, across the river, sees where a hem has been taken up for a second wearer. Nobody here is cruel. That is the discovery that frightens her, insofar as a girl with her hands correctly folded permits herself the word. Nobody is cruel; everybody is competent; the machine runs the way her father's clients' hour runs, on being seen to help in an order nobody can afford to dispute. A girl sings, is priced; another sings, is priced kindly; the soup of it all is excellent and the older women go home and set in train, with letters in upright hands, the next quarter's wars.

Only in the eddies — the supervised eddies, where cups are refilled and conversations are allowed to happen in sight of their inspectors — does the room produce anything that isn't in the programme. The Narbo heiress, marooned beside the refreshments with her vowels, tells Cornelia, in Greek much better than her Latin, that in her province girls learn letters from the estate accounts and nobody calls it a danger because someone has to catch the bailiffs. A young widow — twenty-five, rich, on her second filing by the city's assessors — describes, with the flat calm of a person reading a shipping forecast, the exact clause in her late husband's will that keeps her money out of her next husband's family; three girls listen the way clients listen at doors. And Epicharis, passing for more water with a ministry's indifference to stage management, says, of nobody in particular: “The empress maintains that a household register honestly kept is worth two poems about keeping house. She is very unfashionable.”

Then it is Cornelia's place in the programme. The master takes the far chair. Her mother becomes, at three paces, a country Cornelia will sing in but not emigrate from. She thinks of the room across the city where women are paid and can stop a song. She thinks of the note she held soundlessly all the way uphill, like a basket carried correctly. She sings.

She sings the Roman song, a setting of a poem about a woman keeping a house through a war — all the songs for girls are about women keeping houses through wars the men are having elsewhere, and Cornelia, who is sixteen and has been educated well above the safety limit, has begun to notice that the keeping is always the woman's glory and never her choice. She sings it beautifully. She has her Lentulus grandfather's ear and her mother's discipline, a combination that produces, in a drawing room, something perilously like feeling.

Young Avidius is there. He listens with his whole large, shipping-money attention and afterwards, in the supervised eddies of the room, says to her the thing that renews her mother's faith in the match and Cornelia's in humanity, which are not always the same faith: “You should sing the Greek one for people who'd know. My father says a thing wasted is a thing stolen twice.”

“Your father,” Cornelia says, “sounds like a warehouse.”

“He does. It's why there's money.” Avidius grins, unpatrician, unrepentant, entirely without the sullenness Marcus is currently touring Rome with as a philosophy. “I'm not clever like your brother. I'm not going to be a senator who quotes at you. Ships, accounts, a house that won't leak into the dining room — I can do those. If this marriage happens, nobody'll forbid your wrist to be touched by a music master. They'll just pay him better than your father does, so he respects the merchandise.” He winces. “That came out wrong.”

“It came out honestly,” Cornelia says, surprising them both, “which in my house is what coming out wrong is called.”

“It came out honestly,” his father says, from the other side of a tray of little cakes, arriving in the eddy precisely as a harbour dues negotiation would end it. Avidius the elder has ridden up from the docks with business still in his cloak and has been admitted to the Acilii's afternoon on the strength of a name the Cornelii are in the process of teaching Rome to pronounce as *old friends*. He takes in his son's wince, Cornelia's correct face, and the whole supervised situation with an evaluator's love. “He meant the master answers to the mistress.”

“I meant,” says young Avidius quickly, “the merchandise being the house—”

“Ballast,” says his father, with a joviality that promises a private conversation. “Ships, accounts, a house that won't leak. The young lady has the manifest.” He bows to Cornelia with the deep, practical bow of a man who has unloaded other people's empires and knows the weight of courtesy to the amphora. “You sang like a title deed, young lady.”

“Father.”

“I mean it well. A title deed is the most reassuring music in Rome. Ask your father—” He catches, not the error, but the geography, and converts it in mid-course with the grace he has been buying at such expense for his whole family. “Ask any landlord.”

Servilia materialises, as she materialises everywhere that matters in her children's lives, without seeming to have crossed the room. “The Acilia was saying, Avidius, that your ships came in under their time again.”

“They come in under their time because I pay men to tell me the truth about water. It is a heavy expense and there's no column for it.” He acknowledges the rescue with his eyes only; the rest of him, shipping and all, follows Servilia politely away into the assessed middle of the room, where men of his manufacture learn, year by year, how Roman the occasion requires them to be. Young Avidius is left with Cornelia in the supervised eddy and a silence that has suddenly become the most expensive item on either family's account.

“I do damage with compliments,” he says at last. “At home I pay porters. It's cheaper.”

“You could practise on the furniture.”

“I do. The furniture's ballast.” He grins, unrepaired, and then — the thing Cornelia will remember when the contracts are being read to her quickly, with the clause about her dowry glossed at speed — he gives up performing and looks, for a moment, like someone standing in a doorway he has paid to have measured. “I know what my family costs yours to be seen with. In their books it's entered as *new*.”

“Mine enters yours as *ballast*,” Cornelia says, surprising them both for the second time in one afternoon. “It is why the cargo arrives. My brother explained it. He is collecting nautical explanations this winter, the way other people collect jet.”

“I'm not cargo,” he says.

“No,” she says. “Neither am I.”

They are both sixteen and twenty-odd, respectable and supervised, statues in an eddy, and for eleven seconds — counted, later, by nobody who was present in any column the guild keeps — they discuss a marriage the way the house on the Caelian is reported to discuss everything: in the order of the evening, at their own prices, with the thread separate. Then the music mistress of the Acilii, who has a timetable where a heart should be for professional purposes, announces the refreshments in the garden room, and the eddy is drained, and young Avidius bows to Cornelia exactly as a man bows to cargo he has decided, on reflection, is a person.

On the way home the litter does its slices again, in reverse. Servilia does not ask what was said. She has, after all, been reading shoulders for thirty years; the words are the part a family can afford to lose. What she says, as the curtain lets in the cattle-market smell and the sound of someone else's Rome being driven home for the night, is: “Epicharis will report the phrase *family registry* to Plotina. It will do you good in a room you will never see. That is how sentences survive in the capital, Cornelia. They find work in the household of the period.”

“Mother. If I marry the Avidius boy—”

“When terms are concluded.”

“—will I be allowed to see the terms?”

The litter sways at a corner. Through the curtain the evening is arriving in sections, lit from the back: a baker's door with its day nearly sold, a fuller's boy carrying a clean garment like a person being escorted, lamplight beginning its second shift in a thousand rooms where women keep houses standing and call it nothing at all. Servilia's hands, in the dim, do the thing hands in this family are legislated against: they confess. For one moment they are neither arranged nor instructing; they lie in her lap like tired animals, and Cornelia sees their backs, and the twenty years of re-roping roofs and paying for silence prospectively in them, and loves her mother with a ferocity that has nowhere acceptable to go and therefore goes, as everything in her does this year, into the music.

“You will see the terms when I am satisfied you can use them,” Servilia says, in the voice of a woman opening a window in a locked room and standing well back from what the light does. “That is more than my mother issued me. Spend it better.”

Servilia, watching from across the room with a mother's optics — able to read a conversation by its shoulders at forty paces — revises, by perhaps half a degree, her private estimate of the Avidii. Money from grain. A boy who can wince. There have been worse foundations under the Cornelii; she can name two without leaving her own marriage.

At home Cornelia does a thing she has never done: she asks her father a question in the atrium, at clients' hour, in public — where public means slaves and ancestors, the two audiences a Roman girl is raised to ignore and therefore the only two she entirely trusts.

The clients this morning have been, even by the standard of clients' hours, a curriculum. First a Cornelian freedman with a warehouse boundary and a new-surveyed grievance against the Avidii, already softened, he reports, by the prospect of being resolved at dinner. Then a veteran of the Moesia winter with one hand doing the work of the other and a land petition the household has carried, in the order of patience, to the top of the morning where it belongs. Then — last, because Lentulus scheduled her last without ever having decided to, the way scholiasts preserve the true reading in a margin — the freedwoman with the petition in Greek and Latin whose savings a dead man's son is keeping as *household property*. Her charcoal cross is on the family's side table. The Basilica has heard her, because Lentulus kept a street promise in the Forum of Caesar, and she has come to the house not for more help but with the advocate's receipt, which she presents standing, the way women present children for naming: *restored, with costs*.

Lentulus takes it standing, as his father taught him to receive clients. It is not a large sum. It is the size of sum his dinner clothes cost to clean for a season. In the freedwoman's books, which a clerk in the family's real epic is presently writing up in a hand suddenly taking more care, it is a shop.

“You kept your own copy,” Lentulus says. It is as close as this atrium comes to a compliment.

“A woman told me to, dominus, at the Basilica. Keep your own copy of everything. She sings there sometimes, for the lawyers, in the dinners after. A Greek person. She said paper's a freedman who never forgets his place, only you have to file him yourself.” She bows over the receipt with the air of a person who has concluded a small, complete campaign. “The house is thanked.”

Cornelia, who is not supposed to be in the atrium at clients' hour, who is crossing it on her mother's errand with her music held like a rescued thing, hears *keep your own copy of everything* in a singer's reported voice. It arrives in her the way the note arrived in the litter: soundlessly, and then everywhere. She looks at the masks. She looks at the side table where, for three hundred years, other people's copies have been kept by the Cornelii as a public service, at the household's rates. She looks at her father, receiving a freedwoman's thanks for a clause he hasn't drafted yet, in an accent he doesn't know he is wearing because a woman on the Caelian dictated the first line of it to him in a banker's back room — none of which Cornelia knows, and all of which, the way people know weather from roofs they live under, she does.

That is why, when the atrium has thinned to its permanent population of slaves and ancestors, she does not go on to her lesson. The music can be warmed, trimmed, not lit. The question has been travelling from lower down, the way the Greek songs sit, and it has arrived in the room where her family answers questions professionally, or declines to answer them in excellent Latin at length for three hundred years. Her hands are steady on the roll. That is new this month too. Let the new things all arrive in one quarter; her house specialises in settlements that fall due together.

“Father. If the committee is about houses where men tell the truth after dinner, why does no woman sit on it? We are, I think, the dinners' main consequence.”

Lentulus, who has faced down embassies, experiences the specific vertigo of a man whose child has used his own forms against him correctly. “Committees,” he says, “are composed of senators.”

“Yes. I sat through the tutor too.” She is holding her music, not crushing it this time; her hands have learned something this month. “Marcus says the woman on the Caelian keeps Rome's real minutes. He says it to be troublesome, but he isn't clever enough to invent it, which in Marcus amounts to evidence.”

“You will not repeat your brother's bath-house evidence at the Acilii.”

“I will not repeat anything at the Acilii. I will sing one song, not the Greek, and be a wall.” She looks up at the masks, all those smoke-dark uncles of wax. “Did any of them ever ask their daughters a question, Father? Or is that the Greek song too — the one we keep for people who'd know?”

He should rebuke her. The rebuke is written; his family has been issuing it in fair copy for three hundred years. What he hears himself say, in the atrium, at clients' hour, in front of slaves and ancestors, is: “What would you ask the committee, if you sat on it?”

Cornelia's face does the thing faces do in this family at moments of unminuted honesty: nothing, thoroughly. Only her voice admits the earthquake. “I would ask it why a house where women are paid is a scandal for inspection, and a house where a daughter is dowered to pay a banker is a temple of the ancestors. I would ask it to define *registered*.”

“It would not answer.”

The word stays in the atrium after it has been said, the way a coin stays on a counter after the hand has gone: *registered*. Lentulus hears it in his clerk's voice, in Servilia's letter-voice, in a woman's voice in a cedar-smelling room laying a hand flat on a table so that nothing underdone would be sent out of her kitchen into law. He hears his father's version of it too, upside down on a dealer's tablet — *with her increase*, which is also a registration, of a kind. All his life the empire has been a matter of whose name is in which book in which hand, and his daughter, who is to be filed by vintage under *Avidii, connexion of*, subheading *ballast*, has just asked the only question the books were built to keep her from asking in this room: who does the registering, and at whose rates, and may the registered see the copy.

The clients are gone but the hour is not. Clients' hour in this house has a long tail, like a procession: boys with the morning's petitions being sorted for the day-book, the Arpinum cousins' gratitude being kept warm in a side room against a future in harbour dues, a slave damping the brazier even though the morning is bright because Servilia's standing orders do not argue with the weather, they anticipate it. Into this machinery Cornelia has put a question with no column. Lentulus watches his own household decide, in half a dozen pairs of averted eyes, to have been attending to the brazier, the petitions, the cousins' immortal gratitude, at the exact moment the master's daughter put the committee on trial and the master, instead of rebuking her in fair copy, invited her to draft the indictment.

It will be reported. Everything in an atrium is reported; it is what atria are for, the way dining rooms are for truths after fish. By the baths' second shift it will have become a story about a senator's daughter demanding a seat, which is not what happened. By dinner it will be evidence, in Marcus's phrase, which is worse than the truth and travels better. By next week, in a house on the Caelian where the real minutes are allegedly kept, a singer may slow a street song down about a girl asking her father to define a word, and a room full of men who register women professionally will laugh in the wrong place and then stop. Lentulus cannot draft for all that weather. His committee reports in ten days, now nine, and somewhere in its general, eventual dullness there is a clause taking shape like a stair inside a column drum: no house to profit, refusal, legal existence. It was dictated to him by its principal casualty and subpoenaed by his daughter in his own hall. A Cornelius pays his debts. His are arriving in female voices, in two registers, and neither of them is the Greek one, and both of them, his grandfather's ear tells him down the atrium's wet acoustics, have travelled.

“Go to your lesson,” he says at last, and it is not a rebuke and it is not a dismissal either; the family has no form for it, which is how he knows it might, at his age, be a beginning. “One song first. For the house. The one about the keeping.”

Cornelia looks at him across the ancestral smoke and the day-book's fresh ink. “The Roman one.”

“The one,” her father says, “you'd sing for people who'd know.”

“No,” Cornelia says. “I sit through dinners. I know the sound of the Senate not answering. It is my dowry, in a way. We all bring something.” She bows, correctly, and goes to her lesson, and the lyre begins, and is corrected, and begins again — and Lentulus stands among the masks with his daughter's question filed in no book his Greek keeps, and thinks of another girl, at seventeen, with a stylus behind her ear, correcting the scansion on a line about a woman at a loom, and how the Cornelii have been trying to strike that one useful word — *unpaid* — from the record ever since, at compound interest.
