# Seven

## The File

The file came to her office at six in the evening in the hands of a corporal with a travel warrant and a sealed envelope, and on the envelope, in Wrona's small neat hand: *For the acting director. To be read before the interview on the 20th. Return by hand.*

She signed for it. The corporal saluted and went. Hanna had gone home at five; the building was empty except for the night watchman and a light on the second floor where someone in the legal section was working late. Zofia put the folder in her briefcase, put on her coat and her hat, and walked home, and the whole way she was aware of the weight of it against her hip, like a stone in a pocket.

Jerzy was out. There was a note on the kitchen table in his big slanted hand: *Commission until late. There is barley soup. Do not wait. J.*

She made tea and did not eat the soup. She put the folder on the table under the lamp and untied the tape.

***

The first document she had seen before: the German deposition, two pages, translated in 1949 by someone whose Polish was stiff. She read it once, quickly, and put it aside.

The second she had not seen. It was in German, with a translation, and it was headed *Sonderaktion Brzozówka, 14.6.1944, Bericht*, and it was two and a half pages and it was the most terrible thing she had ever read, because it was dull.

> At 05.40 the detachment, consisting of 2 officers, 22 men of Gendarmerie Krasnystaw and 40 men of the Wehrmacht company stationed at Krasnystaw, moved out...
>
> The village was cordoned at 06.25. The population was assembled in the square before the church. Search of the buildings was carried out by four groups...
>
> In the cellar beneath the smithy was found one wounded Soviet soldier, who was shot while attempting to escape. In the orchard of the property of Józef Krawiec were found four persons of Jewish origin, namely two men and two women, together with one female child aged approximately ten years, who were shot in accordance with the standing order...
>
> The buildings were fired at 07.50. The village was burned to the extent of 11 dwelling houses, 9 barns, the mill, the school, and the shop. The church was spared...
>
> Losses to our side: none. Ammunition expended: 340 rounds.
>
> Remarks: The population is hostile. The action was carried out as a reprisal for the harbouring of bandits and Jews, in accordance with the order of the Kreishauptmann of 12.4.1944.

Thirty-one persons. She had known the number for fourteen years. It was in the report, in a table, with a breakdown: men 14, women 11, children 6. One of the children was eight years old and was called Piotr Krawiec. One of the women was thirty-six and was called Aniela Krawiec.

She turned the page. There was a list of property destroyed, with values in złoty.

She got up and went into the kitchen and stood at the sink with her hands on the edge of it, and ran the water, and stood there until the water was hot, and then she washed the cup she had used, and dried it, and put it away, and came back and sat down.

The third document was the protocol of the interrogation of 14 November 1951. It was nine pages, typed, with the questions numbered and the answers taken down by a stenographer and signed at the bottom of each page by the interrogator and by the subject. The subject's signature was the same upright hand as the declaration, and it had not shaken at all.

> **Q:** You were a member of the Home Army from 1942.
> **A:** Yes.
> **Q:** Under what pseudonym?
> **A:** Dzwon.
> **Q:** Your duties?
> **A:** Courier. Then liaison to the district command. I carried orders and I carried money and twice I carried a cipher book.
> **Q:** You were armed?
> **A:** I was given a pistol in 1943.
> **Q:** Did you use it?
> **A:** No.
> **Q:** Did you take part in any action against the German occupation forces?
> **A:** Yes. Twice, in a supporting role. In April 1943 at Izbica I was on the road with two others and we were to warn of the approach of a patrol. In July 1944 near Krasnystaw I was in a group that destroyed a telephone line.
> **Q:** Did you take part in any action against the Polish People's Army or against the Red Army?
> **A:** No.
> **Q:** You are aware that the Home Army was a terrorist organization in the service of the London government?
> **A:** I am aware that is the official description.
> **Q:** Do you accept it?
> **A:** No.

She read that answer three times. It was not brave — it was worse than brave. It was the answer of a man who had decided, somewhere in the first hour, to say exactly what was true and to let them do what they would with it, and who had kept to that decision for nine days.

The protocol went on. They asked him about the seminary, about the professors, about a discussion circle in Lublin, about a priest who had preached a sermon at Zamość. They asked him whether he had read certain books. They asked him whether he had ever spoken against the collective farms. And then, on page six:

> **Q:** Do you know this document?
> **A:** I have not seen it before.
> **Q:** Read it.
> *(The subject was given a document of one page, typewritten, dated 11 October 1949, and was permitted to read it.)*
> **Q:** Do you know the author of this document?
> **A:** No.
> **Q:** Look at it again. You are mentioned by name.
> **A:** I can read my own name.
> **Q:** Who wrote it?
> **A:** I don't know.
> **Q:** You have a good idea.
> **A:** I have no idea.
> **Q:** A comrade from your village has given us a full account of you. Do you understand what that means?
> **A:** It means someone from my village has given you a full account of me.
> **Q:** Her name.
> **A:** I don't know.
> **Q:** Her name, Wierzbicki.
> **A:** I don't know.
>
> *(The subject was silent for a period of approximately two minutes. He was then shown a second document, being a translation from the German, and was permitted to read it. After reading it the subject asked for water, which was given. He was then asked whether he wished to make a statement. He stated that he did not. The interrogation was concluded at 04.20.)*

She put the page down. Then she picked it up again and read the last paragraph twice, and then a third time.

He had not given her name. In a room in Mokotów at four in the morning, with nine days behind him, he had sat and said "I don't know" four times, and they had written it down, and it had gone into the file, and the file had come to her in a sealed envelope fourteen years later, and she was reading the only act of love anyone had ever performed for her that she had not known about.

***

The declaration was the next document, and she had read it before, but she read it again with attention.

> I, Tomasz Wierzbicki, son of Franciszek, born 6 January 1924 at Brzozówka, priest of the diocese of Lublin, declare as follows. In the years 1942 to 1944 I was a member of an armed organization described as the Home Army, and I did not inform the authorities of the Polish People's Republic of this fact. In the years 1948 to 1951, in the course of my studies and afterwards, I took part in conversations in which the policy of the state towards the Church was criticized, and I did not report these conversations. Among the persons who took part in them were Henryk Zaleski, then a student of the seminary at Lublin, and Michał Bortnowski, then a curate at Zamość. I make this declaration freely, without duress, and I regret my conduct.
>
> *T. Wierzbicki, Mokotów, 28 June 1952*

*Freely, without duress.* The phrase was in every such declaration; she had seen it forty times; it was a formula, like the *etc.* at the end of a prayer.

There was a note in the file, added in 1956 in a different hand: *Zaleski H. — removed from seminary list 1952; employed as clerk, Municipal Board, Lublin; no further interest. Bortnowski M. — released Rakowiecka 1953; died 1956 pulmonary; no further interest.*

*No further interest.*

She made more tea. It was a quarter past nine. The flat was very quiet; the refrigerator, which was a Polish model and had been a great prize in 1957, hummed in the kitchen like a large insect. She could hear a neighbour's radio through the wall, the dance music programme, and someone laughing.

The last document in the folder was not a document. It was an envelope, unsealed, containing one sheet of prison paper, folded twice, with the censor's stamp on the back and a note in pencil: *Not dispatched. Withheld 14.11.51. Szeruda.*

She unfolded it.

The hand was the same upright hand, but smaller, and pressed hard into the paper, and in one place the pen had gone through.

> 13 November 1951
>
> Zosia,
>
> They told me today that you are alive. I did not ask and they did not say it kindly; they said it as a fact about a document. I have thought for four years that you were dead in the summer of 1944, and now I know that you are not, and I find that I am glad, and then I find that I do not know what else I am.
>
> I am in a cell with four men. There is a window with the upper pane painted white, so that we see the light but not the sky. I have a blanket and a bowl and a spoon, and I am allowed to walk in the yard for twenty minutes on alternate days. It is not the worst place I have slept. The camp at Ostashkov was worse, and I have thought about that a good deal, and I have decided that this is because at Ostashkov I was twenty and believed I would be released, and here I am twenty-seven and have stopped believing things of that kind, and a man who has stopped believing is much harder to frighten.
>
> They showed me a paper today with your sentence on it, and then a German paper, and then they asked me your name, and I said I did not know it.
>
> I want to write to you about this and I find that I cannot do it in order. So I will write it as it comes.
>
> I am not angry with you. I have tried for six hours to be angry with you and it will not hold. I know what was in the room with you when you wrote that sentence, because I know you, and because I was in a similar room in 1944 with a German in it, and I know what a person will say in such a room in order to be allowed to go on living. You wrote that I was gifted and should not teach the young. It is a hard sentence and it is not a lie. I am gifted and I do teach the young, and I have thought since that if I had been the officer reading it I would have written the same.
>
> The German paper is another thing. I am not going to write about the German paper tonight. I have read it twice and I will read it again in the morning when I am steadier. I want you to know that I have known since this afternoon that you were the one who was taken in the meadow, and that I have spent the afternoon thinking not about what you said but about what was done to you before you said it, and that I am sorrier for that than I can put down.
>
> I do not know what you have become. They say you are in the Party and that you are doing well, and I find that I believe it, because you were always the cleverest person in the village and the most stubborn and you could not bear to be left out of anything. I hope you have a house with a warm stove. I hope someone makes you laugh. You had a good laugh once and it came out of you all at once, like a door banging, and it used to make my father smile, and he did not smile much after 1939.
>
> If you are alive, and if you ever read this — you will not, they will take it, they take everything — I want you to know one thing, and then I will stop, because my hand is bad and the light is going.
>
> I have not stopped thinking of you as the person I knew. Not because I am forgiving. I am not, particularly. It is because it is the truth, and the truth is the only thing I have in this cell, and I am not going to give it up to make myself feel better. You were seventeen and you stood at a gate in the dark and you said *in August,* and you meant it, and I have never once believed that the girl at the gate was lying. Whatever else has happened since, that girl existed, and I was there, and I am the only person left who was there.
>
> The house at the bend. Two rooms and a porch. I have thought about the porch a great deal in the last four years. It is a stupid thing to think about in a cell, and I have thought about it more than I have thought about God, which I will have to confess to someone eventually.
>
> Do not answer this. I am not certain that I could bear it, and in any case they will not let it out. I am writing it because a man must put something down somewhere, and because if I am shot on Thursday I would like there to have been one piece of paper in the world with the truth on it.
>
> I will pray for you by name at Mass on Sunday, if they let me say Mass, which they do not.
>
> T.

***

She sat with it in her hands for a long time.

Then she folded it along its folds, and put it back in the envelope, and put the envelope in her handbag, and cleared the table, and washed the cup, and turned off the lamp in the kitchen, and stood in the dark of the hall with her hand on the wall.

She did not cry. She had not cried since the sixteenth of June 1944, at the graveside, when Antoni Szeliga had held her against his coat; she had stopped then, and it had closed over, and there had been nothing since but a pressure, like water behind a dam, and she had built her life on the far side of it and had never once looked over.

***

At half past eleven Jerzy came home. He came in quietly, as he always did, and put his coat on the hook, and she heard him in the kitchen, and then he came into the front room and found her sitting in the armchair with her shoes off and the light off.

"You're sitting in the dark."

"Yes."

He turned on the small lamp by the sofa, and looked at her, and then at the briefcase on the floor, and then at her face.

"Zofia."

"It's a file."

"It's the priest," Jerzy said.

She looked up at him.

He was standing there in his shirtsleeves with his spectacles in his hand, a big tired man of forty-three, and he was not angry and he was not surprised, and she understood in that moment that she had been living with a man for five years and had not been paying attention.

"How long have you known?"

"Since 1953." He sat down on the arm of the sofa, at a little distance, and turned his spectacles over in his fingers. "You talk in your sleep. You have talked in your sleep since we were married. It isn't much — a name, sometimes, and once a sentence about a river. I worked it out." He put the spectacles on. "I didn't mind. I want you to know that. I thought: she has a thing in her past and it is not my business and she will tell me if she wants to. And then a year went by, and then two, and then it got to be the kind of thing that cannot be said, and now it is 1958." He looked at her. "Is he going to be a bishop?"

"Yes."

"And you have to decide about it."

"Yes."

Jerzy nodded slowly. He looked at the window, which was black, and at the reflection of the two of them in it.

"I would like to ask you one thing," he said, "and then I will go to bed and you can do what you have to do. Do you love him?"

She sat there. The refrigerator hummed. A tram went by at the end of the street, and the window frames rattled, and were still.

"I don't know," she said. "I don't know what that word means any more. I loved him when I was seventeen and I have spent fourteen years making sure I never found out what became of that. And now there is a file on the table with a letter in it that he wrote in a cell in 1951 and was not allowed to send, and in it he says he is not angry with me, and I find that I would rather he had been." She looked at her husband. "Does that answer your question?"

"Not really," Jerzy said. "But it's an honest answer, and I've had worse ones from the Commission." He stood up, and then bent and kissed the top of her head, which he had not done in a long time, and said, "Come to bed at a reasonable hour. You have a decision to make and you will make it badly if you are tired."

He went out. At the door he stopped.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I think you should see him. Not for the Office. For the other reason." And then he went in and closed the door, and she heard him washing, and then the bed creaking, and then nothing.

***

She sat up until four.

***

At half past four she got up and put on her coat and her hat and her good gloves, and took the letter out of her handbag and put it in the inside pocket of her coat, and went out.

The streets were empty except for a cart and a man hosing down the pavement outside the dairy. It was cold and clear, and the stars were going out one by one from the east, and the first tram of the day went past with its lights on and two men in it, and she walked up Marszałkowska past the Palace of Culture, which was still lit at the top, and turned into Świętokrzyska, and kept walking.

She did not know where she was going until she got there.

St Anne's on Krakowskie Przedmieście had a low door and a baroque façade and a notice board with the times of Masses. The door was open. Inside it was dark and cold, and there was a woman in a headscarf kneeling in the third row, and a verger moving along the side aisle with a long pole, putting out the candles one by one, and the smell of cold stone and wet wool and last night's incense.

Zofia went down the side aisle and sat in the last row and put her hands in her lap.

She did not pray. She had not prayed since 1944, and she had discovered in the intervening years that the cessation of prayer is not a decision but an atrophy, like the loss of a language. She sat in the cold and looked at the altar and at the small red lamp, and she thought about the sentence in the report — *the church was spared* — and about the fact that all the houses around it had burned, and about the fact that she had never in fourteen years asked herself why the church had been spared, and that she was asking it now.

The verger came to the last candle and put it out. The church got darker. The woman in the headscarf crossed herself and went out.

Zofia sat there until the first Mass began at six, and a priest came out in green vestments with two boys, and there were eleven people in the church. She did not go up. She listened to the Latin, which she had known by heart at nine, and found that she could still follow it, and that it did not mean anything at all.

At half past six she went out into the grey morning and bought a bun from a woman at the corner and ate it standing up in the cold, and then she went to the office, where Hanna was already at her desk, and said, "Hanna. Send a letter to the Curia. I am to see the candidate on the twentieth at eleven, here, and I am to see him alone."
