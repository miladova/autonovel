The first advocate to make serious money out of the clause does so by losing with it beautifully. His client is not romantic material for law students: a middle-aged bath attendant who claims that his former owner has kept back the savings accumulated toward his freedom after freedom itself had been granted in a will. The praetor distinguishes, narrows and, in the great legal style, applauds the principle while declining to let it interfere with lunch. Still, the phrase enters circulation. Men begin to quote it in porticoes with the inaccurate confidence of people fresh from hearing a song once.

A copyist near the Basilica starts selling a neat extract: the clause, three lines of commentary and a warning that commentary is not counsel. The warning costs extra. Students buy it. A freedwoman who sells ribbons buys it and has it read to her by her brother. A lodging-house keeper buys it to discover whether it can be made to mean the opposite and comes away disappointed in both law and punctuation.

The woman near the Carinae is called Saufeia—no, *Saufia* in her own pronunciation, which the documents repeatedly overrule. She has worked six years in rooms let by the man who once bought her freedom as an investment and then developed a proprietary interest in defining what freedom might reasonably require. Her savings remain in his chest *against breakages*. There have been no breakages. There have been deductions for towels, for lamp oil, for a cracked basin owned jointly by fate and the house. What begins as a dispute over money becomes, when she tries to take other work, a dispute over whether her refusal to remain has any civic substance beyond a noise a creditor can choose to hear.

She goes first to a patron’s clerk, who advises patience as men advise weather to a roof. Then to a scribe, who copies the clause at half price because his sister once worked in a house where accounts were kept *for* women in the way traps are dug *for* deer. Then to a young advocate with more appetite than clients. He reads the clause aloud, slowly, in front of the lodging-house door, with two witnesses he has borrowed from a bread queue.

The proprietor says, “She owes.”

“Itemise,” says the advocate, who has discovered the revolutionary power of asking men to put their habits into columns.

There is an hour of it, at street level, without swords. A towel that was already torn. Oil consumed in the course of business. A basin whose crack predates the reign. Neighbours gather with the moral seriousness Romans bring to any entertainment not requiring a ticket. The chest is opened because not opening it has become more expensive in reputation. Saufia’s papers come out. Her savings come out, diminished but no longer metaphysical.

She does not process in triumph. She ties her bundle twice because her hands are shaking and asks the bread-queue witnesses whether one of them will walk with her as far as her cousin’s street. Only afterward, in baths and bakeries and the coppery afterlife of a story improving by handling, does the paper become a torch. The reality is less flammable and more useful: a woman crosses a threshold carrying her own documents, and a man behind her discovers that the word *breakages* has lost its magic because other people have read it in daylight.

By September, three other cases have cited the clause, two successfully and one into a dignified provincial burial. The law has not remade Rome. It has put a handle on one side of a door. In a city built by men who specialise in walls, handles are not a small technology.
