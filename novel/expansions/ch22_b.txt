Acilius lets that sit in the steam before he disturbs it. He has the manners of an older Rome, in which silence between friends was not immediately subdivided into lots and sold.

“You could still make representations,” he says at last. “Not for Syria. That ship has been caulked. For the kind of work that puts a man back into the manifest in smaller print.”

“You sound as if you have been dining with shipowners.”

“I have been governing long enough to know when a metaphor has stopped being mine. The water board needs a curator with a name heavy enough to frighten contractors and a reputation light enough to be inspected. It is not the Euphrates.”

“No.”

“It does flow.”

Lentulus puts his head back against the warm stone. The shoulder is easing. In the next chamber somebody is telling a Dacian story in which the same river is crossed increasingly well by the same man. The baths are full of revisions. “When I was young, Acilius, I thought public life was a kind of architecture. Put the right name over the right arch and the centuries would keep the roof on.”

“And now?”

“Now I think it is drains. What we praise is mostly the smell not arriving.”

“You have become profound in a very unmarketable direction.”

“My wife says maintenance has no stamina for scandal.”

“Your wife should be set to draft something. With her own seal. It would clarify the century.”

There it is, light as a towel dropped between them: the old male joke made true by the year. Lentulus does not laugh at first, because the idea—Servilia’s hand on an actual public document, uncontained by dinners, weddings and the correct deployment of silence—belongs to a Rome neither of them will live to be inconvenienced by. Then he does laugh, quietly, in the water. “She already drafts the household. The public is merely where we attend the consequences aloud.”

Acilius studies him with an expression that in a less controlled man would be affection. “You are not going to make a scene, then. Athens can be spared a bruised Cornelius arriving by post.”

“I have made scenes in arrears. They were billed to other people.”

The sentence goes through the steam without applause. Acilius has known him too long to pretend it is a casual remark disguised as wit. “And the clause?” he asks. “If Hadrian’s people raise it as evidence that you are—what is the fashionable formulation?—unreliable at boundaries.”

“Then tell them boundaries are where my family has done most of its building.” Lentulus opens his eyes. The ceiling sweats in patterns no architect intended. “No. Do not tell Athens epigrams. Tell them I accept work where competence can be tested by whether people can drink.”

“That,” Acilius says, “is nearly an epigram.”

“Then let it be entered as health.”
