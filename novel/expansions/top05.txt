Stairs have their own historiography. Colonnades get architects; armies get bridges; stairs get the people whose work is to move between the levels other people own. At Baiae there were stairs polished by sandals into opinions, stairs that went down to water carrying heat in summer and up again with bedding that smelled of the sea. A girl learned them first in her calves and only later in arithmetic: so many up with weight, so many down empty, a pause at the turn if the overseer’s shadow had gone elsewhere to be important.

Counting made them smaller. That is the first misuse and first mercy of numbers. You could subdivide a punishment until it had edges. You could tell yourself *ten more*, and sometimes the body believed the fiction for exactly ten.

There were women who sang on the turns—not songs for guests, not music that would be entered anywhere as entertainment, but breaths arranged so the climb did not take the singer by surprise. Antonia would remember these phrases later in rooms where Daphne was being paid to make feeling look classical, and would register the difference with the fairness of a former stair-climber: one kind of singing holds a room, another kind holds the singer.

On the Caelian years afterwards she would catch herself counting the steps between courtyard and office on days when the house was at war with its own success. Seventeen. She knew it was seventeen. The knowledge did nothing to shorten them when a client had to be managed, a tax receipt found, a woman’s contract read at the correct speed in useful light. But it returned the senses to their owner. A person who can count the stairs is not being counted by them.

It is a minor discipline. Empires prefer columns. Lives, for much of their length, are carried up and down in baskets, on trays, under other people’s information, one counted step after another while history breathes at the top and pretends its view is the house.
