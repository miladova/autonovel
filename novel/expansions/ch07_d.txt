Servilia does not wait for his answer to begin hers. This is a marriage's constitutional arrangement, arrived at over nineteen years and never minuted: he composes positions; she composes the correspondence by which positions discover they were decisions. She sits at the little table where the household accounts are reconciled — a table with more real power over Campanian olives than any bench in the Senate — and drafts to her brother in a hand that makes even refusals look like bookkeeping.

“Read it to me,” Lentulus says, from the window, where the light is failing in stripes.

“'*We are sensible of the honour. The figure is a serious one, as a figure should be between houses who intend to be one. Gnaeus will wish to give his answer in person, as the connexion deserves; the vintage is a husbandman's deadline, not a father's, but we see no cause why goodwill should wait on grapes.*'” She lays the stylus down in exact alignment with the tablet's edge, an act of government over a province still obeying her. “It agrees to everything except agreeing. Avidius will read it as a yes with manners. My brother will read it as a family keeping its price off the record. You may read it as a wall.”

“And Cornelia reads it as?”

“Cornelia does not read her own file, Lentulus. She sings in it.”

From beyond the garden wall, as if the house were cueing her, the lyre begins again: the lesson's second hour, the master's metaphors arriving fainter with the light. The figure corrects itself, begins again — the second way. She always takes the second way now. Servilia listens with her head a fraction turned, the way she listened, at the dedication, to an aunt itemising a marriage, and says, in the neutral voice she uses for provincial appointments and family earthquakes, “She asked me yesterday whether the Avidii keep musicians.”

Lentulus looks at her. “What did you tell her?”

“That ship-owners keep whatever a wife insists is ballast.” Servilia's face does its thorough nothing. “She said, 'Then I shall insist loudly.' Your daughter, husband, is composing her instructions to be disobeyed in. One does what one can with the material. You come of a family that chose its currencies for three hundred years. Perhaps she will be the generation that changes the money.”

It is as close to tenderness as this room permits before dinner. Outside, the household is assembling the evening in its order: clients' leavings cleared, the day-book shut with its futures inside, Marcus's absence reported in the formula — *the young master is at the baths*, a sentence which in this house now functions the way *a payment signifying intention* functions at the Basilica, as a climate rather than a fact. Servilia has already instructed the steward that if the young master is at the baths after dark for the third evening this week, the baths are to be sent, with the family's compliments, an invoice for his board. The steward, who is paid prospectively and in kindness, received this wit in complete silence and has docketed it, somewhere in the family's real epic, under *the mistress knows*.

“She knows about the Caelian evenings as she knows about the harbour dues,” Lentulus says, to the failing light, because there are sentences a man can only say to a window in his own house. “In outline. With the costings.”

“Cornelia,” Servilia says, “knows what every girl in a great house knows: the acoustics. We raise them inside the walls where men compose their intentions aloud, and then we are surprised at the quality of their information.” She sands the letter — an old-fashioned touch; Servilia distrusts modern blotting the way her husband distrusts the word *steady* for horses — and holds it a moment before sealing, weighing, as she weighs everything, the difference between what a thing is called and what it is. “Seal it tonight and you are a father accepting a figure. Seal it after the committee and you are a senator accepting a family. The Avidii will pay the same either way. That is the interesting clause, Lentulus. It is always the interesting clause: the sum is fixed; only the wording is still ours to spend.”

The lyre stops. A door in the garden side of the house performs its small announcement. Cornelia crosses the far end of the room's sightline with her music held like something rescued, not looking at her parents in business hours, per the fortifications — and her father, with his agent's warnings, his Greek's unasked questions and his olives ripening toward somebody's autumn all standing in him like clients in an atrium, does the most un-Cornelian thing available to him at the end of such a day: he leaves the letter unsealed on his wife's table, between the quarters of the household's accounts, and goes to stand for a moment where the music was.
