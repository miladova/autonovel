A postponed festival leaves supplies in search of a philosophy. Nuts have been ordered, small gifts wrapped, a musician paid a deposit by a man who now wishes to convert Saturnalia into a serious dinner with togas. Diomedes gives him a schedule for both and charges for the difference between joy and respectability, a transaction Eirene describes as “seasonal government”.

In the kitchen, Ammonius is insulted by postponement. A sauce, he maintains, cannot be told to be merry at a later date by an office. “It has a constituency,” he says, waving towards ingredients gathered like an electorate. “These leeks were promised an inversion of rank.”

“Give them soup,” Eirene says. “It is the usual revolution.”

Prima is at the linen table with work that has come in under the respectable heading of repair. She has heard of Saturnalia at the Caelian in the way one hears of weather on an island owned by rich relations: described too warmly to be believed before the windows are checked. “Do the clients really serve the staff?” she asks.

“Symbolically,” Diomedes says. “With supervision.”

“And then everyone goes back to their columns the next day.”

“Tradition requires recovery time.”

Antonia, passing with a basket of household gifts that have missed their feast, says: “At this house the staff are served first in the kitchen. It saves the guests a performance and gives the hierarchy something private to worry about.”

Prima considers this over a seam. “At the guild we eat standing when there’s a death. It is not so much rank upside down as everybody making room where the chair isn’t.”

The sentence travels no farther than the linen room. It is not the sort of thing to be carried across a house for applause. But later, when the festival food is redistributed under duller names—staff supper, stores, customers’ samples—Eirene sees that a portion is set aside in clean cloth for the night workers and porters, the people who usually hold other people’s merriment steady from the doors.

“Saturnalia postponed,” Barbatus says, receiving his share at the street. “Empire’s in.”

“Eat before information,” Eirene tells him. “It is a rule with holidays.”

He does. The city beyond is turning rank upside down for the evening and putting it carefully back by morning. The house, richer in forms of in-betweenness, feeds first the people who know where the ladders are kept.
