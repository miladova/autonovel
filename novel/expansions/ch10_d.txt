After supper — soup at the front of it, in the position of honour, so that Mother Livia's truce is properly entered in the house's own books — Prima does the thing she has been not doing since the vats woman left. She takes out the leaves.

They live under her needles in the work-box, folded into a square of undyed linen, and they smell, after two years, of the box's cedar and faintly of the vats, which is to say of every true thing in her life. They are not a book. They will never survive an editor, a fire, or a winter in a damp room, and her mother, who understood material better than any woman Prima will ever meet on either bank, clearly intended exactly that frailty: these are accounts meant to be presented, not preserved; evidence for a hearing in a jurisdiction that keeps no copies.

The hand is the counting hand, learned late and written large. *House at Baiae, the widow's, ninety stairs.* Under it, columns that are not columns yet: names, with the work beside them, the way the guild book writes arrivals. *Aemilia, linen. Paid festival only.* *Io, lamps. Gone north with the sale.* *Girl from Puteoli, name not asked, carried water the hard winter, owed the summer.* Page by leaf-leaf they go, through houses whose owners Rome has filed under furniture, until the hand changes: smaller, later, the letters coming the way steps come when the basket is full. A new column, headed with a word Prima has read a hundred times without its ever having explained itself, the way household gods don't: *Kept.*

Under *Kept*, names again. Hers is not among them. She has noticed this before, in the noticing way of girls who read other people's news held steady: by expecting the omission so completely it has never had a shape. Tonight, with the old woman's voice still in the room — *sign yours, girl; I have it on the best authority across the river* — the omission stands up off the leaf like a figure mis-carried into the second column. Her mother kept a ledger of the unpaid and the kept, of stairs climbed with nobody's name in the house's books, and of the women the houses owed when they died. In all those leaves there is no entry for a daughter arrived. There is a blank space, wider than the others, after the last *Kept* name, with a stitch-mark beside it — Prima puts her thumb on it, the customs officer's granddaughter by trades rather than blood — a little cross, carefully, like a count being taken of something that would be finished later.

“Father,” she says. The room's noises arrange themselves the way troops arrange in other houses' atria. Vibius is in the doorway of the sleeping room with his honey, supervising. “In Mother's leaves. Under Kept. There's a space.”

He looks at her for a long time, and she understands that she has walked a question back to the wrong end of an evening that has not happened yet, and that her father — who keeps his feelings the way he keeps accounts, accurately and at the back — is deciding, at a pace the cough sets, what can be carried and what must be set down in this doorway, tonight, between the soup and the honey.

“Your mother,” he says at last, “left spaces where the count wasn't finished. She said a completed book is a dead one.” He coughs, and drinks, and goes through to his bed with the competence of a man banking a fire he will not see lit. “Sign nothing till the count comes right, Prima. She'd have said that. It'll be among the sayings.”

She folds the leaves face inward, the way she folds her best work, so that whatever the world does to them in the night happens to the back of the flowers. Then she takes the day's last job off the good chair — her own hem, as it happens, parting at the ankle like a sentence deciding to be continued elsewhere — and sews it in the light going blue at the shutter, counting stitches with her mouth open, unaware, as her mother once put it, that the stairs or the centuries are counting her back.
