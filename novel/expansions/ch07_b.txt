Eirene hears about it by noon, which is Diomedes' way of telling no one. She comes into the small dining room while Antonia is tasting a sauce that Ammonius has rebuilt for the second time that week out of spite and genius, and stands with a bowl of olives she has entirely forgotten to put down.

“His olives,” Eirene says. “In the ground. Ripening toward your autumn. Antonia. If a slave in the Subura house had prophesied that when we were seventeen, you'd have had her whipped for poetry.”

“I was nobody in the Subura house. I held mirrors. Poetry was above stairs.” Antonia sends the sauce back with a message consisting of one word, which the kitchen boy delivers as he delivers all such messages: at a run, for the sport of the return journey. “And they are not his olives. They are his grandfather's olives, technically. The Cornelii have always been technicians. It is why their debts are so well drafted.”

Eirene sets the bowl down at last and eats an olive out of it, house privilege, thirty years in the accruing. “What will you do?”

It is the question. It has been the question since the mirror, going round under the morning's work like a song under a hallway: her singer's phrase, Daphne's disease, the tune under every room today. What she will do with it. The menu of doings is long and Diomedes has, tactfully, already priced it in the margins of his own mind. She could call the paper in the autumn, before the vintage, in a message routed through the Greek so deniable that Lentulus would learn of his creditor the way Rome learns of everything: in the baths, unattributed, at the exact moment it becomes unusable. She could sell the paper on, to a collector of embarrassments — there are two at the Basilica, Diomedes says, and a third in the Senate who collects nothing else and calls it public life. She could let it be known, in the invisible denominations, that the committee's chairman is mortgaged to the woman his committee inspected at dawn, and then watch Syria subside, in Acilius' phrase, like steam.

Or she could hold it. Holding is also a doing. Holding is the doing that women in her family have always done with the valuable and the unbearable alike: mirrors steady, ledgers closed, children — no. The thought is put down where the first catastrophe lives, uncounted. Hold the paper; take the interest quarterly; let his olives ripen toward her autumn and his marriage settlement ripen toward his; say nothing to the Greek, who will know anyway in his Greek's way, the way bankers know weather; and keep, in the box between the quarters, beside an unsigned note that taught her a phrase, the knowledge that once — for one quarter of one year, from a grey morning when the damp came through her wall in the shape of Spain — the arithmetic of twenty-one years balanced itself on paper, without her having to lift anything heavier than a stylus.

“I have imagined it wrong for twenty years,” Antonia says, and is surprised to say it aloud, in a dining room, over olives. Eirene has that effect: thirty years of locked rooms in common will make a corridor of any woman. “I imagined a scene. A basilica, a letter. My voice carrying.” She looks at her hands, a hairdresser's daughter's hands, at thirty-eight still doing everything before she has decided. “I never imagined it arriving as *yield*.”

“Rome,” Eirene says, “is bringing everything to you as business these days. It is the house style. You invented it, love. In Baiae.” She says the place-name the way they both do: like a word in another language that they are, for professional reasons, fluent in and will not translate in front of the flowers. “If you call it in, you will get olives and a story. If you hold it, you will get interest and a longer story. I note, as your junior partner in catastrophe, that this house has always collected the longer story at the shorter rates.”

“The story has a girl in it across the river who counts,” Antonia says, so quietly that the room has to decide whether it heard. “Stories with girls in, Eirene, are not to be spent at the Greek's table. That is the whole of my policy. It is the whole of my religion, second service.”

Eirene does not refill the silence. She has kept Antonia's books in ways Diomedes never sees — the fourth set, unwritten, in the freemasonry of two women who were once entered, in a dead man's property, in the same column. After a moment she picks up the olives, remembers they are for a table, and goes to set them where guests will presently eat them without the slightest idea that they are dipping into a secured loan. It pleases her. Eirene's pleasures at forty are principally structural.

What Antonia does not say, then or later, to Eirene or to the mirror or to the third book's melting wax, is this: that holding the paper is the first revenge she has ever been offered that does not require anybody across a river to pay for it. The basilica scene, in the form she imagined it at seventeen on the stairs, had a cost column too, and the cost was never going to be billed to a Cornelius. Rome bills its scenes to women; it is a tax she has paid quarterly and knows by heart. Paper bills nobody. Paper just sits in a Greek's strongbox earning like furniture, while a man in the Senate discovers — at his own pace, in his own committees, with his wife scheduling the discovery between the oysters and the aqueducts — the exact rate of interest on a sentence accepted at twenty-four.

“Hold it,” she says to Diomedes, when he comes back for the tablets, in the level voice of the first book, upright and dull. And then, because the room is empty except for a bowl of olives with one missing and a clerk who has brought her a fact with a face on it across three evenings, she adds the rider that belongs in no book in any hand: “And if the olives do come to us in the autumn, Diomedes — I will not have them pressed in this house. Sell the oil forward. A woman's kitchen should not have to render her own metaphors.”

Diomedes bows over a joke that is not one and goes to write *hold* in a hand the Greek will read as investment advice.
