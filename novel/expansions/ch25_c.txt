Cornelia’s first letter to Prima is not, properly speaking, a letter. It is an order, routed through a Secretary, for two lengths of work suitable for a west-facing room: measurements, light, the warning that red can be reduced. Prima’s estimate comes back in her firm hand with thread separately costed and an unimpeachable sentence about hanging in strong sun. They correspond for some months as women in Rome often do, by making a commercial instrument carry more weather than its face declares.

Only later does a second enclosure begin to appear, on smaller paper: a question about a song’s refrain, a note that the music floor still speaks under the stairs, a request—phrased as household interest—whether a certain stitch can be made to disappear while still holding. Prima answers at invoice length. Sometimes shorter. The household gods of Transtiberim are not consulted on fashion, and Mother Livia reads nothing she has not been given with intent.

Cornelia’s unsent letter lives in her box for most of a season. She takes it out after evenings when the family silence has taken a particular shape across a table. She has written, on the first attempt, an appalling amount of Greek, because strong feeling in her class comes with metre pre-installed. On the second she has written: *I think I know why my mother’s house goes quiet when I sing a line about accounts.* On the third, simply: *May I ask the order of the questions?*

She does not send any of them. Not from cowardice—Cornelia has begun paying musicians in advance and growing a backbone under management—but from a sudden, mature suspicion that ignorance can also be other people’s room. Perhaps Prima has not been waiting to be solved by a sister in a west-facing house. Perhaps a question, sent at the wrong rate, is a demand dressed for dinner.

Servilia sees the page of questions while helping her daughter select which papers should travel on an Avidian estate visit. She reads the top line because it is there and because mothers, even in the fourth ledger, retain vast powers of accidental reconnaissance.

“Keep them at the front of the box,” she says.

“Mother.”

“I am not answering. I am approving storage.” She closes the lid. “When a thing is told for your benefit, Cornelia, it will not be delivered by musicians.”

“Is that a promise of a date?”

“It is a promise of subject matter.”

Cornelia almost laughs. “You are becoming less governed.”

“I am governing smaller territories. It looks like softness from the provinces.”

That week Servilia sends, through correct channels and at a respectable hour, a request for the recipe for almond cakes. It is addressed to a house in the abstract, as if recipes arose spontaneously among institutions. The reply comes copied clearly, with quantities, by Diomedes in the hand that allows things to live. There is also, in Eirene’s inimitable afterthought delivered verbally through Barbatus, an instruction to let the committee wife beat the mixture until it changes its politics.

Servilia makes the cakes herself—supervised by a kitchen staff browsing catastrophe—for an assembly of widows concerned with fountain repairs. They are pronounced excellent by women whose husbands used to complain about taxes and are now, through widowhood and improved pressure, allocating them. She sends no acknowledgement. A week later a second request arrives at the Caelian for the name of the almond supplier.

Diomedes enters it under Correspondence.

“Not family?” Eirene asks.

“Not between the quarters.”

Antonia hears, and for once makes no suggestion about the accounts.
