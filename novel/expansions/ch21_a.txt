The morning begins long before the auspicious hour, in rooms where women have been awake since the less sacred one. Cornelia’s dress hangs ready like a small state awaiting inauguration. Her hair must be parted and bound in the ancient style; her veil must lie over it; her girdle must be tied by hands that can pretend symbolism is primarily a matter of knots. The old nurse cries while doing competent work. Servilia, whose capacity for emotion in an emergency is usually expressed through seating plans, discards two arrangements of flowers and then restores the first with the air of a general conceding that the frontier has views.

Cornelia sits still under ministrations. “Do brides always feel like a room being prepared for other people’s furniture?” she asks.

“They are not encouraged to file reports,” her mother says.

“I am filing this one in the box.”

“At the front.”

The box—papers, copies, schedule of her music master’s fee, the new plain notebook she has begun to imitate from some influence her father affects not to identify—has already been sent ahead under her maid’s personal guard. It matters to Cornelia obscurely that the box will enter the Avidian house before she does. A person should have an advance party.

Downstairs Marcus is being made presentable by a household that has stopped expecting catastrophe from him and therefore finds his cooperation faintly suspicious. Onesimus removes an imaginary thread from his shoulder. “Master Marcus is early,” he observes.

“I am practising instalments.”

“Of punctuality, sir?”

“Of being a brother.” The word is out before he can amend its scope. Onesimus, whose diplomacy has survived the administration of three generations, treats the singular as perfectly adequate. “In this house, practice is commonly mistaken for a plot. Bear it.”

Lentulus receives the Avidian party in the atrium beneath the masks. There are formal words and hands and documents, torches even at the wrong time of day, because tradition is a landlord that expects rent regardless of light. Avidius the elder stands with a harbour-man’s intolerance for ceremony that cannot be weighed. Avidius the younger is pale in a way that flatters neither his tailor nor his self-image.

When Cornelia comes in, saffron-veiled, the younger man’s relief is so undisguised that the flute players pause in sympathy with it. It is possible to see at once that she is not entering a romance manufactured for ballads. She is entering a negotiation in which one party has already learned not to look surprised when she speaks. In Rome, this is a door. Many marriages have less furniture.

The contract Latin proceeds. Somebody’s child throws nuts too early and is removed by an aunt with battlefield efficiency. Marcus stands where a brother should, gives the expected answers with the expected face, and privately amends the official seating by thinking of another woman across a river who has corrected him in a dining room. He does not confuse the sisters. That would be cheap. He holds the two kinds of good fortune in separate hands: one veiled at noon among ancestors, the other, probably at this minute, naming a price for cloth with her mouth full of pins.

At the wedding breakfast Cornelia asks—not silently, not through a steward, not by making the request sound as if it had originated in furniture—whether she may sing one song before the formal music begins. It takes the room a second to understand that a bride has used a verb in the first person. Servilia looks at the ceiling. There is, on the ceiling, a fresco concerning agricultural abundance. She studies it as if it had been painted by the household gods for emergency consultation.

“One,” she says.

Cornelia sings the Greek song, not too fast, with the turn in the middle verse her master no longer corrects. Young Avidius cries when the flutes can cover it. Marcus does not; he has become too well educated this winter to waste the moment on an easily misfiled grimace. Lentulus hears his declared daughter take breath where she chooses and understands, among torch smoke and marriage nuts, that exits can be forms of arrival when they are not conducted by stewards.
