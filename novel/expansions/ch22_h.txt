Acilius’s letter to Athens is a masterpiece of the second column. It reports that Gnaeus Cornelius Lentulus has accepted heavy responsibility for the city’s water; that his health, though not delicate, no longer invites provinces to compete for it; that the famous drafting hand is now employed on estimates whose consequences can be tasted. It suggests, without saying, that a man who has stopped applying for exile in the form of honour may be of more use to an emperor designing a reign than another polished governor with a clean procession of secrets.

Lentulus sees the copy because Acilius, who is old and honest in the expensive way, sends it entire. “You make me sound like a ladle,” he says.

“You should hear yourself in other people’s versions. Half of them have you building a temple to freedwomen, which even I think hasty before lunch.”

They are walking after a hearing through a porticoed street where petitioners cluster like weather at a temple. A freedwoman with a folder under her arm is arguing cheerfully with a Secretary. The word *clause* crosses the marble at a distance. Lentulus does not turn. The discipline pleases Acilius.

“Hadrian will remember you,” he says.

“That is not necessarily the mercy it advertises itself as.”

“No emperor’s memory is. But it is better than being remembered only by column height.”

In the Forum, work on the frieze is high now above readable distance. Figures climb and fall around the shaft in a sequence designed by men who assume posterity owns ladders. Lentulus and Acilius stand for a moment with other hereditary spectators, heads back, mouths not quite open because they have been trained by ancestry to congratulate the future without looking hungry.

“I used to think that was how to survive,” Lentulus says.

“What, spiral?”

“To keep telling the same story upward until nobody could ask whose hands did the carrying.”

Acilius watches a crane’s rope take the weight. Men shout in several provinces. “You are becoming a difficult companion at monuments, Gnaeus. Your conversation is all underneath things.”

“I have had a year of plumbing.”

“Write that to Athens.”

“I shall let you. My hand has caused enough provincial correspondence.”

They walk on towards a district where a fountain is failing. At the corner, a news-seller cries an inaccurate summary of appointments, embroidered with titles that may not survive the afternoon. The Syrian appointee’s name comes second in the list and attracts no theatrical attention whatsoever. It is, Lentulus notices, almost possible to hear his own prospects becoming history, and history is quieter than ambition expected.
