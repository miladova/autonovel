The first client is the poet, because poets are always first, having the least to do after. He is called Quintus Valerius Soranus, after three men who would have paid to stop him, and he carries his epic in a bucket because the book-boxes are full — twelve books on the Dacian wars, in which the emperor appears, by the poet's own proud census, two hundred and forty times, and the Cornelii, by a creative interpretation of the campaign rolls, four. He does not want money, which is the first thing poets say in atria. He wants the work *heard by a person whose hearing signifies*, which is money in a laurel wreath. Lentulus hears the proem standing, as he hears everything at this hour, and finds, to his own mild disgust, that it is not contemptible. The man can do a river crossing. Rivers are his metre's home address.

“The passage where the bridge goes over at night,” Lentulus says. “Cut the catalogue of timbers by half.”

Soranus clutches the bucket like a hostage. “The timbers are documentary.”

“So is a tax return. We do not recite them at dinner.” Lentulus takes, from the tray his steward holds at the correct angle of invisibility, a small purse that the household prepares each morning in three sizes — Servilia calls them *encouragement, dismissal,* and *danger* — and gives the poet the middle one. “Read the bridge, and only the bridge, at Acilius' next evening. I will see that you are invited. If Acilius' guests are still awake at the far bank, we will discuss the remaining eleven books.”

The poet bows so low the bucket tips. Somewhere in the arithmetic of patronage a bridge has just been funded, and a poet's winter, and a small lien placed on the memory of the Dacian wars, where the Cornelii now appear four times, by purchase. This is how history is made in Rome before it is carved. The carving comes later, when the embarrassing parts have been paid for and can be omitted respectably.

The second client is not one man but a delegation: three freedmen of a Cornelian grain depot at Ostia, come up to Rome with a lawsuit in one satchel and, in another, smoked fish for the household, which Servilia's steward accepts with the gravity due to tribute between nations. Their case is a warehouse boundary, a neighbour's wall, a measurement disputed to the width of a man's hand — matters on which, as Lentulus listens, some hundreds of people's dinners actually depend, though no epic will ever spend a river on them. He asks the one question their advocate has evidently forbidden them to answer — who owns the wall's other side — and watches them decide, in a three-way flicker of eyes, to trust the atrium instead.

“The Avidii,” says the eldest, in the voice of a man confessing weather. “The new depot. Since the autumn.”

Lentulus looks at the wax masks, who look back with three centuries of practice at receiving this kind of news before breakfast. His daughter's possible marriage has just acquired a party wall. Rome is a village with aqueducts; he has known it since he was twenty and it has never once been convenient.

“You will not sue,” he says. “You will survey — a surveyor of my choosing, paid from this house, so that his findings are a favour and not a missile — and the Avidii will be shown his rods at a dinner, not a tribunal. A hand's width, gentlemen, is a negotiating instrument. In court it becomes a principle, and principles eat warehouses.”

They bow over the solution, relieved in the way of men who came for a war and are being given a boundary stone and a hot breakfast. The steward moves them toward the kitchens. On their way out they pass the two cousins from Arpinum, standing in togas of a whiteness that argues long storage and short funds, rehearsing, without moving their lips, the case that the Cornelii owe their grandfather a magistracy. Lentulus can see the case's whole future from where he stands: ten minutes of genealogy, produced like a sword from a walking stick; his own reply, in the formula his father used — *the family's gratitude is a matter of record* — which is Latin for a door; and then Servilia, later, telling him the Arpinum cousins have a cousin of their own in the harbour dues office, and that gratitude ought therefore to be put on the record in a more spendable denomination. She will be right. She is the quartermaster of gratitude. He merely issues it at parades.

Between clients, in the breath the atrium takes, he is aware of the household performing the morning as his wife has scored it: the door slave admitting no one whose name the steward has not tasted first; the boy with the guest-towels; the clerk at the side table writing, in a book that is the family's true epic, who came, what they asked, what they were given, what it will cost when it comes back — and everything comes back; that is the aqueduct. Cornelia crosses the far end of the atrium on her way to her lesson with her scroll held like something she is rescuing rather than carrying, and does not look at the clients, who must not be given a daughter to look at in business hours; the rule is Servilia's, older than the girl, part of the house's fortifications. Lentulus watches his daughter not look at his morning and feels, obscurely, audited.

A veteran is waiting too, further back, in the place men put themselves when they have been clients before and know the tariff of patience. He has Moesia in his face and one hand that the years since have taught to do the work of the other one. Lentulus served — *served* is the household word; his own, privately, is *attended* — with the man's legion in a winter he does not recount at dinners. He will keep the veteran last, because what the man needs is not a purse-size but a recommendation to a land commission, which takes the time it takes, and because the Arpinum cousins must be seen to be received before the veteran is *seen to matter more than they do*, which everyone in the atrium already knows and the order of the morning must conspire not to say. Patronage, he thinks, for the thousandth time, in the exact tone of a man reading his own inscription as someone else carves it: it is not a system for distributing help. It is a system for distributing the appearance of help in an order nobody can afford to dispute. His father would have called that a provincial sentiment too, and then received his clients in the same order, standing, for fifty years.
