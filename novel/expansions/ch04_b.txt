Prima finishes the row as the music changes in the far rooms — a singer resting, instruments being argued with in low voices, the house taking the breath it takes between selves. Her eyes are dry from the lamp; her back, where the stool's edge has been conducting a four-hour negotiation, presents its invoice all at once. She rolls the work the way her mother taught her, face inward, so that whatever the world does to it in transit happens to the back of the flowers. Out of provinces of habit like this, her whole life is governed. A year ago she would not have had the thought; tonight, in a house where rules arrive with reasons attached and women correct each other's words in passing, she notices her own government and wonders — it is the second treacherous comparison of the week, and she lets this one stand, being tired — what a life would feel like in which the rules were *hers* in more denominations than cloth.

Eirene is waiting at the courtyard door with the lantern and a shawl that is not Prima's.

“It belongs to the house,” Eirene says, before the refusal can get itself itemised. “Houses own shawls the way harbours own rope. You'll bring it back with the spring work and Diomedes will enter it as thread. Everything here is entered as thread. I have been entered as thread myself, in difficult years.”

The street at night is another Rome, let out of its daytime filing. The watch goes past with its buckets and its air of a department that has seen every fire twice. A drunk is being walked home by two slaves with the tenderness of men carrying a sofa through a doorway in their employer's best room. Somewhere above the street a woman is singing a child down in a language Prima half knows from the vats — one of the river tongues, part Latin, part everywhere the river has been — and Eirene, carrying the lantern high the way she carries everything, as if it had a right to be seen with, says, “Your father will be awake.”

“He's awake when I'm out. It's his watch. He says the house makes noises a man should supervise.”

“Men say that about houses, money and daughters. It's their word for standing in a doorway.” Eirene steps around a puddle that holds the lamplight up to them like an offering. “Mine stood in doorways professionally — a porter, in the Subura, in the house where Antonia and I —” She stops. It is only the smallest check, a stitch counted twice, but Prima, whose trade is noticing the place where a pattern hesitates, notices. “— where we were girls,” Eirene finishes, at the same pace, like a woman stepping over the puddle she has just pointed out. “Count your blessings, Prima. Your doorways are honest.”

Prima nods under the borrowed shawl, not trusting the moment with a question. She has grown up in rooms where the past was present as furniture: things not sat on, a mother's saying about stairs used daily and never explained, an old woman at the vats who looks at her sometimes the way Mother Livia looks at a hem — as evidence being kept for a later inspection. She knows, the way you know weather from a roof you have lived under, that her life has a sealed room in it. She has always known. What she does not know is whether other people's lives don't, or whether, as she is beginning, tonight, walking home beside a freedwoman with a lantern from a house that files kindness as thread, to suspect: it is only the poor who are expected to live in one room and call it honesty.

At the bridge, the river is doing its night work, carrying the city's lights away in pieces too small to invoice. Tibur stone, island temple, a barge going down unlit on the current's own authority. Eirene stops at the crossing by arrangement — *to the crossing*, the mistress said, and Prima, who thought it a measure of distance, understands at the lantern's turning that it is also a measure of neighbourhood: this far a Caelian lantern may escort a fuller's daughter without becoming a story; beyond this, the street is hers again, and stories, like debts, should be contracted at home.

“Thursday,” Eirene says, “for the border's end. Bring your appetite; the mistress has lost patience with your father's foreign policy about her kitchen and is filing lunch as a materials inspection. You may tell him the house inspects *you* with soup. It has the advantage of being true in every particular the guild would check.”

Prima goes down into Transtiberim in the dark she was raised in, which receives her without commentary. Her father is awake, as advertised, supervising the house's noises from the doorway with his honey cooling beside him. He looks at the shawl. He looks at the work-box. He does not ask the questions, because keeping, in his book, includes keeping a daughter's day unopened until she chooses a knife.

“Flowers done?” he says.

“Flowers nearly. Soup Thursday,” Prima says, hanging the borrowed shawl where the damp won't find it. “Filed as an inspection. You can't refuse an inspection, Father. It's the law of houses.”

Vibius regards her with the expression she will remember, later, when she has a whole archive of his expressions and no more being added to it. “Then inspect thoroughly, girl,” he says. “And tell the house from me: we know good work across the river. We can smell it through the vats.” He coughs, and drinks, and lets her heat the honey again without either of them saying why it has gone cold.
