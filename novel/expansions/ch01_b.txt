The dealer in purple is called Gaius Septimius, he dyes for half the bridal trade of Rome, and he owes Antonia nine hundred sesterces on a note she bought from his landlord in the autumn, at a discount, for reasons that were partly business and partly that the landlord's son had beaten a servant in her house two years ago and she collects such accounts in whatever currency presents itself. Septimius does not know she holds the note. He knows she is a woman who once found him, at two days' notice, a buyer for a consignment of cloth ruined — *improved*, he said at the time, sweating — by an apprentice's error with the mordant. Gratitude of that vintage does not thin. He makes room for her and Eirene beside his wife, a gaunt, handsome woman with dyer's hands she hides in public inside her sleeves, and launches, by way of rent on the standing space, into the cost of everything in sight.

“You see the gilding on the basilica entablature? Gold leaf, Antonia. Not wash. Leaf. My cousin had the contract for the glue alone and bought a farm at Tibur.”

“Then the empire is in good health,” Antonia says. “When the glue men are buying farms, the world is sticking together.”

Septimius' wife makes a sound inside her veil that is either a laugh or a cough being strangled for respectability. Eirene, who has the stillness of a woman listening with her whole skin, says, “Tell her about the library steps. He has been telling the street all morning. It improves with an audience that pays by the piece.”

But Antonia is not listening to Septimius, not with the front of her mind. She is doing what she does in every crowd: reading it as a ledger. There — a contractor who supplied lime for the Forum vaults, gone rich and strange with it, wearing a ring on every finger as if his hands were a manifesto; his wife assessing other wives' jewellery with the care of a money-changer at a doubtful coin. There — two young equestrians she dined last month, deep in an attempt to look senatorial, an aspiration their tailors have funded and their posture betrays. There — very still, in the grey that costs more than colour — Servilia. Antonia has seen her perhaps a dozen times in twenty years, always at this distance, which is the distance Rome keeps between the women in its arrangements: close enough to evaluate, too far to require a greeting. Servilia's veil is arranged as if by engineers. She is listening to an older woman — an aunt, an Acilia, somebody with a priesthood in the family — with an attention so complete it must be costing her something, and Antonia, who prices attention professionally, thinks: *that is a woman being told the cost of a marriage. It takes one to itemise one.*

She does not permit the thought a second sentence. Servilia is filed, has been filed for nineteen years, under *the settlement*, and Antonia does not open other women's files in public; she has a house that lives by the principle.

The crowd has its own weather. It thickens toward the imperial party, thins at the altars where the smoke will be, eddies around a man selling, with an entrepreneur's instinct for a captive Senate, little clay columns no taller than a thumb, already spiralled, already triumphant. A child is crying somewhere in the particular key that carries. A legionary veteran on a crutch is being helped, by two strangers and a third man's elbow, up onto a plinth for a better view, and the crowd makes that small, satisfied noise cities make when they have done a decency within sight of each other. Somewhere a rope gives way at a barrier and there is a surge and a counter-surge and a voice raised in official Latin, and a pickpocket — Antonia watches his whole career: the drift, the gather, the repaired innocence of the hands — decides, feeling Barbatus's attention from twenty paces like weather on his neck, to become a spectator instead. Barbatus is not beside her today; Barbatus is never beside her at dedications, it would be a statement, and statements are for clients. But a man who has worked a door for eleven years radiates. She has seen his attention work from across a courtyard full of wine. A Forum is merely a courtyard with an emperor in it.

“What do you suppose it cost?” Eirene asks, meaning the whole thing: forum, basilica, libraries, the column still in its scaffold of timber like a tree not yet allowed to be stone.

“The Dacians,” Antonia says. “Personally. That is the official answer and for once it is also the accounts. Gold in the mountains, men in the mines, marble in Rome.” She watches a team of oxen, garlanded to the edge of dignity, being walked in a wide, patient circuit to keep them calm before the sacrifice. Their hides shine. Handlers talk to them in the low, continuous murmur men use for animals they respect and intend to kill, a tone not unknown in the Senate. “Everything here has been somewhere else first, Eirene. That's the dedication they're actually performing.”

“You should write speeches.”

“I should charge for them. Septimius — your wife's sleeve is better dyed than the priest's border, and if you tell her I said so she will charge you for the compliment in kind.”

She says it because Septimius' wife has taken her hands out of her sleeves to settle her veil and the purple under her nails is the deep, true, sea-snail purple that money cannot quite buy and work cannot wash out. The woman hears. Under the veil something changes — not a smile, which would need witnesses; an acknowledgement, tradeswoman to tradeswoman across a dyer's husband, that the hands have been seen and priced correctly. It is the kind of transaction Antonia values: exact, unrecorded, impossible to tax. Rome runs on them underneath everything it inscribes.

Now the procession of office is forming on the far side: lictors, priests, the colleges in their order, boys with incense swinging their arms wide the way boys have swung incense since Romulus, half devotion, half showing off. The emperor is visible for whole seconds at a time between shoulders: a soldier's head, grey now, the famous plainness of dress that costs more to maintain than purple, since it must never look maintained. Antonia has fed half the men walking behind him. She catalogues them without appetite: who is up, who is being carried by his wife's money, who has begun, in the last year, to have his dinners at home and his truths told by others, which is the first symptom. Power in Rome is mostly this, she thinks again — and then the crowd shifts, in that organic, prescient way it has, a lane beginning to open before the reason for it is visible, the way water finds out about the stone before the stone arrives.
