After the laughter settles back into saucepans, Prima climbs the stool again. The summer hangings are lighter than the spring set, less red in the border, intended for rooms where heat makes men imagine that their opinions have become thinner too. Barbatus stands beneath her without being asked and takes pins one at a time from a cushion. It is absurd and touching, a former gladiator inducted into the auxiliary forces of interior decoration.

“Did she wear flowers?” Prima asks suddenly.

The kitchen knows which *she*. Eirene says, “Saffron veil, flowers according to rank, a knot a ship’s chaplain could preach from.”

“And she chose the song?”

“Chose it, sang it, survived the ceiling.”

Prima works for a while. “I do not want to sing at mine.”

“Nobody has proposed it,” Antonia says.

“I know. I am registering objections before the committee develops.”

Daphne, arranging figs into a pattern no guest will appreciate before ruining, says, “You can have me at a discount.”

“The neighbours would think Titus had allied with Alexandria.”

“Your mother-in-law would inspect the alliance and find the thread separate.”

It draws a laugh from Prima that Antonia is learning to let pass without making it into ancestry. Not every sound must be filed as an inheritance. Some sounds are just a girl being funny before lunch.

Still, the wedding on the Palatine has thrown its shadow across the room in the way a large public event can remeasure smaller private ones. Prima’s own marriage is approaching in the guild calendar—after mourning, after contracts, after Mother Livia has inspected every object that may legally move between houses. There will be food at sane hours, a procession without a vehicular empire, witnesses who work with cloth and fire. Yet suddenly people in this kitchen are speaking to her as if Cornelia’s saffron veil has issued a challenge in couture.

“I do not want a bigger wedding,” Prima says, as if someone had accused her of ambition.

“Good,” says Eirene. “Big weddings are mostly other people eating your future in public.”

“I want the papers at my table.”

“They will be.” Antonia speaks before caution can make it legalistic. “Your papers, your witnesses, your mother-in-law’s calendar at the correct weight.”

“And no letters arriving in Greek.” She says it lightly, then sees the room take it too seriously. “That was a joke.”

Daphne says, “In this house jokes are minuted as early drafts.”

Prima descends and inspects the hanging from floor level. One corner falls wrong. She goes up the stool again, Barbatus shifting below her without touching her ankle or making a drama of gods. Her competence is a relief from symbolism, and Antonia stands among sauce steam and copper pans thinking how much of a life arrears of love expresses itself as: somebody competent, in the middle height of a room, being handed pins at the exact moment they are needed.

“Titus holds ladders,” Daphne says, meditatively, “and Barbatus hands pins. We are becoming a support organisation.”

“Revolution,” Eirene says, “at catering prices.”
