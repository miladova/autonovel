Daphne’s knowledge, insofar as the house can map it, advances by the method of all gifted listeners: she notices where the music has been altered. Barbatus no longer announces Prima in the tradeswoman’s formula he uses for the chair-menders and lamp men. Eirene has begun to ask whether soup is needed *for Tuesday*, as if a day of the week had acquired a pulse. Diomedes, whose face in an emergency could be filed as blank stationery, has invented a door-book category so boring that its very dullness shines.

Daphne says nothing. This, in Daphne, is not discretion so much as rehearsal.

She begins to leave fruit where Prima will find it. Not at the table—too public, too near contract. On the windowsill of the linen room: two early plums, a small bunch of grapes out of season and therefore scandalously eloquent. Prima eats one plum and leaves the other, divided into exact halves on a clean square of cloth. Rome is full of languages spoken by women who are not supposed to have anything to say to each other except through men.

One afternoon, when rain has emptied the courtyard, Daphne finds Prima washing dye from her hands at the basin. “You have her way of listening,” Daphne says. “As if a mistake may drop something valuable.”

“Whose way?”

“Now you have her way of answering too.” Daphne examines her own nails, which have survived the winter better than her illusions. “I don’t require the score. I sing in this house; I am not its treasury. But if you ever need someone to not know something *at* Marcus, I have become very accomplished.”

Prima dries her hands carefully, finger by finger. “Did you like him?”

“I liked being looked at by a future that hadn’t yet learned its price.” The answer is too polished, and Daphne knows it; a brief, less musical honesty alters her face. “Yes. Sometimes. That is exactly why the necklace went home. If it had meant nothing, I could have kept it.”

It is the first confidence offered to Prima at the Caelian house that has not arrived via Antonia, and it changes the room’s weights. Prima does not reciprocate with the vault. She says instead, “My betrothed once brought me hot bread in the rain and tried to pretend he had been passing the street on other business. Four ovens, no other business.”

Daphne laughs, startled into her speaking voice. “Did you accept the fiction?”

“I accepted the bread. The fiction is still under review.”

After that, the fruit exchange becomes regular and unspoken, like any sound treaty. Antonia observes it and says nothing to either woman. She has paid enough for the lesson that kinship, once discovered, does not report to a single office.

Marcus remains the more theoretical relative. Prima had seen him through a doorway as a young man made of assumptions and expensive cloth. Now he occupies a category no guild register provides. She tries out the word *brother* while counting and drops three stitches. She tries *the senator’s son* and produces a border so regular it looks machine-made. She settles temporarily on *Marcus*, a name with no instructions attached.

“What if he writes badly?” she asks.

“He will.”

“What if he writes well?”

“That will be more troublesome. We shall have to believe him.”

“And if I don’t answer?”

Antonia is testing a cushion seam and does not look up too quickly. “Then you will not answer. It is a reply in every office except his family’s, which is learning.”

Prima nods, but the needle pauses. Freedom, when other people describe it on your behalf, can sound suspiciously like another arrangement. She has begun to notice how often her choices are being pre-approved by people eager to prove that they are not arranging her. “I might answer.”

“You might.”

“I might ask him a question.”

“You might ask him several. The Cornelii prefer questions in committee, but he is showing signs of independent governance.”

“What would I ask?”

This time Antonia’s restraint is more expensive. There are questions she has wanted put to that house for twenty years, elegantly sharpened questions, questions with subclauses. She leaves them all in their drawer. “That would be yours,” she says. “Not mine in your handwriting.”

Prima takes a new length of red wool and begins the next flower. “I would ask what he was told to call my father.”

Outside, Barbatus tells somebody to move a mule in a voice that suggests the mule is the reasonable party. The courtyard fills with ordinary racket. Antonia looks down at her own imperfect seam and understands, again, that entrusting the girl with the truth includes not renting her out as the courier of other people’s unfinished business.
