Marcus’s departure is the first family ceremony at which no one tries to improve the cast by leaving somebody out of the reported version. He goes east not to a governorship wrapped in ancestral expectation but to a staff appointment where his duties include supply returns, interpreters and the mapping of roads that exist mostly as opinions held by tired men. Servilia sees to his equipment as if outfitting a province she may need to repossess. Lentulus sees to his debts, which is more loving and less photogenic.

Onesimus packs a spare cloak strap without being told. It has been mended by a needlewoman across the river at trade rates and finished during a series of transactions nobody at the Palatine is expected to declaim at breakfast.

“You will write plainly,” Servilia instructs him at the gate. “Greek is for showing Athens you were educated. Latin is for telling your mother whether your socks correspond to the manifest.”

“Mother, the army has quartermasters.”

“The army has quartermasters in the way this family has clerks. Their existence is not a substitute for accuracy.”

Lentulus gives him no deathless family sentence. The cupboard under his grandfather’s mask contains a letter in Latin with four lines in it and a sealed tablet whose terrible imperative has lately stopped shouting. He says, “Do nothing where other men’s names are spoken that you would not enter in your own hand.”

Marcus hears the riverine arithmetic in it and does not disfigure the moment by attribution. “Noisily,” he says.

“Preferably not during a night march.”

The letters improve as promised. They begin full of roads. They become, over months, interested in the men who move baskets along them. At Antioch, in a room where the heat enters through every architectural boast, Marcus writes to Transtiberim with the third-person discipline of a man who has learned the price of putting himself at the head of other people’s weather. He sends tablets “for accounts when required” and does not mention an unborn child because he does not know, yet, in a form he is entitled to act on. Fate, receiving this, registers a small protest at the loss of its usual office.

Servilia reads his home letters and annotates the supply figures. Lentulus reads them and says less each time, which in their household is becoming the sound of a man contributing accurately. In one, Marcus writes: *The general is much praised for the river crossing. The river was crossed by engineers who had been up since the fourth hour and a Syrian woman who told us which landing would not eat the mules. We put the praise where the despatch could reach it. I am learning.*

Lentulus takes this letter into the atrium and stands for a while among the masks, not seeking permission. He puts it, after a considered moment, not in the cupboard, but in the ordinary family archive where a clerk can retrieve it during business hours. Progress, in the Cornelii, has begun to look like filing.
