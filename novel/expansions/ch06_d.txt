“Then have the pet's keeper to dinner with an explanation,” Servilia says, in the tone of a woman closing a tablet, and goes out to do the household's night round — kitchen damped, doors witnessed, the day's small treasons of waste and gossip corrected before they harden into systems. She does it on feet that make no announcement. Lentulus has sometimes thought his wife and the mistress of the Caelian are the two people in Rome who understand that authority is mostly the management of approach: no noise until she is in a room, then the room owes her an explanation. He does not say it. There are comparisons a man may think in his own house at night that would constitute, in daylight, a foreign policy.

Marcus is summoned to the study, where being spoken to has been conducted for three generations under a bust of the grandfather whose mask is in the atrium and whose opinions are therefore present twice, in case the room forgets whose walls these are. He comes in still in his dinner clothes, with the particular brightness of a young man who has been drinking just enough to have located his honesty and not yet enough to have lost the address.

“You discussed the committee at my table,” Lentulus says. “In front of the Avidii. In front of your sister.”

“I described the committee. As the baths describe it. As the aediles' own clerks describe it, Father, after the second cup, in establishments your committee is inspecting.” Marcus stands because the study only offers one chair to juniors and he is too angry, or too accurate, to take it. “If the description is a problem, the committee might try being something else.”

“The committee is a registry of—”

“One house,” Marcus says. “At dawn. With a rod.” He does a thing then that the room notices: he stops performing. The brightness goes out of him like a lamp being carried into another room, and what is left is twenty years old and tired in a way his education has no form for. “Cornelia looked at me when I said it as if I'd sold *her*, at the table, in front of the buyers. I keep thinking: that is the look this house teaches. I have been practising it in mirrors since I was twelve, I just didn't know the occasion.”

Lentulus looks at his son the way he looks at provincial reports he suspects of being true in the wrong direction. “Your sister,” he says, choosing the words as if they were good coin from a bad purse, “will marry where the family can honourably place her, with a settlement her mother will negotiate to the last clause, into a house whose money is newer than ours and whose son, by every report including his conduct at my table under provocation, is not a fool. If that is selling, then every farm my ancestors bought with a bride's dowry is a market stall, and the masks in the atrium are shop signs. We call it *alliance* because the word obliges both parties to behave as if the transaction were nobler than appetite. The obligation is real even if the word is upholstery. Most of Rome is."

It is a good speech. It has the columns: family, honour, the etymology of a euphemism audited like a harbour due. Marcus listens to it the way a man listens to weather in a country he means to emigrate from.

“And the house on the Caelian?” he says. “What word obliges us there? Because I dine there, Father — you know I dine there; Onesimus knows by professional forgetting, every bath attendant in Rome knows by retail — and the woman who runs it sends back my messengers with lessons in drinking slowly, and her singer returned a necklace this week because the stones were—” He catches himself, too late, as young men do; the sentence completes itself in his father's face.

“Glass,” Lentulus says. “You gave a woman glass and called them emeralds, and she returned them with a compliment, and now you are hers for a month and truthful for a week.” Marcus stares. Lentulus, who does not know he is quoting the mistress of the house word for word — who cannot know, unless the plumbing now runs in both directions and uphill, which in this city it generally does — finds he has produced the analysis out of whatever men of his experience produce such things: the archive behind the ribs. “It was done to your grandfather by a singer from Alexandria in the consulship of — it doesn't matter. The technique is older than the aqueducts. What matters, Marcus, is whether you learned the price of the stones or the price of the lie. A Cornelius may pay either. He may not remain ignorant of the exchange rate.”

“I learned she wouldn't be bought at my rate,” Marcus says, quietly. “Which in this family makes her either a bad investment or—”

“Or kin to nobody we can afford.” It comes out before Lentulus can file it. The room with the duplicated grandfather receives the sentence in the order of the evening, not the order of the questions, and Marcus — who is twenty, and who will remember this winter in a scroll-case for years — looks at his father with the first intimation that the locked rooms of this house are not a style of carpentry.

“Go to bed,” Lentulus says, dismantling the moment with a father's authority, the cheapest tool in the house and the only one that always fits. “Not out. Not at dawn, not by the garden gate. Tomorrow you will call on the Avidii with a note in your mother's Latin apologising for your advocate's manner at dinner. You will not mention committees, emeralds, or honesty. Honesty, Marcus, is a long position. Take it from a house that has been short of it before.”
