“The premises, then,” the inspector says, in the tone of a man moving to evidence he can measure, and Antonia rises and gives him the house the way she gives a new guest the menu: in full, in order, with nothing saved for a second, more expensive telling. Diomedes walks a pace behind with the tablets. The clerk, who is younger than the rod he carries and in love, as clerks are, with the prospect of scandal in good rooms, walks a pace behind Diomedes with his eyes drinking.

They begin, at Antonia's direction, in the kitchen, which is not where an inspector of morals expects to begin, and is therefore exactly where she begins them all: landlords, tax men, a praetor's steward two years ago with a warrant he eventually used as a napkin. A kitchen argues. Hers argues in copper — pans scoured to mirrors, the new stove drawing cleanly, Ammonius enthroned among his fires with a ladle in his hand and an expression that suggests the aediles may inspect his saucepans when they have published their own. The stores are chalked and dated. The wine cellar book matches the seals. The inspector, who has inspected the kitchens of the Subura and does not speak of them the way veterans do not speak, lets his clerk write *stores in order* with a hand that has had, Antonia guesses, little practice at the phrase.

“The women eat from the same kitchen?” he asks.

“The women eat first from the same kitchen, before the guests are admitted. A house rule.” She does not offer the reason. The reason is twenty years old and lives in Baiae, in a house where the staff ate what was left and learnt their value from the leavings. “You may ask them. It is permitted to ask them anything that is written in your commission.”

He asks Philomela, because Philomela is in the corridor with a lyre and the air of a woman it would be a clerical error to overlook. Philomela is twenty-six, freeborn in Corinth, professionally a singer in the way generals are professionally soldiers: it explains her rank in any room she enters. She gives the inspector her registration particulars the way she gives a difficult interval — precisely, and with a faint suggestion that lesser instruments exist. Age. Status. The name of the agent who brought her to Rome, a man whose contract Antonia bought out in the first month and burnt in this kitchen's predecessor, a ceremony Philomela attended with a cup of the house's second-best wine, *not the best*, she still says, *she's not sentimental, our mistress; she filed it under fuel*.

“Are you content in this house?” the inspector asks. It is not in his commission. It escapes him the way questions do in clean kitchens.

“Officer,” Philomela says, “I am a singer. Contentment is for audiences.” She tunes a string; the note goes through the corridor like a wire through cheese. “I am paid, I am registered, I sing what I choose and I refuse what I don't, and when my voice goes — voices go, officer, write that down too — I have money with a Greek at the Basilica that no husband, patron or tax farmer has the address of. If that is contentment in your Latin, then I am content. In mine it is called *not being a fool*, which my mother taught me in Corinth at her usual rates.”

The clerk writes nothing. There is no column for it. Antonia watches the inspector decide — she can see him do it, the way you can see a man at her tables decide not to say the true thing over the fish — to make the rest of the inspection be about doors.

So doors it is: widths, hinges, the number of dining rooms and their disposition, the little courtyard room where linen is kept and needlewomen are given the light, the bedrooms upstairs that are, for the register, *resting rooms for artists*, and are in fact whatever paying adults have agreed between themselves behind them, under house rules the inspector has not been commissioned to imagine. He measures. The rod reports that the house is the size it is. In the great dining room the new hanging stops him — not its beauty, though Eirene, passing with flowers, notes him registering that too and tells the kitchen later — its border. A man whose trade is paper looks at two hundred small red flowers, each one stitched, each one paid for, and has for a moment the expression of a person who has wandered out of his commission into the actual economy.

“From Transtiberim,” Antonia says. “A fuller's daughter. The house commissions its cloth work across the river. Better light, better prices, better women. You may record the neighbourhood as a moral influence, if the form has room.”

“The form,” the inspector says, surprising himself into honesty, which clean kitchens will do, “has very little room, madam. It was designed by men who expected smaller facts.”

They return to the small dining room by way of the courtyard, where the damp map is drying in a bar of sun and the roses are looking, as they always look in this house, handsomely overwatered, like guests who will not be refused a third cup. Barbatus is at the street door admitting a delivery of lamp oil, checking seals with thumbs the size of small hammers. The inspector looks at him and looks away — not fear; a kind of professional reassessment, as of a wall discovered to be structural.

“Your doorkeeper is ex-arena.”

“My doorkeeper is ex-everything Rome could charge admission for. He has killed no one on these premises, which is more than the Senate can say of its foreign policy, and he breaks up fights by standing up. The house recommends the technique to the committee.”

It is more than she usually gives an official, and she gives it deliberately, because a man who has been honest about his form deserves a sentence he can carry back like a souvenir. He almost smiles — the same almost-smile the house will see again from the prefect's man in the spring, though neither of them knows it yet — and sits down to his tablets and his watered wine with the air of a soldier returning to a frontier he suspects of being quieter than Rome.
