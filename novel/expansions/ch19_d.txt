The marriage papers generate their own subsidiary marriages: between families and accountants, between ancestral pride and shipping schedules, between the bride’s musical education and a father-in-law who regards every recurring expense as a vessel requiring inspection before launch. Servilia sits at the centre of it all with three clerks, two alternative versions of the property schedule, and the calm of a general who has inherited a battlefield already mapped by men who assumed the women would walk around it.

Cornelia is required to be present for portions of the proceedings and decorative for others. She distinguishes the two with dangerous accuracy. When the question of her personal slaves arises, she interrupts a fluent exchange between stewards to state that her maid will accompany her, that the old nurse will be pensioned rather than transferred like a chair, and that nobody is to sell her music master’s replacement out of the household accounts in her name without asking her. The clerks look toward Servilia for instructions. Servilia looks at her daughter and says, “Enter it.”

It is not rebellion. Rome would have a word for rebellion and a flute-girl waiting outside court to illustrate it. It is something quieter and harder to vaccinate against: a young woman discovering that a contract, if read carefully, contains surfaces on which a hand may rest.

Avidius the younger visits under the usual diplomatic constraints. He brings, after consultation with his father and, one suspects, several maritime deities, not jewellery but a schedule of the house he is preparing: which rooms catch morning light, where a music room might be made without disturbing the stores, whether the roof can bear the modification his mother insists is traditional. He lays the plan before Cornelia as if it were cargo for inspection.

“You need not look so surprised,” he says. “I was told you ask questions.”

“By whom?”

“By a person who said it as a warning and another who said it as praise. I chose the more economical interpretation.”

Cornelia studies the plan. “This room will be dark after noon.”

“Yes.”

“Then it should not be mine.”

Avidius leans over, pencil in hand. “This one is larger, but my mother says the floor complains.”

“All floors complain. It is how they participate.” She says it deadpan, and watches him fail to decide whether she is joking. He laughs a fraction late, which is better than the prompt, well-trained laughter of young men who have been told their bride is accomplished.

Servilia observes from the third chair, her face performing genealogy. She has not procured love; even her powers have provincial boundaries. She may have procured the conditions under which respect does not have to be smuggled.

Later, alone with her mother among the dress lengths, Cornelia asks why everyone has been behaving as though the house contained an expensive silence.

“Every house does,” Servilia says. “Ours has recently improved its manners.”

“Is it about Father and the committee?”

“It is about money, family, and the difference between the two.”

“That is not an answer.”

“No. It is a direction.” Servilia takes the girl’s wrist, not to correct it, and turns the hand palm-up as she might inspect a glove for fit. There is no pale mark there, no household legend. “One day you will be told a thing which was kept from you for reasons partly good and partly ours. When it happens, remember that I said *partly*. It is a more useful inheritance than rage.”

Cornelia looks at their joined hands. “That is ominous.”

“It is Roman. We put our omens indoors and give them dinner.”

“I shall put it with the other things nobody explains until after the contract.”

“Put it at the front of that box.”

The girl goes back to the fabrics, choosing not the richest but the one with a colour that deepens in low light. Her mother watches the choice with an approval she does not cheapen by explanation. There is more of the household in the girl than gloom predicts. That is perhaps what households are for: to surprise the management without overthrowing it altogether.
