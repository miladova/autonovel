“He meant it well,” Servilia repeats, testing the phrase as if it were a coin her family used to issue without adequate silver. “My husband meant nothing for thirty years, which gave his intentions an excellent credit rating. Marcus is overcorrecting. He will require supervision until meaning and manners converge.”

“Prima will supervise by not being impressed.”

“That is a form of instruction our tutors never mastered.” Servilia looks down at the table, an unusually honest direction for her. “There is another matter. The wedding settlement has released a small amount which my husband wanted routed—let us not soil the afternoon with the Greek’s exact architecture—to the girl’s household.”

“No.”

Antonia says it without heat, and consequently it fills the room. Servilia does not look startled. She looks, if anything, reassured that the frontier is defended by someone who knows its geography.

“I expected opposition.”

“Then your husband expected a refusal and wanted it delivered by a better diplomat.”

“He wanted to be told whether a further contribution would be accuracy or interference.”

“Prima has been provided under forms she can show her guild without lying at it. Any additional money now begins to look like your house purchasing a son’s correspondence rights, or a father’s relief at post-wedding prices.”

Servilia inclines her head. “Interference.”

“With excellent table manners.”

“Those are the expensive kind.” She sets down her cup in an exact circle of damp left by some earlier conference. “The amount will remain where it is, in case need ever drafts a better argument. I shall tell my husband a refusal is not an interruption.”

“He is learning the phrase at several frontiers.”

“He learned it here.” Servilia rises a fraction, then sits again, which for her constitutes an earthquake. “One more discourtesy. When Cornelia asks, one day, whether her father was—” Words present themselves in all registers: wicked, weak, young, Roman. She dismisses the parade. “What will your house say?”

Antonia considers the other woman across the cakes, neither ally nor defendant. “My house will say your husband wrote a clause and that women made it work. If she comes to me herself, she will have more than our party line. Until then she has enough houses speaking from behind curtains.”

Servilia nods, once. It has the weight of a treaty clause nobody will ever sign. “A woman should know which questions are hers in her own handwriting.”

“Did Prima say that?”

“No. I have some original sentences.”

“I am relieved. The family was becoming a quotation bureau.”
