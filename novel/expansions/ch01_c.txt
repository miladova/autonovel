Servilia leaves him to the dressing, which in this house is a liturgy with a staff. The toga is not put on a senator; it is built around him, fold by negotiated fold, by a slave whose grandfather dressed a Cornelius for a triumph and whose professional opinion, delivered through his hands, is that the modern chest is narrower and the modern occasion fatter. Lentulus stands for it the way he stands for clients: as an act of office performed by other people's competence. In the bronze mirror his reflection is assembled by degrees out of the room's grey light — first a shape, then a senator, then, if you know where to look, which Felix does and the dresser does and Servilia has for nineteen years, the man inside the arrangement, holding still.

“Your father,” the dresser says — he is old enough to be allowed uncommissioned sentences, a liberty the household grants by age the way Antonia's grants by invoice — “wore his to the left an inch further. For the sword arm, he said. There was no sword, in the Forum, in my memory.”

“There was always a sword in my father's memory,” Lentulus says. “It is what the memory was *for*.”

He does not say — the room is not one of his locked ones, it is full of hands — that he has started, this winter, to hear his father's sentences arriving in his own voice at committee pitch: *order, decency, precedent*, the washed words, the ones that have been through the fullers so often they have lost their original colour and can be worn to anything. At twenty-four he believed a man chose his vocabulary the way he chose his friends. At forty-five he knows a man's vocabulary is issued to him, like the toga's folds, by people who dressed his grandfather, and that the freedom lies, if it lies anywhere, in the inch to the left or right that nobody else notices.

Cornelia is in the corridor when he comes out, which means she has timed it, which means Servilia knows she has timed it and has permitted the corridor as a theatre. She is dressed for the dedication in the white of a senator's unmarried daughter — a colour that is also a locked room, and better guarded than most in this house — and she has her mother's way of asking a question by arranging to be present when it answers itself.

“Will the emperor speak?” she asks.

“He will dedicate. Speaking is what the inscriptions do afterwards, at greater length and with fewer interruptions.”

“Marcus says the column will have the whole war on it, going round and round.”

“Marcus says a great many things going round and round.” But he says it without the edge he keeps for the boy in public, and his daughter receives the distinction the way she receives everything in this family: filed, accurately, for a future she is not yet charging interest on. “The war will go round and round, yes. Wars do, on stone. It saves the survivors the trouble of describing the parts no one can see from the ground.”

She thinks about this with her whole forehead. At sixteen her thinking is still visible, a condition her education is designed to cure by the vintage. “Who is it for, then? The part nobody can see.”

“The gods,” her father says, because it is the answer the dedication requires, and then — the inch to the left, unnoticed by anyone except the girl it was always going to matter to — “and the people who carried the baskets. Someday somebody will climb up inside it and read it all. Historians,” he says, “are just clients who arrive very late and cannot be turned away.”

Cornelia nods as if a purse of the middle size has been placed, correctly, in her hands. Servilia, appearing at the corridor's end with the household deploying behind her like a fleet in line, takes in father, daughter, and the exact quality of the moment she has scheduled, and says only, “The litters,” which in her Latin covers a multitude of approvals she would regard it as wasteful to itemise.
