The door-book version arrives in fragments at dinner. A client of the Avidii, full of wedding food and maritime equity, reports that the Cornelian bride has a voice; a cousin of somebody says the Greek song was “a compliment to the shipping interest”; a man who has been wrong about every public event since the dedication announces that Servilia has lost control of her daughter. The house records the first two accounts under Amusements and leaves the third to natural decay.

The Greek’s version arrives on paper and is therefore more dangerous. Diomedes reads it in the office and gives Antonia the shape without the seals: dowry funds moving out of the Palatine, certain Campanian obligations eased, an Avidian settlement entering Cornelia’s name through channels a hostile accountant would find unusually well lit. There has been, in addition, a payment to a music master described so blamelessly that any future husband will have to be a philosopher to unpick it.

“Servilia,” Diomedes says, with professional awe, “has taught the harbour to put money where her daughter can reach it and call the arrangement respect for art.”

“Art is an excellent cloak. It has pockets.”

“Also, madam, a modest sum has passed from the Campanian paper toward our side of the current obligations. Nothing addressed. Nothing named. The olive affair remains in its columns like a well-trained citizen.”

Antonia looks at the figures without touching them. There was a time—seventeen, nineteen, Baiae, Naples—when money arrived in her vicinity only in sleeves, reports, threats or stories other people could deny by breakfast. Now sums travel across the city trailing clerks. It is not justice, which would require a different empire. It is at least weather that has learned to sign.

“Enter it as received.”

“It is entered.”

“Without a metaphor, Diomedes.”

“The third book has no metaphors before dinner.”

From the Acilii, by Eirene’s cousinly intelligence service, comes the version with knives in it: which aunt approved of the song and which required smelling salts; whether Marcus’s sobriety is seen as reform or as an ominous novelty; whether young Avidius looked at his bride like a bill of lading or like a person. Eirene delivers the report while cutting up a chicken for stock, a process that lends all social intelligence a useful sense of proportion.

“The boy looked as if he’d been handed weather and asked to govern it kindly,” she says. “Could be worse. I have seen bridegrooms look like tax collectors discovering arrears.”

“And Cornelia?”

“Like a singer who has read the contract.”

Antonia files that, privately, in the book with no formal headings. A girl who reads the contract. It is the difference between generations compressed into one domestic verb.

“Would Servilia approve of your cousin?”

“She approves of all information. She disapproves of being seen to receive it. There are management styles worse, love.”

The three accounts settle, as accounts do, into a picture none of them individually owned: a wedding accomplished, a troublesome young woman exported into an allied house with papers of her own, a son behaving at a rate that has destabilised bathhouse economics, a father discovering that being present in the present tense has administrative consequences.
