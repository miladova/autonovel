Prima's day, when she has a commission on the right bank, begins before the vats are lit, in a room that smells of yesterday's work whatever she does about it. This is not a complaint. Prima's relationship to smell is professional and, in its way, loyal: fullers' earth is honest, stale urine is merely chemistry with a history, and a clean garment coming off the frames has a smell she would put, in any honest census of Rome's perfumes, above rose oil bought to impress other people's wives. She heats her father's honey drink before she washes, because his cough is worst at the day's cold hinge and because if she washes first he will get up to do it himself, out of a doctrine — learned across a marriage and now carried alone like a hod — that a man who lets a house wake around him in bed is being buried by instalments.

“You're a garrison of one,” she tells him.

“I'm a fuller,” Vibius says. “We rise so Rome can pretend it was always clean.”

He drinks. She combs her hair at the window where the light comes in off the yard — grey light, working light, nothing like the Caelian courtyard's bar of sun, and she catches herself making the comparison and is annoyed at her own treachery. The comparison is new. It has come with the commission. She has sewn for good houses before — a priest's widow on the Aventine whose hems Prima still dreams about, badly; a contractor's wife who paid in compliments and festival cakes — but the Caelian house is the first place that has ever paid her in a way that made her father's face do that thing over the soup. *Itemised.* The mistress across the river writes Prima's work down the way the guild writes a master's: by the piece, the thread separate, the sum at the bottom with a line under it. Prima has been an entry in other people's books before, usually under her father's name, as *daughter, sewing*. Under her own name she is taller. She knows this is paperwork and she knows it is not only paperwork, and she is savage with herself, combing, for being the kind of girl — woman, the mistress would say; the mistress corrects words the way Prima corrects seams, in passing, without permission — who can be bought with bookkeeping.

Her betrothed's mother has no such doubts, only different ones. On the stairs of the insula, where she stations herself on market mornings with the tactical instinct of a customs post, she receives Prima's news of a third day's work on the Caelian with a stillness you could hang cloth on.

“Three days for a border.”

“Two hundred flowers, Mother Livia. I counted them to the mistress. She pays by the piece.”

“She pays.” The older woman's eyes go, as they always go, to Prima's hands, her hem, the place where a reputation would show wear if reputations were garments — and to Mother Livia, who has raised four sons on bread and inspection, they are exactly garments. “A house like that pays everyone at the door, so the street can see. It's what happens off the street that gets into the cloth.”

“Then I shall stay on the street side of the courtyard,” Prima says, “where the light is, with the doorkeeper, who frightens the customers for a living. He is very respectable, Mother Livia. He has killed people in the arena for the emperor's grandfather and now he checks lamp oil. Rome promotes.”

It earns her, after a moment in which the customs post considers a seizure, a snort that in another woman would be a laugh. “You read too much.”

“I read Titus' accounts when his sums won't. Tell him his yeast man's bill is wrong by eleven sesterces in the yeast man's favour, which is the direction bills are usually wrong in. It's in the second column. He doesn't check second columns because the first one is his.”

This is Prima's armour and she knows it: usefulness, laid on thick as fuller's earth. A reading bride is a defect; a bride who finds eleven sesterces in a second column is a dowry with eyes. She goes down the stairs, with her work-box clutched against her ribs, rehearsing the ferry money — two coins for the boat if the bridge crowd is bad, one if she walks by the island and the water is low — and by the time she reaches the river the morning has become the kind of Roman morning that makes foreigners write letters home: light on the water, gulls from the sea quarrelling over the offal boats, the hills of the right bank standing up out of woodsmoke with their temples and tenements indistinguishable at this distance, which is the city's best angle and it knows it.

She walks. Prima always walks when the choice is hers; the ferry is full of men making room in ways that require management, and she prefers to spend management on thread. The island bridge, with its temple and its hospital slaves being aired like bedding on the parapet; the cattle market smelling of the beginning of dinner; the long climb through streets that change their prices every hundred paces. She likes the climb. She likes — she has never said this, she files it under things a fuller's daughter need not say — arriving at the Caelian house slightly out of breath, as if at the top of a sentence. Inside, the porter gives her the small room off the courtyard and a stool with its back to the traffic, *by the mistress's instruction*, and Barbatus, who frightens customers for a living, moves his own stool six feet without being asked, so that his shadow falls between her and the dining rooms. She has a fuller father's understanding of what is being laundered here. Her reputation is being washed in advance, by experts, at a cost somebody is itemising as thread.

She sets the frame. She counts, chin tucked, mouth open on the number. The red flowers have a rhythm like a work-song and she goes into it the way the men in the vats go into the treading, not thinking, in the approved manner, at all — which is when, as every needlewoman and fuller and soldier knows, the thinking arrives that has been waiting its turn. Today it is her mother's voice, two years dead and clearer than the living: *count the stairs, so the stairs don't count you.* Ninety, in Baiae. Prima has seen the sea once, from Ostia, on a guild outing that cost her father a week's coughing; Baiae lives in her head as a staircase standing in blue light, with her mother on it, young, carrying linen, counting. She does not know why the mistress of this house went quiet — *the way rich women go quiet at a price*, she told her father, and he had held his blanket like a rail and not answered — when Prima passed on the stairs saying. Perhaps the mistress has been to Baiae. Rich women go to Baiae the way other people's linen goes to the fullers: regularly, and to be improved. Perhaps the mistress once had a laundress.

The needle goes in. The needle comes out. Somewhere beyond the courtyard a singer is practising, running a phrase up a staircase of notes and down again, counting, counting, and Prima — who does not eat in this house, whose father has protected her reputation from its kitchen, who will drink water in a moment from the mistress's own hand because the mistress will not have a needlewoman faint among her roses — feels, without any language for it that her street would accept, that she is being watched the way work is watched by someone who has done it. Not the customers' watching. A tradeswoman's. It straightens her back without her permission. She sews the next flower as if it were evidence.
