Cornelia watches the latter part of the dedication from the Avidian enclosure, where the harbour interest has arranged itself with better cushions than the nobility and a much more realistic supply of water. Her husband is explaining the frieze to a younger cousin by translating every military feature into tonnage. “That is not a siege tower,” he says, “that is a warehouse that has made a decision.”

She has been married long enough now to recognise which of his jokes are educational policy. This one is both.

Her music room floor has been repaired but retains, as he promised, a limited right of complaint under the stairs. The master is paid in advance, quarterly, in a schedule so irreproachable that even Servilia has run out of improvements to suggest at breakfast. Cornelia sings there most mornings. She does not sing today. A dedication is not a music room, and a young matron of two houses has learned which rooms are hers because other people are standing in them applauding a column.

And yet, as the flutes take a phrase she knows, she feels the Greek song enter her breath without knocking. Her mother is across an official distance in grey. Their eyes do not meet; Servilia has never wasted alliance-scale emotion on line of sight. Still, Cornelia puts both feet flat on the stone, the way Daphne once instructed her in a sentence delivered through three intermediaries and one argument about lemons.

Her husband notices. “The floor is not complaining here.”

“It is municipal. It has other management.”

“You look as if you might sing at it.”

“I am saving myself for rooms where the accounts are opened.”

He considers this with the care of a man who knows her sentences now sometimes have sisters behind them. “Should I be alarmed?”

“Only if your invoices are late.”

The joke goes through their marriage at the proper small scale, unrecorded. Below them Marcus is doing his public nothing. Her father is doing his older nothing with more expense of spirit. Her mother is being an institution in a veil. The whole family, Cornelia thinks, is being carved in a medium less permanent than stone and much harder to read from any single side.

She does not know yet what will be told her, or at whose table, or under which heading of family information arriving in the wrong century. She knows a box with copies in a room she can reach. She knows a husband who pays musicians on time and a mother who says *partly* as if it were a province to be governed with respect. She has begun to keep, in the box, a page of questions for which no adult has provided the order.

At the top, in her own hand, it says: *Why does the house go quiet when the Caelian singer rests?* Underneath, later and smaller: *Why do I know the Greek tune in my breath before I have decided?*

Questions are cheap to store. That is why families prefer them to answers. Cornelia folds the dedication programme around nothing in particular and keeps it anyway. Paper improves with the bearer.
