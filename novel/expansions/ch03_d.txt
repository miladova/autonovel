Daphne stays in the small room when Antonia has gone, with the glass stones cooling in her palm like a story cooling into evidence. She has been free for three years, and freedom, she is learning in instalments, is mostly the right to decide which instructions to be angry about. Antonia's instruction is good. That is what makes it galling. A bad order can be disobeyed with a clear conscience and a song; a good one must be followed in full view of one's own judgment, nodding.

Eirene comes in with the day's flowers for the small room — winter roses, forced and faintly resentful — and looks at the necklace the way she looks at everything expensive and unearned in this house: as a management problem with a clasp.

“He'll bring a real one next time,” Daphne says.

“He'll bring a worse lie and a better dinner story. They learn in that order, those boys; it is a family curriculum.” Eirene sets the flowers down and begins, out of thirty years of habit, to sort them by the life left in them. “I was given a necklace once by a man who owned the house I worked in, the street it stood in and, as the law then filed it, me. Real stones. Wonderful stones. I wore them at his table for two years and when he died his son's steward took them off my neck with a receipt. *Household property*, he said. He wasn't even unkind. Kindness wasn't in the inventory either.”

Daphne closes her hand over the glass. “Antonia never tells that story.”

“Antonia tells nobody's story, including Antonia's. It is why this house can charge what it charges.” Eirene holds a rose up to the light, finds it acceptable, forgives it. “Give the glass back beautifully, as instructed. Keep the information, as instructed. And when you are my age, Daphne, buy your own stones with money that has your name in a Greek's book, and let some senator's son explain to the room what they are. There is no music like it. I have heard Philomela. I know whereof I speak.”
