There is a pause then in which both women become aware of being forty—not old by the standards of their households, not young by the standards of their memories. Servilia’s hands are unadorned by afternoon policy except for one ring that could buy a boat if boats were sold by women who understood rings. Antonia’s carry a lifetime of work disguised as management: ink near one nail, a small burn from supervising a sauce at too close a range, no rings at all when she is about her own house.

“You did not come for an audit,” Antonia says.

“I have auditors. Half my family now qualifies.” Servilia takes water. “I came because a woman of my order is eventually required to look at the part of the map the roads were built around.”

“An expedition.”

“Do not let it go to your provincial importance.”

“Madam, I am an institution now. Rome writes to me by appointment.”

The smallest alteration occurs around Servilia’s mouth—not a smile, an adjustment to light. “You would have enjoyed certain rooms.”

“I was not invited to enjoy them.”

“No.” The admission is unadorned. Servilia does not arrange it into an apology, which would be an insult to both their intelligence. “Your exclusion was part of my marriage settlement. I did not draft that clause. I lived well under it.”

Antonia feels the sentence settle where so many others have failed to land. It does not open a compensation schedule. It does something more useful and less theatrical: it gives the room two adults in it.

“Would you have married him knowing everything?” she asks, because the afternoon has already broken several laws whose jurisdiction neither woman respects.

Servilia considers. She is not a woman who answers hypothetical questions from the surface. “I married the Cornelii. He was included as resident management. Had I known the child lived, I would have required a better settlement for the child—and a worse one for my father-in-law. Whether that is morality or pricing, I leave to priests and the Greek.”

“He would charge for the distinction.”

“All the best authorities do.”

From elsewhere in the building comes the muted beginning of dinner: crockery, water, Ammonius discovering a philosophical objection to mint. Servilia listens as to an allied province through a wall. “My daughter has your clause in her music schedule, by the way. Not the words. The structure. Fees in her name. Refusal preserved in accountancy. My husband believes it is his influence.”

“Men require a pedigree for ideas.”

“Hers comes from several houses.” Servilia looks towards the hanging with the corrected corner. “When I tell her—about the river—it will not be as a scandal. I shall tell her as family information that arrives in the wrong century, like most of our property.”

“That is your business.”

“Yes. I am informing you as a courtesy, not consulting you as an annex.”

Antonia nods. Courtesy, between these two, is the only form of diplomatic relations currently solvent. “Prima decides what she receives from your house. One letter at a time. No choir.”

“My son has been instructed that sincerity is not a ram.”

“Then there has been progress in the siege department.”

This time the adjustment around Servilia’s mouth does qualify. “We are,” she says, “a modern family. We communicate by withholding correctly.”
