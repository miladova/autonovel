She has considered the visit since the contract signing, and considered is the correct Servilian verb: weighed, routed, stripped of accidental witnesses. She does not tell Lentulus. He would formulate a policy about it and then spend the afternoon obeying the policy in a room full of masks. She does not tell Marcus, whose new integrity is still young enough to be startled into literature. She tells Onesimus that she is paying a call connected with the pastry at the Acilii, which is true in the sense that Rome is full of pastry and false in every operational detail.

The chair goes not directly to the Caelian but by a route that takes in a shrine where she is known to leave offerings. She makes an offering. Management, to Servilia, includes not lying to gods she may later need at short notice. The slave who waits has belonged to her side of the marriage for twenty-six years and has reached a stage of discretion that borders on geology.

At the Caelian door Barbatus looks at the veiled lady, at the chair, at the hour, and reaches a series of conclusions without altering his posture. “House is between services,” he says.

“So am I.”

Something in the voice travels downward through his training like a familiar signal in an unfamiliar camp. He does not say a name. He opens the door by the six inches necessary for revelation to enter without procession, and announces her in a formula that should be inscribed over the public records of the empire.

Inside, the house is not as Servilia expected. She has known brothels by reputation, dinner clubs by marriage and this establishment principally as a problem of columns. In the dead afternoon it smells of linen and stock and floor oil. A girl is practising scales somewhere without theatrical intent. Furniture is better than it needs to be and plainer than rumour has supplied. Eirene passes through the courtyard with an armful of napkins, sees the visitor, and becomes, by instantaneous professional decision, entirely elsewhere.

“Your porter is well trained,” Servilia says, when Antonia has come down with a working woman’s lack of fanfare.

“He was trained by arenas. We merely provide doors.”

“My household could use his taxonomy. I am *a lady by appointment with nobody* at least twice a week.”

It is as close as either comes to an introduction. Servilia removes neither veil nor dignity at once. Antonia does not ask her to. She indicates the small dining room, where afternoon light falls in a shape later useful to painters and presently useful to women who need to see one another without being blinded by symbolism.

Before sitting, Servilia looks at the hangings: summer issue, red reduced, the border falling correctly now. “The needlewoman from across the river,” she says.

“Vibia Prima.”

“Yes.” The name is received, not repeated. It may be the first concession of the visit.

“Cakes,” Antonia says. “Water. The house can provide wine, but negotiations have been known to improve without it.”

“Water,” Servilia says. “My husband has developed an interest in it as governance. I should hate to be underinformed.”
