Across the river, the child acquires a calendar of women. There is the baker’s mother, who regards sleep as a municipal resource and has ideas about its allocation. There are guild women with practical views on swaddling and subversive views on priests. There is a widowed neighbour whose contribution is chiefly to sit near the cradle looking severe at any man who imagines a baby in a working house should be made compatible with his notion of silence.

Prima goes back to work by instalments: estimates at the table, fine work when Lucia permits, teaching a younger fuller’s girl how to make stitch disappear without making the argument look dishonest. She prices the teaching. This scandalises exactly one guild official, who is bought off by the discovery that his own daughter wishes to enrol.

“Education,” Mother Livia says at the hearing, “has become a tradeswoman. I hope the city is paying attention to what arrives at its name.”

Letters arrive too, at intervals no one mistakes for an invasion. Cornelia’s commissions carry a marginal household weather. Marcus’s tablets are now used for Lucia’s accounts—receipts, weights, a fastidious record of which relations by bakery, guild and invoice have brought useful objects during the first quarter of a life. Prima signs nothing on her daughter’s behalf except the facts. Even facts, she has reason to know, need supervision.

One Tuesday the work-box comes to the Caelian with a tiny woollen cap in it awaiting a seam. Lucia does not. The separation is deliberate and, Antonia thinks, educational for everybody queuing inside her own ribs. Prima mends the cap while they talk of strong sun and reduced red. At the door she says, “She has discovered counting.”

“At five months?”

“She watches fingers as if a customs officer is doing something unreliable.”

“An inherited scepticism.”

“Taught,” Prima says. “We are a great family for acquiring characters by watching the room.”

Antonia gives her the revised estimate and no surplus wisdom. Barbatus carries the work to the street cart. The cap goes home in the box, completed, with no hidden flower at all. Sometimes a seam is merely a seam. The house is learning the difference between underwriting a life and leaving a signature in its lining.
