Large events always generate small taxes. By the end of wedding week, three separate clients have asked Antonia whether Greek music is now to be the fashion at respectable houses, which is Rome’s way of discovering whether it should take offence before or after paying. Daphne’s price for teaching a banker’s niece one discreet song doubles without a meeting being held. The niece, it transpires, wants only to annoy a stepmother whose ears are so patrician they require a veil. Daphne takes the commission and declines the bonus for secrecy. “Secrecy is a surcharge that always comes back wearing somebody else’s face,” she says, and wonders aloud when she learned to talk like the house.

Antonia could tell her. It happens around the time a woman sees her name correctly entered under her own money and discovers that charm does not have to report to a committee.

That evening Diomedes presents the wedding in balance-sheet form: not the money—money has already had its audience—but the shifts of risk. Marcus is less likely now to be used as a casual weapon by men who want his father’s attention. Cornelia has an allied roof, her own schedule, and a bridegroom whose family understands cargo insurance. The Caelian house has neither gatecrashed its safety nor appeared at the wedding in any form more material than a border’s maker’s knife-work perhaps known to three people and God.

“Prima’s position?” Antonia asks.

“Guild: rising. Bakery: entered. Household: hers. Correspondents: under discipline.” Diomedes closes the tablet. “In the third book, madam, she is no longer represented as an omission.”

Antonia looks out at the courtyard where the overwatered roses are doing their best with ill-applied love. “What is she represented as?”

“A tradeswoman with columns.”

It is, from Diomedes, a coronation.
