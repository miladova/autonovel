“Define discreet,” Antonia says, because she has never in twenty years let a man of his family issue her an adjective without presenting its papers. “In this city it usually means the women are silent in a more expensive register.”

“It means your door-book and my son's name keep separate quarters until Cornelia is married.” He says it flatly, a man stating a case before he has been briefed and hating the advocate. “It means if Marcus comes, Barbatus finds the house full. It means Daphne—” He stops at the singer's name the way men stop at a threshold in a house they are inspecting at dawn. “It means no second necklace becomes a baths story with my family's name for a clasp.”

“The necklace was glass, Lentulus. Its return was the education. Your son is twenty, briefly truthful, and learning manners at the full rate. If Rome must gossip about him, it will do it without my glass.” She lets the brazier tick once. “Barbatus can find the house full on the evenings your committee sits. He will enjoy it; it gives the door a constitution. Beyond that, I do not police your heir for his father's convenience at a discount. If the Cornelii want Marcus invisible, the Cornelii may purchase his invisibility themselves. I believe Syria is the traditional shop.”

It lands. She watches it land — guest's face at the fish course, the truth under the arrangement: not anger, which would be negotiable, but the small, wry Senate mouth that precedes concessions he will later describe as strategy. “You would have made an advocate.”

“I would have made several advocates unemployed. It is not the same career.” Antonia un-folds one hand and lays it flat on the cedar table between them, palm down, the gesture she uses in her own house to stop a sauce being sent out underdone. “Now the registry. Quarterly, by appointment, at stated hours when women may be dressed for an audience with the state instead of surprised by one. Papers shown to officials who can read them, in rooms that are not bedrooms. Minors and the unfree given the same entry as citizens — a name, a status, a person whose *no*—” She chooses the next words the way she chooses seating, for their geography. “A house to keep a person at all must be able to show her refusal exists somewhere in the paperwork. Put it better in Latin. I am told you draft.”

The sentence goes into him and stays. She can see it doing what sentences do in Lentulus when they are going to be carried for years: it finds the locked room and knocks once, correctly. Outside, the Basilica bargains on. Inside, a senator of ancient lineage looks at a freedwoman's hand flat on his banker's table and hears, as if from a committee he has not yet survived, his own future clause being dictated to him by its intended casualty, in the order of the evening.

“No house to profit—” he begins, half aloud, the drafting habit outrunning the statesmanship.

“Finish it at your leisure. I am not in the room to invoice you for authorship.” She withdraws the hand. "Rome will attribute it to a Cornelius with a conscience, and my women will cite it over their contracts, and both of us will have got our preferred anthems out of one sentence. That, Lentulus, is what a settlement looks like in my trade. Yours takes longer and has flutes.”

He laughs then — not the cup-on-glass laugh of the first joke, something shorter and less managed, a sound she last heard in a house that no longer stands, over scansion, in a corridor. Her body files it before she can. She lets the filing happen and keeps her face on business, because business is the language they are both still fluent in, and because the next item on her private agenda has been sitting in the Greek's strongbox earning like furniture for two quarters and it is time it was introduced to its debtor.
