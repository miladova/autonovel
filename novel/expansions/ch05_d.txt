The Naples widow — let the record, which is hers at night and Rome's by daylight, give the woman her charges in full — was called Octavia Rufa, she was sixty, and she freed Antonia in her will for the stated reason, read out by a notary in a room full of relatives behaving like weather over someone else's harvest, that *this woman kept my books and my temper for four years, the second being the harder account*. The son-in-law challenged it. The son-in-law is one of the people the Caelian house now overcharges on principle whenever his agents book a room, a tax he has never identified across fifteen years of dinners. Antonia attended the hearing in a borrowed veil and watched a praetor's clerk read her freedom aloud in the tone men use for the minutes of other people's earthquakes. Then she walked out into Naples owning: one set of good clothes, one potsherd she no longer needed and could not throw away, a sum the will called a legacy and the son-in-law called an insult, and the knowledge — it sat on her like a cloak cut for somebody taller — that every paper in the world now had to go through her own hands before it could happen to her.

Freedom, she discovered that winter, is a staircase with a toll at every landing. A freedwoman of no family may keep books for widows, lend small sums, stand surety for women the banks will not see, and be cheated by exactly the same men on exactly the same terms as before, except that now the law will call her a party. It is not nothing, being a party. It is not what the young man's talk in the library promised, either, back when manumission was a gift he had personally invented. She kept the widow's successor's books for a year, at a salary she negotiated standing up — the first thing in her life she had ever negotiated; the sensation, she told Eirene later, in another city, in another life, was like being allowed to be *late* — and she lent the legacy out in pieces so small a Greek banker laughed at the ledger: to a laundress for a mangle, to a midwife (unhennaed, competent, terrifying) for a room with a door, to a freedman baker whose ovens a landlord was converting into leverage. Every loan was written. Every repayment was witnessed. She charged interest she could defend to the women's faces and collected it with a memory they came, in time, to treat as a neighbourhood utility: *ask Antonia, she'll know what your husband signed*. It is a short walk, in Rome's sister cities, from being the woman who knows what the paper says to being the woman the paper is brought to before it is signed. Short, and uphill, and lit by other people's lamps.

Eirene arrived in the third year, out of the Subura, out of a house whose owner's death had distributed its staff like an estate — because they *were* the estate, in the older papers — with a freedom bought by a subscription among the laundry women and a scar of temper where her trust used to be itemised. They recognised each other in a Naples street the way people from sunken houses do: not with embraces, at first, but with information, traded at full value across a bakers' stall — who was dead, who was sold north, whose mother, whose stairs. The embrace came later, in lodgings, with the same economy. Eirene looked at Antonia's books, at the little loans marching like a border of red flowers, each the same, each slightly alive with difference, and said, “You're building a fuller’s shop for money. Takes it in dirty, gives it back clean, charges by the piece.”

“I'm building the thing that would have caught us,” Antonia said.

“Then it wants a bigger door,” Eirene said, “and a kitchen. In Rome. Nobody was ever caught in Naples except by the view.”

So: Rome, two tables, a lamp-seller's credit, a dining room over a fuller's that smelled, on wet days, of home. The rest the city knows in its baths version — *a freedwoman with a Greek banker and a pastry cook, who collects senators' secrets like other women collect jet* — and the house version, which is Diomedes' three books and the truth filed between the quarters, is nobody's but hers. She has been asked, by men who think a life like hers is a menu they have paid to have explained, how she *rose*. She considers the word the way she considered a carter's amphora in a January yard. She did not rise. Rising is for bread and empires. She climbed, professionally, a staircase Rome built to be carried up by other people, counting as she went, and at some landing the toll-keepers began, one by one, to be her tenants. That is the whole miracle. It took twenty years. It is still, on the nights when the accounts are done and the house is loud with other people's appetites, the only story in the world she is certain she can tell in the order of the evening and not the order of the questions — because the questions, all of them, arrive from Baiae, and Baiae is a room she owns, whose door she keeps locked not from shame but from housekeeping.
