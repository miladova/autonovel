The baker's mother has chosen this morning, from a calendar of inspections that the neighbourhood keeps the way the Senate keeps its committees, to find out whether reading in a wife is a dowry or a defect. She has devised a test. It arrives in the form of Titus's yeast account, delivered by Titus himself, broad-handed and apologetic, with flour on one forearm like a senator's stripe and a story about the yeast man's widow that is clearly intended to be the reason the account is in Prima's hands at all.

“Mother says will you cast it, since you're here with nothing but the hanging done.”

“I am here,” Prima says, “with the hanging done. It took a month. Rome was built slower, Titus, but with more help.”

She casts it at the fullers' table, with her father supervising the house's noises from the doorway and coughing disapproval of all secondhand arithmetic. The yeast man's bill is wrong by eleven sesterces in the yeast man's favour. It is in the second column. It is always in the second column. She shows Titus where his own hand, in a hurry at the oven door, has let a figure be carried like a tray — seen, applauded, set down in the wrong room.

“Eleven,” Titus says, with the injured respect of a man watching his future being competently itemised. “That's a day's buns.”

“That's a day's buns a month, which is a roof in ten years.” Prima sands the tablet with the edge of her thumb, a trick she has lately acquired from watching better clerks than Titus, in houses he does not ask about. “Tell your mother reading is a dowry. In my case it comes with tools.”

Titus laughs, and the laugh does the thing it does in small rooms over vats: it recruits the father, the doorway and a neighbour carrying a garment past the window into brief, unreasonable citizenship. This is what her betrothal looks like from inside, on a good winter morning: not the contract read once, quickly, with her dowry of tools stumbled over; not Mother Livia's hems; but a boy with flour up his arm holding a ladder steady while she climbs into a column and comes down with eleven sesterces and the neighbourhood's opinion, revised by half a degree, in her favour.

Mother Livia herself arrives at noon with soup, which in that family is a flag of truce with vegetables in it. She has heard about the eleven sesterces at the speed Servilia hears things in another tax bracket. She sets the soup down, inspects the hanging — finished now, folded face inward over the back of the good chair, two hundred flowers sleeping on each other like a cohort at rest — and runs her thumb, the customs officer's thumb, along the border's edge.

“The mistress on the Caelian paid on the day?”

“On the day, Mother Livia. Thread itemised.”

“Itemised,” the older woman repeats, tasting the foreign coin. “Your mother would have liked a woman who itemises. She kept that book of hers — the leaves — stairs and owings. A body would think she meant to present it in the next world as evidence.” She says it without looking at Vibius, and Vibius, in the doorway, looks with great attention at the soup, and Prima, between them, understands that a conversation she is not in has just taken place over her finished work, in the dialect adults use where children used to stand. She files it where she files all such exchanges: under her needles, where the leaves themselves live, weighing nothing.

The guild women come in the afternoon, in the plural that in Transtiberim constitutes an institution. They have come about Vibius's cough — a syrup, a visitation rota, the serious business of whether the guild physician is to be asked for a second time at the guild's expense or whether the fuller will be allowed, as one of them puts it, to *die at his own rates like a contractor* — and they stay, as the plural always stays, for the unfolding of the hanging. Prima shakes it out over the table. The red flowers march. A silence performs the neighbourhood's true review.

“Put your name in it,” says the oldest of them, the woman from the vats, who has lately been looking at Prima the way Mother Livia looks at a hem. “In the border. Where it won't show unless you're looking for who held the work.”

“Tradeswomen don't sign table linen,” Prima says.

“Tradeswomen sign whatever will be read after they're filed.” The old woman's hands stay in her sleeves. She has lately sold a great deal of thread to a Greek from the right bank, at her price, and the transaction has completed itself in her like a sum coming right at the second count. “Your mother signed stairs, in Baiae, with her feet. Ninety of them, twice a day, with her name in no book the house kept.” She looks at the flowers as if they were a procession she has decided to let pass. “Sign yours, girl. It is a new fashion. I have it on the best authority across the river.”

Prima does not sign. Not yet. She folds the hanging face inward, so that whatever the world does to it in transit happens to the back of the flowers, and the guild women go out into the winter street with the syrup administered, the rota arranged, and the neighbourhood's whole unwritten archive adjusted by one entry, somewhere between the quarters.
