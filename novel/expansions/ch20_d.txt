The aqueducts committee suits him more than he first understands. Water, unlike ambition, announces its failures promptly. A channel silts; a pressure calculation is wrong; a district complains in language no rhetorician's Secretary can entirely beautify. Lentulus finds himself in the company of engineers, maintenance contractors and one unglamorous genius from Campania who speaks of gradients as moral philosophy. It is the first committee of his life in which the minutes frequently describe things that will happen.

“You have a talent for flow,” the Campanian tells him, without any observable irony. “Everybody important wants a fountain. Somebody has to ask where it comes from and what stops on the way.”

Lentulus hears the Caelian registrar in the words and keeps his face in the respectable columns. “I am lately interested in what does not reach its destination.”

“Leakage,” the engineer says, delighted. “The empire is mostly that.”

His old set has divided on him, as sets do. Some have filed the Syrian disappointment under weather. Some have discovered with hindsight that they always doubted him. A few, unexpectedly, have begun to invite him for precisely the qualities that were supposed to have disqualified: he can sit through a drainage estimate; he does not perform youthful hunger at the table; he knows what a clause costs when quoted by people who were not at the dinner where it was drafted.

Acilius, whose correspondence arrives as if from a better century, has taken to sending him extracts of other men’s talk. Hadrian in Athens, asking after the draftsman. A jurist in Baiae describing the clause as *rough carpentry, but a door*. An anonymous wit—there is a thriving population—saying that the Cornelii have at last contributed plumbing to both public morality and the water supply.

Lentulus writes back less often than courtesy recommends. What would he say? That the sentence was written by a man still believing a committee could launder history? That its authority was improved by a freedwoman in the Subura and two witnesses borrowed from a bread queue? He composes, in his head, several versions of the truth, each insufficiently senatorial.

His agent at the Basilica has no such inhibitions. “They are calling you the conscience of the drainage interest,” he says, nursing a contract that has gone through three languages and now means something in none. “I could sell that in a mild winter.”

“Kindly do not.”

“Humility as product has a short season, senator.”

“Then let it perish unsold.”

The agent looks at him as at cargo abandoned on a dock. “It is a name,” he says. “Most men in this building are paying interest on theirs.”

Lentulus thinks of the olive transaction, which has proceeded exactly as the Greek said it would: modestly, with papers. The debt is respectable, the obligation minuted, the temptation to disguise it as patronage resisted mostly by the fact that the Greek would have corrected his metaphors. He is learning the dignity of small transactions from people his family formerly employed to disappear them.

At a family dinner that week, an elderly cousin says, “At least Syria would have had weather,” and the table waits for the wound to declare itself. Lentulus watches Marcus put down his cup before it can become an answer. Servilia says, “Syria has weather whether Cornelius attends it or not. It is one of the province’s efficiencies,” and the evening is saved by administration.

Afterward Lentulus tells her, in the corridor between generations of wax, “You make even disappointment sound governed.”

“It is governed. I do it.”

“And the clause?”

“Also governed,” she says, “though not by you. Do not look so tragic. Things improve when your family’s authorship is diluted. Rivers, marriages, law.”

He has no answer in the first hand. In the second, after thirty years, he is beginning to think that dilution may be a form of survival his ancestors neglected because they preferred to remain undrinkable.
