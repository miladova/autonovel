The draft has been there for two days because Marcus, still apprentice to the new domestic order, has not yet decided whether unfinished honesty should be hidden or merely not exhibited at dinner. He left it under the pretext of asking for a stylus. Lentulus recognised the manoeuvre from his own youth: a sensitive document abandoned in a parent’s landscape in the hope that weather will take responsibility for reading it.

He does not. It is the most difficult act of authorship occurring in the study that week.

Instead he calls Onesimus and asks for a fresh sheet, ostensibly for committee business. When Marcus appears at the door after dinner—too promptly, as if summoned by paper—Lentulus is writing a note about maintenance access to a channel outside the walls. Both men contemplate this respectable labour.

“Your Greek improves,” Lentulus says.

“You have not read it.”

“No.”

“Then how—” Marcus stops, and with real discomfort identifies the trap his own question has set. “Mother said you would not.”

“Your mother knows my current policies.”

“She writes most of them.”

“She improves their expression.”

Marcus sits, uninvited and already normal. His father keeps the fresh sheet in place with two fingers, not yet using the mirror as a weight; that small theatre belongs to later, when he is alone with all the unlit centuries.

“I keep saying *sister* too soon,” Marcus says. “Then I take it out. Then the letter sounds as if I am writing to a guild.”

“Perhaps you are writing to a person.”

“Father, I am attempting profundity. You might open a door.”

“I am avoiding one.” Lentulus puts the stylus down before it can be conscripted. “If I compose the sentence, Prima will receive a document from this house. She already has enough of those by inheritance.”

“She has none.”

“She has the consequences of several.”

The word *Prima* on his tongue is still a managed impropriety. He says it because the new discipline requires precision even where precision has no public use.

Marcus looks at the covered draft. “What did you want to say, when you were twenty-four?”

The study receives the question as it receives all dangerous furniture: by appearing unchanged. Lentulus has had the entire winter to formulate a reply suitable for the record. None of the versions have survived Antonia’s exit lines, his son’s third drafts or his own notebook. “I wanted the consequences to be administered elsewhere and the feelings to be admired at home. In that order.”

Marcus winces. “That is very plain.”

“It has been trying to enter the family language for some time.”

“And now?”

“Now I endeavour to keep my hands where I can see them.” He holds them, absurdly, on either side of the aqueduct note. It makes Marcus laugh once, not heralded, and the study—scrolls, mirror, corridor—allows a moment in which father and son are not competing to be the ruin of a narrative.

“She may never answer,” Marcus says.

“That possibility is not a defect in the letter.”

“Mother says a refusal is not an interruption. It is part of the sentence.”

“Your mother has been reading the committee.”

“No, Father. The committee has been catching up with her.”

He takes the draft away with him, folded once, not smoothed. Lentulus leaves the fresh sheet where it was and, after a moment, sets the dented mirror on it. The gesture is now less about keeping a document covered than about marking an empty place correctly.
