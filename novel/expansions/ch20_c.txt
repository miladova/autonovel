The renewals happen over six weeks, because nothing intended to be real at the Caelian house is ever done in a single evening for effect. Diomedes prepares each tablet separately. Eirene sits in, not as chaperone—the word would make her spit into an expensive rug—but as a witness whose memory has jurisdiction in places courts do not reach. The Greek, or one of his terrifyingly polite subordinates, attends when savings are discussed, bringing with him the odour of cedar and the implication that numbers can be made to stand in a room without apologising.

Some women sign at once. Some take the tablet away overnight and return with questions in hands that have clearly consulted sisters, lovers, bathhouse philosophers or the small hours. One asks whether the right of departure includes departure in anger. Antonia says it includes departure in weather, provided the fees for work already promised are settled as the contract states. “I am not purchasing your gratitude,” she says. “I have enough furniture.”

Another, a dancer with an actor’s superstition about written promises, wants the clause read by someone not paid by the house. Diomedes makes no face. He sends for the registry clerk on his free afternoon and pays him an interpretation fee which is entered, with scrupulous deadpan, under Educational. The clerk reads. He explains the difference between a legal phrase and a key: one can be carried without fitting every lock. The dancer listens with her chin in her hand.

“And if the lock wins?” she asks.

“Then the fact that you have a copy proves somebody thought you might have the right to try the door,” says the clerk. “It is not nothing. In my office, nothing has a whole archive.”

She signs.

There is also the question of jewels. Antonia has never allowed the house to hold gifts as informal hostages, but other customs die more slowly than rules. Necklaces are produced from drawers, from the Greek’s office, from secret linings and one memorable pot of face powder. Each is entered as the property of its owner, with any stones valued and storage chosen. The exercise produces the atmosphere of an audit at a temple and two confessions about men whose names improve by being omitted.

“Mine came from a Syrian who believed garnets prevented seasickness,” says one woman. “I have never been on a ship.”

“Prevention,” Eirene says, “proven.”

Daphne is present for more of these sessions than her own contract requires. At first Antonia thinks it is curiosity. Then she sees the singer explaining, in simpler language, the difference between money held *for* a woman and money held *from* her. She has the born advocate’s talent for giving a hard sentence a human pulse. She no longer performs every feeling as if an audience were paying by the shiver; some expressions she now says in her speaking voice.

Near the end of the six weeks, the youngest of the musicians—twenty, ambitious, and not yet resigned to the fact that talent ages more slowly than skin—asks Antonia directly: “Did you wish someone had done this for you?”

The room becomes very interested in its lamps.

Antonia might make a house policy of the moment. She might make a tragedy. Instead she puts her hand flat on the table, a gesture whose meaning has changed over the year from testing for damp to keeping things from being swept away. “I wished for a copy of anything I had supposedly agreed to,” she says. “That was the first form of it. Grandeur came later and cost more.”

The musician signs her renewal and then, shyly, initials an additional line acknowledging receipt of her own savings statement. It is the least poetic act in the house that month, and Eirene, who has buried three musical careers and assisted at the birth of several bank accounts, regards it as one might a civic monument.

Later Antonia finds the Educational fee in Diomedes ledgers and approves it. “We are becoming an institution.”

“With curriculum,” Diomedes says. “Rome will have us on a plinth by winter if the accounts maintain their morals.”

“Let it begin at floor level. Plinths are hard to sweep.”
