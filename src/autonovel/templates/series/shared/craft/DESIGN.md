# DESIGN.md: how a novel is designed before it is outlined

A study text for whoever designs, outlines or re-conceives a book in this
series. `/autonovel:design` reads it in full every run;
`/autonovel:gen-outline` reads the card (§0) and Part II. It is
package-owned prompt material: `autonovel refresh-templates` keeps it
current, so don't edit it here; a book's own decisions belong in that
book's `design.md`.

`EDITING.md` is the editor's half: how a finished manuscript is read and
diagnosed. This is the author's half: how the decisions those diagnoses
test are made *before* a word is drafted. Every design decision below
names the EDITING.md question it is built to pass (P3, S1, A2 …), so a
design can be checked against the same questions an editor will later
ask of the book.

**Why a separate stage.** Drafters execute the outline; the outline
executes the design. When the design is missing, the outline is a list
of good scenes in date order, and the book drafts into a string of
well-written episodes. No amount of in-chapter revision repairs that.
One book in this pipeline was revised four times after an editor's
diagnosis: its causal links between chapters went from 15 of 23 to 23
of 23, every narrator flash-forward was cut, no chapter remained
removable, and four independent cold reads still found that 17 of its
24 chapters opened and settled their own contest. The fault was the
design (a life told contest by contest, under a rule that every chapter
is its own wager), and it could only be fixed by re-designing. Design
faults are found last and cost most; this stage exists to make them
first and cheaply.

**How claims are sourced.** As in EDITING.md: every claim names its
source (author, work, year). A claim marked **(unverified)** comes from
memory and was not checked against the source when this was compiled
(2026-10-18). Check it before you rely on it in front of an author.

## §0 The design card: twelve decisions, in order

Each decision is made before the next, because each constrains the
next. A design that starts from the opening and works forward drifts;
one that starts from the climax and works back converges.

1. **The climax.** The scene in which the protagonist, by his own act
   and at his own cost, answers the book's question. Say who is in the
   room, what he does, what he risks, what he spends. (§2; E1)
2. **The dramatic question** that scene answers, phrased so a reader
   waits for it, and the chapter (in the first quarter) where a
   character on the page first poses it. (§4; P2)
3. **The one action** the book is, and the span: the shortest stretch
   of the life that earns the climax. (§3; P3, S8)
4. **The opponent** who wants the same prize, his plan with dates, and
   where he is on stage in every act. (§6; A1–A3)
5. **The protagonist's want, need and misbelief**, and the **skills
   and knowledge the climax spends**. (§5; C1–C3)
6. **The plants**: for each skill or fact the climax spends, the scene
   where it is learned (inside a contest of its own), the scene where
   it is tested, and the scene where it pays. (§8)
7. **The rehearsals**: two or three smaller versions of the climax,
   each teaching what the climax needs and at least one failing. (§9)
8. **The cast web**: every principal a different answer to the book's
   question, with a want, a fear, a secret, and a first entrance in
   action. (§7; C4–C6)
9. **The sequences**: six to ten mini-stories, each with a question
   whose answer forces the next. Time is distributed by crisis, not by
   calendar. (§10; S1, S8)
10. **Clocks and the stakes ladder**: the deadline(s) and how each act
    raises the same bet. (§11; S3)
11. **The places**: the set pieces and what each place does in its
    scene. (§12)
12. **The voice**: register, rhythm, viewpoint, how much description
    and interiority. (§13)

Then run the tests in Part II, and have someone who did not write the
design read it cold (D14).

# Part I — The decisions

## §1 Design before the outline

- **Professional practice.** Novels sold on proposal are sold on a
  synopsis that states the premise, the protagonist's goal, the
  opposition, the turning points and the ending; the editor approves
  the shape before the draft (Jane Friedman, *The Business of Being a
  Writer*, 2018 **(unverified)**). Shawn Coyne's *Story Grid* (2015)
  reduces a novel to a one-page "Foolscap": genre, conventions and
  obligatory scenes, point of view, the protagonist's objects of desire,
  the controlling idea, and the beginning hook, middle build and ending
  payoff, before any scene is planned.
- **Poe.** "Nothing is more clear than that every plot, worth the name,
  must be elaborated to its dénouement before any thing be attempted
  with the pen. It is only with the dénouement constantly in view that
  we can give a plot its indispensable air of consequence, or
  causation" (Edgar Allan Poe, "The Philosophy of Composition", 1846).
- **Why it matters more here.** An AI drafter writes the chapter it is
  given; it does not know what the book needs that chapter to plant.
  Whatever the design does not carry, the outline cannot, and whatever
  the outline does not carry, no chapter will. (EDITING §5: the
  drafter's defaults are a tidy, self-contained chapter that resolves
  its own stake. That default *is* the episodic book.)

## §2 Start from the climax

- **The rule.** Decide the climax first and design backward: every
  earlier sequence exists to make that scene possible, legible and
  costly. Katherine Anne Porter: "If I didn't know the ending of a
  story, I wouldn't begin. I always write my last lines, my last
  paragraph, my last page first" (*Paris Review*, "The Art of Fiction
  No. 29", 1963 **(unverified)**). Robert McKee treats the last-act
  climax as the story's master scene, from which the writer works back
  to the inciting incident (*Story*, 1997 **(unverified)**).
- **What a climax design must say.**
  - Who is in the room and what each wants from it.
  - What the protagonist does that no one else could do, and why he
    can: the skills and knowledge he spends (each one a plant, §8).
  - What it costs him, and what he chooses between (EDITING C3, E1).
  - How the book's open threads meet in it (EDITING E2): the rival,
    the love, the mentor's lesson, the secret.
  - What the reader must already understand for the scene to be
    followed at speed (the rules of the game, taught by rehearsals, §9).
- **Failure on the page.** A climax decided by other people, by a
  vote the protagonist only waits on, or by a sum of money with no
  choice attached; a climax followed by a second and third climax,
  each answering a different question.

## §3 One action, chosen through the climax

- **The question (EDITING P3).** Aristotle: unity of plot is not unity
  of hero; a poem about everything that happened to Heracles is not one
  action (*Poetics* VIII). Irving Stone on the biographical novel:
  "Never the whole story; always the essence."
- **The choice is made from the climax.** Once the climax is fixed,
  the action is whatever the protagonist must do, learn and survive to
  arrive at it able to win it. The span is the shortest that earns it.
  Everything outside is cut or carried as memory, reputation and
  consequence.
- **Patterns for a long life.** When the climax needs decades of
  preparation, these shapes keep a long span one action:
  1. **The education and the campaign.** Part one trains the
     protagonist; part two spends every lesson in one campaign. Dumas,
     *The Count of Monte Cristo* (1844): in prison, the Abbé Faria
     teaches Dantès languages, science, history and finance; free, he
     ruins each of his enemies through the one secret or weakness his
     intelligence uncovers (Danglars by a false telegraph message that
     moves the bond market, Fernand by the truth about Janina,
     Villefort by his buried child). The training is not dull because
     it has its own contest (escape) and because the reader knows what
     it is for. *Risk:* an education with no stakes of its own reads as
     a montage. Each lesson must be learned inside a contest that
     matters then (§8).
  2. **The campaign with many locks.** The climax is a campaign whose
     goal is guarded by several independent gatekeepers, each needing
     a different key: the heist, the election, the siege, the
     conclave. Robert Harris, *Conclave* (2016), is structured by
     ballot rounds; each round is a reversal and exposes a candidate's
     secret. The design lists the locks, the key each needs, where the
     protagonist got the key, and the order in which they open or fail
     to.
  3. **The rehearsal ladder.** Smaller versions of the climax at
     rising stakes, each teaching what the next needs (§9). The
     climax is then the same game the reader has watched played twice,
     at the highest stake, with the rules understood.
  4. **The long project and the recurring opponent.** Ken Follett,
     *The Pillars of the Earth* (1989), spans about fifty years. It
     holds because one project (the cathedral) runs through all of it
     and the same opponents (William Hamleigh, Bishop Waleran) return,
     escalate and plot across every time jump. Each jump lands on a
     new crisis in the same contest.
  5. **The present campaign with the past intercut.** A dated present
     action with a deadline, intercut with the past that explains each
     of its moves. It works only if each past strand has its own
     suspense and arrives where the present needs it (EDITING S9; a
     frame without suspense of its own is hindsight).
  6. **Late start, the past as memory.** Hilary Mantel, *Wolf Hall*
     (2009): one scene of the boy in 1500, then 1527 onward; the
     intervening life comes back only when a present scene needs it.
- **How to choose.** Ask what the climax needs the reader to have
  *seen*, not been told. If it needs the reader to have watched the
  protagonist acquire each skill, use 1 or 3. If it needs only that he
  has them, use 6. If the climax is a campaign against several
  gatekeepers, 2 is the climax's own shape whatever comes before it.
  Shapes combine: an education built as a rehearsal ladder, ending in
  a many-locked campaign, is one design.

## §4 The question, and suspense when the reader knows the outcome

- **The question (EDITING P2).** One question the reader waits on,
  posed on the page by a character in the first quarter, kept in doubt
  in every act, answered by the climax. A theme is not a question.
- **Known outcomes.** In historical fiction the reader may know who
  won. Suspense moves from *whether* to *how*, *at what cost*, and
  *to whom*. Frederick Forsyth's *The Day of the Jackal* (1971) holds
  readers who know de Gaulle was not assassinated, because the
  question is how close it came and how the hunter is stopped. Alfred
  Hitchcock to François Truffaut: the bomb the audience knows is under
  the table makes fifteen minutes of suspense out of what would have
  been fifteen seconds of surprise (*Hitchcock/Truffaut*, 1966).
- **Design for it.**
  - Make the question *how*, and make its parts genuinely open: will
    this gatekeeper hold, will the money arrive in time, will the
    secret be found, will the ally betray him.
  - Give invented characters fates the record does not fix; their
    outcomes are fully open.
  - Give the protagonist a private stake the record does not settle
    (a marriage, a friendship, a promise, his own good name).
  - Let the reader know what the characters don't (the bomb). Mantel:
    suspense "grows in the gap" between what the reader knows and what
    the character knows (see EDITING V2).

## §5 The protagonist: want, need, misbelief, and the skills the climax spends

- **Want, need, misbelief (EDITING C1–C3).** The want is concrete and
  is the climax's goal. The misbelief is what he is wrong about,
  attacked once per act (K. M. Weiland, *Creating Character Arcs*,
  2016; Lisa Cron, *Story Genius*, 2016). The need is what the attack
  forces him to face. The climax forces a choice between want and need.
- **The competent protagonist.** Readers enjoy watching an expert work,
  on two conditions: the expertise solves a problem with stakes *now*,
  and the reader is let in on enough of the method to follow and
  anticipate it. The detective tradition made this a rule of fair
  play: the clues are shown to the reader before the solution (Ronald
  Knox's "decalogue", 1929; S. S. Van Dine, "Twenty Rules", 1928).
  For a protagonist whose weapon is knowledge, the design shows the
  reader *how he knows* before it shows what he does with it.
- **Skills are learned at a cost.** A skill learned by being told is a
  lecture; one learned by losing something is a scene. Each skill the
  climax spends should first be learned from a failure, a humiliation,
  or someone else's ruin that the protagonist watches up close.
- **The skills ledger.** List the skills and pieces of knowledge the
  climax spends. Each becomes a plant (§8). If the climax spends a
  skill the reader never saw acquired, the win reads as luck or
  author's fiat.

## §6 The opposition: one rival for the same prize, with a plan

- **Same prize (EDITING A2).** John Truby: the best opponent wants the
  same goal as the hero and is uniquely able to attack the hero's
  great weakness (*The Anatomy of Story*, 2007 **(unverified)**). A
  new obstacle per act is not an opponent.
- **A plan with dates (EDITING A3).** Write the opponent's plan as a
  dated sequence of his own moves, most of them offstage. Each offstage
  move must reach the page as a consequence the protagonist meets (a
  price that has moved, a clerk who has been bought, a letter that
  arrives first). The opponent wins on the page at least once before
  the climax.
- **On stage in every act.** Write an appearance ledger: the chapter in
  each act where the opponent is in the room.
- **A rival across a long span.** Over decades, the opponent who
  persists is usually a rival from the protagonist's own beginning:
  the same apprenticeship, the same patron, the same market. His rise
  is the counter-plot. Where the record supplies no single rival, the
  design may make one of a recorded figure's documented rivals
  recurrent, or invent one who serves a real opposing power, and says
  which.
- **Levels.** Opposition at full strength on every level (EDITING A1):
  the outer rival, the institution, the people closest to him, and his
  own misbelief.

## §7 The cast as a web, and characters who come alive

- **The web (EDITING C6).** Truby's "character web": each principal is
  a different answer to the book's moral question, so that every
  relationship tests the protagonist differently. Two characters with
  the same function should be merged.
- **Functions a long design usually needs.** The mentor, who gives a
  skill and whose lesson costs (Christopher Vogler, *The Writer's
  Journey*, 1992); the ally who has his own want; the rival (§6); the
  intimate (a spouse, a sibling, a friend) whose stake is the
  protagonist's soul rather than his success; the *influence
  character*, who challenges the protagonist's way of seeing until one
  of them changes (Melanie Anne Phillips and Chris Huntley,
  *Dramatica*, 1993); the gatekeepers of the climax, each a lock with
  its own key.
- **What makes a character come alive.**
  - **A want in every scene.** Kurt Vonnegut: "Every character should
    want something, even if it is only a glass of water" (*Bagombo
    Snuff Box*, preface, 1999).
  - **An entrance in action**: first seen doing something that shows
    what they want, not described in a paragraph.
  - **A contradiction**: a quality at odds with the role (a pious
    usurer, a bored prince, a vain clerk who keeps perfect books).
  - **A physical and verbal signature** that recurs: a gesture, an
    object, a habit of speech. Real people supply these from the
    record (portraits, letters, documented sayings); use them.
  - **A secret** the plot can expose.
  - **A voice of their own** in dialogue: vocabulary, rhythm and what
    they never say. Cover the speaker tags; the reader should still
    know who is talking.
  - **Change**: at least the principals end the book different, and
    the change is caused by the plot.
- **Design for it.** For each principal the design writes want, fear,
  secret, contradiction, signature, first entrance, function in the
  web, and arc.

## §8 Plants and payoffs

- **The rule.** Anton Chekhov: "One must never place a loaded rifle on
  the stage if it isn't going to go off. It's wrong to make promises
  you don't mean to keep" (letter to A. S. Lazarev-Gruzinsky, 1 Nov
  1889). And its converse: no rifle goes off that was not on the stage.
- **Promises, progress, payoff.** Brandon Sanderson's teaching frame:
  what a story promises early it must show progress on, and pay
  (BYU creative-writing lectures, 2020 **(unverified)**). A plant is a
  small promise.
- **Plant inside a contest.** A plant is never a lecture. The skill or
  fact is acquired while something else is at stake in that scene, so
  the scene is about its own contest and the reader carries the plant
  away without noticing it was planted. The reader should feel the
  scene was about something else.
- **Plant, test, pay.** Show it once (the plant), use it once at small
  stakes or remind the reader of it in a new light (the test), then
  spend it where it decides the outcome (the payoff). A reader cannot
  recall at the climax a plant seen once two hundred pages earlier.
- **Surprising and inevitable.** Aristotle: the strongest effect comes
  when events "come on us by surprise" and yet "follow as cause and
  effect" (*Poetics* IX, Butcher trans.). The payoff should be
  unexpected in the moment and obvious in retrospect: the reader saw
  the plant and did not see what it was for.
- **Foreshadowing is a plant, not a prophecy.** The narrator does not
  announce the future ("he would one day…"). The book plants a skill, a
  fear, a debt, a question asked and not answered, a danger the reader
  can see and the character can't (EDITING V3; PROSE §7.3).
- **Distribution.** The climax should spend plants from different acts
  (the youngest lesson and the newest). Not every early scene needs to
  plant something, but every skill the climax spends needs a plant.

## §9 Rehearsals: smaller versions of the climax

- **The ladder.** Before the climax, the protagonist plays smaller
  versions of the same game: the same kind of prize, gatekeepers and
  opponent, at lower stakes. Each teaches something the climax needs,
  and each costs something. At least one fails, and the failure is the
  lesson.
- **Try-fail cycles.** The "yes, but / no, and" pattern: each attempt
  either succeeds with a complication or fails and makes things worse
  (Mary Robinette Kowal and the *Writing Excuses* podcast
  **(unverified)**). A run of clean successes is a plateau (EDITING S3).
- **The failed first attempt.** A rehearsal that fails just before the
  climax (the deal that collapses, the promise that proves worthless)
  is the strongest set-up: it shows what the climax must do
  differently, and it raises the stakes because the protagonist has
  already lost once.
- **Rules of the game.** Rehearsals teach the reader how the game is
  played (how an election is bought, how a mine is pledged, how a bill
  is honoured) so that the climax can move at thriller speed without
  explanation.

## §10 Sequences, time and proportion

- **Sequences.** Frank Daniel's sequence method, as taught by Paul
  Gulino (*Screenwriting: The Sequence Approach*, 2004): a feature
  film is eight sequences, each a mini-story with its own tension and
  question, whose resolution sets up the next. A novel of 80–110,000
  words carries six to ten. A sequence spans several chapters; a
  chapter that settles its own question and hands nothing to the next
  is the episodic unit to design out.
- **Time by crisis (EDITING S8).** The book slows where the story
  is decided and jumps where nothing is. Each jump lands on a new
  crisis in the *same* contest, so a gap of years reads as a held
  breath, not a new story.
- **Proportion.** Give the climax and the campaign that leads to it
  the room they need, typically the last third to half of the book in
  dense time. An education or rehearsal phase that takes more than
  half the book delays the question too long unless the question is
  posed early and each phase carries its own suspense.
- **Reader questions at act boundaries (EDITING R1).** For each act's
  last chapter, write what the reader wants to know next. If the honest
  answer is "what happens to him next year", the act boundary is a
  calendar, not a turn.

## §11 Clocks and the stakes ladder

- **Clocks.** A deadline the reader can count down (an election day,
  a fair's settlement, a fleet's sailing, a loan's term). The climax
  campaign needs at least one; earlier sequences each benefit from
  their own.
- **The same bet, raised (EDITING S3).** Each act raises the stake on
  the one question: more to lose, or a deeper kind of loss. A bigger
  sum with a new counterparty is a reset.

## §12 Place

- **Place is chosen for the scene's use.** Put the scene where the
  place makes its pressure: the deal in the crowd where it can be
  overheard, the threat on the stair, the confession in the cold.
  Then describe what bears on the pressure.
- **Through the viewpoint's attention.** Describe what this person, in
  this mood, wanting this, would notice. A merchant sees weights,
  prices, who is talking to whom and what is short; a boy new to a
  city sees what frightens or dazzles him. Description becomes
  character and suspense at once.
- **A sensory signature.** Each major place gets two or three
  recurring sense details (a sound, a smell, a texture) that let it
  return in a line.
- **The past is a foreign country.** L. P. Hartley: "The past is a
  foreign country: they do things differently there" (*The
  Go-Between*, 1953). Readers of historical fiction come for the
  strangeness of the period made concrete: how a day was measured,
  what things cost, what was dangerous, what people believed. The
  design lists, for each major place, the "only here" moments a
  scene can stage.
- **Orientation before texture.** A reader who doesn't know the period
  needs the map before the street. At a major place's first appearance
  the design records, and the chapter gives, a plain picture of the
  place in its own time: how big it was, who ruled it, what it lived on,
  what made it unlike anywhere else and unlike today. Then the sensory
  detail, which only means something once the reader knows where they
  are. An author who keeps asking "but what was it like then?" is
  telling you the orientation is missing, however rich the texture.
- **Introduce a major place at a moment of pressure**, at arrival,
  not in a survey; return to it changed.
- **Density.** Rich description belongs at arrivals, set pieces and
  turns, where the reader has time and wants to see; it thins in the
  chase. A uniformly sparse book reads as a script; a uniformly thick
  one stalls.

## §13 Voice, decided at design level

- **Decide it from the author's preferences and the genre**, then let
  `voice-discovery` work out the detail.
- **Rhythm.** Gary Provost: "This sentence has five words. Here are
  five more words. Five-word sentences are fine. But several together
  become monotonous… So write with a combination of short, medium, and
  long sentences" (*100 Ways to Improve Your Writing*, 1985). A voice
  built on short sentences and short paragraphs reads clipped and
  breathless over a long book; variety is the default.
- **Register.** A modern register for a historical subject means
  contemporary syntax and diction, period-true things and ideas, no
  pastiche and no anachronistic idiom (PROSE.md).
- **Viewpoint plan.** Single close third; or a principal with a few
  chapters from a second viewpoint where the action goes where the
  protagonist cannot (EDITING V1). Decide which, and why.
- **Interiority and description** as proportions: how much of a
  chapter is in the head, how much in the room.

## §13a Terms the reader must be told

- **The reader is a stranger to the period.** Every institution,
  office, title, coin, trade word and foreign word the story turns on
  (an elector, a fontegher, a ducat, fustian, a bell with a name) is a
  wall unless it is explained, plainly, the first time it appears.
  George Orwell's fifth rule: "Never use a foreign phrase, a scientific
  word, or a jargon word if you can think of an everyday English
  equivalent" ("Politics and the English Language", 1946). Where the
  period word is the point, keep it and explain it once.
- **The design lists them.** For each term the story depends on: the
  chapter where the reader first meets it and a one-line plain gloss.
  The drafter explains each in that chapter, inside the scene where it
  can (a question asked, a thing seen in use), never in a footnote or a
  paragraph of lecture.
- **The designer's own jargon is not the reader's.** Words a design
  uses about itself — *locks*, *keys*, *plants*, *spends*, *beats* —
  never reach the page or the author: the author is told "what wins
  each elector's vote", not "the seven locks".

## §14 The genre contract and obligatory scenes

- **The contract (EDITING G1, P5).** Coyne: each genre promises
  conventions and obligatory scenes, and the reader feels cheated
  when they are missing. List them in the design, with the chapter
  that delivers each.
- **A historical thriller usually promises:** a clock; an opponent
  with a plan the reader can watch working; the hero at the mercy of
  the opponent; a reversal that makes the hero's plan fail; the plan
  repaired by something planted earlier; a climax the hero decides;
  the period made concrete; real dates and outcomes honoured; the
  private cost of the public win (EDITING Part IV).

## §15 Earned outcomes: knowledge, not luck

- **The rule.** Emma Coats (Pixar's "22 rules of storytelling", 2011,
  no. 19): "Coincidences to get characters into trouble are great;
  coincidences to get them out of it are cheating." A protagonist who
  wins by nerve, a gamble, or the odds is lucky; one who wins because
  he knew something, prepared something, or read someone, is earned.
- **For a protagonist whose trade is money or information**, design
  his wins as moves that follow from what he knew earlier than others
  and how he knew it, never as wagers that happen to pay. Risk is
  real; the edge is his. Where the record shows him taking a risk,
  the design shows the knowledge that made it a smart risk and the
  danger that remained.
- **The reader must see the edge.** Show the source of the knowledge
  (the courier, the ledger, the cipher, the overheard word) before the
  move that spends it (§5's fair play).

# Part II — The design tests (D1–D14)

Run on the finished `design.md` before the outline. Each names the
EDITING.md question it anticipates. A test that fails changes the
design, not the test.

- **D1 One page (P1).** The pitch, question, climax, opponent and cost
  fit on one page and a stranger could retell them.
- **D2 Climax (E1, E2, C3).** The protagonist's own act decides it; it
  spends at least three plants from at least two different acts; it
  answers the question; it costs him something he wanted; the rival
  and the open threads are in it.
- **D3 Question (P2).** Posed on the page by a character in the first
  quarter; for each act, the design says what keeps it in doubt.
- **D4 Anecdote (P3, S1, S2).** Every sequence's contest is a step in
  the spine contest, or plants something the climax spends, or both.
  For each sequence, name the later sequence that would fail without
  it. A sequence that settles its own question and hands nothing
  forward is cut or rebuilt.
- **D5 Opponent (A1–A3).** One rival wants the same prize; his plan is
  dated; he is on stage in every act; he wins on the page at least
  once before the climax.
- **D6 Plants (S1; §8).** Every skill or fact the climax spends has a
  plant inside its own contest, a test, and a payoff; no payoff lacks
  a plant; no narrator forecast stands in for one.
- **D7 Rehearsals (§9).** At least two smaller versions of the climax;
  each teaches what the climax needs; at least one fails.
- **D8 Cast (C4–C6).** Every principal has want, fear, secret,
  contradiction, signature, first entrance in action, and a function
  no one else has; each answers the book's question differently.
- **D9 Time (S8, S9).** The span is the shortest that earns the
  climax; the climax campaign has the room it needs; every jump lands
  on a crisis in the same contest.
- **D10 Reader questions (R1).** For each act boundary, a question the
  reader wants answered that is not "what happens next year".
- **D11 Place (§12, §13a).** Each set piece's place does work in its
  scene; each major place has an orientation (what it was like then), a
  sensory signature and an arrival at a moment of pressure; every term
  the story depends on is listed under `## Terms` with the chapter that
  explains it.
- **D12 Earned outcomes (§15).** No major win turns on luck or a
  gamble; each turns on knowledge the reader saw acquired.
- **D13 Author's intent.** Every explicit direction in the seed and the
  author's notes is honoured, or the departure is stated with a reason
  the author can accept or overrule.
- **D14 A cold reader (EDITING §5).** Someone who did not write the
  design reads it cold and answers: what is the book about in one
  sentence; what will I be waiting to find out, and from when; who is
  against him; which sequence could I skip. Self-preference makes the
  designer the worst judge of the design.

# Part III — Re-designing a drafted book

When a developmental edit's problems are design-level (no single
action, no lasting opponent, a question posed late, a span told by
calendar, a frame, the protagonist's wins unearned), the remedy is a
new design and a redraft, not chapter briefs (EDITING §1.1: the big
picture first).

- **Start a new book**, isolated from the old one's prose
  (`context: isolated`), so the drafters cannot copy the old shape.
- **Carry forward the facts, not the shape**: research notes, canon of
  real events, the cast's documented traits, the art. Invented people
  and scenes carry forward only if the design gives them a function.
- **Write a keep-ledger** of what the old book's readers praised and
  what the editor's letters named as working: individual scenes,
  lines, characters. Each is kept only if the new design has a place
  for it, and is then redrafted in the new voice, not pasted.
- **Write a fault-ledger** of what the editor's letters diagnosed and
  name the design decision that prevents each fault.
- **The author's notes win.** When the author has said what the book
  should be, the design starts there; craft decides how.
