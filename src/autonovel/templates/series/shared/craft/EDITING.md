# EDITING.md: how professionals read, diagnose and fix a novel

A study text for whoever writes, revises, reviews or edits a book in this
series. Read it in full before any whole-book editing or reviewing task
(`/autonovel:developmental-edit` does, every run). Read the card (§0)
before any writing task. It is package-owned prompt material: `autonovel
refresh-templates` keeps it current, so don't edit it here; a book's own
departures from it belong in that book's `voice.md`.

Why it exists. The pipeline's own instruments (chapter scores, a
dual-persona review, a reader panel) can all praise a book's prose and
miss its story: dialogue that explains to its listener, no felt spine,
unglossed names, and a book that reads as a string of episodes rather
than one story building to a climax. When that happens it is
usually the author, not the instruments, who notices. Professional
editors, agents and critics have named and fixed these failures for a
century. This document puts their questions, with sources, where the AI
will meet them.

**How claims are sourced.** Every claim names its source (author, work,
year) and gives a URL or page where one was found. Quotations were
checked against the source, or against a named secondary source that
quotes it ("quoted in …"). A claim marked **(unverified)** comes from
memory or a search excerpt and was not checked when this was compiled
(2026-10-17). Check it before you rely on it in front of an author.

## §0 The card

### The ten questions that well-written manuscripts most often fail

Each one links to its full entry. They are ordered by how much damage a
"no" does to the book, not by how often the literature names them.

1. **P3 — Is this one action, or a sequence of things that happen to one
   person?** Aristotle: "Unity of plot does not, as some persons think,
   consist in the Unity of the hero." Tay Hohoff judged Harper Lee's first
   manuscript "more a series of anecdotes than a fully conceived novel."
2. **S1 — Does each chapter happen *because of* an earlier one, or only
   *after* it?** Forster's queen "died of grief". Parker and Stone's
   "therefore" or "but", never "and then".
3. **P2 — What question is the reader waiting to have answered, and is it
   posed early, with the answer genuinely in doubt?** Tiffany Yates Martin
   asks first of any manuscript: "Is the main story question clear?"
4. **A1/A2 — Who opposes the protagonist across the whole book, at full
   strength, competing for the same prize?** McKee: a story is only as
   compelling "as the forces of antagonism make them." Ken Brosky's essay
   on this point is titled "The Biggest Mistake Even Expert Writers Make".
5. **C2 — What is the protagonist wrong about, and does the plot attack
   it?** Cron: most submissions are "a bunch of things that happen" to
   someone whose misbelief nothing tests.
6. **C1 — Does the protagonist drive events, or do events carry him?**
   Cat Rambo: "Are the characters acting or reacting?" Cron calls the
   second kind "plot puppets".
7. **S3 — Do the stakes rise on the same bet, past points of no return,
   or do they plateau and reset?** Maass, as an agent: "Most writers
   don't have enough. They're afraid to push it too far."
8. **O1 — Does the book begin at the point of change?** It is the first
   reason agents give for stopping. Fitzgerald told Hemingway to cut the
   opening of *The Sun Also Rises*, and he did.
9. **SC1 — Does every scene turn a value, or are some of them
   nonevents?** Coyne: "if you never shift a value in a scene, it's not
   a scene."
10. **E1 — Is the climax the protagonist's own costly choice, and does it
    answer question 3?** Rambo: "Is it the result of character actions?"
    The common failure is history, luck or another character deciding it.

### Five habits

1. **Read as a reader first; diagnose second.** Martin calls the first
   read your "first, really, only best chance" to see the book as a
   reader will. Gottlieb: "an editor is a reader who edits." Do not read
   the outline, the seed or another reviewer before that first read.
2. **Strong prose is not a working story.** A vivid scene, a good line
   or a paid-off image is not evidence that the book builds. Say what
   works so revision protects it, then judge the story on its own terms.
3. **Count; don't assert.** Every whole-book claim rests on a list of
   chapters or a number: how many links are "and then", where the
   antagonist is on stage, when the question is first posed.
4. **Diagnose, then suggest a direction; the author supplies the
   solution.** Gottlieb: "Your job as an editor is to figure out what the
   book needs, but the writer has to provide it" (quoted in Seliger,
   2011).
5. **Judge the book against its own aim, and doubt yourself most when
   you like it.** Updike's first rule is "Try to understand what the
   author wished to do." Then remember that the judge and the writer are
   the same model family, which favours its own text (§5).

# Part I — How professionals work

## §1 How an editor reads a full manuscript

### 1.1 The order of work never changes: the big picture first

Every source agrees on the order: premise and intention; then character,
stakes and plot; then structure and pacing; then scene craft; and line
editing last. A note at a lower level is wasted if a higher-level change
will delete the passage.

- The Editorial Freelancers Association: developmental editors "deal with
  content, organization, and genre considerations". Their revision letter
  "outlines the big-picture issues and offers suggestions for how to
  address them" (EFA, "Editorial Services Definitions",
  https://www.the-efa.org/editorial-services-definitions/).
- Scott Norton, *Developmental Editing* (University of Chicago Press,
  2009): developmental editing "denotes significant structuring or
  restructuring of a manuscript's discourse", usually worked from a
  completed draft (https://press.uchicago.edu/Misc/Chicago/595146.html).
- Tiffany Yates Martin, *Intuitive Editing* (FoxPrint, 2020) and her
  Creative Penn interview (4 Apr 2022,
  https://www.thecreativepenn.com/2022/04/04/intuitive-editing/):
  1. the macro edit: character, stakes and plot, her "holy trinity of
     story";
  2. the micro edit: suspense, intention, show and tell, POV, momentum,
     structure, voice;
  3. the line edit ("don't start with that, because it's
     counterproductive");
  4. the copy edit.
- Sol Stein, *Stein on Writing* (1995), ch. "Triage": fix first what most
  often causes rejection (unverified for page detail).
- Susan Chang, quoted by Sangeeta Mehta on Jane Friedman's site (15 Dec
  2022): "the assessment is the diagnosis" and "The developmental edit is
  the prescription"
  (https://janefriedman.com/before-you-hire-a-developmental-editor-what-you-need-to-know/).

### 1.2 The passes

No two editors describe identical passes, but the published accounts
converge on the following. Do them in this order. Each pass has one job.

**Pass 1 — the reader's read (cold).** Read the whole book in order, at
reading speed, without the outline, the seed or any other review. Make
only light marks: where attention dropped, where you were confused, what
you wanted to know at each chapter end, what moved you.
- Martin calls it your "first, really, only best chance to take in your
  work as close to the way a reader will see it as possible" (Creative
  Penn, 2022).
- Jessica Page Morrell's guidelines: these problems are "very hard to
  discern … unless you are reading the manuscript with fresh eyes"
  ("Developmental Fiction Guidelines", Chanticleer, 2018).
- Gottlieb: an editor is "a reader who edits" (Fresh Air archive,
  https://freshairarchive.org/segments/remembering-acclaimed-editor-robert-gottlieb).
  His dispute with Robert Caro over repetition was that "he was thinking
  as a writer, and I was thinking as a reader" (Fresh Air, via GPB,
  3 Jan 2023).
- Susan Bell, *The Artful Edit* (Norton, 2007), on self-editing: "split
  yourself into two people: the person who wrote the book" and the
  stranger who comes to read it (Publishers Weekly interview, 25 June
  2007).

**Pass 2 — the noted read.** Read again with notes, then step away and
come back. Sarah Chauncey: a developmental editor does "multiple deep
reads (and then look away and come back)"; a full edit can carry
"hundreds of comments" (Jane Friedman's site, 10 Oct 2019,
https://janefriedman.com/manuscript-evaluations-what-they-are-and-what-to-expect/).
She also warns that a 5,000-word sample can show prose habits but not
structure, arcs or theme. Only the whole book shows those.

**Pass 3 — the map (reverse outline or scene spreadsheet).** Build one
row per scene from what is on the page, not from the plan.
- Shawn Coyne's Story Grid spreadsheet has these columns: scene, word
  count, story event, value shift, polarity shift, turning point, POV,
  period, duration, location, on-stage and off-stage characters (Story
  Grid podcast "How to Spreadsheet Your Novel", 15 Nov 2018,
  https://storygrid.com/how-to-spreadsheet-your-novel/). His view of what
  it is for: the grid "is going to show you everything that you did
  right and everything that needs work" (Creative Penn, 1 Feb 2015).
- Leslie Watts adds a cause-and-effect column. In it she looks for causes
  with no effect, effects with no cause, and inversions
  (https://storygrid.com/cause-and-effect/).
- Matt Bell, *Refuse to Be Done* (Soho, 2022), makes the reverse outline
  "less a document of what exists and more a plan for the better book you
  want the second draft to be" (Shelf Awareness review, 7 Feb 2022).
- No industry-standard column set was found. The core that recurs across
  practitioners: number, words, POV, story time, place, cast, goal,
  conflict, outcome (No / Yes-but / Yes), value at open and close, cause
  (which earlier scene), function, threads touched, keep/cut/revise.

**Pass 4 — the tracking passes.** Follow one thing at a time through the
whole book: the antagonist, one subplot, one relationship, the dramatic
question, the clock.
- Cat Rambo makes separate passes for Character, Story, World and
  Chapter, so each can "address various aspects one at a time" (on Bryan
  Thomas Schmidt's blog, 27 Feb 2012,
  https://bryanthomasschmidt.net/write-tips-cat-rambos-developmental-editing-checklist/).
- Coyne's spreadsheet makes cast balance visible: "You don't want to
  have 36 scenes that only have two people in them."

**Pass 5 — the one-page test.** Try to fill a one-page summary of the
whole book. Coyne's "Foolscap" holds:
- the genre and its conventions and obligatory scenes;
- the point of view;
- the objects of desire (want and need);
- the controlling idea;
- each of Beginning Hook, Middle Build and Ending Payoff, with its own
  inciting incident, complication, crisis, climax and resolution.

Coyne calls it "a checklist". Any field the editor cannot fill means
"you didn't make the choice necessary" (Story Grid podcast "The Foolscap
Story Grid", 30 May 2017). His "Editor's Six Core Questions" are the same
fields as questions: genre; conventions and obligatory scenes; point of
view; objects of desire; controlling idea; the beginning hook, middle
build and ending payoff (Story Grid podcast, 19 Dec 2017).

**Then triage, then the letter.** Rank the problems by what they cost
the reader. The few that, fixed, would change the most go in the letter
(§2).

### 1.3 What to write down per chapter in passes 1–2

A working template distilled from the sources above and from
`/autonovel:developmental-edit`'s reader's pass:

- **What happens.** Three to six lines.
- **What I want to know now.** The open questions at the chapter end
  (Child; Martin's "Why do we keep turning the page?").
- **Where I'd put it down, and why.**
- **Caused by / sets up.** Which earlier chapter's result this chapter
  answers, and what it plants (Watts; Forster's test of what the reader
  must remember).
- **Opposition.** Who or what is against the protagonist here, and
  whether they were here before and will be again (Truby).
- **The protagonist.** What he wants, what he is wrong about, and
  whether that error costs him here (Cron; Weiland).
- **The scene turns.** For each scene: the value at the open and at the
  close (McKee; Coyne).
- **Time and narration.** The gap since the last chapter, and every step
  outside the story's present (Matt Bell's "set the clock"; Stone's ban
  on the "a posteriori prophet").

Every four to six chapters, write a running diagnosis. Revise it openly
when later chapters change your mind.

## §2 The editorial letter, and how a note is phrased

### 2.1 What a letter contains, in what order, at what length

- **Contents and order.** Independent Book Review describes three parts
  (https://independentbookreview.com/editorial-letter/):
  1. "What the editor enjoyed about your manuscript"
  2. "What the primary issues of the manuscript are"
  3. "Specific recommendations for revision"
  
  Julie Scheina describes a critique as "a big-picture assessment of the
  manuscript's core strengths and weaknesses", usually "around two or
  three pages", and insists that feedback be actionable (Mehta, Jane
  Friedman's site, 2022).
- **Length.**
  - An evaluation letter "typically runs a few pages, usually no more
    than 10 pages". A full developmental letter can exceed 30 (Chauncey,
    2019).
  - Independent Book Review gives about 1,000–5,000 words. A Reedsy
    editor gives about 3,000 words (unverified).
- **A real letter.** The Masters Review published one on a short story,
  with names removed (https://mastersreview.com/?p=7580). It runs:
  1. concrete praise;
  2. "In revision, there are a few things to consider to make this piece
     even better";
  3. about four issues (where the story starts, verb tense, an
     undeveloped thematic parallel, the protagonist's emotional
     response);
  4. a hopeful close.
  
  Its central structural note: "I think the story in its present version
  begins before it needs to … I would consider where you think the story
  truly begins."
- **Perkins's Gatsby letter (20 Nov 1924)** runs the same way. It praises
  the cast at length, then makes one central complaint: "Among a set of
  characters marvelously palpable and vital … Gatsby is somewhat vague …
  the reader's eyes can never quite focus upon him." That is followed by
  small, dramatised suggestions (quoted in Rob Schwartz, Substack, 10 Apr
  2025; and Richard Beynon, 8 Feb 2021). See §19.1.

### 2.2 How a note is phrased

- **Frame it as the reader's experience, not as a rule.** Martin's
  example: "Stakes here are not really clear. Could you put that on the
  page more." For a critique group: "I didn't understand what the
  character wanted in this part of the story" (Creative Penn, 2022).
- **Diagnose, then suggest a direction. Do not write the scene.** The
  FoxPrint Editorial guide (attributed to Martin's firm, unverified):
  > "This may strain reader credibility; perhaps she does try to call the
  > police but for some reason she can't reach them or they don't
  > respond?"
  
  Its gloss: "The onus is on the author to find a way to solve the
  problem, but the editor has suggested a direction."
  
  Gottlieb, in the Paris Review's "The Art of Editing No. 1" (1994,
  quoted in Seliger, 2011): "You can't be the one who says, Send him to
  Hong Kong at this point." His diagnosis without prescription: "This
  book needs something at this point: it needs opening up, it needs a
  direction, it needs excitement."
- **Test the page against the author's intention.** FoxPrint: the
  editor's job is to help the author see "whether what you think you've
  put on the page is actually there."
- **Stay inside the book's own aim.** Flannery O'Connor told the editor
  who wanted *Wise Blood* made conventional that she was "amenable to
  criticism but only within the sphere of what I am trying to do"
  (*The Habit of Being*, 1979; unverified wording). She moved to Giroux.

  Diana Athill: "Respect the writer. Don't try and impose yourself" and
  "Keep the writer's voice always and never do anything without telling
  him you're doing it" (Gayle Feldman, *Publishers Weekly*, 2 Apr 2001).
- **Whose book is it?** Lish cut about half of Carver's *What We Talk
  About When We Talk About Love* and rewrote 10 of 13 endings. Carver
  wrote begging him to stop: "my very sanity is on the line here" (D. T.
  Max, "The Carver Chronicles", *NYT Magazine*, 9 Aug 1998). Critics
  still disagree about whether the book improved (§19.9). An edit the
  author cannot defend as his own is a failure, even when the prose gets
  better.

### 2.3 What the letter is not

It is not a line edit. Chauncey: an evaluation flags a recurring error
but does not correct it. Line notes go in the margins. A letter that is
mostly about sentences has answered the wrong question. That is the
known failure of a prose-weighted review: it praises a recurring image as
"the spine" and hedges its one structural observation.

## §3 Agents and acquiring editors: where they stop, and what they must be able to say

### 3.1 Where they stop reading

- **Four agents, one slush submission read aloud** (Chuck Sambuchino,
  *Writer's Digest*, 14 May 2009). Their first five reasons to stop:
  1. "Telling instead of showing"
  2. "Characters are MIA until bottom of page 2"
  3. "Not giving the reader a sense of place or where the story is going"
  4. "Yanking the reader out of the action with backstory"
  5. "Opening with a cliché"
- **Jane Friedman** ("The First Five Minutes", *Writer's Digest*, 12 Mar
  2009). Red flags on the first pages:
  - a flashback on page one, or too much backstory;
  - waiting for the protagonist to appear;
  - routine activity, or "nothing happens";
  - a wrong starting point that is not a point of change;
  - confusion without a motive to work it out.
- **Livia Blackburne** (*Writer's Digest*, 27 Oct 2012) adds "Loss of
  Focus" (scenes jumping between themes with no clear thread) and
  "Unrealistic internal narrative" (thoughts that exist only to inform
  the reader).
- **Worked example: Allison K Williams** (Jane Friedman's site, 14 June
  2023, https://janefriedman.com/?p=62274). A professionally edited
  manuscript kept being rejected. Its first 6,500 words held about 450
  words of story: a chapter of the parents' history, then the
  grandparents'. That was "the preliminary discovery phase" the writer
  needed and the reader does not. The story started about 25 pages too
  late. The fix was to open on the 450 words.
- **Noah Lukeman**, *The First Five Pages* (2000): an agent's catalogue
  of rejection triggers, running from surface faults to deep ones
  (unverified for detail).

### 3.2 What they must be able to say

- **The query test** (Janet Reid, Query Shark #345, 26 Mar 2023,
  https://queryshark.blogspot.com/2023/03/345.html): "1. Who the main
  character is 2. What they want 3. What's getting in the way of getting
  what they want. 4. What's at stake if they don't get it." Query Shark
  #344 adds "What choices do they face?"
- **The one-sentence pitch** (Nathan Bransford, 2010, rev. 2023,
  https://nathanbransford.com/blog/2023/05/how-to-write-killer-one-sentence-pitch-logline-novels-memoirs):
  "When INCITING INCIDENT happens to CHARACTER(s), they have OVERCOME
  OBSTACLE to COMPLETE QUEST in order to/or else STAKES."
  
  "Vagueness will kill a one sentence pitch". The pitch describes "what
  actually happens in your novel", not its themes. His caveat is fair:
  failing the test is a strong signal, not proof.
- **The acquisition meeting** (Phoebe Morgan, Hodder & Stoughton,
  "Acquisitions Meetings", 14 Apr 2025,
  https://honesteditor.substack.com/p/acquisitions-meetings). The editor
  writes a vision document with:
  - a "one line pitch";
  - selling points;
  - "comparison covers" with their sales;
  - "realistic" and "aspirational" figures;
  - "what it is, why we want it, what their vision is".
- **What a Penguin editor looks for in a debut** (Katy Loftus,
  https://www.penguin.co.uk/articles/company-article/the-editor-what-im-looking-for-in-a-debut):
  a hook ("the part of the plot or the subject matter that captures the
  imagination"), a distinctive voice, originality, a gap in the list,
  and a first page that will "grab the attention straight away".

**Use.** Before reviewing any book, write its one-line pitch and its
query paragraph. If you cannot fill in "what they want" and "what's
getting in the way" with one person and one opposing force that hold for
the whole book, you have found the problem before page one (P1, P2, A2).

## §4 How a critic structures a review

### 4.1 The aim test

- **The three questions.** Criticism in this tradition asks what the
  author was trying to do, how well it was done, and whether it was
  worth doing. H. L. Mencken traces the doctrine through Spingarn and
  Croce back to Goethe ("Criticism of Criticism of Criticism",
  *Prejudices: First Series*, 1919, Gutenberg #53538). He quotes
  Carlyle's form of it: find out "what the poet's aim really and truly
  was, how the task he had to do stood before his eye, and how far … he
  has fulfilled it." The common three-question wording is attributed to
  Goethe, but no primary Goethe text was found (unverified).
- **Henry James**, "The Art of Fiction" (1884): "We must grant the artist
  his subject, his idea, what the French call his donnée; … our criticism
  is applied only to what he makes of it." And: "The only obligation to
  which in advance we may hold a novel … is that it be interesting." And:
  "What is character but the determination of incident?"
- **John Updike's rules**, foreword to *Picked-Up Pieces* (1975), reprinted
  by NBCC Critical Mass, 8 June 2006
  (https://www.bookcritics.org/2006/06/08/reviewing-101-john-updikes-rules/):
  1. "Try to understand what the author wished to do, and do not blame
     him for not achieving what he did not attempt."
  2. Give "enough direct quotation – at least one extended passage – of
     the book's prose".
  3. Confirm your description with quotation, "if only phrase-long".
  4. "Go easy on plot summary, and do not give away the ending."
  5. "If the book is judged deficient, cite a successful example along
     the same lines, from the author's ouevre or elsewhere."

### 4.2 The parts of a serious review

As practised, and as in New Humanist's review of *Lincoln in the Bardo*
(9 Mar 2017, https://newhumanist.org.uk/articles/book-review-lincoln-in-the-bardo/):

1. **Situate.** The author, the earlier work, the kind of book.
2. **Describe without spoiling.** Premise, setting, method.
3. **State the claim.** What the book is doing, its ambition.
4. **Evidence by quotation.** Every judgement backed by the text
   (Updike 2–3).
5. **Ambition against achievement.** The axis that orders the verdict.
   "Splendidly ambitious" is a description, not yet praise.
6. **Comparison.** If it falls short, name the book that did this well
   (Updike 5).
7. **Was it worth doing?** What the book illuminates, beyond its own
   competence.

### 4.3 What critics say about historical and biographical novels

**Faulted for structure:**
- Kirkus on Don Gutteridge, *Lily's Story* (2015): "seemingly unable to
  separate an interesting historical fact from one that might be of
  service to his narrative, the novel comes across as shapeless and
  meandering"
  (https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/don-gutteridge/lilys-story/).
- Kirkus on Gail Tsukiyama, *The Brightest Star* (2023): "an elevated
  Wikipedia entry". Booklist, otherwise a rave: it "breezes rather quickly
  through Anna's accomplishments" (both via Book Marks).
- Anthony Cummins on Colm Tóibín, *The Magician* (*Observer*, 26 Sept
  2021): "stranded in a stylistic no man's land between biography and
  fiction". The decades-wide panorama loses the intimacy of the
  five-year *The Master*.

**Praised for solving the problem.** *Wolf Hall*, as excerpted by
complete-review (https://complete-review.com/reviews/mantelh/wolfhall.htm):
- Andrew Holgate (*Sunday Times*): Mantel focuses "not on the famous
  set-pieces, but on the human interaction taking place around them".
- Michael Caines (*TLS*), on the present tense: "We are not looking back
  at a path through time, but trying to find our way onward."
- Joan Frank (*SF Chronicle*): readers are "greedy for particulars of a
  story whose outcome, in theory, we already know".

**James Wood**, *How Fiction Works* (2008), tests whose language a
sentence is in and whether a detail is chosen or merely accumulated
(unverified for wording).

## §5 Where an AI writer and editor go wrong

Read this as a description of yourself.

**What the research found:**
- **LLM stories lack turning points and suspense.** Tian et al., "Are
  Large Language Models Capable of Generating Human-Level Narratives?"
  (EMNLP 2024, https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.978): LLMs
  "struggle to develop critical turning points, such as the major setback
  and climax", which diminishes suspense and arousal. Their stories are
  "homogeneously positive and lack tension". Explicitly modelling arcs and
  turning points improved suspense and arousal by over 40%.
- **LLM stories fail creativity tests, and LLM judges cannot tell.**
  Chakrabarty, Laban, Agarwal, Muresan and Wu, "Art or Artifice? Large
  Language Models and the False Promise of Creativity" (CHI 2024,
  https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.14556). Ten creative writers applied 14
  Torrance-derived tests to 48 stories. LLM stories passed "3-10X less"
  tests than professionals' stories. None of the LLMs used as judges
  correlated positively with the experts.
- **Professional editors find the same defects in every model family.**
  Chakrabarty, Laban and Wu, "Can AI writing be salvaged?" (CHI 2025,
  https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.14509). Their taxonomy of idiosyncrasies
  includes clichés and unnecessary exposition. No model family
  outperformed the others; the limits are shared.
- **A model can recognise its own text, and favours it.** Panickssery,
  Bowman and Feng, "LLM Evaluators Recognize and Favor Their Own
  Generations" (NeurIPS 2024, https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13076). LLM
  judges recognise their own generations, and self-recognition
  correlates with self-preference. Zheng et al., "Judging LLM-as-a-Judge
  with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena" (NeurIPS 2023,
  https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685), list self-enhancement bias with
  position and verbosity bias.
- **AI stories favour stability.** Rettberg and Wigers (2025), cited in
  CRAFT.md §7: AI stories favour stability over change.

**Consequences for how you work:**
1. **Treat a score you give the book as weak evidence.** Your
   self-preference inflates it, and LLM judges did not track experts in
   the one controlled study. Trust a count, a list or a quoted passage
   over an impression.
2. **Assume the book lacks turning points, a real setback and tension
   until you have found them on the page.** Your default output lacks
   them (Tian et al.).
3. **Do not read the plan before the book.** You will see the outline's
   intended causation in prose that does not deliver it. The
   developmental-edit ground rule "Diagnose the reader's experience, not
   the plan" exists for this reason.
4. **Never let a ledger stand in for a story.** Paid callbacks, promise
   lines and recurring motifs are continuity, not cause. That confusion
   is how every reviewer of a book can praise a string of anecdotes.
5. **Argue against your own verdict before you give it.** Name the
   strongest case that the book does not work, and answer it with the
   text.

# Part II — The diagnostic questions

Each entry gives five things:

- **Tests:** what the question measures.
- **Source:** where it comes from.
- **On the page:** what failure looks like in a manuscript whose prose
  is competent.
- **Fix:** what a professional does about it, at the level of story.
- **Example:** a published novel, film or editorial letter.

## §6 Premise and pitch

### P1. Can the book be stated in one sentence: who wants what, against whom, at what cost?

- **Tests:** whether the book has one person, one want, one opposing
  force and one stake that hold from beginning to end.
- **Source:** Bransford's template and caveat (§3.2). Reid's four query
  questions (§3.2). Cat Rambo's first preliminary question: "What is the
  story? Can you sum it up in a few sentences? If not, there's a
  problem." Debra Dixon, *GMC: Goal, Motivation and Conflict* (1996):
  who wants what, why, and why not.
- **On the page:**
  - The pitch lists themes ("a story of ambition and conscience") or a
    chronology ("the life of X from 1601 to 1660").
  - The want changes every few chapters.
  - The stakes are generic.
- **Fix:** write the sentence with concrete nouns: the wanted thing, the
  opposing person, the cost. If no sentence holds for the whole book,
  the book lacks a spine. Find one before any line work (P3, S1, A2),
  and treat it as an outline problem, not a prose problem.
- **Example:** Tony Kushner's first *Lincoln* draft ran 500 pages. He
  proposed "Let's just do the last four months, everything", because
  "There's something in the last four months that can stand in for every
  single major kind of conflict" Lincoln faced (Bill Moyers, 21 Dec
  2012). The film became pitchable in one sentence: Lincoln must pass the
  13th Amendment through the House before the war ends (this
  formulation is the editor's, not Kushner's).

### P2. What question is the reader waiting to have answered? When is it posed? Is the answer genuinely in doubt?

- **Tests:** the presence of a dramatic question that runs from early in
  the book to the climax and organises everything between.
- **Source:**
  - Martin's first question of any manuscript, verbatim: "Is the main
    story question clear?" (#AmWriting ep. 240, 4 Dec 2020).
  - Lee Child: "As novelists, we should ask or imply a question at the
    beginning of the story, and then we should delay the answer"
    ("A Simple Way to Create Suspense", *NYT* Opinionator, 8 Dec 2012).
  - Larry Brooks, *Story Engineering* (2011): the First Plot Point
    launches "the movement toward answering the dramatic question"
    (search excerpt; unverified wording).
  - Brewer and Lichtenstein (1982) show that suspense needs an
    initiating event with significant possible consequences and a
    delayed outcome (§14).
- **On the page:**
  - The reader cannot say what they are waiting to find out.
  - Each chapter asks and answers its own question.
  - The only book-length question is one accumulation answers ("will he
    get richer?"), which the next chapter always answers yes.
  - The real question appears after the midpoint.
- **Fix:**
  - Pose one question about the protagonist, with his want and the
    opposing force both on the page, in the first quarter.
  - Make each act a stage in answering it.
  - Make the answer depend on a choice the protagonist makes at the
    climax.
  - If history fixes the outcome, move the question to *how* and *at
    what cost* (H3).
- **Example:** *The Silence of the Lambs*: will Clarice stop Buffalo
  Bill before he kills Catherine Martin? Every scene with Lecter is a
  sub-question of it. Coyne maps its 64 scenes against two value lines
  ("Building The Story Grid", https://storygrid.com/746/).

### P3. Is this one action, or a sequence of things that happen to one person?

- **Tests:** unity of action, as distinct from unity of protagonist. This
  is the first question to ask of any biographical or historical novel.
- **Source:**
  - Aristotle, *Poetics* ch. VIII (Butcher trans., Gutenberg #1974):
    "Unity of plot does not, as some persons think, consist in the Unity
    of the hero." "Hence, the error, as it appears, of all poets who have
    composed a Heracleid, a Theseid, or other poems of the kind." Homer,
    by contrast, built the *Odyssey* round one action and left out
    episodes of Odysseus's life that did not belong to it. The Fyfe
    translation (Perseus, 1451a): "A plot does not have unity, as some
    people think, simply because it deals with a single hero."
  - Irving Stone, the biographical novel's founding practitioner: "Not
    every life will fit into the form of the biographical novel." A life
    needs "an overall, perceivable pattern into which the parts can be
    fitted to make an organic whole." His rule: "Never the whole story;
    always the essence" ("The Biographical Novel", lecture essay,
    c. 1957, https://eemaata.com/misc/The%20Biographical%20Novel_IrvingStone.pdf).
  - Jessica Page Morrell: "Write about the most important or interesting
    segment in a protagonist's life, not birth to death biography."
- **On the page:**
  - Chapters correspond to documented episodes, in date order, from
    youth to death.
  - Each is vivid; none needs the others.
  - Reviewers' words for it: "shapeless and meandering", "an elevated
    Wikipedia entry", "no man's land between biography and fiction"
    (§4.3).
- **Fix:**
  - Choose the one action the life is best seen through: a contest, a
    project, a campaign, a reckoning.
  - Cut or foreshorten what lies outside it. Fold the earlier life in as
    memory, reputation and consequence.
  - Then check P1 again.
- **Example:**
  - Tay Hohoff on Harper Lee's first manuscript: "more a series of
    anecdotes than a fully conceived novel" (Wikipedia, "Tay Hohoff",
    citing Mahler, *NYT*, 2015, and Shields, *Mockingbird*, 2006). She
    steered Lee toward the childhood material and one central action
    (§19.4).
  - Aaron Sorkin built *Steve Jobs* as three real-time scenes before
    three product launches, to avoid the cradle-to-grave biopic and
    "identify points of friction and focus on them" (MacTrast, 15 Nov
    2012).

### P4. What is the book's argument (its controlling idea), and does every part bear on it?

- **Tests:** whether the story means something that the ending's
  outcome and the protagonist's choice express, rather than a topic.
- **Source:**
  - McKee, *Story* (1997): the controlling idea is the value at the
    climax plus its cause, stated in one sentence (page unverified).
  - Truby, *The Anatomy of Story* (2007): a great story is "a sequence of
    actions, with moral implications and effects, designed to express a
    larger theme" (search excerpt; unverified wording).
  - Susan Bell: intention is the macro edit's first element, a
    "gravitational force", expressed consistently rather than
    front-loaded (secondary).
  - Irving Stone, near the end of a draft, wrote himself a five-page note
    asking "What is the cement that holds this book together?"
- **On the page:**
  - The theme is a pair of nouns ("money and the soul") that the
    narrator restates.
  - Scenes do not bear on it.
  - The ending's value (win or loss, corruption or redemption) has no
    cause in the protagonist's choices.
  - The tone drifts between kinds of book.
- **Fix:**
  - Write the controlling idea as "[value] because [cause]" and test
    every scene against it.
  - Give the opposing force the strongest form of the other side of the
    argument (Truby's moral argument; A1).
  - Cut scenes that serve neither side.
- **Example:** K. M. Weiland's analysis of *A Christmas Carol* states the
  argument as a Lie and its Truth.
  - Lie: "a man's worth can only be measured by the money he has earned."
  - Want: as much money as possible.
  - Need: to recognise that true wealth lies in love for others.
  
  Every spirit's visit attacks the Lie ("Creating Stunning Character
  Arcs, Pt. 2", 16 Feb 2014,
  https://www.helpingwritersbecomeauthors.com/character-arcs-2/). A novel
  about money needs an argument this clear, from either side.

### P5. What genre is it, and what has the premise promised the reader?

- **Tests:** the genre contract. What the reader of this kind of book
  expects to receive.
- **Source:**
  - Coyne's Six Core Questions begin with genre (§1.2). A book works when
    "fans of the genre the book is in find it satisfying." His advice:
    pick one genre and nail it (Story Grid podcast, 19 Dec 2017).
  - Chauncey's genre lens: does it meet the conventions?
  - Penguin's Loftus: the hook is "the part of the plot or the subject
    matter that captures the imagination".
- **On the page:**
  - The cover copy says "thriller" and the book has no villain, no
    clock and no moment when the hero is at someone's mercy.
  - Or the book alternates between the promises of two genres and keeps
    neither.
- **Fix:**
  - Name the global genre.
  - List its conventions and obligatory scenes (§18 for the thriller).
  - Check that each one is on the page, dramatised rather than reported.
- **Example:** Coyne says a thriller that lacks the
  hero-at-the-mercy-of-the-villain scene fails. In *The Silence of the
  Lambs* that scene is Clarice in Buffalo Bill's dark basement, watched
  through night-vision goggles (Savannah Gilbo's Story Grid mapping,
  https://www.savannahgilbo.com/blog/thriller-obligatory-scenes).

## §7 Book structure

### S1. Does each chapter happen *because of* an earlier one, or only *after* it?

- **Tests:** causation between the book's units. This is the line between
  plot and chronicle.
- **Source:**
  - Aristotle, *Poetics* ch. IX: "Of all plots and actions the epeisodic
    are the worst. I call a plot 'epeisodic' in which the episodes or
    acts succeed one another without probable or necessary sequence."
    Ch. X: "It makes all the difference whether any given event is a case
    of propter hoc or post hoc."
  - E. M. Forster, *Aspects of the Novel* (1927; Gutenberg #70492):
    story is "a narrative of events arranged in their time sequence";
    plot is "a narrative of events, the emphasis falling on causality."
    "The king died and then the queen died" is story. "The king died, and
    then the queen died of grief" is plot. Of story we ask "and then?";
    of plot, "why?"
  - Trey Parker and Matt Stone (NYU class, 2011, filmed for mtvU's
    *Stand In*): "What should happen between every beat that you've
    written, is either the word 'therefore' or 'but'" (as reposted by
    Cracked, 2024).
  - Leslie Watts (Story Grid): early drafts are often "chains of events
    that may create a beginning, middle, and end, but don't include a
    causal thread".
  - Morrell: "Nothing should happen at random and all fiction is causal."
- **On the page:**
  - Transitions are dates ("In the spring of 1508…").
  - Each chapter opens on a new problem or counterparty and settles it by
    the last page.
  - Continuity is carried by recurring characters, objects, callbacks and
    narrator forecasts, not by consequence. Several chapters could be
    reordered with no loss.
  - The synopsis reads "and then".
- **Fix:**
  - Write the chapter list as a beat list and put "therefore", "but" or
    "and then" between every pair. Count the "and then"s and find where
    they cluster.
  - For each "and then", make chapter N's result the cause of chapter
    N+1's problem. The usual means is Swain's sequel (SC3): the
    protagonist reacts to N's outcome, faces a dilemma and decides, and
    the decision is N+1's goal.
  - Add a cause column to the scene map and fix the orphans (Watts).
  - Keep the surprises, but make them caused. Aristotle's strongest
    effect is "when the incidents are unexpected and yet one is a
    consequence of the other" (Fyfe, 1452a).
- **Example:** Watts traces *Treasure Island*'s fifteen key scenes as a
  chain: "Bones arrives at the Admiral Benbow Inn, which draws the
  pirates who want the map." Each scene's result is the next one's
  trigger.

### S2. If you deleted this chapter, what later chapter would fail?

- **Tests:** whether each part is organic to the whole: the removal test.
- **Source:**
  - Aristotle, ch. VIII: the parts must be so connected "that, if any one
    of them is displaced or removed, the whole will be disjointed and
    disturbed. For a thing whose presence or absence makes no visible
    difference, is not an organic part of the whole."
  - Forster: "unless we remember we cannot understand". A plotted book
    makes the reader use what earlier chapters gave.
  - Rambo: "Should pieces be removed?"
  - Stone: "No use of material that does not tie into focal core of
    book."
- **On the page:**
  - A chapter is a fine short story, and no later chapter's choice
    depends on it.
  - Its only legacy is a fact (a name, a sum, a date) that one sentence
    elsewhere could carry.
- **Fix:**
  - Cut it.
  - Or merge it into a neighbour.
  - Or give it a consequence that a later choice depends on. A changed
    relationship counts as much as a changed plot.
  - Or, if the material is good but halts the main line, distribute it
    through the book.
- **Example:** In *Of Time and the River*, Perkins and Wheelock judged
  "A Portrait of Bascom Hawke" a digression. Rather than cut it, they
  "sliced it into sections" placed through the book, so it stopped
  halting the main line (Elizabeth Hardwick, "The Torrents of Wolfe",
  *NYRB*, 16 Nov 2000). For the cut, see Monteith and *Lord of the Flies*
  (§19.5).

### S3. Do the stakes rise on the same bet, past points of no return, or do they plateau and reset?

- **Tests:** progressive complication: escalation in kind, not just in
  size.
- **Source:**
  - McKee, *Story*: complications must escalate past points of no
    return, after which the character cannot go back to lesser actions
    (unverified for page).
  - Coyne's "power of 10": score each complication's intensity and check
    that the scores rise. Repeating the same fear level "feels flat"
    (Story Grid podcast, 25 Apr 2019).
  - Maass, *Writing the Breakout Novel* (2001): "How could things get
    worse?" and "When would be the worst moment for them to get worse?"
    (quoted by Julie Musil). Raising stakes means "making things worse,
    showing us that there is more to lose" ("The 3 Key Types of
    'Stakes'", *Writer's Digest*, 19 Oct 2010).
- **On the page:**
  - The sums, ranks or body counts grow, but nothing already at risk is
    still at risk, so each act opens a fresh bet with a new counterparty.
  - Complications are the same size and kind: "a character failing to
    get a job again and again" (Coyne).
  - Nothing in the middle is irreversible.
- **Fix:**
  - Score the stake of each act. Rewrite so each act raises the same
    bet: more to lose on the book's question, or a deeper kind of loss
    (a life over money, a self over a fortune).
  - End each act on a door that cannot be reopened.
  - Ask Maass's two questions of every chapter, then do what the answers
    say.
- **Example:**
  - Coyne on *The Silence of the Lambs*: Thomas Harris "takes the book to
    the end of the line by the mid-point" (Creative Penn, 2015). He
    spends the escalation early, then raises it again.
  - Ken Brosky uses Josh Malerman's *Bird Box* to show antagonism that
    escalates on several levels at once: the creatures, the group, the
    dangerous newcomer, the river (Jane Friedman's site, 3 Jan 2023).

### S4. Does one desire line run nearly to the end, or does the protagonist's goal reset?

- **Tests:** continuity of want across episodes.
- **Source:**
  - Truby, *The Anatomy of Story*: a single concrete desire drives the
    story almost to the end.
  - Truby flags journey stories as prone to episodic structure: the hero
    meets a string of opponents and does not change between them. Of
    *Huckleberry Finn* he names the challenge of "maintaining story
    momentum and strong opposition in a traveling, episodic structure"
    (reader highlight, Goodreads).
  - Weiland's critique of the film *Thor* faults its choppy pacing and an
    antagonist absent for much of the story.
- **On the page:** the protagonist has a new mini-goal each section. Each
  section brings its own antagonist, who is introduced, beaten and gone.
- **Fix:**
  - Name the desire that runs from the first act to the climax.
  - Make each episode a stage of it.
  - Connect the episodes' opponents to one main opponent as allies,
    proxies or instruments (A2).
  - For a journey or a career, find the designing principle that makes
    each stop a stage of the protagonist's change (Truby).
- **Example:** Twain holds *Huckleberry Finn*'s river episodes together
  with one desire (Jim's freedom), a recurring threat and a moral
  question that each stop sharpens. That reading is the editor's;
  Truby names the challenge.

### S5. Is there a midpoint that changes the protagonist's understanding of the conflict and of himself?

- **Tests:** whether the book turns in the middle from reaction to
  action, rather than carrying on.
- **Source:**
  - Weiland: the revelation is "the single most important job of your
    story's Midpoint". It should be both a plot revelation and a
    character revelation, ideally one moment ("How to Transform Your
    Story With a Moment of Truth", 8 Jul 2016).
  - James Scott Bell, *Write Your Novel From the Middle* (2014): the
    "mirror moment", where the lead sees what the story is really about
    (secondary summaries).
  - Blake Snyder: a false victory or false defeat at 50%.
  - Jessica Brody: if readers say the second half drags, check whether
    the midpoint is too early or the All Is Lost too late (Gilbo
    podcast).
- **On the page:**
  - The middle is the densest run of similar episodes.
  - The protagonist ends the middle doing what he did at the start, for
    the same reasons.
  - The plot revelation and the inner revelation have nothing to do with
    each other. Weiland says this may mean "plot and theme don't belong
    together".
- **Fix:**
  - Place at the centre a revelation that changes what the protagonist
    believes the conflict is, and what he believes about himself.
  - Let the second half pursue a changed goal or method.
- **Example:**
  - In *Thor*, the midpoint is Thor failing to lift Mjolnir. His Lie
    (worthiness comes from birth and power) meets its proof in the same
    moment (Weiland).
  - In *The Silence of the Lambs*, the midpoint gives Clarice what the
    antagonist wants: to become a woman. That changes the hunt (Gilbo).

### S6. Where does the momentum flag, and why?

- **Tests:** the sagging middle.
- **Source:**
  - Martin's fourth question: "Where does the momentum flag (because it
    will, somewhere) and why?"
  - Marianne Ward's checklist (Ryerson course, 2020): "Does the pace vary
    and build or is it uniform throughout?"
  - Diana Stout, "Dead Zone Ahead: The Sagging Middle" (Writers in the
    Storm, 13 May 2024).
- **On the page:**
  - The same obstacle returns in new clothes.
  - The protagonist stops deciding and only reacts.
  - The big confrontation sits too early.
  - Every chapter carries the same emotional charge.
- **Fix:** Stout's diagnosis:
  1. List the events.
  2. Mark whether the protagonist decides or only reacts in each.
  3. Rate each event's emotion from 1 to 10. A flat midline shows the
     sag.
  
  Then add a midpoint turn, a betrayal or a discovery that the
  protagonist is on the wrong path, or raise the emotional cost.
  Alternate the charge between consecutive chapters (McKee's law of
  diminishing returns; search excerpt).
- **Example:** Stout's 1-to-10 rating applied to a middle that reads
  "fine" usually shows a plateau of 5s. One decision scene at 8 placed
  in that plateau changes the curve.

### S7. Does the space each thing gets match its importance? Is every main event a scene?

- **Tests:** proportion, and the scene/summary decision.
- **Source:**
  - Renni Browne and Dave King, *Self-Editing for Fiction Writers* (1993;
    2004), from Mike Barker's chapter notes (1994,
    https://web.mit.edu/mbarker/www/writers/t029673.txt):
    - "Every main event should be a scene!"
    - Summary is "a kind of rest".
    - "Time (words spent)… roughly indicates importance to the reader."
    - Watch for "pet interests out of hand".
  - Stone: "Do only symptomatic scenes; step up pacing and speed; in
    perspective distant scenes are always foreshortened. Don't fight the
    entire Civil War, only those elements that come into the White
    House."
- **On the page:**
  - Five pages on a trade process, one paragraph on a betrayal.
  - The decisive meeting reported in a sentence while the journey to it
    gets a chapter.
  - A biographical novel that "breezes rather quickly through" the
    subject's accomplishments (Booklist on *The Brightest Star*).
- **Fix:**
  - List the five to ten events that change the protagonist most. Each
    must be a full scene.
  - Summarise or cut the rest, and jump-cut between them.
  - Cut the author's research where it outweighs its story value (H5).
- **Example:** Stone's Civil War rule above. His Lincoln novel stays in
  the White House and lets the war arrive through it.

### S8. Is the time span chosen, and is time distributed by crisis rather than by calendar?

- **Tests:** whether the book's clock serves the story.
- **Source:**
  - Matt Bell, *Refuse to Be Done*, third-draft step "Set (or Reset) the
    Clock": condense the time span (Heavy Feather Review, 11 Apr 2022).
  - Stone: distant scenes "are always foreshortened".
  - Mantel: *Bring Up the Bodies* spans about nine months but
    concentrates on three weeks of them (*The Creative Process*
    interview).
  - Ward: "Does the reader know how much time has passed…?"
- **On the page:**
  - Decades at an even pace, a chapter every year or two.
  - Time jumps fall where nothing has changed, while the crisis gets a
    summary paragraph.
  - The reader loses track of how old the protagonist is.
- **Fix:**
  - Compress the span to the action (P3).
  - Jump over stable stretches and slow to scene-time at crises.
  - Organise long spans into campaigns, each with its own deadline (H4).
  - Consider a frame with its own present clock (S9).
- **Example:**
  - George Saunders's *Lincoln in the Bardo* charts "one night in the
    graveyard" (New Humanist, 2017).
  - Robert Harris's *Imperium* covers fifteen years in two parts, each
    built round a goal with a date: a prosecution, then an election
    (Wikipedia; the deadline structure is unverified).

### S9. Does the order of telling serve the book?

- **Tests:** whether the arrangement of information creates the right
  curiosity, suspense and surprise.
- **Source:**
  - Meir Sternberg, *Expositional Modes and Temporal Ordering in Fiction*
    (1978): the order in which information reaches the reader creates
    curiosity (a gap in the past), suspense (about the future) and
    surprise (a gap noticed only afterwards).
  - Perkins on Gatsby's backstory (§19.1).
- **On the page:**
  - A chronological march from childhood, so that the opening chapters
    are preliminaries.
  - Or a scrambled order with no gain in curiosity or irony.
- **Fix:**
  - Ask what the reader would lose by knowing a fact earlier, or gain by
    learning it later.
  - Start late and fold the past in as a gap the reader wants filled.
  - Use a frame only if it carries its own suspense (Vidal's *Burr*,
    §17).
- **Example:** *Tender Is the Night* (1934) opens through Rosemary's
  eyes and tells Dick Diver's beginnings later. Fitzgerald came to blame
  that order. Malcolm Cowley's posthumous edition put the story in
  chronological order, following Fitzgerald's notes. Most critics and
  later editions went back to 1934's order, because glamour first and
  rot later is the book's emotional design (Wikipedia; the critics'
  reasons are unverified). Lesson: before "fixing" an order, ask what it
  does.

## §8 Scene

### SC1. Does every scene turn a value?

- **Tests:** whether something meaningful changes in each scene.
- **Source:**
  - McKee: a scene is "an action through conflict in more or less
    continuous time and space" that "turns the value-charged condition of
    a character's life on at least one value". If the condition "stays
    unchanged from one end of a scene to the other, nothing meaningful
    happens… It is a nonevent." His questions: "What value is at stake in
    my character's life at this moment?" "How is that value charged at
    the top of the scene?" "Where is this value now?" ("Do Your Scenes
    Turn?", https://mckeestory.com/do-your-scenes-turn/).
  - Coyne: "if you never shift a value in a scene, it's not a scene."
    And: "If you can't find the turning point in your scene, either your
    value never shifts…"
- **On the page:**
  - Conversations in which information transfers and no value changes.
  - The only shift is "uninformed to informed" (Coyne calls this too
    weak).
  - Three scenes running carry the same charge.
- **Fix:**
  - Write down the value and its charge at the open and at the close of
    every scene.
  - Cut or merge each nonevent, or rebuild it round a turning point the
    protagonist cannot ignore.
  - Convert exposition into ammunition inside a conflict (McKee).
- **Example:** Coyne's grid of *The Silence of the Lambs* tracks two
  values across 64 scenes:
  - the external life value, moving toward "the fate worse than death";
  - Clarice's worldview, moving from belief to disillusionment.
  
  The two curves cross at scene 12, the Beginning Hook's climax
  (https://storygrid.com/746/).

### SC2. Is there a crisis: a real choice between two costs?

- **Tests:** whether the protagonist reveals himself by choosing.
- **Source:**
  - Coyne's Five Commandments (inciting incident, turning-point
    complication, crisis, climax, resolution) apply in every unit, from
    beat to book. The crisis is a best-bad choice or a choice between
    irreconcilable goods, and "is an active choice."
  - Randy Ingermanson (after Swain): "A Dilemma is a situation with no
    good options."
  - Lisa Cron: "They always have to make some sort of tough choice in
    every scene" (Creative Penn, 7 Nov 2016).
  - Truby's moral question, as reported by Emily Short: "will the hero do
    the right thing, and will he do it on time?"
- **On the page:**
  - The protagonist's clever move works, and the right choice is
    obvious.
  - Nothing he gives up costs him.
  - Chapter after chapter he wins by being shrewder than the man across
    the table.
- **Fix:** give each major scene a dilemma in which both options cost
  something the protagonist values. Then let the choice show who he is,
  and let the cost stick.
- **Example:** In *The Silence of the Lambs*, Lecter's quid pro quo makes
  Clarice pay for each clue with a private memory. Every exchange is a
  crisis: the case or her privacy, and in the end the lambs.

### SC3. Does each scene's outcome become the next scene's goal?

- **Tests:** the scene–sequel chain, the mechanism of "therefore".
- **Source:**
  - Dwight Swain, *Techniques of the Selling Writer* (1965): a scene is
    goal, conflict, disaster; a sequel is reaction, dilemma, decision.
    The decision is the next scene's goal (Wikipedia "Scene and sequel",
    citing Swain pp. 84–100).
  - Jack Bickham, *Scene and Structure* (1993).
  - Ingermanson: "A Scene leads naturally to a Sequel, which leads
    naturally to a new Scene", and "If your POV character reaches his
    Goal with no Conflict, then the reader is bored."
- **On the page:**
  - "Happenings": the character has no goal and things occur.
  - "Incidents": the goal is reached without resistance.
  - "Leaps": a disaster followed at once by an unrelated new goal, with
    no reaction, dilemma or decision. Cause and effect break across the
    chapter boundary.
- **Fix:**
  - Give each scene a concrete goal, opposition and a setback.
  - Insert or sharpen the sequel so the decision births the next goal.
  - Lengthen sequels to slow the pace; shorten them to speed it up.
- **Example:** O. Henry's "The Gift of the Magi". Della's goal (a present
  for Jim) is stated at once. Each attempt meets a complication that
  forces the next decision, ending in the sale of her hair
  (be-a-better-writer.com, after Bickham).

### SC4. Is there a gap between what the character expects and what he gets?

- **Tests:** whether action provokes resistance rather than success.
- **Source:** McKee, *Story*: the engine of a scene is the gap between
  what a character expects when he acts and what the world returns. Each
  widening gap forces a riskier action (unverified for page). Bickham:
  the scene question is answered "No", "Yes, but…" or "No, and
  furthermore…" (unverified wording).
- **On the page:** the protagonist does something and it works, every
  time. Brosky's example: a hero who spends long stretches talking with
  allies, unopposed, while readers drift away around the midpoint.
- **Fix:** deny the expectation, or grant it with a complication that
  costs more than the gain. Widen the gap across each act.
- **Example:** Child's own description of *Killing Floor*. Each answer to
  the opening question (what is going on?) is partial and opens a
  narrower one, so the reader's expectation is never simply met (NYT
  2012).

### SC5. Is there tension on every page, including the quiet ones?

- **Tests:** micro-tension: the reader's unease about the next few
  seconds.
- **Source:** Donald Maass:
  - micro-tension is "the moment-by-moment, line-by-line" tension, and
    in quiet passages it lives "inside the point of view character" as
    "the emotions underneath" ("Invisible Tension", Writer Unboxed, 2 Jun
    2010);
  - "A stream of constant micro-tension causes readers to read every word
    on every page";
  - "The issue generally isn't what's 'wrong' in the pages but what isn't
    there" (Mythic Scribes interview).
- **On the page:** arrivals, journeys, meals, description and exposition
  with no conflicting feeling under them. Readers skim these, though
  every sentence is good.
- **Fix:** in every quiet passage, give the POV character two feelings
  that pull against each other, and let the sensory detail carry the
  uncertainty.
- **Example:** Maass cites Michael Cunningham's *The Hours*. Woolf pauses
  "On the steps of Hogarth House", and the stillness carries her
  fragility and fear of her own madness. It is tension without action.

### SC6. Is the turn the chapter exists for played on the page, at the speed a reader can believe?

- **Tests:** whether decisive changes are dramatised, not reported or
  rushed.
- **Source:**
  - Browne and King: "Every main event should be a scene!"
  - Diana Athill on Jean Rhys's *Wide Sargasso Sea*: the collapse of the
    marriage was "too abrupt". She made "one suggestion of a mechanism to
    overcome a stalled plot", and Rhys rewrote (Mary Tomlinson, *Times
    Higher Education*, 2000; *Dublin Review of Books*, quoting *Stet*).
  - `shared/craft/PROSE.md` §5 rule 9.
- **On the page:**
  - The confrontation is recalled in the pluperfect from after the
    event.
  - A change of heart happens between chapters.
  - A betrayal is announced rather than seen.
- **Fix:**
  - Play the turn in continuous time, with direct speech for the
    decisive lines and the other party's reaction.
  - If the change is too fast to believe, add the mechanism (a letter, a
    rumour, a witness, a slight) that lets it happen on the page.
- **Example:** Athill and Rhys, above (§19.8).

## §9 Character

### C1. What does the protagonist want, concretely, and does he drive events?

- **Tests:** agency: whether the plot is made by the protagonist's
  choices or happens to him.
- **Source:**
  - Kurt Vonnegut, preface to *Bagombo Snuff Box* (1999): "Every
    character should want something, even if it is only a glass of
    water."
  - Rambo: "Are the characters acting or reacting?" "Does the reader know
    what the character wants?"
  - Morrell: "Protagonist is proactive, taking charge of events,
    formulating goals and plans."
  - Ward: "Who drives the plot?"
  - Cron: when plot drives character, "you have plot puppets."
  - Weiland: the Want "will almost always be something external,
    something physical."
- **On the page:**
  - Events (often the historical record) arrive and the protagonist
    responds.
  - Other characters, luck or nature solve his problems. Chapters end
    with him watching.
  - The opposite failure: he is so competent that he drives every scene
    and is never pressed, which is agency without struggle.
- **Fix:**
  - Give him a goal and a decision in every major scene.
  - Make his earlier choices cause his later troubles. That is where
    agency and causation (S1) meet.
  - Let him be wrong, and pay for it (C2).
- **Example:** Mantel on her Cromwell: "Thomas Cromwell is not an
  introspective character"; he "is what he does" (*The Creative
  Process*). *Wolf Hall*'s historical protagonist drives each scene by
  fixing, bargaining and threatening, and the record arrives as the
  result of his work.

### C2. What is the protagonist wrong about, and does the plot attack it?

- **Tests:** the inner problem the story exists to test. This is the
  difference between a story and "a bunch of things that happen".
- **Source:**
  - Lisa Cron, *Wired for Story* (2012) and *Story Genius* (2016): story
    is "about how what happens, affects someone in pursuit of a
    deceptively difficult goal"; "story isn't about the plot." The
    protagonist's misbelief "usually was deeply ingrained early in life."
    Most submissions are "a bunch of things that happen" (Creative Penn,
    2016). Everything must touch the "third rail" joining inner struggle
    to outer events (secondary summaries).
  - K. M. Weiland, *Creating Character Arcs* (2016): the Lie "is a
    specific belief, which you should be able to state in one short
    sentence"; "The Change Arc is all about the Lie Your Character
    Believes."
  - Truby: the first of the seven steps is weakness and need, both
    psychological and moral.
- **On the page:**
  - The protagonist leaves the book believing what he believed at the
    start.
  - Events test his skill, never his beliefs.
  - His flaw is described ("ruthless", "proud") but never costs him a
    thing he wants.
  - His interior summarises events rather than misreading them.
- **Fix:**
  - State the Lie in one sentence and find its origin.
  - Design the external events to attack it, harder each act:
    - the midpoint gives the first true glimpse;
    - the third plot point forces the choice;
    - the climax enacts it (Weiland).
  - Make the Want and the Need conflict. "The tighter the Want and Need
    are linked, the more cohesive the plot and theme will be" (Weiland's
    post, paraphrased by the research notes).
  - A flat-arc protagonist who already holds the truth must change the
    world instead, and the world must resist (Weiland).
- **Example:**
  - Scrooge's Lie, Want and Need (P4).
  - Weiland reads Jane Eyre's Lie as a qualified one: she is unworthy of
    love but can earn it through servitude.
  - Cron insists that even an unchanging series lead, such as Jack
    Reacher, needs the reader inside his way of making sense of events.

### C3. Does the climax force a choice between what he wants and what he needs?

- **Tests:** whether the inner arc is resolved by a decision, on the
  page.
- **Source:**
  - Truby's sixth step, self-revelation, psychological and moral.
  - Weiland's Third Plot Point, the low point that forces the final
    choice between Lie and Truth, after which the climax proves the
    choice.
  - Coyne: the crisis "is an active choice".
- **On the page:**
  - The climax is all action.
  - The revelation is announced in narration or a speech afterwards.
  - The change at the end is a change of circumstance (richer, older,
    bereaved), not of person.
- **Fix:** put the Lie-or-Truth choice at or just before the climax, and
  make the external climax the enactment of that choice.
- **Example:** Truby's reading of *The Godfather* traces Michael
  Corleone's negative arc. His "basic action" is revenge (as reported by
  Emily Short). The climax intercuts his vow at the baptism with the
  killings he has ordered, so the choice and the act are one sequence.

### C4. Is the protagonist as vivid as the people around him?

- **Tests:** a common, invisible failure: a sharp supporting cast round
  a blurred centre.
- **Source:** Perkins to Fitzgerald, 20 Nov 1924: "Among a set of
  characters marvelously palpable and vital … Gatsby is somewhat vague …
  the reader's eyes can never quite focus upon him" (quoted in Schwartz,
  2025).
- **On the page:**
  - The rivals, servants and bishops have tics, bodies and lines the
    reader remembers. The protagonist has a role.
  - The author cannot say what he looks like, or how exactly he makes
    his money.
- **Fix:**
  - Give him physical and behavioural specifics.
  - Add one or two dramatised incidents that imply what he is, rather
    than explanations. Perkins suggested Gatsby be "seen, once or twice,
    consulting at parties with people of some sort of mysterious
    significance" (quoted in Beynon, 2021).
- **Example:** Fitzgerald's reply is reported as "I myself didn't know
  what Gatsby looked like or was engaged in & you felt it" (*Dear Scott/
  Dear Max*, 1971; unverified wording). He rewrote chapters VI and VIII
  in galleys (§19.1).

### C5. Why does this matter to *him*, and to whom else?

- **Tests:** personal and public stakes, and their widening.
- **Source:**
  - Maass: "the more it matters to your hero, the more it will matter to
    your readers, too." Public stakes are the consequences beyond the
    hero, widening as things get worse ("The 3 Key Types of 'Stakes'",
    *Writer's Digest*, 2010).
  - Martin: if the character doesn't care profoundly about what he
    pursues, "then we don't care."
  - Maass's question: does the hero have many reasons to solve the
    problem? If not, why doesn't someone else handle it?
- **On the page:**
  - The stake is a sum, a title or a contract, with no private reason
    attached.
  - Or private stakes only, with no one else's fate bending because of
    him.
- **Fix:**
  - Tie each public consequence to a private cost: a person he loves, a
    self he is protecting, a debt of honour.
  - Widen the circle of people affected as the book goes on.
- **Example:** Maass uses *Mystic River* for personal and ultimate stakes
  and *The Red Tent* for personal stakes (*Writer's Digest*, 2010).

### C6. Is the cast a web of different answers to the book's question? Can any characters be merged?

- **Tests:** the economy and design of the supporting cast.
- **Source:**
  - Truby: the web of characters, in which every major character
    embodies a different response to the moral problem and defines the
    hero by contrast.
  - Rambo: "Are there too many characters? Can any be combined?"
  - Forster: flat characters are built "round a single idea or quality";
    round ones are "capable of surprising in a convincing way."
  - Ben Ray Redman, reviewing Thomas B. Costain: the real figures are "no
    more than names" (search excerpt; unverified).
- **On the page:**
  - Names appear for a chapter and vanish.
  - Famous figures make cameos.
  - Every ally agrees with the hero, and every obstacle is the same kind
    of obstacle.
  - No one but the protagonist changes, and often not him either.
- **Fix:**
  - Merge minor figures into composites who recur.
  - Give each principal a distinct stance on the book's argument (P4).
  - Make at least one secondary character round: let them surprise the
    hero convincingly, and change.
- **Example:** Forster's flat Mrs Micawber ("I never will desert Mr.
  Micawber") against his round Becky Sharp and Emma Bovary.

## §10 Antagonism

### A1. Is the opposition at full strength, on every level?

- **Tests:** whether the forces against the protagonist are strong
  enough to make the story matter.
- **Source:**
  - McKee's Principle of Antagonism: "A Protagonist and his story can
    only be as intellectually fascinating and emotionally compelling as
    the forces of antagonism make them" (quoted by Ken Brosky, "The
    Biggest Mistake Even Expert Writers Make", janefriedman.com, 3 Jan
    2023).
  - McKee asks for opposition on every level (inner, personal,
    extra-personal) and for its worst form, the "negation of the
    negation".
  - Story Grid's thriller form says the same: the story need not reach
    damnation, but "the potential for damnation and the vehicle for
    damnation must be expressed" ("Thriller Story Cheat Sheet").
- **On the page:**
  - The opponents are fools, and the protagonist outwits each in a
    scene.
  - The antagonist is mostly offstage.
  - No level of opposition reaches its worst form, so the reader never
    fears for the outcome.
- **Fix:**
  - Ask what the worst form of the opposition would be, and write it.
  - Make the main opponent smarter or stronger than the hero in the
    dimension that matters.
  - Layer the opposition: an inner flaw, a personal opponent, an
    institution or a crowd.
- **Example:** Brosky's analysis of *Bird Box* (S3). In *The Silence of
  the Lambs* the opposition is doubled: Buffalo Bill threatens life, and
  Lecter threatens Clarice's self.

### A2. Does one opponent compete for the same prize, and persist across the book?

- **Tests:** whether conflict compounds instead of restarting.
- **Source:**
  - Truby: "It is only by competing for the same goal that the hero and
    the opponent are forced to come into direct conflict" (*Anatomy of
    Story*, pp. 46–47, via Wikipedia "Antagonist").
  - Story Grid's thriller convention "making it personal": "The villain
    needs the hero to get his/her MacGuffin."
  - Mantel on the Cromwell–More contest: "I realised I was writing the
    climax of a novel, not merely another chapter" (*Guardian* Book Club,
    7 Dec 2012, as quoted by varnam.org).
- **On the page:**
  - A new rival each chapter, beaten in that chapter.
  - The "persistent" opposition is an abstraction (the Church, the
    market, fate) with no face, no plan and no lines.
  - The opponent of the last act is first met in the last act.
- **Fix:**
  - Choose one opponent who wants the same thing the protagonist wants.
  - Put him on stage, or acting through proxies, in every act.
  - Let him age, gain power and change tactics.
  - Turn the episode-level opponents into his allies, proxies or
    instruments.
  - Across a very long span, pass the role along a lineage (father to
    son, one emperor to the next) and say so on the page.
- **Example:**
  - Ken Follett's *The Pillars of the Earth* spans 1123 to 1174, about
    fifty years. Throughout, William Hamleigh and Bishop Waleran Bigod
    oppose the cathedral and the people building it. William is finally
    hanged; Waleran's perjury is exposed (Wikipedia).
  - Dorothy Dunnett's *House of Niccolò* follows a fifteenth-century
    merchant-banker from 1460 to 1483. Across all eight volumes Simon de
    St Pol of Kilmirren denies Nicholas's legitimacy (Wikipedia).
  - Truby reads Barzini in *The Godfather* as a competitor for the same
    prize, control of New York, not a mere obstacle (search excerpt).

### A3. Does the opponent have a plan, and can the reader see it working?

- **Tests:** the opponent as an agent with his own spine, not a reaction
  to the hero.
- **Source:**
  - Story Grid's MacGuffin is "the villain's object of desire…what the
    villain wants".
  - Forsyth's *The Day of the Jackal* (1971) opens with "Anatomy of a
    Plot".
  - Rohan Maitzen on Dunnett's withholding: she had "stopped even trying
    to grasp what exactly Nicholas is up to and why" (*Open Letters
    Monthly*, 18 June 2016). A hidden plan builds curiosity only if the
    reader can see that there is one.
- **On the page:**
  - Opposition appears only when the hero acts, and vanishes between
    his moves.
  - The opponent's schemes are explained after they have worked ("it
    later emerged that…").
- **Fix:**
  - Give the opponent a plan with steps and a deadline.
  - Show the reader some of the steps. That is suspense (V2).
  - Reveal the rest in stages.
  - Keep his goal clear even when his method is hidden.
- **Example:** Forsyth's Part 1 is the Jackal's preparation: forged
  identities, the custom rifle. The reader holds the bomb while the
  police do not (§18).

## §11 Point of view, narration and information

### V1. Whose story is it, and is the viewpoint the one with the most to lose and discover?

- **Tests:** the choice of narrator and viewpoint at book level.
- **Source:**
  - Ward: "Whose story is it?"
  - Georg Lukács, *The Historical Novel* (1937; trans. 1962): Scott's
    hero is "always a more or less mediocre, average English gentleman"
    (p. 33, as quoted by Bruce Robbins). Lukács reads that as Scott's
    strength: a middling figure can stand between the opposing forces of
    history.
  - Perry Anderson summarises the classical form: famous figures appear,
    but "their roles in the tale will be oblique or marginal"
    ("From Progress to Catastrophe", *LRB*, 28 July 2011).
- **On the page:**
  - The viewpoint gives no access to the forces that are at stake.
  - Or the protagonist's interior is withheld, so that he stays a
    monument.
  - Or the viewpoint character is the person in the scene with least at
    risk.
- **Fix:**
  - Choose the viewpoint with most to lose and most to learn.
  - Consider a witness narrator who has stakes of their own.
  - Consider a second viewpoint inside the opposition.
- **Example:** Hohoff persuaded Lee to write from the young Scout's point
  of view. Lee: "My editor, who was taken by the flashbacks to Scout's
  childhood, persuaded me to write a novel" from it (PBS NewsHour, 3 Feb
  2015). Robert Harris tells Cicero's career through his secretary Tiro
  (*Imperium*, 2006).

### V2. What does the reader know that the characters don't, and is that knowledge being used?

- **Tests:** information design: suspense against surprise.
- **Source:** Hitchcock to Truffaut (*Hitchcock*, 1967, p. 52, as
  discussed by David Bordwell, "Hitchcock, Lessing, and the bomb under
  the table", 29 Nov 2013): "Let us suppose that there is a bomb
  underneath this table between us."
  - If it goes off without warning, the audience gets seconds of
    surprise.
  - If "The public is aware that the bomb is going to explode at one
    o'clock," then "we have provided them with fifteen minutes of
    suspense."
  - So the public must be informed whenever possible, except when the
    surprise is a twist.
  
  Bordwell traces the principle to Lessing.
- **On the page:**
  - Dangers are revealed after they detonate.
  - Every chapter ends on the reveal of something hidden from the
    reader.
  - The reader is never ahead of anyone, so there is no dramatic irony.
- **Fix:**
  - Plant the danger (an exposure, a secret loan, an enemy's dossier)
    before a calm scene, and stage the calm scene over it.
  - Turn most of the surprises into suspense by moving the reveal
    earlier.
  - Keep a few true twists.
- **Example:** Hitchcock's bomb, and Forsyth's Jackal, whose
  preparations the reader watches (§18).

### V3. Does the narration refuse hindsight?

- **Tests:** whether the story is lived forward.
- **Source:**
  - Stone: the novelist "may not turn himself into an a posteriori
    prophet." Stage the story "as though it were happening right now."
  - Mantel: "the writer knows the outcome, so does the reader - but the
    characters don't." "The suspense grows in the gap; the reader wants
    to know not so much what happens" (*The Creative Process*
    interview).
  - Michael Caines on *Wolf Hall*'s present tense: "We are not looking
    back at a path through time, but trying to find our way onward"
    (*TLS*, via complete-review).
  - Emma Darwin, quoted in PROSE.md §7: don't make characters
    "unnaturally perceptive or farsighted, let alone clairvoyant."
- **On the page:**
  - Narrator forecasts: "he would one day…", "years later he would
    remember…", "it was the first of many…".
  - Characters who speak with textbook significance.
  - Chapters that open on a famous date like a heading.
- **Fix:**
  - Give the characters only their own horizon, and let the reader's
    foreknowledge do the work as dread and irony.
  - Use a forecast only where a declared retrospective narrator earns it.
    Even then, a forecast tells the reader the outcome, so it trades
    suspense for something else and must buy something worth the price.
- **Example:** Mantel's Cromwell in close present tense, "he": the
  reader knows the axe is coming and he does not. `shared/craft/PROSE.md`
  §7.3 follows this rule: no narrator forecasts by default.

### V4. Is backstory released when it pays off, or dumped in blocks?

- **Tests:** the placement of the past.
- **Source:**
  - Perkins on Gatsby: let the truth of his claims, like Oxford and the
    army, come "out bit by bit" (quoted in Beynon, 2021).
  - Sambuchino's agents: "Yanking the reader out of the action with
    backstory."
  - Browne and King: "Don't stop the story to give us a summary of
    character."
  - Rambo: "Where are the info dumps and what strategies surround them?"
- **On the page:**
  - A chapter of family history before the story starts.
  - A late confession that tells a whole life in one speech.
  - A flashback on page one.
  - A paragraph of the character's résumé on first entrance.
- **Fix:**
  - Break the backstory up, and release each piece at the moment it
    changes how the reader sees the present.
  - Leave the rest out.
- **Example:** Gatsby's history moved and broke up in galleys (§19.1).
  Hemingway's cut of the opening dossier on Brett (§19.2).

## §12 Theme and worth

### T1. Is the meaning carried by choices and action, or announced?

- **Tests:** whether theme is embodied.
- **Source:**
  - Charles Monteith's edit of *Lord of the Flies*: he cut Simon's
    encounter with a figure implied to be a god (Wikipedia, citing
    Monteith in Carey, 1987).
  - Browne and King's best-known rule, "resist the urge to explain"
    (search excerpt, p. 16).
  - Truby's moral argument (P4).
- **On the page:**
  - The narrator or a character states what the story means.
  - Symbolism is announced: the god appears, the river is "the river of
    his life".
  - Scenes end on a sentence that generalises them.
- **Fix:**
  - Carry the meaning in what characters choose and what it costs.
  - Cut the announcement.
  - Let the opponent's best argument stand unrefuted until the climax
    answers it in action.
- **Example:** Monteith's cut of Simon's divine encounter, §19.5.

### T2. Was it worth doing? What does the book see that a history of the same events would not?

- **Tests:** ambition and illumination: the critic's third question.
- **Source:**
  - Carlyle via Mencken (§4.1).
  - James: "the deepest quality of a work of art will always be the
    quality of the mind of the producer."
  - Stone asks of the biographical novel whether it must not "illuminate
    a great deal".
- **On the page:** competent and inert. The book proves the facts and
  illuminates nothing. It takes no position on what the life meant,
  and the reader learns what a biography would have taught.
- **Fix:** this is not a craft repair. Sharpen what the book argues or
  sees (P4). Choose the episodes that make the argument (P3), and let the
  structure serve it.
- **Example:** Kushner's reason for the last four months is itself the
  book's argument: they "can stand in for every single major kind of
  conflict" (P1).

## §13 Genre contract and form

### G1. Are the genre's conventions and obligatory scenes on the page?

- **Tests:** whether the genre contract is honoured.
- **Source:**
  - Coyne's Six Core Questions.
  - The Story Grid genre lists (§18 for the thriller).
  - Coyne, from the Six Core Questions podcast: is every obligatory
    scene of the global genre on the page, rather than reported offstage?
- **On the page:**
  - The obligatory scene is reported, or happens between chapters, or is
    missing.
  - The conventions (clock, MacGuffin, red herrings) appear once and are
    dropped.
- **Fix:** list the genre's conventions and obligatory scenes, map each
  to a chapter, and dramatise each one.
- **Example:** Gilbo's map of *The Silence of the Lambs* against the six
  thriller scenes: an inciting crime, stakes made personal, learning what
  the antagonist wants, the hero becoming the victim, the hero at the
  villain's mercy, and justice.

### G2. Does the book's form make its premise convincing?

- **Tests:** the fit between the way the book is told and what it asks
  the reader to believe.
- **Source:** Gottlieb to Michael Crichton on *The Andromeda Strain*.
  Gottlieb "said he would not even consider publishing it unless I was
  willing to completely rewrite it from beginning to end." He also "said
  that the novel should read like a New Yorker profile, that it should
  be absolutely convincing" (Crichton's site,
  https://michaelcrichton.com/works/the-andromeda-strain/).
- **On the page:** a premise that strains belief, told in a form that
  does nothing to earn it.
- **Fix:** consider documents, a report, a frame, testimony or letters,
  whatever form makes the reader accept the premise.
- **Example:** *The Andromeda Strain* became a mock classified report,
  with documents and a bibliography.

## §14 Reader experience

### R1. At the end of each chapter, what does the reader want to know?

- **Tests:** page-turning: the open questions carried across each break.
- **Source:**
  - Child: "The basic narrative fuel is always the slow unveiling of the
    final answer", working "page to page, paragraph to paragraph, line to
    line" (*NYT* Opinionator, 2012).
  - Martin's macro question: "Why do we keep turning the page?"
- **On the page:**
  - Chapters end on a closed deal, a reflection or a summary.
  - Questions are opened and closed inside the same chapter, so the chain
    resets each time.
  - "What happens next?" is the only question, and it is too vague to
    pull.
- **Fix:**
  - For each chapter end, list the open loops.
  - Carry at least one specific, cared-about question into the next
    chapter.
  - Keep loops of different scales (scene, act, book) open across every
    break. A run of chapters that each close all their loops is an
    anecdote chain.
- **Example:** Child's description of *Killing Floor*. The opening
  question is answered in a ladder of partial answers, each opening a
  narrower question, with the full answer at the end.

### R2. Which of suspense, curiosity and surprise is working in each chapter, and are its lines resolved?

- **Tests:** the three interests that make readers judge a text a
  story.
- **Source:**
  - William F. Brewer and Edward H. Lichtenstein, "Stories are to
    entertain: A structural-affect theory of stories" (*Journal of
    Pragmatics* 6, 1982, 473–486) and "Event schemas, story schemas, and
    story grammars" (1981):
    - suspense: an early initiating event with significant possible
      consequences, its outcome delayed;
    - surprise: critical information omitted without the reader
      noticing, then revealed;
    - curiosity: the reader knows something is missing and wants it.
  - In their "The Trip Home" study, the standard suspense version scored
    highest on liking (4.85) and on being judged "a story" (5.05). A
    suspense version with no resolution fell to 2.75 on "a story".
    Without resolution, readers stop calling it a story.
  - Sternberg: curiosity looks to a gap in the past, suspense to the
    future, and surprise to a gap recognised only afterwards.
- **On the page:**
  - Chapters in which none of the three is working: an event with no
    initiating stake and no delayed outcome.
  - Suspense lines raised and dropped.
- **Fix:**
  - Name the dominant interest of each chapter. If there is none, give
    the chapter an initiating event with consequences and delay its
    outcome past the break.
  - Keep a ledger in which every raised question gets a payoff or a
    visible, deliberate denial.
- **Example:** The Brewer and Lichtenstein data above. The no-resolution
  version is the experimental form of the dropped thread.

### R3. Does the reader want a particular outcome, and does it look unlikely?

- **Tests:** the conditions of suspense, including for outcomes the
  reader already knows.
- **Source:**
  - Noël Carroll, "The Paradox of Suspense" (1996; in *Beyond
    Aesthetics*, 2001): suspense needs only entertained uncertainty (SEP
    entry by Aaron Smuts, 2021). His further conditions, a moral
    preference and an outcome that looks improbable, are unverified here.
  - Richard Gerrig, "Suspense in the absence of uncertainty" (*Journal of
    Memory and Language* 28, 1989): a story that put an obstacle in the
    way of George Washington's election slowed readers' verification of
    that known fact. The effect vanished once the suspense was resolved.
- **On the page:**
  - The protagonist is so plainly in control that his success is never
    in doubt.
  - Or the reader does not care whether he succeeds, because nothing the
    reader values is attached to the outcome.
- **Fix:**
  - Make the odds visible and stacked against him at each crisis.
  - Attach the reader's wish to something concrete: a person he
    protects, his survival, a promise.
  - If the protagonist is morally compromised, make the opposition worse,
    or move the reader's wish to what he protects.
- **Example:** Gerrig's Washington experiment. Known history becomes
  uncertain moment to moment when the text puts a specific obstacle in
  its way.

### R4. What shape is the book's emotional arc?

- **Tests:** the large rhythm of fortune.
- **Source:**
  - Reagan, Mitchell, Kiley, Danforth and Dodds, "The emotional arcs of
    stories are dominated by six basic shapes" (*EPJ Data Science* 5:31,
    2016). Sentiment analysis of Project Gutenberg fiction finds six
    basic arcs (rise, fall, fall-rise, rise-fall, rise-fall-rise,
    fall-rise-fall; the names are unverified here).
  - Tian et al. (2024): LLM stories are "homogeneously positive".
- **On the page:** a monotonic rise, where the protagonist is richer or
  higher at every chapter end. Or a flat line.
- **Fix:** plot fortune chapter by chapter. Build in at least one deep
  fall in the middle and one late fall before the climax.
- **Example:** Vonnegut's "man in a hole", one of the six shapes Reagan
  et al. found, which his rejected thesis inspired (EPJ highlight).

### R5. Does the ending make the beginning mean something new?

- **Tests:** retrospective design.
- **Source:** Peter Brooks, *Reading for the Plot* (1984): readers read in
  "the anticipation of retrospection", "the master trope of its strange
  logic" (p. 23, as quoted by Mark Currie, *About Time*, 2007).
- **On the page:** the last chapter is simply the last event, and nothing
  in the opening is reinterpreted by the end.
- **Fix:** plant an opening mystery or image that only the ending
  explains, and let the ending recast the first chapter.
- **Example:** In *The Pillars of the Earth*'s 1123 prologue, a
  red-headed man is hanged on the word of a priest, a knight and a monk,
  and his pregnant lover curses all three. Fifty years later the book
  reveals the conspiracy behind the hanging (Wikipedia).

## §15 Openings

### O1. Does the book begin at the point of change, with the protagonist acting?

- **Tests:** where the story starts.
- **Source:**
  - Friedman's "wrong starting point that is not a point of change";
    Sambuchino's backstory; Williams's 450 words (§3.1).
  - The Masters Review letter: "begins before it needs to."
- **On the page:**
  - The opening gives routine, weather, ancestry, a dossier on the cast,
    or the protagonist's childhood before the story's action.
  - The protagonist arrives late or watches.
- **Fix:**
  - Find the first moment of real story and cut everything before it.
  - Fold what the reader needs into later scenes.
- **Example:** The cut opening of *The Sun Also Rises* began "This is a
  novel about a lady". The published book opens "Robert Cohn was once
  middleweight boxing champion of Princeton" (Matthew Duffus, *Craft*,
  27 Jul 2021; §19.2). Faber cut the nuclear-war evacuation that opened
  *Lord of the Flies*, so the book begins with the boys on the island
  (§19.5).

### O2. What question does the first page ask, and does the last page answer it?

- **Tests:** the frame of the book's promise.
- **Source:**
  - Rambo: "Look at the first and last page. Do they hook the readers in
    13 lines or less? Does the story end strong?"
  - Child (R1).
  - Loftus's first page that will "grab the attention straight away".
- **On the page:** a generic first page that asks nothing. A last page
  that is summary or epilogue rather than an image or a choice.
- **Fix:** imply on page one a question that only the end answers (R5),
  and end on the image or choice that answers it.
- **Example:** The *Pillars* prologue's hanging and curse (R5).

## §16 Endings

### E1. Is the climax the protagonist's own costly choice, and does it answer the book's question?

- **Tests:** whether the ending is earned by the protagonist.
- **Source:**
  - Rambo: "Is the ending set up in a satisfying way? Is it the result of
    character actions?"
  - Morrell: "Avoid problems being solved by another character, a
    rescuer, or a force of nature."
  - Story Grid's thriller core event: "the moment when the Hero unleashes
    his/her gift when all is lost".
- **On the page:**
  - History decides it: the emperor dies, the war ends, the plague comes.
  - Another character rescues him.
  - The protagonist watches.
  - The "climax" is one more episode, the same size as the others.
- **Fix:**
  - Arrange that the climax's outcome depends on a decision only the
    protagonist can make, at a cost, using a gift or a weakness set up
    early.
  - Make the decision answer P2's question.
  - When the record fixes the outcome, make the choice decide what it
    costs him, and whom (H3).
- **Example:** Clarice alone in Buffalo Bill's basement. No one rescues
  her, and what saves her is what the book has trained.

### E2. Do the open threads converge in one decision, or close one by one?

- **Tests:** convergence.
- **Source:**
  - Story Grid: the climax as the point where all the book's lines meet.
  - `shared/craft/CRAFT.md` §1 rule 5.
- **On the page:** the last fifth of the book is a roll-call. Each thread
  gets its own scene of closure: a death here, a reconciliation there.
- **Fix:** force two or more threads into the climactic decision, so
  that its outcome depends on all of them, and let the rest support it.
- **Example:** *The Day of the Jackal*. The assassin's plan and Lebel's
  hunt converge on 25 August 1963, Liberation Day, the one day de Gaulle
  reliably appears. The shot misses because de Gaulle leans forward to
  kiss a veteran (Wikipedia; Otto Penzler, CrimeReads, 16 Oct 2019).

### E3. Is the turn of the ending surprising, yet caused?

- **Tests:** reversal and recognition.
- **Source:** Aristotle, ch. XI:
  - "Reversal of the Situation is a change by which the action veers
    round to its opposite."
  - Recognition is "a change from ignorance to knowledge."
  - "The best form of recognition is coincident with a Reversal of the
    Situation, as in the Oedipus."
  
  And ch. IX: the effect is greatest when things happen unexpectedly
  and yet because of one another.
- **On the page:**
  - A twist from nowhere (surprise without cause).
  - Or a climax that goes exactly as predicted (cause without surprise).
- **Fix:** plant the knowledge whose recognition will reverse the
  action, so that the reader looks back and sees it had to happen.
- **Example:** In *Oedipus*, "the messenger comes to cheer Oedipus and
  free him from his alarms about his mother," and achieves the opposite:
  reversal and recognition in one moment (ch. XI).

### E4. Is there a false ending?

- **Tests:** the genre's double ending.
- **Source:** Story Grid "Thriller Story Cheat Sheet": a false ending is
  obligatory. "There must be two endings."
- **On the page:** the climax resolves everything at once, and the
  resolution is long and calm.
- **Fix:** let an apparent victory or apparent defeat stand, then reopen
  it with the real reckoning.
- **Example:** In *The Silence of the Lambs*, Buffalo Bill's death
  appears to close the book. Lecter's escape and his last letter to
  Clarice reopen it (the editor's reading).

### E5. Does the ending trust the reader?

- **Tests:** restraint, and the difference between unprepared abruptness
  and deliberate abruptness.
- **Source:**
  - Gordon Lish's endings for Carver (§19.9).
  - Steinbeck's refusal of Viking's request to prepare the ending of
    *The Grapes of Wrath* (unverified wording; *Steinbeck: A Life in
    Letters*, 1975).
  - `shared/craft/PROSE.md` §8 (Bickham, Darwin, Bell).
- **On the page:**
  - The ending explains the emotion it has just shown.
  - Every character is given a final scene.
  - The book closes on a moral.
  - Or the ending is abrupt because nothing prepared it, which is a
    different fault.
- **Fix:**
  - Stop on the turn, a beat early.
  - Before accepting an editor's request to prepare an abrupt ending,
    ask what preparation would destroy.
- **Example:** Lish ended Carver's "One More Thing" on L.D. unable to
  think what the one more thing "could possibly be", where Carver's draft
  had a long tableau and the question "Is this what love is, L.D.?"
  (Giles Harvey, "The Two Raymond Carvers", *NYRB*, 27 May 2010).

# Part III — Historical fiction

## §17 Imposing a story on the record

### 17.1 The problem: the record is episodic, and a life is not a plot

- **Aristotle.** *Poetics* ch. XXIII asks epic too to centre on a single
  action. It should not be like history, which records a single period
  and everything that happened in it, connected or not (chapter
  confirmed; wording and Bekker numbers unverified).
- **Irving Stone** ("The Biographical Novel", c. 1957) lists the charges
  brought against his own form: it "oversimplifies," "reshapes that
  history to fit the novel form," and "makes character the victim of
  plot." He also warns that a writer who forces history to fit a plot
  produces propaganda.
- **The historical-fiction trap** is therefore double:
  - follow the record, and the book is a chronicle (P3, S1);
  - bend the record to make a plot, and the book is a lie.

  The professional answer is the same from Mantel to Harris: keep the
  record and invent the *therefore*. That means the motive, the private
  stake, the opponent between the recorded events, and the choice each
  event punishes or rewards.
- **Robert Harris** (Walter Scott Prize interview, quoted in
  `shared/craft/PROSE.md` §7): never "directly to contradict the historical record…
  What I do feel is legitimate is to condense and simplify: to dig out
  the bones of a narrative. And of course I invent scenes to fit in and
  around the known facts."

### 17.2 Hilary Mantel's principles

- **What the record is.** "history is not the past – it is the method we
  have evolved of organising our ignorance of the past." "Evidence is
  always partial. Facts are not truth, though they are part of it –
  information is not knowledge." (Reith Lecture 1, "The Day Is for the
  Living", BBC, 13 June 2017, as quoted in published accounts.)
- **Where invention goes.** "I make up as little as possible." "it's
  really in the gap—it's in the erasures—that I think the novelist can
  best go to work, because … we know a lot about what happened, but we
  may be far hazier on why it happened" (NPR *Fresh Air*, 26 Nov 2012, as
  quoted by Mental Floss). And: the truth "is better, stranger, stronger,
  than anything you can make up" (Reith series).
- **Known outcomes.** "the writer knows the outcome, so does the reader -
  but the characters don't." "The suspense grows in the gap" (*The
  Creative Process* interview).
- **Structure.** "I try to find the structure of my book through writing
  individual scenes." The shape of the Cromwell–More contest arrived
  late. When it did: "I realised I was writing the climax of a novel,
  not merely another chapter" (*Guardian* Book Club, 2012).
- **Compression.** *Bring Up the Bodies* spans about nine months and
  concentrates on three weeks of them (*The Creative Process*).
- **The protagonist.** "Thomas Cromwell is not an introspective
  character"; he "is what he does."

### 17.3 Lukács and the great-man protagonist

Lukács argued that Scott's historical novel works through a middling
hero, standing between the opposing forces of history. Famous figures
appear, "oblique or marginal" (Anderson, *LRB*, 2011). What matters "is
not the retelling of great historical events" but the human experience
of people inside them (p. 42, as quoted on the IHR blog, 4 Nov 2011).
Anderson notes that later historical novels reverse the rule. They "take
leading historical figures as central rather than marginal characters."

A novel whose protagonist *is* the world-historical figure runs three
risks that Lukács's argument predicts:

1. **The monument.** The great man appears already finished, and his
   choices feel inevitable.
2. **The backdrop.** The social forces his actions bend (workers,
   peasants, rivals, the Church, the crowd) are reduced to scenery and
   numbers.
3. **The missing middle.** No viewpoint feels the stakes on both sides.

The remedies authors use:

- **A middling witness** who has stakes of their own: Tiro in Harris's
  *Imperium*; Charles Schuyler in Gore Vidal's *Burr* (1973), whose own
  plot is to prove Van Buren is Burr's son.
- **Becoming, not being.** Show the great man before he is great,
  choosing under uncertainty and making mistakes with consequences.
- **Faces for the forces.** Give the opposing social forces recurring
  people whose fates bend because of him.

### 17.4 Stone's working rules (verbatim, from his essay)

- "Never the whole story; always the essence."
- "Do only symptomatic scenes; step up pacing and speed; in perspective
  distant scenes are always foreshortened. Don't fight the entire Civil
  War, only those elements that come into the White House."
- "No details that illuminate little but themselves." "No exhibitionism:
  'See what I know!'" "No use of material that does not tie into focal
  core of book."
- He asked himself before the last chapters: "What is the cement that
  holds this book together?"
- The novelist "may not turn himself into an a posteriori prophet."
- Research is an iceberg, with one-ninth showing.

### 17.5 Techniques for a long span

| Technique | What it solves | Example |
|---|---|---|
| One question across the span | gives a spine | *The Pillars of the Earth*. Follett: "the two things I had to blend were the lives of the characters and the building of the church" (*Otago Daily Times*, Dec 2010). "It took at least thirty years to build a cathedral," so the story "covers the entire lives of the main characters" (via Wikipedia). |
| A persistent, ageing personal antagonist | escalation with a face | Hamleigh and Waleran (*Pillars*); St Pol (*Niccolò*) |
| A prologue mystery answered at the end | turns the span into a loop | *Pillars* 1123 hanging → the White Ship conspiracy |
| Campaigns, each with a deadline | local suspense inside a long life | *Imperium*: a prosecution, then an election |
| A retrospective frame with a present clock | turns episodes into testimony | *Burr* (1833 frame); Yourcenar, *Memoirs of Hadrian* (1951), a dying emperor's letter (form unverified) |
| Start late, fold the past in | avoids the juvenile prologue | *Wolf Hall*: a brief 1500 scene, then 1527 (structure unverified) |
| Concentrate weeks within months | scene density | *Bring Up the Bodies* |
| Points of friction instead of a life | avoids the biopic | Sorkin's *Steve Jobs*; Kushner's *Lincoln*; *Lincoln in the Bardo* |
| A new self-contained question per segment, or a generational relay | when no single question will stretch | C. J. Sansom's Shardlake books; Edward Rutherfurd's place-sagas (unverified) |

A book that spans decades needs either one question across the whole
span (Follett) or a self-contained question per segment (Sansom,
Rutherfurd). A manuscript with neither reads as anecdote. Follett's
pacing rule holds whichever is chosen: "the story needs to turn every
four-to-six pages", and "For a popular novel, you can't have any boring
bits" (Jonathan Small, *Entrepreneur*, 22 Sep 2020).

### 17.6 Suspense when the reader knows the end

- **Gerrig (1989).** Readers feel suspense about outcomes they know, if
  the text puts a concrete obstacle in the way (R3).
- **Carroll.** Suspense needs only entertained uncertainty (R3).
- **Bordwell on Lessing.** The audience's foreknowledge is "the source of
  the most violent emotions". Greek tragedy's known catastrophe "remains
  suspense nonetheless" (Bordwell, 2013).
- **Forsyth.** Four publishers rejected *The Day of the Jackal* in 1970
  because everyone knew de Gaulle had not been killed. Otto Penzler: "It
  is difficult to imagine that a book or film in which the end is known
  could be a successful suspense story" (CrimeReads, 2019). It is one,
  because the question becomes *how close, by what method, at what
  cost* (§18.3).
- **Mantel.** "The suspense grows in the gap" between what the reader
  and the characters know.

### 17.7 Research and exposition

- **Emma Darwin**, *Historia* (28 Apr 2019): there are tools, not rules:
  - straightforward telling (don't "despise straightforward Telling");
  - filtering through a viewpoint;
  - a narrator's wider lens;
  - slipping information into a sentence about something else;
  - leaving it out.
  
  Ask "Why this? Why now?" Every sentence should do at least two jobs.
- **Darwin on research.** It is "necessary but they're never sufficient
  and sometimes counter-productive" (Substack, 4 Apr 2025).
- **Stone's iceberg and his ban on exhibitionism** (17.4).
- **The failure, as Kirkus names it** (*Lily's Story*): "unable to
  separate an interesting historical fact from one that might be of
  service to his narrative."

### 17.8 The reader contract

- **Darwin.** Drawing on real history creates a "fundamental contract"
  with readers, to be dealt with "honestly and responsibly" ("Stick,
  Twist, Fudge or Change?", Substack, 14 June 2024).
- **The author's note** is where the contract is settled. It names the
  invented characters, the composites, and the dates moved or telescoped.
  Bernard Cornwell ends each Sharpe and Uhtred novel with a "Historical
  Note" (unverified detail). Jerome de Groot, *The Historical Novel*
  (Routledge, 2009), treats such paratexts as part of the genre (his
  treatment of the author's note is unverified).

### 17.9 Questions for a historical novel

**H1. Does the novel supply the "therefore" that the record lacks?**
- *Fail:* chapters exist because something documented happened that
  year, and recorded events arrive on their dates without reference to
  anything the protagonist did.
- *Fix:* for each recorded event, invent the earlier choice of the
  protagonist that it punishes or rewards, and the opponent who acts
  between events (A2).
- *Example:* *Pillars* makes the White Ship, a recorded disaster, the
  secret cause of its fictional plot.

**H2. Is the invention in the gaps (motive, private talk, the why) rather
than against the record?**
- *Fail:* invented events that redirect known history, which knowing
  readers feel as cheating. Or no invention at all: each chapter retells
  a documented episode with no interior.
- *Fix:* keep the documented *what*; invent the *why* (Mantel).
- *Example:* *Wolf Hall* adds More's household and Cromwell's motives,
  not new outcomes.

**H3. Since the reader knows the outcome, where does the suspense
live?**
- *Fail:* famous outcomes are announced, and the bribes and battles
  summarised.
- *Fix:* move the question to how, how close and at what cost. Put a
  vivid obstacle in front of every famous outcome (Gerrig). Add a rival
  or hunter who closes in on the protagonist's method, and a hard date.
- *Example:* Forsyth.

**H4. Is the span justified, compressed and organised into campaigns?**
- *Fail:* uniform jumps of one to three years, and every documented
  episode gets a chapter.
- *Fix:*
  - choose the points of friction;
  - render them at scene length;
  - let the gaps between them be summary or memory;
  - give each act a campaign with a deadline (17.5).
- *Example:* Kushner's four months; Sorkin's three launches; *Bring Up
  the Bodies*.

**H5. Is the research in the story's service?**
- *Fail:* encyclopaedic paragraphs, pet interests, "See what I know!".
- *Fix:* route the information through a character's need at that
  moment, or cut it (Darwin; Stone).
- *Example:* Kirkus on *Lily's Story* (the failure).

**H6. When the protagonist is the world-historical figure, what stops
him becoming a monument?**
- *Fail:* he is right from the first page, and the forces he bends are
  scenery.
- *Fix:*
  - show him becoming: early mistakes whose consequences echo later;
  - give the social forces recurring faces;
  - consider a witness with their own stake (17.3).
- *Example:* Tiro; Schuyler.

**H7. Do the characters live forward, inside their own horizon?**
- *Fail:* textbook significance in their mouths, and forecasts in the
  narration (V3).
- *Fix:* Stone's rule against the "a posteriori prophet". Mantel's gap.
- *Example:* *Wolf Hall*'s present tense.

**H8. Does the author's note say what was invented, compressed or
moved?**
- *Fail:* no note, or a list of sources only.
- *Fix:* a short note naming the deliberate liberties (17.8).

# Part IV — The commercial thriller

## §18 What a thriller must deliver

### 18.1 The engine

Lee Child, "A Simple Way to Create Suspense" (*NYT* Opinionator, 8 Dec
2012). The wrong question is "How do you create suspense?" The right one
is "How do you make your family hungry?", and the answer is "You make
them wait four hours for dinner." So: "ask or imply a question at the
beginning of the story, and then … delay the answer". The big answer
comes after "a string of smaller drip-drip-drip answers". Sympathetic
characters and elaborate entanglements are "all luxuries. The basic
narrative fuel is always the slow unveiling of the final answer."

### 18.2 The genre contract (Story Grid)

From Story Grid's "Thriller Story Cheat Sheet" (storygrid.com):

- **Global value:** "LIFE AND DEATH". The spectrum runs life,
  unconsciousness, death, damnation. The story need not reach damnation,
  but "the potential for damnation and the vehicle for damnation must be
  expressed."
- **Controlling idea:** "Justice (or Tyranny) prevails when the
  protagonist unleashes (or denies) his/her special gift."
- **Obligatory scenes:**
  1. an inciting crime "indicitive [sic] of a master villain. There must
     be victims";
  2. a speech in praise of the villain;
  3. the hero becomes the victim ("the villain makes his crimes
     personal");
  4. the hero at the mercy of the villain, "the Core event of the
     thriller, the moment when the Hero unleashes his/her gift when all
     is lost";
  5. a false ending: "There must be two endings."
- **Conventions:**
  - the MacGuffin, "the villain's object of desire…what the villain
    wants";
  - investigative red herrings;
  - making it personal: "The villain needs the hero to get his/her
    MacGuffin";
  - a clock: "There is a limited time for the hero to act."
- **Subgenre:** the cheat sheet lists "Financial", with Christopher
  Reich's *Numbered Account* (1998) as its example.

### 18.3 Case: *The Day of the Jackal* (Forsyth, 1971)

Sources: Wikipedia; Penzler, CrimeReads, 16 Oct 2019.

- **Structure.** Three parts: "Anatomy of a Plot", "Anatomy of a
  Manhunt", "Anatomy of a Kill", and an epilogue.
- **History as the starting point.** It opens on the real OAS attack at
  Petit-Clamart (22 Aug 1962). The rest is invented.
- **Where the suspense comes from:**
  - procedure: forged identities, the custom rifle;
  - near-misses;
  - a leak inside the council;
  - the killer's unknown identity;
  - the parallel between hunter and hunted.
- **The ending.** The climax falls on Liberation Day, 25 August 1963.
  Lebel deduces the date because it is the one day de Gaulle reliably
  appears. The epilogue leaves open who the Jackal was.
- **Why it works:**
  - The reader's question moves from *whether* to *how close, how, at
    what cost*.
  - The antagonist's plan is the spine, and Part 1 hands the reader the
    bomb.
  - A worthy opponent arrives as a second clock.
  - A hard date makes the deadline absolute.

### 18.4 Midpoint, all-is-lost, false ending

- **The midpoint.** James Scott Bell's "mirror moment": the lead sees
  what he has become, or that death (physical, professional,
  psychological) is now likely. The second half follows from it (S5).
- **All Is Lost** (Snyder, in CRAFT.md §1) and **the hero at the mercy of
  the villain** (Story Grid) are the same low point seen from two sides.
- **The false ending** comes after them: an apparent resolution, then
  the real one (E4).

### 18.5 When the protagonist is the powerful one

The thriller form assumes a hero more vulnerable than the villain. A
financier, a king or a spymaster as protagonist breaks that assumption.
Coyne's criticism of one *Killing Eve* core event was that it was unclear
"who the hero was, who the villain was, and who was at whose mercy"
(storygrid.com; search excerpt). The options:

- **(a) The villain-protagonist.** Invert the form, and give the hero's
  role to his opponent.
- **(b) The underdog scale.** Find the scale on which the powerful man
  is weaker: a commoner against emperors, a firm against a league of
  states, a sinner against the Church.
- **(c) The villain who needs him.** Make a greater power the master
  villain whose MacGuffin requires the protagonist's gift. This is
  Story Grid's "making it personal" in reverse.

Carroll's work on suspense raises a further problem: readers must want
an outcome (R3). If the protagonist bribes and ruins, either the
opposition must be worse or the reader's wish must attach to something he
protects.

### 18.6 Financial-thriller conventions

The editor's synthesis, unverified as a published list:

- the money trail as the investigation;
- asymmetric information as the weapon;
- a deal with a deadline (a closing, a settlement, an election, an
  audit);
- the conspiracy of the respectable;
- exposure, rather than death, as the stake that can still kill.

### 18.7 Questions for a thriller

**TH1. What does the villain want, and why does he need the hero?**
- *Fail:* no MacGuffin. The opposition reacts.
- *Fix:* give the opponent an object of desire that requires the
  protagonist (18.2).

**TH2. What is the clock: for the book, and in each act?**
- *Fail:* deadlines are set and paid in summary, or there are none.
- *Fix:* one hard date for the book, one per act, each paid in a
  dramatised scene (CRAFT.md §4 rule 3).

**TH3. Who is at whose mercy at the core event?**
- *Fail:* the protagonist is never at anyone's mercy.
- *Fix:* a scene where a court, a mob, a ruler or a rival holds his life
  or fortune, and only his particular gift saves him, at a cost.

**TH4. Is there a speech in praise of the villain?**
- *Fail:* opponents are dismissed as fools.
- *Fix:* an ally warns the hero how dangerous the opponent is, and the
  book then proves it (A1).

**TH5. Has the villain made it personal?**
- *Fail:* the stakes stay public and financial.
- *Fix:* the opponent strikes at what the hero loves or is (C5).

**TH6. Does the midpoint change the hunt?**
- *Fail:* the middle is a run of similar operations.
- *Fix:* a mirror moment, or a reversal that redefines the goal (S5).

**TH7. Are there two endings?**
- *Fail:* the climax resolves everything at once.
- *Fix:* an apparent victory, then the real reckoning (E4).

# Part V — Case studies of structural editing

## §19 What famous editors actually changed

Each case gives the problem in the editor's words, the proposal, the
outcome, and the question it teaches. Quotations come from the named
secondary sources unless stated; cases rest partly on Wikipedia where
the primary letters were not reachable.

### 19.1 Maxwell Perkins → F. Scott Fitzgerald, *The Great Gatsby* (1924–25)

- **The problem.** In his letter of 20 Nov 1924 Perkins praised the book
  at length, then wrote: "Among a set of characters marvelously palpable
  and vital … Gatsby is somewhat vague … the reader's eyes can never
  quite focus upon him" (Schwartz, Substack, 2025). Wikipedia paraphrases
  the letter: Gatsby was "too vague as a character", and his wealth and
  business "needed a convincing explanation" (citing Perkins 2004,
  pp. 27–30).
- **The proposal.** Small dramatised touches, not explanation: let Gatsby
  "be seen, once or twice, consulting at parties with people of some sort
  of mysterious significance". Let the truth of his claims, Oxford and
  the army, come out "bit by bit" (Beynon, 2021). The letter also noted
  that the block of biography Gatsby tells Nick departs from the book's
  method of letting things come out in the flow of events (unverified
  wording). Perkins also persuaded Fitzgerald that the title *Trimalchio
  in West Egg* was "too obscure and that people would be unable to
  pronounce it" (Wikipedia, citing *Dear Scott/Dear Max*, p. 87).
- **The outcome.**
  - Fitzgerald made "extensive revisions of the sixth and eighth
    chapters" in galleys (Wikipedia, citing Bruccoli 2002).
  - The galley text, published in 2000 as *Trimalchio*, shows the scale.
    Its publisher says "two chapters of Trimalchio were completely
    rewritten" and "Jay Gatsby's past is revealed in a wholly different
    way" (OverDrive description).
  - The Dan Cody story now opens chapter VI (unverified detail).
- **The question.** Is the protagonist the vaguest person in the book?
  Does the author know what he looks like and how he got his money?
  Does backstory arrive as a block, or bit by bit? (C4, V4)

### 19.2 Fitzgerald → Hemingway, *The Sun Also Rises* (1926)

- **The problem.** A peer acting as editor, in a ten-page handwritten
  letter. He accused the opening of "elephantine facetiousness" (Town
  Topics, 2021) and wrote: "I think that there are about 24 sneers,
  superiorities, and nose-thumbings-at-nothing that mar the whole
  narrative up to P. 29." Also: "You can't play with people's attention"
  (both quoted in Carly Cassano, *Literary Traveler*, 1999).
- **The proposal.** Fitzgerald wanted the expository opening removed, not
  polished.
- **The outcome.** Hemingway wired the publisher to drop the opening
  pages. Accounts give anything from 16 pages to a chapter and a half.
  The cut text began "This is a novel about a lady" and filed Brett's
  marriages and drinking in a dozen paragraphs. The book now opens on
  Jake's voice at work: "Robert Cohn was once middleweight boxing
  champion of Princeton." Brett's past leaks out through scenes (Duffus,
  *Craft*, 2021; Wikipedia, citing Wagner-Martin).
- **The question.** Where does the story actually start? Is the opening a
  dossier or a scene? (O1, V4)

### 19.3 Perkins and John Hall Wheelock → Thomas Wolfe (1929, 1935)

- **The problem.** Size and shapelessness, not weak prose. Estimates of
  the cut from *O Lost* to *Look Homeward, Angel* run from 60,000 to
  90,000 words. The draft of *Of Time and the River* was "more than one
  million words long, and still growing" (*Writer's Almanac*, 8 Mar
  2016).
- **The proposal.** Perkins later wrote that the cutting had been
  exaggerated: "Really, it was more a matter of reorganization"
  (*Harvard Library Bulletin*, 1947, via Wikipedia). "A Portrait of
  Bascom Hawke" was judged a digression, and was sliced into sections
  placed through the book (Hardwick, *NYRB*, 2000).
- **The outcome.** Wolfe accepted, then "became convinced that Perkins had
  ruined his book" (*Writer's Almanac*), and left Scribner. The restored
  *O Lost* (2000) was judged "lumbering and ungainly" by Kirkus.
- **The question.** Is this material wrong, or only in the wrong place?
  And, once the editor supplies the shape, whose book is it? (S2, §2.2)

### 19.4 Tay Hohoff → Harper Lee: *Go Set a Watchman* → *To Kill a Mockingbird* (1957–60)

- **The problem.** Hohoff saw that "the spark of the true writer flashed
  in every line", and judged the manuscript "more a series of anecdotes
  than a fully conceived novel" (Wikipedia, "Harper Lee" and "Tay
  Hohoff", citing Jonathan Mahler, *NYT*, 12 July 2015, and Charles
  Shields, *Mockingbird*, 2006).
- **The proposal.** Lee: "My editor, who was taken by the flashbacks to
  Scout's childhood, persuaded me to write a novel" from the young
  Scout's point of view (PBS NewsHour, 3 Feb 2015). It was a
  reconception, not a cut:
  - the time moved about twenty years earlier;
  - a child narrator, told in retrospect;
  - the Tom Robinson trial, a backstory element, became the engine;
  - Atticus became the moral centre.
  
  (The time, POV and trial details are partly sourced; Mahler credits
  Hohoff with "reconceiving the story".)
- **The outcome.** About two years of rewriting. "When she disagreed with
  a suggestion, we talked it out, sometimes for hours" (Hohoff, via
  Wikipedia). When Lee threw the manuscript out of the window into the
  snow, Hohoff told her to go and pick it up (Shields, p. 131).
- **The question.** Where in this draft does the prose come alive? Is
  that the real book? What single line of cause and consequence would
  turn these anecdotes into a novel? Would a different narrator or time
  unlock it? (P3, S1, V1)

### 19.5 Charles Monteith → William Golding, *Lord of the Flies* (Faber, 1954)

- **The problem.** The manuscript, *Strangers from Within*, had been
  rejected by a first reader as "absurd … Rubbish & dull"; the title was
  "too abstract and too explicit" (Wikipedia).
- **The proposal.**
  - Cut the opening section about an evacuation from nuclear war, so the
    book begins with the boys already on the island.
  - Edit Simon "to remove an encounter with a mysterious figure implied
    to be a god".
  - Find a new title.
  
  (Wikipedia, citing Monteith in Carey, 1987.)
- **The outcome.** Golding accepted, saying he had lost objectivity. The
  title became *Lord of the Flies*, after "Beelzebub".
- **The question.** Does the book begin before the story does? Is the
  meaning embodied or announced? (O1, T1)

### 19.6 Robert Gottlieb: Crichton, Caro, Heller

- **Michael Crichton, *The Andromeda Strain* (1969).** "Bob said he would
  not even consider publishing it unless I was willing to completely
  rewrite it from beginning to end." Gottlieb "said that the novel should
  read like a New Yorker profile, that it should be absolutely
  convincing." Crichton rewrote it, then rewrote half again (Crichton's
  site). The question is form and credibility (G2).
- **Robert Caro, *The Power Broker* (1974).** "we cut 350,000 words out
  of the original manuscript." They disagreed over repetition, which Caro
  used deliberately: "he was thinking as a writer, and I was thinking as
  a reader" (Fresh Air, via GPB, 2023). The question is where a writer's
  deliberate repetition helps and where it nags.
- **Joseph Heller, *Catch-22* (1961).** The house found it "repetitive and
  offensive" and contracted it once Heller agreed to revisions. Gottlieb
  "was the person who came up with the number 22" (Wikipedia, citing
  Eller, 1992, and Erica Heller, 2011). The structural cuts are not
  sourced here.
- **Gottlieb's method.**
  - "Your job as an editor is to figure out what the book needs, but the
    writer has to provide it."
  - "It's not our book … It's his book or her book."
  - "an editor is a reader who edits."

### 19.7 Michael Pietsch → David Foster Wallace, *Infinite Jest* (1996)

Pietsch "made suggestions and recommendations to Wallace, but every
editing decision was Wallace's". Wallace "accepted cuts amounting to
around 250 manuscript pages" and "resisted many changes for reasons he
usually explained" (Wikipedia, citing Pietsch, "Editing Infinite Jest",
2009). The endnotes stayed, as "a way to disrupt the linearity of the
text". The question: for each cut, can I give a reason, and can the
author give one to keep it?

### 19.8 Diana Athill → Jean Rhys, *Wide Sargasso Sea* (1966)

Athill felt "the demise of Mr Rochester and Antoinette's marriage was too
abrupt" (Tomlinson, *THE*, 2000). She made "one suggestion of a mechanism
to overcome a stalled plot", and otherwise "had to do virtually nothing
with the text of the book" (*Dublin Review of Books*, quoting *Stet*,
2000). Rhys rewrote. Athill's creed: "Respect the writer. Don't try and
impose yourself." And: "Not often did one have to do much shaping with
fiction" (*PW*, 2001). The question: is a key turn happening too fast to
believe, and what is the smallest mechanism that lets it happen on the
page? (SC6)

### 19.9 Gordon Lish → Raymond Carver, *What We Talk About When We Talk About Love* (1981)

- **The edit.** Lish cut about half the words and rewrote 10 of the 13
  endings. "A Small, Good Thing" was cut by about a third and retitled
  "The Bath", losing the baker's reconciliation (D. T. Max, *NYT
  Magazine*, 1998; Frank Kovarik, *The Millions*, 2010).
- **The author.** On 8 July 1980 Carver begged him to stop: "my very
  sanity is on the line here … Please, Gordon, for God's sake help me in
  this and try to understand" (Max). He restored "A Small, Good Thing" in
  *Cathedral* (1983): "Eating is a small, good thing in a time like
  this."
- **The verdicts.** Giles Harvey thinks the restored *Beginners* "twice as
  long" and "about half as interesting" (*NYRB*, 2010). Kovarik thinks
  Lish removed "the moments that are most tender and beautiful".
- **The questions, both ways.** Does the ending trust the reader, or
  explain? Is the cut making this author's book better, or making it the
  editor's book? Could the author keep writing in this voice afterwards?
  (E5, §2.2)

### 19.10 When the author was right to refuse

- **Steinbeck.** Viking worried that the final scene of *The Grapes of
  Wrath* was unprepared. Steinbeck refused to change it, arguing that the
  stranger must be a stranger and the act quick and unsentimental
  (*Steinbeck: A Life in Letters*, 1975; unverified wording).
- **Flannery O'Connor.** She answered an editor who wanted *Wise Blood*
  made conventional that she was amenable to criticism "only within the
  sphere of what I am trying to do", and moved to Giroux (unverified
  wording).
- **The lesson.** A note must stay inside the book's own aim (James's
  donnée; Updike's first rule). When the author's refusal shows what the
  book is for, record it and move on.

### 19.11 *Tender Is the Night*: do not "fix" the mechanism

Fitzgerald came to believe that his flashback structure was the book's
fault. Malcolm Cowley's posthumous edition put it in chronological
order. Most critics and later editions went back to the 1934 order
(Wikipedia; the reasons are unverified). The question: before
reordering, what does the current order make the reader feel? (S9)

### 19.12 Toni Morrison as editor (Random House, c. 1967–83)

Her approach "depended largely on the writer, the project and their
relationship to the words". Of Toni Cade Bambara's text: "very little
room to move things around" (Ravynn Stringfield on Dana Williams's
*Toni at Random*, 2025). She described editing as "Piercing knots in
language and in ideas" (*PW*, 2019). As an author she said of editors:
"The good [editors] make all the difference … if you get the wrong one,
then you are better off alone" (*Paris Review*, 1993, via Virago). The
question: is the knot in the language or in the idea? Untie the right
one, and know when a text is already tight.

### 19.13 What the cases teach

1. **Shape before sentences** (Perkins, Gottlieb, Hohoff).
2. **The real book may be inside the draft**: in its flashbacks or its
   minor backstory (Hohoff).
3. **Find where the story starts, and cut the dossier** (Fitzgerald,
   Monteith).
4. **Backstory comes bit by bit; the centre must be in focus** (Perkins).
5. **Misplaced material can be distributed instead of cut**
   (Perkins and Wheelock).
6. **A turn that is too fast needs a mechanism, not a summary** (Athill).
7. **The form must make the premise believable** (Gottlieb and Crichton).
8. **Diagnose; the writer supplies the cure** (Gottlieb; FoxPrint).
9. **Stay inside the book's own aim, and let the author refuse**
   (O'Connor, Steinbeck).
10. **Whose book is it after the edit?** (Lish, Wolfe).

# Part VI — The checklist on one page

## §20 Questions to put to a full manuscript

**Process** (§1–§5)
- Read cold, in full, before the plan (Martin; Gottlieb).
- Keep per-chapter notes (§1.3).
- Build the scene map from the page (Coyne; Watts; M. Bell).
- Track the antagonist, the question and the clock in separate passes
  (Rambo).
- Fill the one-page Foolscap. A blank field is an unmade decision
  (Coyne).
- Write the letter: what works, the few big problems in order, the
  directions (IBR; Masters Review).
- Count; don't assert. Discount your own score (§5).

**Premise**
- P1 One sentence: who wants what, against whom, at what cost?
  (Bransford; Reid; Rambo)
- P2 What is the reader waiting to find out? Posed by when? In doubt?
  (Martin; Child)
- P3 One action, or the life of one man? (Aristotle VIII; Stone;
  Morrell)
- P4 Controlling idea: "[value] because [cause]". (McKee; Truby; Stone's
  "cement")
- P5 Genre and its promise. (Coyne; Loftus)

**Structure**
- S1 Because, or only after? (Aristotle IX–X; Forster; Parker and Stone)
- S2 What fails if this chapter goes? (Aristotle VIII; Forster)
- S3 Same bet raised, past points of no return? (McKee; Coyne; Maass)
- S4 One desire line to the end? (Truby)
- S5 A midpoint that changes understanding and strategy? (Weiland;
  J. S. Bell)
- S6 Where does momentum flag? (Martin; Stout)
- S7 Words in proportion to importance; main events as scenes? (Browne
  and King; Stone)
- S8 The span chosen; time spent by crisis? (M. Bell; Stone; Mantel)
- S9 Does the order of telling serve the book? (Sternberg; *Tender*)

**Scene**
- SC1 Does it turn a value? (McKee; Coyne)
- SC2 A real crisis between two costs? (Coyne; Ingermanson; Cron)
- SC3 Does the outcome become the next goal? (Swain; Bickham)
- SC4 A gap between expectation and result? (McKee)
- SC5 Tension on every page? (Maass)
- SC6 The turn on the page, at a believable speed? (Browne and King;
  Athill)

**Character**
- C1 A concrete want; does he drive events? (Vonnegut; Rambo; Morrell)
- C2 What is he wrong about, and does the plot attack it? (Cron;
  Weiland; Truby)
- C3 A climax choice between want and need? (Truby; Weiland)
- C4 Is he as vivid as those round him? (Perkins)
- C5 Personal and public stakes, widening? (Maass; Martin)
- C6 A web of answers; can any characters merge? (Truby; Rambo; Forster)

**Antagonism**
- A1 Full strength, on every level? (McKee)
- A2 Same prize, persisting? (Truby; Follett; Dunnett)
- A3 A plan the reader can see? (Story Grid; Forsyth)

**Narration**
- V1 Whose story; the right viewpoint? (Ward; Lukács; Hohoff)
- V2 What does the reader know that the characters don't? (Hitchcock;
  Sternberg)
- V3 No hindsight? (Stone; Mantel)
- V4 Backstory bit by bit? (Perkins; Browne and King)

**Theme**
- T1 Embodied, not announced? (Monteith; Browne and King)
- T2 Worth doing: what does it illuminate? (Carlyle; James; Stone)

**Genre and form**
- G1 Conventions and obligatory scenes on the page? (Coyne)
- G2 Does the form make the premise convincing? (Gottlieb)

**Reader**
- R1 An open question at every chapter end? (Child; Martin)
- R2 Suspense, curiosity, surprise; lines resolved? (Brewer and
  Lichtenstein; Sternberg)
- R3 Does the reader want an outcome that looks unlikely? (Carroll;
  Gerrig)
- R4 The shape of fortune? (Reagan et al.)
- R5 Does the end recast the beginning? (Brooks)

**Openings and endings**
- O1 Starts at the point of change? (Friedman; agents; Fitzgerald)
- O2 A first-page question the last page answers? (Rambo; Child)
- E1 The protagonist's costly choice answers P2? (Rambo; Morrell)
- E2 Convergence, not a roll-call? (Story Grid)
- E3 Surprising yet caused? (Aristotle XI)
- E4 Two endings? (Story Grid)
- E5 Does the ending trust the reader? (Lish and Carver; Steinbeck)

**Historical** (§17.9)
- H1 The invented therefore.
- H2 Invention in the gaps.
- H3 Known-outcome suspense.
- H4 Span and campaigns.
- H5 Research in service.
- H6 Not a monument.
- H7 Living forward.
- H8 The author's note.

**Thriller** (§18.7)
- TH1 The villain's MacGuffin.
- TH2 The clocks.
- TH3 At whose mercy.
- TH4 The speech in praise of the villain.
- TH5 Made personal.
- TH6 The midpoint mirror.
- TH7 Two endings.
