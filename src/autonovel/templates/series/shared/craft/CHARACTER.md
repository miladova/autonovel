# CHARACTER.md: people with depth

How a novelist turns a name with a function into a person a reader
believes in, and how to keep a large cast distinct. Read in full by
`/autonovel:cast` (which writes each book's dossiers, `books/<book>/cast.md`),
and by `/autonovel:draft` (step 3d), `/autonovel:revise`,
`/autonovel:deepen-character` and `/autonovel:evaluate`
(`character_depth`). DESIGN.md §7 decides the cast as a web; this
document is how each member of it comes alive. Package-owned prompt
material: `autonovel refresh-templates` keeps it current.

**Why it exists.** A design gives each person a line — want, fear,
secret, contradiction, signature. That is enough to plan a book and not
enough to write a person. A drafter given a line renders the line: the
habit recurs (the receipt, the straightened sleeve), the contradiction is
stated once, and the person under it never appears. Readers then find the
characters simple, and they are right: each is a trait and a habit. Depth is not more description. It is a person who wants things of
their own, acts against their own grain for reasons we can see, carries a
history with each of the others, and chooses, under pressure, between
two things they value.

## §0 The card

1. Every person who matters wants something of their own in every scene
   they are in.
2. The contradiction is acted, never labelled.
3. Each principal makes a choice under pressure in each act, and it costs.
4. The viewpoint has an inner life beyond the plot.
5. What is not said carries as much as what is said.
6. Every important pair has a history, a balance and a register.
7. A signature deepens each time it recurs, or it is a tic.
8. A round character can surprise us convincingly.
9. Scenes of secrets let the reader track who knows what.
10. Principals end changed, and the plot is the cause.
11. Every member of an ensemble is re-anchored when they return.
12. Real people are built from the record, never from their famous trait.

# Part I — The method

## §1 What depth is

- **Flat and round.** E. M. Forster divided characters into flat, which
  "in their purest form … are constructed round a single idea or
  quality", and round; and gave the test: "The test of a round character
  is whether it is capable of surprising in a convincing way. If it
  never surprises, it is flat" (*Aspects of the Novel*, 1927, ch. 4).
  Flat characters are not a fault in themselves — Forster defends them,
  and so does James Wood, who finds many of literature's most alive
  people "flat" (*How Fiction Works*, 2008) — but a protagonist and the
  people the plot turns on must be round, and a minor player must at
  least be vivid: specific enough in one detail and one want that we
  could pick them out of a crowd.
- **Characterisation and true character.** Robert McKee separates the
  observable surface (age, looks, manner, job, habits) from true
  character, which "is revealed in the choices a human being makes under
  pressure — the greater the pressure, the greater the revelation"; and
  "dimension means contradiction", either inside the deep character or
  between the surface and what lies under it (*Story*, 1997, ch. 5).
  A habit is surface. A choice is character.
- **Three dimensions.** Lajos Egri's "bone structure": physiology (age,
  body, health, looks), sociology (class, money, work, religion, family,
  standing) and psychology (temperament, ambition, frustration, fears,
  the private standards a person lives by) (*The Art of Dramatic
  Writing*, 1946). A person drawn only in the third dimension floats; the
  first two are what make them a particular person in a particular place
  and time, and in historical fiction they are where the period lives.
- **Inner conflict.** Donald Maass: the characters readers remember are
  at war with themselves — two desires that cannot both be satisfied, a
  belief the story will test — and do and say what the reader would not
  dare (*Writing the Breakout Novel*, 2001; *The Fire in Fiction*,
  2009).
- **Want, need, misbelief.** John Truby builds a character from a
  weakness and a need (psychological, and moral — how they hurt others),
  a desire, and a ghost, the past event that still haunts them (*The
  Anatomy of Story*, 2007). Lisa Cron calls the false belief the
  *misbelief* and tells the writer to find the scene in which it was
  formed (*Story Genius*, 2016). The want is what they chase; the need is
  what the story will cost them to learn.

## §2 Who gets a dossier

- **Principals** get the full dossier (§3): the viewpoint characters, the
  intimate, the rival, the mentor, the influence character, every ally
  on stage in three or more chapters, and anyone whose choice the plot
  turns on.
- **Minor and ensemble players** — a council, a court, seven princes who
  must each be won — get the short form (§3, last bullet): who, where,
  what they want, their price or their fear, one concrete detail, and a
  return tag (§6).
- **Merge** two characters who serve the same function and pass the same
  tests in the same way (DESIGN.md §7, the web).

## §3 The dossier

Elizabeth George writes, for every important character before drafting,
a prompt sheet of facts and then a free, present-tense
stream-of-consciousness analysis in which the character comes into
focus (*Write Away*, 2004). The dossier is that work, done for a writer
who forgets nothing it is told and invents only what it is asked to.
Each field must be specific to this person: a field that would be true of
half the cast is empty.

- **Role:** the function in the web (DESIGN.md §7) and what question of
  the book this person answers differently.
- **Record:** real, invented or composite; for a real person, the
  research notes that hold their record.
- **Body** (Egri): age in each part of the book, health, what hurts,
  appetite, looks, how they move and sit; what age and the body are doing
  to them now.
- **Standing** (Egri): class, money, office, religion, family; what they
  owe and are owed; whom they must defer to and who defers to them.
- **Temperament:** how they meet trouble — attack, wait, joke, pray,
  count, run.
- **Want** (the book) and **need** (Truby); **misbelief** (Cron) and the
  scene that formed it; **fear**; **wound** or ghost.
- **Contradiction:** two qualities in tension, each with the act that
  shows it. Not "pious and venal" but "prays an hour before dawn, in
  earnest; then prices the funeral".
- **Values in order:** what gives way to what when they collide — the
  ranking a pressure choice will reveal and, once in the book, reverse.
- **Secret:** one the plot can expose.
- **Private life:** what they do when the protagonist isn't there; whom
  they love; one thing they want that has nothing to do with the plot.
- **Perception:** what they notice first in a room, and the words they
  name it with (a banker sees weights and debts, a soldier exits, a
  priest sins). Free indirect style lets the narration take on the
  character's own words; Wood's account of it is the model (*How Fiction
  Works*).
- **Humour:** what makes them laugh and how they joke, or that they
  never do.
- **Self-deception:** what they believe about themselves that is not so.
- **Voice:** pointer to `voice.md` Part 4 when it has their fingerprint.
- **Relationships:** one line per person who matters to them — history,
  the balance (who owes whom what), what is never said, how it changes.
- **Pressure choices:** in each act, by chapter, the moment they must
  choose between two things they value, and what it costs.
- **Arc:** first appearance to last, and the event that causes each turn.
- **Signature, and how it deepens:** the gesture, object or phrase, and
  what its first, second and third appearances each show.
- **On the page:** for each chapter they are in, the one thing that
  chapter shows of them that no earlier chapter has.
- **Return tag:** one clause that tells a reader who has forgotten them
  who they are and what they want; per act when the want changes.
- **Private monologue:** 150–300 words, first person, present tense, as
  they would think it at their first appearance, unguarded, blind spot
  showing. For the writer only; never pasted into a chapter.
- **Short form** (minor and ensemble players): who, where, age, want,
  price or fear, one concrete detail of face or manner (from the record
  when real), return tag.

## §4 On the page

The dossier is useless if it stays in the file. These are how depth
reaches the page.

1. **A want of their own in every scene.** Kurt Vonnegut: "Every
   character should want something, even if it is only a glass of
   water" (*Bagombo Snuff Box*, 1999, preface). The courier who brings
   the news also wants to be thanked for the ride; the clerk wants to get
   home to his mother; the wife wants her husband to feel what he is
   about to count. A person who has come into the scene only to serve the
   protagonist's want is a function.
2. **The contradiction acted.** Show both halves in what the person does,
   close enough together that the reader feels the strain. Never name it.
3. **The choice under pressure.** Once an act at least, put each
   principal where they must give up one thing they value for another,
   and let the reader see the cost (McKee). This is where a reader
   learns who someone is.
4. **The inner life beyond the plot.** A viewpoint character who thinks
   only about the plot is a calculator. Give them, each scene: a feeling
   they refuse to have, a memory the moment calls up, the body (cold,
   tired, old, hungry), something they notice that has nothing to do with
   the business at hand, and at least once a thought at odds with what
   they say.
5. **Subtext.** What people leave unsaid, or say instead of the thing
   they mean, is where relationships live (Charles Baxter, *The Art of
   Subtext*, 2007). Let a request go unmade, a grief be answered with
   arithmetic, a quarrel be about the wrong thing.
6. **Relationships with a history.** In every important exchange the past
   between the two shapes what is said — an old debt, an old affection, a
   grievance neither mentions. Each pair has its own register: a man
   talks to his wife, his clerk and his rival in three different
   languages.
7. **Signatures that deepen.** A habit or object is worth repeating only
   when each appearance shows something new: the receipt that is a joke
   in chapter 1 is a pride in chapter 5 and a grief in chapter 19. The
   same tic three times, meaning the same thing, is a tic.
8. **Surprise in a convincing way** (Forster). Somewhere in each act a
   principal does what we did not expect, and on reflection we see they
   always would have.
9. **Who knows what.** Readers enjoy tracking minds within minds — what
   A believes B suspects C knows; Lisa Zunshine traces the pleasure of
   fiction to this exercise of our theory of mind (*Why We Read
   Fiction*, 2006). In scenes of secrets, deals and betrayals, make each
   person's belief about the others' knowledge visible.
10. **Change caused by the plot.** Principals end the book different, and
    the reader can point to the events that changed them.
11. **The others are heroes of their own stories.** Secondary characters
    have wants that cross the protagonist's, arcs of their own and lives
    off the page (Maass). The rival is right about something; the mentor
    wants something the lesson serves; the friend has a reason to sell
    you.
12. **Show by act and speech, not by the narrator's verdict.** "He was
    vain" is a label; a man who orders his mourning clothes the day the
    Emperor's courier leaves is a person.

## §5 Real people

- **Start from the record.** Acts, letters, recorded words, portraits,
  what friends and enemies wrote. The record is full of contradictions —
  the usurer who builds houses for the poor, the reformer's protector
  who collects relics — and those are the depth a novelist would have to
  invent for a made-up person, already true.
- **Invent in the silences.** The novelist's licence begins where the
  record stops: the private conversation, the feeling, the night before.
  Mark invention in the dossier so the drafter knows which is which.
- **Give them the ordinary.** Health, money worries, family quarrels,
  boredom, appetite. Great people were also tired and hungry, and the
  ordinary is what makes them people rather than monuments.
- **Never shrink them to the famous trait.** The richest man in Europe
  is not only rich; the vain bookkeeper is not only vain. A real person
  reduced to the line an encyclopedia gives them is a type with a name.
- **Use their words, plainly.** A recorded saying in the person's own
  mouth, in the book's register, is worth a page of description; never
  quote more than the scene needs.

## §6 Ensembles and returning characters

- **Distinguish by want and price, then by one concrete detail.** Seven
  princes are seven different wants (cash in hand, the crown for
  himself, to be left alone) before they are seven faces. Give each one
  physical or verbal detail from the record and keep it.
- **The return tag.** Tolstoy keeps a cast of hundreds straight partly
  by tags: a person returns with the same small physical detail, so the
  reader knows them at once. A return tag is that, plus what they want:
  one clause that re-anchors a person a reader may have forgotten. Use it
  the first time someone comes back after an absence, and vary the
  wording.
- **Re-anchor the whole group.** When an ensemble comes on stage, give
  each member their line again until the book has shown each often enough
  to stand alone. A reader who meets seven princes in the first chapter
  will not remember them four chapters later without help.
- **A cast list** at the front of the book helps a reader who wants to
  check, and is customary in historical novels with large casts; it is
  not a substitute for re-anchoring on the page.

## §7 How characters come out flat

| Pattern | What it looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A trait and a habit | the courier and his receipts, the vain clerk and his sleeves, nothing else | §4.1 a want in the scene, §4.3 a choice, §4.7 the signature deepened |
| A function | the messenger who exists to deliver news | §4.1, §4.11 a life off the page |
| The calculator | the protagonist's inner life is plot reasoning | §4.4 the inner life beyond the plot |
| The label | "pious and venal, both in earnest" written, never shown | §4.2 the contradiction acted |
| Strangers who have known each other for years | an exchange with no history, no debt, no grievance | §4.6 |
| The tic | the same gesture three times, meaning the same thing | §4.7 |
| The villain without reasons | the rival who is merely bad | §4.11 he is right about something |
| The nice people | no edges, no wants that cross the hero's | §4.1, §4.11 |
| The narrator's verdict | "she was clever", "he was the vainest man in town" in place of an act | §4.12 |
| One voice for all | every character witty, or every character plain, in the same rhythm | `voice.md` Part 4; cover the speaker tags |
| The monument | a real person reduced to their famous trait | §5 |
| The forgotten | an ensemble member returns and the reader can't place him | §6 the return tag |

# Part II — The tests (C1–C12)

`/autonovel:cast` records a verdict for each principal; `evaluate`'s
`character_depth` asks the same questions of a chapter.

- **C1 Own want.** In every scene they are in, they want something that
  is not the protagonist's want.
- **C2 Contradiction acted.** Both halves shown in acts, at least once
  per act they appear in.
- **C3 Pressure choice.** At least one per act, between two things they
  value, with a visible cost.
- **C4 Inner life** (viewpoint characters). Every scene has a feeling
  refused or felt, a memory, the body, or a thought at odds with speech.
- **C5 Relationships.** Every principal pair has a history, a balance, an
  unsaid thing, and changes over the book.
- **C6 Perception.** They notice things only they would, and name them in
  their own words.
- **C7 Private life.** One want that has nothing to do with the plot, seen
  at least once.
- **C8 Secret.** One the plot exposes, or nearly does.
- **C9 Change.** They end different, and the plot is the cause.
- **C10 Signature deepens.** Each recurrence shows something new.
- **C11 Distinct.** No two principals pass the tests the same way; cover
  the speaker tags and the reader still knows who is talking; every
  ensemble player has a return tag.
- **C12 From the record** (real people). Contradiction, fears and
  recorded words come from the research and are cited; invention is
  marked; they are more than their famous trait.
