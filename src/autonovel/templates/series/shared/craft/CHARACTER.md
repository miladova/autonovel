# CHARACTER.md: people with depth

How a novelist turns a name with a function into a person a reader
believes in, and how to keep a large cast distinct. Read in full by
`/autonovel:cast` (which writes each book's dossiers, `books/<book>/cast.md`),
and by `/autonovel:draft` (step 3d), `/autonovel:revise`,
`/autonovel:deepen-character` and `/autonovel:evaluate`
(`character_depth`). DESIGN.md §7 decides the cast as a web; this
document is how each member of it comes alive. Package-owned prompt
material: `autonovel refresh-templates` keeps it current.

**Why it exists.** A design gives each person a line — want, fear,
secret, contradiction, signature. That is enough to plan a book and not
enough to write a person. A drafter given a line renders the line: the
habit recurs (the receipt, the straightened sleeve), the contradiction is
stated once, the viewpoint character thinks only about the plan, and the
people around him exist to hand him news or obstacles. Readers then find
the characters simple, and they are right. Robert McKee describes the
result exactly: "a cluster of traits stuck on a name. Decorating a
protagonist with quirks does not open his character and draw empathy.
Rather, eccentricities may close him off and keep us at a distance"
(*Story*, 1997, p. 378). Depth is not more description and not more
quirks. It is a person who wants things of their own, acts against their
own grain for reasons we can see, carries a history with each of the
others, behaves in a particular way under stress, and chooses, at rising
cost, between two things they value.

**How sources are marked.** Quotations marked *[seen at second hand]*
were found quoted by a named secondary source rather than read on the
page; the rest were read in the text itself.

## §0 The card

1. Everyone in the scene wants something in this scene.
2. Character is choice under cost, never a label.
3. Each principal has one contradiction, and it acts in at least three
   scenes.
4. Under stress each principal does one particular thing, for a reason,
   at a cost — not a tic.
5. The viewpoint has a hinterland: feelings, memories, opinions and
   misreadings beyond the plan, one of them pulling against it.
6. The protagonist is hurting someone at the start, and owns his
   mistakes.
7. Every secondary character is the hero of their own story and acts
   out of the protagonist's sight.
8. Each person sees the room in their own words, and says less than they
   mean.
9. Scenes of secrets let the reader track who knows what, to about four
   layers and no deeper.
10. A round character can surprise us convincingly.
11. Whoever returns after a long absence is re-anchored at once, in a
    few words, and the tag recurs at a moment that matters; nobody the
    reader remembers is introduced twice.
12. Real people are invented only inside the record's silences, in the
    mind of their own time, and never shrunk to their reputation.

# Part I — The method

## §1 What depth is

- **Character is choice.** Aristotle: "Character is that which reveals
  moral purpose, showing what kind of things a man chooses or avoids"
  (*Poetics*, trans. Butcher, ch. VI). McKee: "TRUE CHARACTER is revealed
  in the choices a human being makes under pressure — the greater the
  pressure, the deeper the revelation, the truer the choice to the
  character's essential nature"; and "choices made when nothing is at
  risk mean little" (*Story*, p. 101). Characterisation, "the sum of all
  observable qualities" (p. 100), is the surface: age, looks, manner,
  habits. A habit is surface. A choice is character.
- **Dimension is contradiction.** "Dimension means contradiction: either
  within deep character (guilt-ridden ambition) or between
  characterization and deep character (a charming thief). These
  contradictions must be consistent. It doesn't add dimension to portray
  a guy as nice throughout a film, then in one scene have him kick a
  cat" (McKee, p. 378). Lajos Egri: "A human being is a maze of seeming
  contradictions. Planning one thing, he at once does another; loving,
  he believes he hates" (*The Art of Dramatic Writing*, 1946, p. 50).
- **Flat and round.** E. M. Forster: flat characters "are constructed
  round a single idea or quality"; "the test of a round character is
  whether it is capable of surprising in a convincing way. If it never
  surprises, it is flat. If it does not convince, it is a flat pretending
  to be round" (*Aspects of the Novel*, 1927, ch. IV). Forster defends
  flat characters in their place — they "are easily recognized whenever
  they come in", and "a novel that is at all complex often requires flat
  people as well as round" — and shows one sentence rounding a minor
  character for a moment. James Wood would drop "roundness" as an ideal
  altogether: "It is subtlety that matters … a very small point of entry
  will do" (*How Fiction Works*, 2008) *[seen at second hand]*. So: the
  protagonist and the people the plot turns on must be round; a minor
  player must be vivid, through one freshly observed trait and one want.
- **Three dimensions.** Egri's "bone structure", which "the author must
  know thoroughly": physiology (age, build, health, defects, posture),
  sociology (class, work, education, home life, religion, place in the
  community, amusements) and psychology (moral standards, ambition,
  frustrations, temperament, complexes, abilities) (pp. 33–37). "It is
  not enough … to know if he is rude, polite, religious … You must know
  why" (p. 33). In historical fiction the first two dimensions are where
  the period lives.
- **Want, need, wound.** John Truby: the desire is "a goal outside the
  character"; the need is inside, and "your hero should not be aware of
  his need at the beginning of the story"; a moral need means the hero
  "must be hurting at least one other person at the beginning of the
  story", or he risks "being perfect or being a victim. Both of these are
  the kiss of death in storytelling"; the ghost is "an event from the
  past that still haunts the hero in the present", and works as a
  counter-desire (*The Anatomy of Story*, 2007, chs. 3, 8). Lisa Cron
  calls the false belief the misbelief and asks the writer to draft the
  scene in which it formed (*Story Genius*, 2016).
- **No self-explaining.** "Characters with lucid self-knowledge … are not
  only boring but phony. Self-explanation must be validated or
  contradicted in action" (McKee, p. 377).

## §2 Who gets a dossier

Elizabeth Bowen: "The novelist must allot (to the point of rationing)
psychological space" ("Notes on Writing a Novel", 1945).

- **Principals** get the full dossier (§3): the viewpoint characters, the
  intimate, the rival, the mentor, the influence character, every ally
  on stage in three or more chapters, and anyone whose choice the plot
  turns on.
- **Secondary and ensemble players** — a council, a court, seven princes
  who must each be won — get the short form: role, relation to the
  viewpoint, their own want and stake, one concern of their own, one
  fresh trait, a return tag, and for a real person the record line.
- **Bit parts** get one freshly observed trait and nothing that promises
  more than the book will deliver: "Bit parts should be drawn
  deliberately flat … but not dull. Give each a freshly observed trait"
  (McKee, p. 380).
- **Merge** two characters who share both temperament and stance:
  "If all the characters are the same type … it will be like an orchestra
  of nothing but drums" (Egri, p. 114).
- **An observer from below.** A book centred on the great needs at least
  one lesser person — a clerk, a courier, a secretary — with a life and a
  stake of their own who sees them close up, as Robert Harris tells
  Cicero through his secretary Tiro.

## §3 The dossier

Elizabeth George writes, for every important character before drafting,
a prompt sheet and then a free analysis "in stream-of-consciousness style
and in present tense", acting as "the character's psychiatrist,
psychoanalyst, probation officer, and biographer"; the sheet's two key
terms are the core need, the force "that drives a person to do the
things he does", and the *pathological maneuver*, "the action the
character takes under stress" (*Write Away*, 2004, ch. 19) *[seen at
second hand]*. Forster: a character "is real when the novelist knows
everything about it. He may not choose to tell us all he knows" (ch.
III). Mantel: "The more you know, as a writer, the less you have to do on
the page" (Reith Lecture 4, 2017). The dossier is that knowledge. Every
field must be specific to this person: a field that would be true of half
the cast is empty.

1. **Record and gap** (real people). What the record fixes in the book's
   span (dated acts, offices, recorded words, letters, portraits, known
   relationships); where it is silent or contradictory — Mantel's "area
   of doubt", which is "where you go to work"; who the sources are and
   what each wanted believed ("who is telling me this, and why does he
   want me to believe it?", Lecture 4); the reputation, and what the book
   shows against it.
2. **Three dimensions** (Egri): body — age in each part of the book,
   health, what hurts, how they move, the portrait; social world —
   class, office, money, household, faith, allegiances, what they owe and
   are owed; psychology — temperament, ambition, chief disappointment.
3. **Want, need, wound:** the want, in a sentence with a deadline; the
   core need; the misbelief and the scene that formed it; the ghost (a
   documented event for a real person where there is one), and when and
   by whom it is revealed; **whom they are hurting at the start.**
4. **Contradiction:** one line ("a pious man who prices the funeral"),
   the three scenes where it acts, and what they would never do — and the
   pressure that will make them do it (Donald Maass's question,
   *Writing the Breakout Novel Workbook*, 2004 *[seen at second hand]*).
5. **Under stress:** the pathological maneuver — what they do when the
   core need is blocked, what it costs them or others, and how it worsens
   as the pressure rises; and what they put first when rank, kin, faith,
   oath and profit collide.
6. **Period mind** (real people above all): their beliefs about God, sin
   and the afterlife; their duties of rank, lineage and oath; what would
   shame them; and one value a modern reader will find hard — keep it.
   "We shouldn't condescend to the people of the past, nor distort them
   into versions of ourselves" (Mantel, Lecture 2).
7. **Hidden life:** whom they love, desire, fear or mourn; their opinions
   of the people around them, and one opinion they will reverse ("take
   any one of those opinions and find a spot to reverse it", Maass,
   2011); what they do alone; a secret the plot can expose.
8. **Perception and voice:** what they notice first in a room, and their
   words for it; their idiom and tempo; what they never say aloud
   (pointer to `voice.md` Part 4 where it has their fingerprint).
9. **Place in the web:** their stance on the book's central question;
   against each other principal, three ways alike, one way opposite, the
   shared history, and one thing they will never understand about each
   other (Maass, *The Fire in Fiction*, 2009, ch. 2); the opponent's
   shared goal.
10. **The choice ladder:** their test choices by chapter, each costing
    more than the last, and how their options narrow — "by the end of a
    novel the character's alternatives, many at the beginning, have been
    reduced to almost none" (Bowen).
11. **On the page:** for each chapter they are in, the one thing that
    chapter shows of them that no earlier chapter has.
12. **Return tag:** one clause plus their relation to the viewpoint
    character, per act when the want changes — and the moment of meaning
    where the tag will recur.
13. **Private monologue:** 150–300 words, first person, present tense, as
    they would think it at their first appearance, unguarded, blind spot
    showing (George's free analysis). For the writer only; never pasted
    into a chapter.

If time is short, fields 1, 6 and 7 do most of the work for real people;
3, 4 and 5 for every principal; 9 for the cast as a whole.

## §4 On the page

The dossier is useless if it stays in the file. These are how depth
reaches the page.

1. **Everyone in the scene wants something in this scene.** Kurt
   Vonnegut: "Every character should want something, even if it is only
   a glass of water" (*Bagombo Snuff Box*, 1999, introduction). David
   Mamet's questions for every scene: "Who wants what? What happens if
   [they] don't get it? Why now?" (memo to the writers of *The Unit*,
   2005) *[seen at second hand]*. The want shows in what they ask for,
   hide, refuse or reach for. The courier who brings the news also wants
   to be thanked for the ride; the clerk wants to get home to his mother;
   the wife wants her husband to feel what he is about to count.
2. **Choice, not label.** No narrator's verdict ("a careful man", "the
   vainest man in town") and no self-description that the action doesn't
   test. Henry James: "What is character but the determination of
   incident? What is incident but the illustration of character?" ("The
   Art of Fiction", 1884). Show the man who orders his mourning clothes
   the day the courier leaves.
3. **The contradiction acts.** In each act each principal does at least
   one thing on the page at odds with their dominant trait, for a reason
   the reader can see by the end of the chapter — "unpredictable before
   it has been shown, inevitable when it has been shown" (Bowen). It must
   be consistent with the rest of them, not a single kicked cat.
4. **Stress behaviour, not a tic.** A tic is a habit with no cause. A
   pathological maneuver has a cause, costs something, and gets worse as
   the stakes rise. A recurring habit or object is worth repeating only
   when each appearance shows something new; the same gesture three
   times, meaning the same thing, is a tic.
5. **The hinterland.** Hilary Mantel: "when two people are talking in a
   room, they have a hinterland, and you must suggest it … The motives,
   conscious or unconscious. The wishes, dreams and desires, all held
   invisibly within the body whose actions you describe" (Lecture 4). A
   viewpoint character who thinks only about the plan is a calculator.
   Each chapter's interior gives him at least three of: a feeling he
   won't admit, a memory the moment calls up, a desire apart from the
   scheme, an opinion of someone, a fear, a misreading of another person —
   and at least one of them pulls against his own plan. The body belongs
   here too: cold, tired, old, hungry.
6. **Feelings for the reader to weigh.** Maass: "Readers instead react:
   weighing, judging, comparing and creating, moment by moment, their own
   emotional journey"; "when characters struggle with their feelings,
   readers must referee" (2016). "She felt betrayed" gives the reader
   nothing to weigh; the struggle against the feeling does.
7. **Perception is character.** Describe through what this person would
   notice, in their own words, mostly at points of change; two people in
   one room notice different things. Mantel: "Landscapes, streetscapes,
   objects, are dead in themselves. They only come alive through the
   senses of your character" (Lecture 4). John Gardner's exercise: describe
   a barn as seen by a man whose son has just been killed in a war,
   without mentioning the son, the war or death (*The Art of Fiction*,
   1983) *[seen at second hand]*.
8. **Under- rather than over-articulate.** Bowen: "Speech is what the
   characters do to each other"; "Characters should, on the whole, be
   under rather than over articulate. What they intend to say should be
   more evident … than what they arrive at saying." People deflect,
   mishear, answer a grief with arithmetic, quarrel about the wrong thing,
   don't listen. Charles Baxter's subtext — "the unsaid and unseen" (*The
   Art of Subtext*, 2007) *[seen at second hand]* — and his *request
   moment*: a request that sets a character moving and tests the person
   asked (*Wonderlands*, 2022) *[seen at second hand]*. A book of deals is
   a chain of requests.
9. **Relationships with a history.** In every important exchange the past
   between the two shapes what is said — an old debt, an old affection, a
   grievance neither names — and each pair has its own register. Card:
   "people act differently around different people" (*Characters and
   Viewpoint*, 1988) *[seen at second hand]*.
10. **Secondaries bring their own business.** Orson Scott Card: "I try to
    treat every character who is more than a place holder as the hero of
    his or her own story" (interview, Penguin Random House). Each
    secondary brings into the scene a concern that isn't about the
    protagonist, and it changes how they respond; somewhere they act on
    it out of his sight. Ken Follett, after Trollope: "There's a circle of
    characters and every time there's a development in the story, he goes
    around the circle" (interview, Pan Macmillan, 2023) — at each turn of
    the plot, ask what it means to each of them.
11. **Who knows what — four layers at most.** Lisa Zunshine: novels "rely
    on, manipulate, and titillate our tendency to keep track of who
    thought, wanted, and felt what and when", and intrigue "demands
    outright that we process complexly embedded intentionalities" (*Why
    We Read Fiction*, 2006, pp. 5, 159). Plan the layers ("A wants B to
    think C has already paid"); readers hold about four nested states
    easily and lose the thread beyond that, so set up the deepest layer
    before it pays off.
12. **Owning mistakes.** Characters in "interesting trouble" make
    "interesting mistakes that they may take responsibility for" (Baxter,
    *Burning Down the House*, 1997) *[seen at second hand]*. Things should
    not simply happen to them; the protagonist's own flaw should cause at
    least one of his worst moments. Bernard Cornwell: "perhaps flawed
    characters are more interesting because they are forced to make a
    choice" (2012).
13. **Witness, don't judge.** Chekhov: "The artist must be not the judge
    of his characters and of their conversations, but merely an
    impartial witness" (to Suvorin, 30 May 1888). The narration passes no
    verdict on a character or on the values of their time.

## §5 Real people

- **Invent the inside, not the outside.** Mantel: "From history, I know
  what they do, but I can't with any certainty know what they think or
  feel … I would make up a man's inner torments, but not, for instance,
  the colour of his drawing room wallpaper" (Lecture 1). Forster: the
  novelist's function is "to reveal the hidden life at its source: to
  tell us more about Queen Victoria than could be known" (ch. III).
- **Don't go against the record.** "Don't go against known facts … You
  can select, elide, highlight, omit. Just don't cheat" (Mantel, Lecture
  4). The record is full of contradictions — the usurer who builds houses
  for the poor, the reformer's protector who collects relics — and those
  are depth already true. Mark invention in the dossier so the drafter
  knows which is which.
- **Ask who is telling you.** A chronicler devoted to one family, an
  enemy pamphleteer, a diplomat writing home: each wanted something
  believed. Use the bias; don't inherit it.
- **The period mind.** "A good novelist will have her characters operate
  within the ethical framework of their day — even if it shocks her
  readers" (Mantel, Lecture 2). Their choices follow from their own
  beliefs about salvation, rank and oath.
- **Walk forward in ignorance.** The viewpoint "is ignorant of the
  future, and free" (Mantel, Lecture 4): he knows only what could be
  known that day. "Privilege what matters to your characters, not just
  what has proved in hindsight to be important."
- **Surprise in how and why.** Dorothy Dunnett: the historical frame
  leaves no leeway for major characters to change course, but "scope for
  them to display their given (and changing) characteristics in
  unexpected ways" *[seen at second hand]*. The record fixes what a real
  person does; the novel can surprise in how and why.
- **Against the reputation.** Robert Harris on Chamberlain: "much tougher
  than people realise, a wiry, obstinate fellow. Not at all the weakling
  with the umbrella" (*The Spectator*, 2017). A real person reduced to the
  line an encyclopedia gives them — the miser, the tyrant, the
  richest man — is a type with a name. Give them the ordinary: health,
  money worries, family quarrels, appetite, merriment.
- **Their own words, plainly.** A recorded saying in the person's own
  mouth, in the book's register, is worth a page of description.

## §6 Ensembles and returning characters

- **Distinguish by want and price, then by one fresh detail.** Seven
  princes are seven different wants (cash in hand, the crown for
  himself, to be left alone) before they are seven faces; give each one
  physical or verbal detail from the record and keep it. Truby: "Make
  sure each of the major characters deals with the same moral problem,
  but in a different way" (ch. 5).
- **Tags that carry meaning.** Tolstoy's little princess has a short
  upper lip "on which a delicate dark down was just perceptible"; it
  recurs as she talks, is on her dead face, and years later her son
  "quite unconsciously lifted the upper lip of his pretty little mouth
  just as the little princess used to do" (*War and Peace*, Maude trans.).
  A tag identifies the person at once and, used well, comes back at a
  moment that matters. It is never their only behaviour.
- **Re-anchor on a long return, never twice.** A person returning after
  a long absence (three or more chapters away, counting every chapter in
  between whatever its time or strand, or about 20,000 words) is
  re-anchored within two sentences, in a few words: the tag plus their
  relation to the viewpoint and what they want. Someone the reader saw a
  chapter or two ago is not introduced again: repeated introductions read
  as a roll call, and cold readers of a long book name them as the thing
  that made them skim. When an ensemble comes on stage, give each member
  their line on the group's first few appearances, until each has been
  shown about three times; then they stand alone. A reader who meets
  seven princes in the first chapter will not remember them four
  chapters later without help, and will not need it by the fourth
  meeting. A place or a term is explained once in the book.
- **A cast list.** Customary in historical novels with large casts:
  *Wolf Hall*'s is grouped by place and time, one line each, often giving
  the person's relation to the protagonist. It helps a reader who wants
  to check, and it is what lets the narration stop re-introducing
  people; it is not a substitute for re-anchoring someone back after a
  long absence. `/autonovel:appendix --sections cast` writes one.

# Part II — The tests (C1–C12)

`/autonovel:cast` records a verdict for each principal; `evaluate`'s
`character_depth` and `structure-review` ask the same questions of the
draft; the first reader's "Who felt real" is the cold check.

- **C1 Own want.** In every scene they are in, they want something that is
  not the protagonist's want, and it shows in what they ask for, hide,
  refuse or reach for. Target for principals: no scene without one.
- **C2 Contradiction acted.** One named contradiction, acting in at least
  three scenes, consistent with the rest of them.
- **C3 Stress, not tic.** A pathological maneuver with a cause and a cost
  that worsens with pressure. Tic audit: list each person's recurring
  behaviours and phrases; any that repeats with no change in meaning,
  cause or consequence fails, as does a person whose tag is their only
  distinctive behaviour.
- **C4 Choice ladder.** A costly choice in each act, each costing more
  than the last, options narrowing; name the choice in each chapter or
  flag its absence.
- **C5 Hinterland** (viewpoint characters). Each chapter's interior has
  at least three of feeling, memory, desire apart from the scheme,
  opinion, fear, misreading, and one pulls against the plan; a chapter
  whose interior is more than about three-quarters calculation fails.
- **C6 Moral need.** The protagonist is hurting someone at the start, and
  his own flaw causes at least one of his worst moments.
- **C7 The web.** Each principal is set against at least two others
  (alike, opposite, history, the thing never understood) and takes a
  different stance on the central question; no two share temperament and
  stance; the opponent wants the same thing as the hero.
- **C8 No satellites.** Each secondary pursues an aim of their own and
  would have something to do if the protagonist left the room.
- **C9 Perception and voice.** They notice what only they would and name
  it in their own words; in charged talk they say less than they mean;
  cover the speaker tags and the reader still knows who is talking.
- **C10 Surprise** (Forster). One convincing surprise per principal over
  the book; none means flat, an unconvincing one means a flat pretending
  to be round.
- **C11 Return.** Every principal and ensemble player has a return tag; a
  cold reader can place anyone back after three or more chapters away
  within two sentences; nobody seen in the last two chapters is
  introduced again; the tag recurs at a moment of meaning.
- **C12 From the record** (real people). The record and the gap are
  separated and the sources' biases named; invention sits only in the
  gap and is marked; the period mind drives their choices; no hindsight;
  more than their reputation.
